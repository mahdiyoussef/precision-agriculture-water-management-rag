18    Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

19    Tile drainage systems are sometimes not sufficient to provide favorable unsaturated [SOURCE: Page_2]

20    conditions in the rootzone. These drainage systems then need to be supplemented with an [SOURCE: Page_2]

21    additional high conductivity material in the trenches above the tiles or by implementing mole [SOURCE: Page_2]

1 [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   drainage. The HYDRUS (2D/3D) model was used to evaluate the impact of such additional [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   measures for heavy clay soil. Three types of drainage systems were simulated: i) tile drains, [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   ii) tile drains with gravel trenches, and iii) tile drains with gravel trenches and mole drains, [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   using either two-dimensional (the former two systems) or three-dimensional (the latter one) [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   transport domains. Three scenarios were considered to test the efficiency of each system: i) [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   time to drain an initially saturated system, ii) high intensity rainfall, and iii) a real case [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   scenario. Different horizontal spacings between tile drains with or without gravel trenches [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   were also compared with the system which included mole drainage. The results showed that [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   the drainage system that included mole drains and gravel trenches was the most efficient. This [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   system provided the largest drainage rate, was the first to reach steady-state in the time to [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   drain scenario, and also efficiently reduced surface runoff. Adding mole drains to a system [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   with tile drains and gravel trenches resulted in a large reduction of surface runoff (75%). [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   Simulations showed that the spacing of tile drains with or without gravel trenches would have [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   to be 40% or 55% smaller, respectively, in order to reproduce the same water table levels as [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   those observed for the drainage system with mole drains. Therefore, introducing mole drains [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   in drainage systems is an efficient practice for reducing waterlogging and runoff. [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   Keywords: Tile drainage; Mole drainage; Numerical simulation; Heavy soil; Three- [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   dimensional modeling [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   Soil drainage systems aim at limiting saturated conditions in the soil profile that can arise due [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   to hydrological processes from above (downward water percolation) and from below [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   (elevated groundwater). The main goal of drainage systems is to remove excess water and [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   maintain favorable unsaturated conditions in the rootzone. The most common agricultural [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   drainage system consists of perforated PVC tile drains installed in soil at various spacing’s 2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   and depths, depending on soil hydraulic properties, climatic conditions, and cultivated crops. [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   When designing drainage systems, it is crucial to increase the hydraulic functioning of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   entire system. These systems are mostly installed in soils with high clay content, i.e., heavy [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   soils with very low hydraulic permeability (Tuli et al., 2005). In such soils, subsurface [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   drainage systems can substantially reduce surface runoff, shorten periods of surface ponding, [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   and lower water table (Konyha et al., 1992; Skaggs et al., 1994). Under certain conditions, [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   including high groundwater table, large and intensive precipitation, and heavy-textured soils, [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   the installation of tile drains may not be sufficient to provide favorable conditions for growing [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   crops. Additional measures may then be needed, such as using a backfill material (gravel) [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   with a high hydraulic conductivity above tile drains or performing mole drainage. The [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   presence of a gravel layer above tile drains up to the tilled layer may increase the efficiency of [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   the entire system by promoting by-pass flow of water from the tilled layer directly into the [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   drains. Mole drains are closely-spaced unlined channels of limited duration that are formed in [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   clay subsoil using a ripper blade with a cylindrical foot, often with an expander, which helps [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   to compact and stabilize the channel walls. Mole drainage has been recommended for heavy [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   soils with low permeability, which would otherwise require a small drain spacing (Hudson et [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   al., 1962). During the construction of the mole channels the foot and expander create a 'leg [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   slot' directly above the mole as well as fissures in the soil upwards from the mole towards the [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   plough layer. These cracks and fissures can promote preferential flow towards mole drains [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   (Leeds-Harrison et al., 1982). Mole drains are intended to improve lateral flow to tile drains [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   and are usually used only in soils with a high clay content (>35%). If established properly and [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   under the right conditions, they may still be operating after five years (Harris, 1984). Tile and [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   mole drains are nonconductive under unsaturated conditions because positive pressure must [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   occur before water can start flowing into them (Stormont and Zhou, 2005). [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   In recent years, numerical models have been developed to simulate water flow in tile-drained [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   soils. One-dimensional (1D) models (e.g. MACRO, Larsbo and Jarvis, 2003) use an [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   approximate analytical solution of Darcy’s equation to account for water loss through the [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   drainage system (e.g., Hooghout, Ernst, or Boussinesq’s equations), while two- or three- [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   dimensional (2D or 3D) models (e.g. HYDRUS (2D/3D), Šimůnek et al., 2006) use the [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   Richards’ equation to explicitly model the dynamics of the water table. In 1D models, the [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   flow to tile drains is implemented as a sink term in the mass balance equation. The [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   Houghoudt's equation is based on the Dupuit-Forschheimer assumptions with corrections for [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   convergence of radial flow near the tiles. When combining the Houghoudt's equation (for the [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   saturated zone) with the Richards equation (for the unsaturated zone), the Houghoudt's [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   equation gives instant lateral fluxes to tile drains, while the Richards equation gives transient [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   fluxes in the unsaturated zone, leading to a rise or drop of the water table. An example of such [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   model is DrainMod, which has been used in many applications. For example, Skaggs et al. [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   (2012) used DrainMod to simulate water flow in a subsurface-drained agricultural field in [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   eastern North Carolina. The performance statistics indicated that the model with calibrated [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   input data accurately predicted daily water table depths, daily drainage rates, and monthly [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   drainage volumes. Singh et al. (2006) calibrated and tested DrainMod on two types of soils in [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   Iowa and used it to simulate the impacts of different designs of subsurface drainage systems. [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   Simulation results suggested that a drainage system designed for a drainage intensity of 4.6 [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   mm d-1 with a drain depth of 1.05 m and a drain spacing of 25 m was sufficient to maximize [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   crop production under the prevailing local agricultural conditions. [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   Water flow and solute transport to tile drains has been also evaluated using the HYDRUS [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   family of codes (Šimůnek et al., 2008). For example, the HYDRUS-2D software package was [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   used by De Vos et al. (2000, 2002) to simulate nitrate transport in a tile-drained layered silt [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   loam soil in a reclaimed Dutch polder, or by Castanheira and Serralheiro (2010) to evaluate [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   the impact of mole drains on salinity of a vertisol under irrigation. [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   Numerical modeling of drainage systems involving both tile and mole drains is much more [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   limited. Snow et al. (2007) used the APSIM-SWIM model to predict drainage rates and runoff [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   in a mole‐tile drained silty loam soil of New Zealand. There was an excellent agreement [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   between simulated and measured drainage, as well as a reasonable agreement between [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   measured and simulated cumulative surface runoff. Armstrong et al. (2000) performed a [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   modeling study on a macroporous clay soil with mole and tile drains, in which they compared [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   four “preferential flow” models (MACRO, CRACK-NP, SIMULAT and PLM) in their ability [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   to simulate isoproturon leaching. MACRO model gave the best results, although globally the [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   simulations showed the difficulty of deriving adequate parameters, even where relatively [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   complete soil physical data were available. A similar study was performed by Besien et al. [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   (1997) on a structured heavy clay soil with tile (0.75 m depth and 50 m spacing) and mole [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   drains (0.5 m depth and 3 m spacing) in which the MACRO model was calibrated and used to [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   investigate the leaching of isoproturon. Madvar et al. (2007) used the SEEP/W model to [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   simulate the hydraulic performance of mole and tile drains in heavy-textured soil. Based on [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   their numerical study the combination of mole and tile drains resulted in economical and [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   hydraulic improvement of the agricultural system. [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   Most of modeling studies considered water flow in one- or two-dimensional soil profiles, [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   either taking into account only tile drainage without mole drainage or mole drainage without [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   tile drainage (e.g., Castanheira and Serralheiro, 2010), but not both in a fully three- [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   dimensional system. This is mainly because mole drains, being installed in a perpendicular [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   direction to tile drains, make the entire system three-dimensional. Since we could not identify [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   any study that simulated water flow in systems with tile and mole drains as a three- [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   dimensional problem, we conducted 3D numerical experiments to evaluate the performance [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   of these systems. In our numerical simulations we considered environmental conditions that [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   are typical for the eastern part of Croatia. Hydromorphic soils in Croatia cover an area of [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   1,618,500 ha, which is approximately one third (29%) of the total area occupied by [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   agricultural soils (Husnjak, 2007). Subsurface tile drainage systems are installed on 161,530 [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   ha (Petošić et al, 2004), with mole drainage often used to improve drainage efficiency. [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   The main objective of this paper was to numerically evaluate the performance of different [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   subsurface drainage systems of increasing complexity in hydromorphic soils using a three- [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   dimensional model that can explicitly account for any drainage system. (i) The simplest [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   system to be considered consists of a layered soil profile with parallel tile drains. Such system [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   can be simulated using a two-dimensional simulation domain that is perpendicular to tile [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   drains. (ii) In the second set of simulations it is assumed that a backfill material (gravel) of [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   higher hydraulic conductivity than the original soil is placed above the drains. Such system [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   can also be evaluated assuming a two-dimensional simulation domain perpendicular to drains. [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   (iii) Finally, the most complex system consists of tile drains with backfilled gravel above [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   drains, combined with perpendicular mole drains. Such system has to be analyzed using a [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   fully three-dimensional flow model. [SOURCE: Page_7]

218   2.3.4. Simulated scenarios [SOURCE: Page_11]

219   Numerical simulations were carried out for each drainage system for three different scenarios [SOURCE: Page_11]

220   with different initial and boundary conditions. In Scenario 1, a "time to drain" scenario was [SOURCE: Page_11]

221   used to compare the drainage response of the three simulated systems, i.e., tile drains, tile [SOURCE: Page_11]

222   drains with gravel trenches, and tile drains with gravel trenches and mole drains, starting from [SOURCE: Page_11]

223   wet soil conditions. The initial pressure head was assumed to be at equilibrium conditions [SOURCE: Page_11]

224   with a positive pressure head of 175 cm at the bottom of the transport domain which left the [SOURCE: Page_11]

225   first 25 cm (corresponding to the tilled layer) unsaturated. No flow boundary condition was [SOURCE: Page_11]

226   considered for all boundaries except for the small semicircle on the left of the transport [SOURCE: Page_11]

227   domain which represented the tile drain (Fig. 1a). A seepage face boundary condition was [SOURCE: Page_11]

228   used to represent the tile drain. Simulation time was equal to 2,400 hours (100 days). [SOURCE: Page_11]

229   However, as there was still some limited outflow after 2,400 hours, the time when drainage [SOURCE: Page_11]

230   was assumed to stop was defined as the time when the drainage rate decreased below 0.001 [SOURCE: Page_11]

231   mm h-1. Simulations for Systems 1 and 2 were also performed in 3D to check if the results [SOURCE: Page_11]

232   were identical to those provided by 2D modeling. [SOURCE: Page_11]

233   In the "rainfall” scenario (scenario 2), the initial conditions were set up as the pressure head [SOURCE: Page_11]

234   distribution from the end of the first scenario (steady state conditions), and a rainfall event [SOURCE: Page_11]

235   (with an intensity of 2 mm/h and a duration set up to get a total of either 50 mm, 100 mm, or 10 [SOURCE: Page_11]

236   150 mm) was applied starting at the first day of simulation. In addition to a seepage face [SOURCE: Page_12]

237   boundary condition representing a tile drain, an atmospheric boundary condition was applied [SOURCE: Page_12]

238   at the top of the transport domain to include rainfall. Evaporation was neglected in this [SOURCE: Page_12]

239   scenario. [SOURCE: Page_12]

240   In the "real case" scenario (scenario 3), 4-year simulations (2009-2012) were performed with [SOURCE: Page_12]

241   meteorological data collected at the Gradište station (45°09' N and 18°42' E) using the same [SOURCE: Page_12]

242   initial conditions as in scenario 2. An atmospheric boundary condition was applied at the top [SOURCE: Page_12]

243   of the transport domain using evapotranspiration values calculated by the Penman-Monteith [SOURCE: Page_12]

244   approach (Monteith, 1981). Transpiration was calculated formaize (Zea Mays L., 2009, 2010, [SOURCE: Page_12]

245   2012) and barley (Hordeum vulgare L., 2011) which were grown at the actual location. [SOURCE: Page_12]

246   Again, a seepage face boundary condition was selected to represent the tile drain. [SOURCE: Page_12]

248   3. Results and discussion [SOURCE: Page_12]

249   3.1. “Time to drain” scenario [SOURCE: Page_12]

250   To evaluate the specific drainage response of each of the three drainage systems, a “time to [SOURCE: Page_12]

251   drain” scenario was performed. The ending time of drainage was reached at 681 h (28.4 days) [SOURCE: Page_12]

252   for the tile system, 594 h (24.7 days) for the tile_gravel_system, and 506 h (21.1 days) for the [SOURCE: Page_12]

253   tile_gravel_mole system. The value corresponding to 90% of the final drainage volume from [SOURCE: Page_12]

254   each system was selected as the time when a quasi steady-state condition was reached. [SOURCE: Page_12]

255   According to this criterion, the systems reached quasi steady-state after cumulated outflow [SOURCE: Page_12]

256   amounts of 4.86 mm, 6.57 mm, 9.18 mm for the tile, tile_gravel, and tile_gravel_mole [SOURCE: Page_12]

257   systems, which corresponded to days 11, 7.1, and 6, respectively (Fig. 2). The difference in [SOURCE: Page_12]

258   time response of the three systems may have a large influence on the crop root system, i.e., [SOURCE: Page_12]

259   the faster the soil is drained the less consequences on the crop root system and yield can be [SOURCE: Page_12]

11 [SOURCE: Page_12]

260   expected (Oosterbaan 1991). The soil horizons had a relatively low permeability and, in the [SOURCE: Page_13]

261   absence of a highly conductive layer, the drainage is very slow. When a gravel trench above [SOURCE: Page_13]

262   the tile is added, the time response is faster (Fig. 2) since the trench is filled up with a material [SOURCE: Page_13]

263   that has a high saturated hydraulic conductivity. The response of the system with moles is [SOURCE: Page_13]

264   fastest since it includes both the trench and the mole drain constructed from the same highly [SOURCE: Page_13]

265   conductive material as the trenches. Since the domain was initially saturated well above the [SOURCE: Page_13]

266   mole drain depth, the response of the system is increasing in the order tile < tile_gravel < [SOURCE: Page_13]

267   tile_gravel_mole. [SOURCE: Page_13]

268   In addition to the time response of each system, a large difference in the cumulative amount [SOURCE: Page_13]

269   of drainage was found. This difference is due to the substitution of soil materials having [SOURCE: Page_13]

270   different water retention properties within the simulation domain. In System 2, parts of the [SOURCE: Page_13]

271   volumes of materials 1, 2, and 3 are replaced by material 5 to represent the gravel trench. In [SOURCE: Page_13]

272   System 3, in addition to the trench, a part of material 2 is replaced by material 5 to represent [SOURCE: Page_13]

273   the mole drain. The difference in water storage between System 1 and System 2 is 1.86 mm [SOURCE: Page_13]

274   and 4.86 mm between System 1 and 3. Material 5 and materials 1, 2, and 3 have very different [SOURCE: Page_13]

275   water retention properties, which explain the different cumulative outflows (Table 2). [SOURCE: Page_13]

276   Inserting a gravel trench above the tile drain increased outflow by 1.86 mm, 90% of it coming [SOURCE: Page_13]

277   from the water initially stored in the trench itself. Adding a mole drain (System 3) further [SOURCE: Page_13]

278   increases the outflow by 3 mm, with more than half of it coming from the gravel material [SOURCE: Page_13]

279   representing the mole drain. From these results one can already expect a significant effect of [SOURCE: Page_13]

280   the mole drain on the recession of the water table. [SOURCE: Page_13]

281   Fig. 3 shows the simulated cross sections of all three systems during the first 24 hours. The [SOURCE: Page_13]

282   results are displayed after 2 and 24 hours. Pressure head values are lower in the tile_gravel [SOURCE: Page_13]

283   system than in the tile system and the lowest in the tile_gravel_mole system. After 2 h of [SOURCE: Page_13]

284   drainage, the influence of the trench in System 2 is limited to the right side of the domain [SOURCE: Page_13]

12 [SOURCE: Page_13]

285   while in System 3 the effect is expanding more to the left side because of the presence of the [SOURCE: Page_14]

286   mole drain. After 24 h, there is a large drop in the water table depth for all three systems in [SOURCE: Page_14]

287   the order (high to low) tile > tile_gravel > tile_gravel_mole. The mean pressure head after 24 [SOURCE: Page_14]

288   hours in the domain above the tile depth (from the surface down to 100 cm) was -75 cm, -78 [SOURCE: Page_14]

289   cm, and -84 cm for the tile, tile_gravel, and tile_gravel_mole system, respectively. These [SOURCE: Page_14]

290   results indicate the higher efficiency of the tile_gravel_mole system in reducing the water [SOURCE: Page_14]

291   table height compared to the tile_gravel system. [SOURCE: Page_14]

292   3.2. High intensity rainfall scenario [SOURCE: Page_14]

293   High intensity rainfall events with an intensity of 2 mm per hour and various durations, to get [SOURCE: Page_14]

294   total amounts of 50 mm, 100 mm, or 150 mm, were applied in the second scenario. The initial [SOURCE: Page_14]

295   condition was set equal to the final pressure head distribution at the end of the first scenario, [SOURCE: Page_14]

296   i.e., the surface pressure head was -108 cm and the bottom pressure head was 92 cm on the [SOURCE: Page_14]

297   right side of the domain. The cumulative outflow increased with the rainfall intensity in all [SOURCE: Page_14]

298   three systems (Fig. 4). In this scenario, the trench and mole drain were not initially saturated [SOURCE: Page_14]

299   as in the previous scenario, which led to the slower response time at the beginning of the [SOURCE: Page_14]

300   experiment. The gravel material (representing the trench and mole) was not initially saturated [SOURCE: Page_14]

301   and the time needed for its saturation induced a delay in the system response, which can be [SOURCE: Page_14]

302   best seen for the tile_gravel_mole system during the first 4 hours (Fig. 4). There is no outflow [SOURCE: Page_14]

303   during the first 4 hours in System 3 since the mole drain needs to be saturated to trigger [SOURCE: Page_14]

304   drainage. The tile system without a gravel layer became almost instantaneously saturated [SOURCE: Page_14]

305   since the rest of the domain had a very low permeability (5 mm h-1). [SOURCE: Page_14]

306   The low permeability of the soil promotes runoff during high intensity rainfalls. In Fig. 5, the [SOURCE: Page_14]

307   total amount of surface runoff is presented for each system. One can see that the cumulative [SOURCE: Page_14]

308   amount of runoff increases according to rainfall intensity, while the opposite was found for [SOURCE: Page_14]

309   cumulative outflow (Fig. 4). The largest runoff occurs in the tile system, then in the 13 [SOURCE: Page_14]

310   tile_gravel system, and the smallest in the tile_gravel_mole system. The mole drain was very [SOURCE: Page_15]

311   efficient in reducing surface runoff. These results are in agreement with several studies, in [SOURCE: Page_15]

312   which authors found that artificial drainage increases the amount of infiltrating water and [SOURCE: Page_15]

313   reduces runoff (e.g., Bengtson et al., 1995). [SOURCE: Page_15]

314   Mass balance information for all three system and three rainfall events is shown in Table 3. [SOURCE: Page_15]

315   Table 3 shows that the effectiveness of mole drainage in reducing surface runoff is not only [SOURCE: Page_15]

316   due to a better efficiency in promoting drainage outflow, but also due to a better capacity for [SOURCE: Page_15]

317   water storage than for the other two drainage systems. [SOURCE: Page_15]

318   3.3. Real case scenario [SOURCE: Page_15]

319   For the real case scenario, agroecological conditions that correspond to East Croatia were [SOURCE: Page_15]

320   taken into account to be able to explain the behavior of each system in real field conditions. [SOURCE: Page_15]

321   Fig. 6a shows the cumulative outflow during 2009-2012 period. At the beginning, one can see [SOURCE: Page_15]

322   that the order of drainage relative to cumulative outflow is a bit different from what was [SOURCE: Page_15]

323   found in first two scenarios in which the tile_gravel_mole system had the largest outflow. [SOURCE: Page_15]

324   This effect is due to the small rainfall amounts at the beginning of the simulated time period [SOURCE: Page_15]

325   which were drained by the tile_gravel system, but not by the tile_gravel_mole system, [SOURCE: Page_15]

326   because mole drains did not become saturated enough to trigger outflow. Long term [SOURCE: Page_15]

327   simulations showed the opposite effect on the 1st of June 2010 when a high intensity rainfall [SOURCE: Page_15]

328   event (72.8 mm) saturated mole drains and promoted a large outflow from the [SOURCE: Page_15]

329   tile_gravel_mole system, while in the same time other two systems generated low outflow and [SOURCE: Page_15]

330   large surface runoff (Fig. 6b). Adding gravel in the trench above a drain resulted in small [SOURCE: Page_15]

331   decrease of surface runoff by (1%) However, inserting a mole drain into the system decreased [SOURCE: Page_15]

332   the runoff by an additional 75%. The tile drain system with gravel and mole drains effectively [SOURCE: Page_15]

333   reduced surface runoff in real field conditions. In such situation, the tile_gravel system cannot [SOURCE: Page_15]

14 [SOURCE: Page_15]

334   conduct such large amounts of water as the system with moles, which leads to waterlogging [SOURCE: Page_16]

335   conditions at the surface or the presence of a high water table. [SOURCE: Page_16]

337   3.4. Drain spacing [SOURCE: Page_16]

338   Finally, simulations with different tile drain spacings were performed in order to compare the [SOURCE: Page_16]

339   water table variations for each system and to try to obtain, either with only tile drains or tile [SOURCE: Page_16]

340   drains with backfilled trenches, the same water table level as for System 3. The simulation set [SOURCE: Page_16]

341   up e.g., initial and boundary conditions was taken from the “time to drain” scenario. The tile [SOURCE: Page_16]

342   with gravel trenches and mole system had the same spacing of 15 m as in above studied [SOURCE: Page_16]

343   scenarios. The tile and tile_gravel systems were set up with various spacings from 6 to 12 m. [SOURCE: Page_16]

344   Figs. 7a and 7b show the water table elevation during 96 h in the middle between two tile [SOURCE: Page_16]

345   drains for the three systems (numbers 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12 corresponds to the drain spacing in [SOURCE: Page_16]

346   meters). At the beginning, there is a rapid decrease in water table depths in all three systems, [SOURCE: Page_16]

347   clearly visible during the first 24 hours. After that, the decrease is smaller and starts to [SOURCE: Page_16]

348   stabilize as time approaches the end of simulation. The tile_gravel system was more efficient [SOURCE: Page_16]

349   than the tile system at each spacing in lowering the water table (by ~2 cm). To reproduce the [SOURCE: Page_16]

350   same water table level after 96 h (-100.2 cm) as in the tile_gravel_mole system, one would [SOURCE: Page_16]

351   need to lower the spacing of the tile_gravel system to 9 m, or to 7 m if the system without [SOURCE: Page_16]

352   gravel is used. System 1 and 2 with a 6-m spacing were able to lower the water table more [SOURCE: Page_16]

353   efficiently than System 3 with a 15-m tile drain spacing and a 2-m mole drain spacing. [SOURCE: Page_16]

354   However, a system with such small tile drain spacing is not economically sustainable. A small [SOURCE: Page_16]

355   tile drain spacing is mostly used only in golf course construction or in civil engineering. Since [SOURCE: Page_16]

356   the construction of mole drains requires only a simple equipment (i.e., a special kind of [SOURCE: Page_16]

357   plough) (Hopkins 2002), this system could significantly lower the overall cost of the drainage [SOURCE: Page_16]

358   system. [SOURCE: Page_16]

360   4. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_17]

361   Numerical experiments have been performed for three different drainage systems: i) tile [SOURCE: Page_17]

362   drains, ii) tile drains with gravel trenches, and iii) tile drains with gravel trenches and mole [SOURCE: Page_17]

363   drains. The tile drain system showed in all scenarios the slowest outflow response and the [SOURCE: Page_17]

364   smallest cumulative outflow. When compared with the other two systems, the drainage [SOURCE: Page_17]

365   system with tile drains, gravel trenches, and mole drains provides the largest efficiency. [SOURCE: Page_17]

366   Under real case scenario, the system with tile drains and gravel trenches had the fastest [SOURCE: Page_17]

367   reaction time and provided drainage outflow for small intensity rainfalls. However, the system [SOURCE: Page_17]

368   with tile drains, gravel trenches and mole drains was able to provide larger outflow during [SOURCE: Page_17]

369   higher intensity rainfall (>15 mm). Adding mole drains to a system with tile drains and gravel [SOURCE: Page_17]

370   trenches resulted in a very large reduction of surface runoff (75%), which can have a very [SOURCE: Page_17]

371   important effect during high intensity rainfalls. Since the use of the drainage systems with [SOURCE: Page_17]

372   mole drains is still limited to special agro ecological conditions, there is only limited [SOURCE: Page_17]

373   extensive field data available which allow to test fully three-dimensional simulations of these [SOURCE: Page_17]

374   particular drainage systems. Research needs to be expanded through field trials in order to [SOURCE: Page_17]

375   have more information on water flow dynamics in such complex systems. [SOURCE: Page_17]
