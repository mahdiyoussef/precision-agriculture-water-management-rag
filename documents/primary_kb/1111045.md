Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

Estimating the response time of a canal is essential for the open-loop [SOURCE: Page_2]

control of an irrigation canal, since upstream flow releases must be [SOURCE: Page_2]

anticipated in order to satisfy scheduled demands at irrigation outlets. [SOURCE: Page_2]

We consider a flow release at the upstream end of a pool in order to [SOURCE: Page_2]

satisfy a side withdrawal at its downstream end. When theflow is [SOURCE: Page_2]

released, wave travel time causes the flow change to arrive some time [SOURCE: Page_2]

later downstream and attenuation causes the flow to arrive gradually [SOURCE: Page_2]

downstream, such that the peak discharge is further delayed. A clear [SOURCE: Page_2]

definition of this response time is proposed, based on volume [SOURCE: Page_2]

compensation. A linear approach is used to calculate the canal response [SOURCE: Page_2]

to a flow release and a withdrawal, and then the volume passing at the [SOURCE: Page_2]

downstream end of the canal. The approach provides an analytical [SOURCE: Page_2]

determination of the time of opening that ensures volume compensation. [SOURCE: Page_2]

A practical method to derive this response time is proposed. It is [SOURCE: Page_2]

illustrated for a canal for which different downstream boundary [SOURCE: Page_2]

conditions are imposed. [SOURCE: Page_2]

INTRODUCTION The timing of the delay in a canal with scheduled water deliveries is essential. If the [SOURCE: Page_3]

scheduled water is released too early, excess water is lost, and if it is released too late, the [SOURCE: Page_3]

delivery is not efficient. This timing is difficult to estimate because it may be influenced by [SOURCE: Page_3]

the hydraulic condition of the canal. The backwater curve and the downstream boundary [SOURCE: Page_3]

condition influence the time delay of a canal pool. These effects were illustrated by Strelkoff [SOURCE: Page_3]

et al. (1998) through a simulation study of the influence of hydraulic structures on the delay [SOURCE: Page_3]

of a canal pool. Several studies have attempted to define and calculate the response time of a [SOURCE: Page_3]

pool [e.g.,Munier et al. (2010)]. Schuurmans (1990) proposed a method to calculate the time [SOURCE: Page_3]

for downstream response to reach any proportion of the total increase, while other authors [SOURCE: Page_3]

give a single value for this response time, based on maximum increase (Ankum, 1995) or [SOURCE: Page_3]

dynamic storage (Schuurmans et al., 1995 ; Bautista et al., 2003). Munier et al. (2010) have [SOURCE: Page_3]

proposed a method to compute the response time of a canal pool based on a first order with [SOURCE: Page_3]

delay model that takes into account the backwater curve and the downstream boundary [SOURCE: Page_3]

condition imposed by a hydraulic structure. However, this study chose to use the response [SOURCE: Page_3]

time at 90%, corresponding to the time when the discharge increase has reached 90% of its [SOURCE: Page_3]

final value. This choice was rather arbitrary. [SOURCE: Page_3]

When the upstream flow is increased in order to deliver water to a lateral outlet, the [SOURCE: Page_3]

discharge in the canal will decrease as soon as the outlet gate is opened. One may then try to [SOURCE: Page_3]

find the proper time in order to deliver water to the outlet without affecting too much the flow [SOURCE: Page_3]

in the canal. One way is to ensure that the total volume of water downstream from the gate [SOURCE: Page_3]

remains constant. If the gate is opened too early, e.g. just as the wave arrives, more flow will [SOURCE: Page_3]

be extracted than has arrived, and thus decreasing the amount of water available downstream. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Conversely, if the outlet gate is opened too late, there will be an excess of flow in the main [SOURCE: Page_3]

channel. [SOURCE: Page_3]

time for the open-loop routing of demand changes. The results are then compared with a full [SOURCE: Page_3]

These results suggest an improved definition of the response time, based on the compensation [SOURCE: Page_16]

Different response times are used in practice, such as the travel time of the diffusive wave [SOURCE: Page_16]

(long waves), the dynamic storage time, the travel time of the gravity waves (short-waves), [SOURCE: Page_16]

the time of maximum flow increase. The principle of volume compensation gives a more [SOURCE: Page_16]

precise definition. It leads to improved distribution efficiency as it specifies that the whole [SOURCE: Page_16]

released volume is given to the targeted outlet. [SOURCE: Page_16]

Effect of the boundary condition [SOURCE: Page_16]

The downstream boundary condition largely affects the canal response. In the case of uniform [SOURCE: Page_16]

flow, the diffusive wave celerity is easily calculated. However, these results show that [SOURCE: Page_16]

water level structure (long-crested weir, automated check gate, hydromechanical gate). In this [SOURCE: Page_16]
