### 1. Building socio-environmental scenarios

1.1 Combining biophysical determinisms with actors’ deliberations in the DPSIR model [SOURCE: Page_7]

Although the evolution of environmental systems is not purely deterministic, determinist [SOURCE: Page_7]

models have previously proved to be powerful tools in better understanding their biophysical [SOURCE: Page_7]

behaviour. However, managers are not always at ease with the use of biophysical models. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Parson (2008) argued that models failed to produce useful outputs for decision makers when [SOURCE: Page_7]

the latter were mere end-users. Such a gap between science and management has become a [SOURCE: Page_7]

growing source of concern for EU governance bodies, notably the European Commission, [SOURCE: Page_7]

which seeks support for policy design from managers and scientists (Robert and Vauchez, [SOURCE: Page_7]

2010). In this context, environmental scenarios have become a key method of translating [SOURCE: Page_7]

integrated assessment models into understandable policy options (Alcamo, 2008; Bailey, [SOURCE: Page_7]

1997; Mahmoud et al., 2009; Therond et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_7]

Scientists producing such scenarios use narratives to imagine how socio-economic drivers [SOURCE: Page_8]

could affect biophysical systems. The SAS method revolves around iterative adjustments [SOURCE: Page_8]

between storylines developed by participatory panels and model simulations (Alcamo, 2001; [SOURCE: Page_8]

2008). Iterations are designed to produce consistent scenarios that integrate qualitative and [SOURCE: Page_8]

al., 2007). By combining determinisms and deliberation, scientists aim to integrate [SOURCE: Page_8]

disciplines, improve communication on complex issues, support the comparison of policy [SOURCE: Page_8]

options, test the robustness of policies, raise awareness about emerging problems, and support [SOURCE: Page_8]

stakeholder involvement at the interface between science and decision making (Alcamo, [SOURCE: Page_8]

2008; Duinker and Greig, 2007; O’Neill et al., 2008). Despite critiques cited in the [SOURCE: Page_8]

introduction (Kallis et al., 2006), proponents of the SAS method argue that it increases the [SOURCE: Page_8]

legitimacy, credibility, salience, relevance, and creativity of scenarios (Alcamo, 2008; Hulme [SOURCE: Page_8]

and Dessai, 2008; Parson, 2008). They advocate a dual improvement: involving more [SOURCE: Page_8]

stakeholders, and making the process more explicit, through the use of causal loop diagrams, [SOURCE: Page_8]

mind mapping, and quantitative models. This in turn means that environmental science and [SOURCE: Page_8]

policy specialists find themselves confronted with representations that are difficult to share [SOURCE: Page_8]

with outsiders. In this context, the DPSIR model is extremely useful in assessing future [SOURCE: Page_8]

options with a limited set of variables. However, few scholars have reflected on the strong [SOURCE: Page_8]

premises in a framework such as the DSPIR model. Indeed, this model draws implicitly upon [SOURCE: Page_8]

the analogy of gravity acting on a pair of scales (see Figure 2). It represents social practices as [SOURCE: Page_8]

deterministic inputs – pressures–, and does not discuss the specific scale used to manage the [SOURCE: Page_8]

environment. We believe that the social practices and the choice of scale deserve greater [SOURCE: Page_8]

attention. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The term “pressures” implies that relations between human practices and environmental [SOURCE: Page_9]

redress the balance, but does not take into account the various political processes and contexts [SOURCE: Page_9]

causal relations, “most human decisions, most political decisions involve (…) non-necessary [SOURCE: Page_9]

conceal conflicting accounts and responsibilities relating to environmental degradation. [SOURCE: Page_9]

The Pressures-State-Impacts part of the model deals with one definition of environmental [SOURCE: Page_9]

degradation, whereas different groups have different interpretations. On the Garonne River, [SOURCE: Page_9]

for example, some would argue that eutrophication relates to the intensive production of corn, [SOURCE: Page_9]

while others would contend that corn production is a given and eutrophication relates to [SOURCE: Page_9]

insufficient summer flows for diluting unavoidable seepages. Forsyth’s notion of problem [SOURCE: Page_9]

what is not, and what are the relevant boundaries to define a specific problem. The DPSIR [SOURCE: Page_9]

framework does not open the problem-closure to a pluralistic discussion, because the [SOURCE: Page_9]

alignment of political scales with physical ones is problematic. This relates to Hart’s broader [SOURCE: Page_9]

critique of the “impact model” in which “time appears as an active force, and space as a [SOURCE: Page_9]

passive recipient or container” (Hart, 2002 :49). In the DPSIR model, time is only considered [SOURCE: Page_9]

within the temporality of the biophysical process at stake. The model ignores the fact that [SOURCE: Page_9]

environmental systems — notably in European contexts — are historically co-produced by [SOURCE: Page_9]

empowerment, and social learning, possibly changing their values. Most environmental [SOURCE: Page_10]

earlier stakeholders with different perspectives become involved in the process, the better they [SOURCE: Page_10]

take ownership of the method. For instance, Wilkinson & Eidinow (2008) seek consensus [SOURCE: Page_10]

There are at least two ways to challenge the DPSIR approach in order to maintain pluralistic [SOURCE: Page_12]

scholars have addressed how things could have been different had expertise and power [SOURCE: Page_12]

developed differently, but they scarcely engage themselves in building scenarios for planning [SOURCE: Page_12]

water management. [SOURCE: Page_12]

Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_35]

The Driving forces-Pressures-State-Impact-Response (DPSIR) approach has become [SOURCE: Page_35]

hegemonic in European environmental policies, notably for designing scenarios for water-use [SOURCE: Page_35]

planning. This approach silences conflicts over the political environmental agenda and [SOURCE: Page_35]

territorial claims, while developing indicators that match specific problems and areas of [SOURCE: Page_35]

responsibility. It portrays systems as separated from any social or historical fabric, and hides [SOURCE: Page_35]

the political work it actually performs. Based on the “gravity” analogy, the DPSIR model [SOURCE: Page_35]

claims to be neutral, whereas in reality it is highly sensitive to spatial boundaries. In this [SOURCE: Page_35]

model, certain social practices and political choices become seemingly inescapable pressures [SOURCE: Page_35]

or driving forces, while other practices need to be adjusted. This kind of framing might be [SOURCE: Page_35]

useful in supporting a top-down decision making process, but it obviously narrows the scope [SOURCE: Page_35]

of debate when it comes to imagining future scenarios for water use planning. [SOURCE: Page_35]

More specifically, the DPSIR model is rooted in three basic assumptions, which are [SOURCE: Page_35]

questionable if the framework is to be used to support public debate. First, the model [SOURCE: Page_35]

considers anonymous and atemporal forces, as if identifying the stakeholders behind such [SOURCE: Page_36]

forces did not matter. Likewise, no attention is paid to when they took decisions resulting in [SOURCE: Page_36]

the current environmental state, nor is any emphasis placed on what benefit they derived from [SOURCE: Page_36]

it. [SOURCE: Page_36]

Secondly, the model assumes that environmental degradation can be compensated. It excludes [SOURCE: Page_36]

Finally, it considers that the system is bound by watershed limits. There is however no such [SOURCE: Page_36]

element among others in several systems. Scholars using the DPSIR framework usually [SOURCE: Page_36]
