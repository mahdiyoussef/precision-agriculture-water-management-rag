Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

Water governance is a complex process to manage and improve due to the large numbers of competing scales [SOURCE: Page_2]

and levels inherent within it. Effectively supporting any water governance process does not just rely on [SOURCE: Page_2]

hydrological knowledge of a water system, but much broader trans‐disciplinary understanding of a whole social‐ [SOURCE: Page_2]

hydrological system. In this paper we show how there are commonly up to eight or more competing scales— [SOURCE: Page_2]

spatial, temporal, administrative, institutional, management, network, knowledge/information and [SOURCE: Page_2]

stakes/issues—present in any water governance situation. Processes in one scale can then create flows or [SOURCE: Page_2]

externalities that produce multi‐level, cross‐scale or rescaling effects. A typology of six types of externalities or [SOURCE: Page_2]

flows is provided—physical or material flows (e.g. water and solute movement), information and knowledge, [SOURCE: Page_2]

political and social control, financial flows, human flows, and an irreversibility effect (e.g. flows, such as that of [SOURCE: Page_2]

time passing, which lead to cumulative effects that are difficult or impossible to reverse)—and used to study the [SOURCE: Page_2]

examples provided in the 15 papers of this special issue. From these insights, conclusions are drawn on how [SOURCE: Page_2]

conflicts across competing scales are currently being managed, the mechanisms involved in bridging scales, and [SOURCE: Page_2]

to what extent currently privileged scales and/or governance structures are appropriate for effectively managing [SOURCE: Page_2]

water and land. The two general trends observed for multi‐level and cross‐scale management remain the need [SOURCE: Page_2]

for collaborative governance processes and facilitators of these interactions, as well as the development of savvy [SOURCE: Page_2]

coalitions for water governance change who understand and can effectively navigate these complex multi‐level [SOURCE: Page_2]

and multi‐scalar systems. There is also a need to recognise that water basins or watersheds are social [SOURCE: Page_2]

constructions and not always the most effective arenas for water governance. Problemsheds based on concepts [SOURCE: Page_2]

such as the French “territoire” (territory), may in fact prove more pertinent in many cases (e.g. highly [SOURCE: Page_2]

engineered systems) due to the unequivocal social nature of their construction and ability to delineate a range [SOURCE: Page_2]

of agreed upon social‐hydrological systems for management. [SOURCE: Page_2]

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Water governance is becoming an increasingly important area of study for hydrologists, as the impacts of human [SOURCE: Page_3]

decisions on water flows and their various management scales are recognised. Hydrology has long tackled issues [SOURCE: Page_3]

of water flow and quality across basins—from rain to soil and sub‐soil, from upstream to downstream, between [SOURCE: Page_3]

surface water and groundwater systems, and through interlinked watersheds—with the understanding that [SOURCE: Page_3]

these stocks and flows can be modified en‐route due to the actions of people, including through organised water [SOURCE: Page_3]

management and governance processes. In this setting, one common aim of water governance is to develop [SOURCE: Page_3]

management processes and infrastructure systems that can control hydrological variability at different levels of [SOURCE: Page_3]

spatial and temporal scales. For example, water storages, distribution systems and drainage networks are [SOURCE: Page_3]

developed for long‐term seasonal and inter‐decadal variability—in the case of large dams and irrigation [SOURCE: Page_3]

systems—as well as shorter‐term variability, such as flooding events, that may take place over hours (e.g. urban [SOURCE: Page_3]

flash floods), days (e.g. catchment‐based river flooding) or months (e.g. basin‐wide flood‐plain inundation [SOURCE: Page_3]

events). Particularly when looking at water supply issues, water allocation rules are elaborated and negotiated in [SOURCE: Page_3]

order to provide water to people when and where they most need it, rather than when and where it would [SOURCE: Page_3]

naturally be available. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Such decisions on water governance systems have an impact on the capacity of people and institutions to make [SOURCE: Page_3]

autonomous decisions about water and land management, both within basins and in their interlinked social‐ [SOURCE: Page_3]

ecological systems: what we will term in this paper the governance of “social‐hydrological systems”. Water [SOURCE: Page_3]

governance affects, and is equally affected by, decision processes in a variety of other areas such as crop [SOURCE: Page_3]

selection, land use policies, energy systems and environmental management. In other words, there is a range of [SOURCE: Page_3]

externalities created by decisions and actions that may lead to problems for other people at different levels or [SOURCE: Page_3]

scales in the governance system. Possible conflicts between the aforementioned decision areas are typically [SOURCE: Page_3]

handled at a range of administrative and institutional scales (linked to geographical regions), which often do not [SOURCE: Page_3]

fit watershed or groundwater reserve boundaries. Conflicts can also occur between jurisdictions and a range of [SOURCE: Page_3]

## 2. DEFINITIONS AND SCALE DESCRIPTIONS

2.1 Scales and levels of organisation [SOURCE: Page_5]

Scale is a commonly used word in many disciplines, including hydrology, as well as in daily language. It is [SOURCE: Page_5]

therefore subject to frequent use without exact definition, which leads to it being used with different meanings [SOURCE: Page_5]

and makes scholarship on scale issues challenging. In the water sector, there is a constant confusion between [SOURCE: Page_5]

scale and levels of organization. We should be rigorous on these definitions, although we note that multiple [SOURCE: Page_5]

definitions that do not match those below are possible (Sayre, 2005). Firstly, levels can be considered as an [SOURCE: Page_5]

extension of scale as a graduated range. Values constituting a whole range defining a scale can be defined within [SOURCE: Page_5]

a continuous set, or a non‐continuous one. The whole range is then a set of classes, which are also named [SOURCE: Page_5]

“levels” or “levels of organization”. In the field of governance, both organisation levels and scales have been [SOURCE: Page_5]

used increasingly interchangeably, leading to some confusion. In this paper, we will use the term “scale” when [SOURCE: Page_5]

dealing with a graduated range of extent, and levels of organisation when dealing with non‐continuous classes. [SOURCE: Page_5]

For more in‐depth discussion on these terms and more, see Gibson et al. (2000). [SOURCE: Page_5]

However, it is also important to acknowledge a second definition of scale from the Oxford Dictionary as “The [SOURCE: Page_5]

relative size or extent of something” as this will be important when we discuss some of the authors’ papers, [SOURCE: Page_5]

especially when issues of the subjective construction of scale, or “re‐scaling”, processes come up. [SOURCE: Page_5]

“levels” can be defined. For example, a temporal scale may include the levels of hours, days, weeks, years, [SOURCE: Page_5]

centuries etc. (i.e. different lengths of time) or a spatial scale may include the levels of ecosystem patch, [SOURCE: Page_5]

catchment, river‐basin or eco‐region, and biome (i.e. different area sizes of geographical space). Expanding on [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 2.2.1. Spatial scale

Space is one of the most common dimensions, along with time (discussed next), that is most commonly [SOURCE: Page_7]

considered in hydrological studies and water governance more generally (e.g. Klemes, 1983; Bergstrom, 1998; [SOURCE: Page_7]

Sivapalan et al., 2004; Merz et al., 2009; Syme et al., 2012). Understanding the spatial scale, and how processes [SOURCE: Page_7]

at different levels on it interact, has also been a major focus of other related disciplines such as ecology (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Levin, 1992; Peterson and Parker, 1996), sustainability studies (e.g. Dovers, 2004); geography (e.g. Harvey, 1969; [SOURCE: Page_7]

Meentemeyer, 1989; Turner et al., 1989), sociology (e.g. Tilly, 1984; Coleman, 1990) and environmental politics [SOURCE: Page_7]

or political ecology (e.g. Swyngedouw, 1997; Brenner, 2001), and to a lesser extent in economics (e.g. Veldkamp [SOURCE: Page_7]

et al., 2011). For water governance and hydrology, the spatial scale can be divided into a range of different series [SOURCE: Page_7]

of levels based on typography or other definitions of areas (e.g. watersheds/ecosystems; administrative areas [SOURCE: Page_7]

(see also 2.3); areas of flow‐dynamics—from the micro molecular level to the macro flood‐plain flow level). [SOURCE: Page_7]

There are also strong debates in and across disciplines about the importance and existence of specific spatial [SOURCE: Page_7]

levels, depending on epistemological and ontological positioning, about the extent to which certain levels “exist” [SOURCE: Page_7]

such as the topological definition of a river basin, or are socially constructed (e.g. Swyngedouw, 2004) such as [SOURCE: Page_7]

the extent of an urban area’s catchment which will depend on the social definition of what is considered to be [SOURCE: Page_7]

“urban” as an example (see Sayre, 2005, for further discussion). [SOURCE: Page_7]

### 2.2.2. Temporal scale

Time is another vital dimension considered in hydrological studies, but perhaps more implicitly considered in [SOURCE: Page_7]

some of the water governance literature. The resolution or level of temporal data (e.g. minutes, hours, days, [SOURCE: Page_7]

floods, rain‐water tank storage levels, riverine and basin flooding, droughts and climate change patterns on [SOURCE: Page_7]

rainfall and runoff. Water governance mechanisms, planning regimes and institutions may also be specifically [SOURCE: Page_7]

designed to manage these phenomena and others, linking them explicitly to different levels of the temporal [SOURCE: Page_7]

scale through the study of rates, durations and frequencies. There are some authors who study water‐related [SOURCE: Page_7]

phenomena explicitly across the temporal scale, such as Carpenter and Kitchell (1987) who investigated the [SOURCE: Page_7]

influences of temporal scale variance in limnetic primary production (ecological productivity of lakes and [SOURCE: Page_7]

associated food webs). Issues of temporal scale are also closely attached to spatial scale in much ecological work [SOURCE: Page_7]

### 2.2.4. Institutional scale

The institutional scale, which is typically closely linked to administrative and spatial scales, is concerned with [SOURCE: Page_9]

This scale is one that is thus commonly evoked in both water governance literature and practice. However, the [SOURCE: Page_9]

title “institutional scale” is rarely directly evoked in the water governance literature, despite the fact that all [SOURCE: Page_9]

studies of water law, policy or development of rules for managing water take it implicitly as a key dimension of [SOURCE: Page_9]

importance in their work. Specifically, this scale is a central interest of legal practitioners and institutional [SOURCE: Page_9]

economists (e.g. Ostrom et al., 1961; 1994; Esty, 2006) and those dealing with other operating rules such as [SOURCE: Page_9]

those of markets (e.g. Samuelson, 1973; Krugman, 1986) or water infrastructure, allocation and valuation issues [SOURCE: Page_9]

(e.g. Dinar et al., 1997; De Blaeij et al., 2011; Foerster, 2011). Ostrom’s (1994) work on the Institutional Analysis [SOURCE: Page_9]

and Development (IAD) framework is perhaps the work that most explicitly treats this scale and the importance [SOURCE: Page_9]

of considering its different levels, although see also Cash (2006). [SOURCE: Page_9]

### 2.2.6. Network scale

The network scale focusses on linkages. In the case of water governance, the levels of linkages (or volume [SOURCE: Page_10]

## 5. CONCLUSIONS AND PERSPECTIVES

From our theoretical framework and analyses supported by the papers in this special issue, we can point to four [SOURCE: Page_30]

specific arrangements in water governance across competing scales that appear particularly important in order [SOURCE: Page_30]

to control the different flow types between levels and scales and to manage their consequences. [SOURCE: Page_30]

First, the emerging set of basin‐based water institutions and administrations cannot be the sole answer to [SOURCE: Page_30]

developing “good” water governance. These organisations are useful in dealing with water and solute flows [SOURCE: Page_30]

(except in inter‐connected trans‐basin settings) but not necessarily the other flow types. They are also good [SOURCE: Page_30]

candidates to serve as arenas to organise collective action, even in clearly open social‐hydrological systems. [SOURCE: Page_30]

However, flows of knowledge and irreversibility effects limit their effectiveness in the operationalization of [SOURCE: Page_30]

agreements (Guerrin et al., this issue). There is thus a need to integrate the “wider socio‐economic catchment [SOURCE: Page_30]

context” (Syme et al., 2012) and hence investigate the viability of developing problemshed governance [SOURCE: Page_30]

arrangements. Polycentric governance (Ostrom, 1999) or multi‐level governance (Bache and Flinders, 2004) [SOURCE: Page_30]

paves the way for adjustment and coordination between various arenas, including at least one in charge of [SOURCE: Page_30]

water and solute flows. [SOURCE: Page_30]

Second, governance needs to include affected parties in the scales impacted in the interactions. This is a way to [SOURCE: Page_30]

internalize externalities, as recommended by Syme et al. (2012), based on an insight that stems from their [SOURCE: Page_30]

research in India. It is also a way to ensure that multi‐level interactions are profitable to all, as could be the case [SOURCE: Page_30]

in the Indus valley investigation of Yang et al. (this issue), provided that sufficient trust can be built around water [SOURCE: Page_30]

reallocation. Such inclusion of externalities requires constant adaptation to cope with the chain of arenas [SOURCE: Page_30]

connected by the various flows. Operational protocols have to be designed to make clear boundary judgements [SOURCE: Page_30]

on these systems and clear analyses of people impacted each time policies or rules are adjusted. In this way [SOURCE: Page_30]

there is a greater possibility for effectively incorporating these actors into the official governance processes of [SOURCE: Page_30]

the social‐hydrological system and for detecting further adjustments that need to be made to the governance [SOURCE: Page_31]

regime. [SOURCE: Page_31]

Third, there is a need for greater coupling of, and coordination between, people and institutions. Several flows [SOURCE: Page_31]
