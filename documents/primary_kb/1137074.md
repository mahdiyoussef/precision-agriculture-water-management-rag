Abstract We describe a rainfall simulator experiment designed to measure the capture, by a fenced [SOURCE: Page_3]

tree belt, of excess water generated as Hortonian flow from a pasture slope. Three rainfall [SOURCE: Page_3]

events (48, 49 and 75 mm/h for 13, 30 and 30 min, respectively) were applied, of which [SOURCE: Page_3]

15%, 29% and 44%, respectively, ran off and drained onto the tree belt. The tree belt [SOURCE: Page_3]

captured 100%, 32-68% and 0-28% of the runoff from the 3 events, respectively. These [SOURCE: Page_3]

captured runoff volumes represented 31-39%, 22-45% and 0-29% increases in water [SOURCE: Page_3]

supply to the trees, in addition to incident rainfall. Infiltration rates within the tree belt [SOURCE: Page_3]

were up to 46% higher than in the pasture zone. This higher infiltration was mainly [SOURCE: Page_3]

attributed to better soil surface conditions in the absence of stock and a 50-mm layer of [SOURCE: Page_3]

tree litter. Overland flows within the tree belt formed tree litter into microterraces, which [SOURCE: Page_3]

spread and slowed flows and allowed greater time for infiltration. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Introduction In the semiarid agricultural regions of the world, there is growing interest in the [SOURCE: Page_4]

placement of tree belts on hillslopes within run-on zones to increase their growth by [SOURCE: Page_4]

increasing local water supply (e.g. by ‘runoff agroforestry’; Dropplemann and Berliner, [SOURCE: Page_4]

2003; Abdelkdair and Schultz, 2005). Such tree belts also reduce hillslope water yield [SOURCE: Page_4]

and hence reduce off-site waterlogging or dryland salinisation (e.g. McJannet et al. 2000 [SOURCE: Page_4]

and 2001; Stirzaker et al., 2002; Turner and Ward 2002). [SOURCE: Page_4]

The success of either endeavour requires that water moving downslope as runoff [SOURCE: Page_4]

[overland flow, sub surface lateral flow (SLF) or both] is captured and used by the tree [SOURCE: Page_4]

belt. Runoff water harvesting for agricultural production has been practiced for millennia [SOURCE: Page_4]

by small farmers (Myers, 1975; Carter and Miller, 1991; Yair, 1983). Typically, this [SOURCE: Page_4]

practice involves the manipulation of upslope catchment areas to shed water as Hortonian [SOURCE: Page_4]

flow (overland flow resulting from infiltration excess). This overland flow is directed to [SOURCE: Page_4]

the root zone of crops and trees via small earth structures where lower slopes and higher [SOURCE: Page_4]

infiltration rates enhance the capture of overland flow and its storage in the soil profile. [SOURCE: Page_4]

These imposed systems mimic some of the hydrologic characteristics of naturally [SOURCE: Page_4]

‘patchy’ or ‘banded’ vegetation systems described by Tongway et al. (2001). Most [SOURCE: Page_4]

reported ‘runoff agroforestry’ is confined to arid regions of North Africa and the Middle [SOURCE: Page_4]

East on small labour-intensive farm enterprises (Dropplemann and Berliner, 2003; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Abdelkdair and Schultz, 2005). However, most reported scientific interest in the use of [SOURCE: Page_4]

agroforestry for managing waterlogging and dryland salinity has emanated from southern [SOURCE: Page_4]

Australia and has focussed on the interception of SLF or deep drainage (e.g. (Lefroy and [SOURCE: Page_5]

Stirzaker, 1999); Turner and Ward 2002; Ellis et al., 2005a). [SOURCE: Page_5]

In Australia, land clearing for agriculture in winter-dominated rainfall environments has [SOURCE: Page_5]

increased deep drainage and remobilised ancient regolith salt stores (Allison et al., 1990); [SOURCE: Page_5]

(Barnett, 1989). Hillslope agroforestry has been proposed to intercept and use excess [SOURCE: Page_5]

water before it moves lower in the landscape to cause waterlogging or dryland [SOURCE: Page_5]

salinisation. However, the success of tree belts on hillslopes in intercepting SLF in [SOURCE: Page_5]

southern Australia is unclear. (White et al., 2002) reported that a tree belt, growing on a [SOURCE: Page_5]

duplex soil in Western Australia, used SLF equal to an additional 33% of rainfall. During [SOURCE: Page_5]

a drought period, Ticehurst (2004) measured SLF of <1% of rainfall arriving at a tree belt [SOURCE: Page_5]

planted at the break of slope (BOS) at Holbrook, New South Wales. McJannet et al. [SOURCE: Page_5]

(2000; 2001) describe a detailed hydrologic study, on a BOS tree belt near Benalla, [SOURCE: Page_5]

Victoria, which failed to show that SLF occurred, or was likely to occur, at their site. [SOURCE: Page_5]

There is very little information reported on the generation of overland flow upslope of [SOURCE: Page_5]

tree belts. Silberstein et al. (2002) provide a ‘ready reckoner’ method, based on [SOURCE: Page_5]

simulation modelling output, to estimate the combinations of slope and hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_5]

conductivity likely to result in significant SLF. There is also little known about the ability [SOURCE: Page_5]

of tree belts to capture and use excess water arriving as overland flow. The redistribution [SOURCE: Page_5]

of overland flow, and associated nutrients carried in suspended sediments, is fundamental [SOURCE: Page_5]

to the ecology and productivity of naturally patchy and banded semiarid vegetation [SOURCE: Page_5]

systems (Noy-Meir 1973; Nulsen et al., 1986; Ludwig and Tongway, 1995). It has been [SOURCE: Page_5]

proposed that the resilience and diversity of agricultural systems could benefit from [SOURCE: Page_5]

mimicking these characteristics (e.g. Hobbs and O’Connor, 1999). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Two fundamental questions about runoff agroforestry have not been answered: 1) which [SOURCE: Page_6]

agricultural landscapes have overland flow as an important process, and 2) what is the [SOURCE: Page_6]

capacity for tree belts to capture overland flow? Recently, Ellis et al., (2005b) provided a [SOURCE: Page_6]

surface water yield model for hillslopes designed as multiple banded agricultural systems, [SOURCE: Page_6]

including runoff agroforestry systems. The water yield model contained two significant [SOURCE: Page_6]

assumptions: 1) overland flow occurred only as saturation excess, and 2) capture of [SOURCE: Page_6]

overland flow by the tree belt was only limited by maximum soil water storage and not [SOURCE: Page_6]

by soil infiltration characteristics. Ellis et al. (2005b) showed reasonable agreement [SOURCE: Page_6]

between the model and observations from naturally-banded mulga (Acacia aneura) [SOURCE: Page_6]

systems. However, assumption (1) may not apply to most agricultural lands where [SOURCE: Page_6]

infiltration is likely to be limited by the formation of a soil crust (Morin and Benyamini, [SOURCE: Page_6]

1977), and assumption (2) is largely untested. [SOURCE: Page_6]

In this study, we extend previous hillslope tree belt hydrology studies (White et al. 2002; [SOURCE: Page_6]

McJannet et al. 2000; 2001; Ticehurst (2004) by testing the above two assumptions made [SOURCE: Page_6]

by Ellis et al. (2005b). We describe a large scale (600 m 2) rainfall simulator experiment [SOURCE: Page_6]

designed to measure overland flow following three rainfall events applied to a pasture [SOURCE: Page_6]

slope and tree belt sequence. In this experiment, the performance of the tree belt as a [SOURCE: Page_6]

sediment trap was also evaluated, but these findings are reported by Leguédois et al. [SOURCE: Page_6]

(2005). [SOURCE: Page_6]

Discussion and conclusions Field observations, soil water measurements and rigorous rainfall and runoff [SOURCE: Page_18]

measurements showed that that runoff generated from upslope pasture and the bare soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

zones can be captured by a tree belt lower on the slope. These findings support the [SOURCE: Page_18]

conceptual source-sink model for a hillslope pasture-tree belt sequence (Ellis et al., [SOURCE: Page_18]

2005b). However, at our experimental site, we found the 2 assumptions made by Ellis et [SOURCE: Page_18]

al. (2005b) to be invalid. First, field observations showed that overland flow was [SOURCE: Page_18]

generated as infiltration excess (Hortonian flow) and not by saturation excess, although [SOURCE: Page_18]

this would not be true for all soil types. Second, the capture of overland flow was limited [SOURCE: Page_18]

by soil infiltration rate, and not by maximum soil water storage. Ellis et al. (2005b) also [SOURCE: Page_18]

used indirect measurements of soil surface condition (Tongway and Hindley, 2004) to [SOURCE: Page_18]

suggest that significantly greater infiltration rates could be expected within non-grazed [SOURCE: Page_18]

tree belts, compared to pasture. Our experiment has confirmed this and demonstrated that [SOURCE: Page_18]

ISS in the tree belt was up to 46% higher than ISS in the pasture. Moreover, the tree belt [SOURCE: Page_18]

captured 100%, 32-68%, 0-28% of the runoff from the pasture, resulting from 1 in 2-year, [SOURCE: Page_18]

1 in 10-year and 1 in 50-year storms, respectively, in addition to rainfall (Table 1). It is [SOURCE: Page_18]

likely that these values were affected by the sequence of the applied events, in particular, [SOURCE: Page_18]

the pre-wetting from the preceding event. Even so, the captured water represented [SOURCE: Page_18]

significant additions to the incident rainfall from the 3 events, respectively, 31-39%, 22- [SOURCE: Page_18]

45% and 0-29%. These values could have significant implications for the productivity of [SOURCE: Page_18]

tree crops in dry areas. [SOURCE: Page_18]

18 [SOURCE: Page_18]

Compared to the pasture, the greater infiltration of water within the tree belt was mainly [SOURCE: Page_19]

attributable to four physical characteristics: 1) the presence of tree litter; 2) initially drier [SOURCE: Page_19]

soil; 3) the greater abundance of large (< 5mm diameter) macro pores open to the surface; [SOURCE: Page_19]

and 4) the absence of a compacted soil crust. Field observations plus D ̄ c , ̄v and n [SOURCE: Page_19]

calculations confirmed that the slowing and spreading of overland flow by microterraces [SOURCE: Page_19]

of tree litter provided a longer period for infiltration to occur (). Drier soil may also [SOURCE: Page_19]

present greater opportunity for infiltration and storage, but alone does not guarantee it. [SOURCE: Page_19]

Significant volumes of overland flow passed over the bare soil zone and into the tree belt, [SOURCE: Page_19]

and a 1 to 2 m backwater formed on this zone during each event. However, only a very [SOURCE: Page_19]

small amount of this water infiltrated the bare soil, as indicated by a very shallow (< 50 [SOURCE: Page_19]

mm) depth of observed wetting and change in S (Erreur : source de la référence non [SOURCE: Page_19]

trouvée). [SOURCE: Page_19]

While selected macro pores were observed before and after the experiment, their role in [SOURCE: Page_19]

the greater infiltration capacity of the tree belt is only postulated. However, it is likely [SOURCE: Page_19]

that that they could have conducted significant amounts of water where water was ponded [SOURCE: Page_19]

and the soil surface was saturated. Time did not allow a comprehensive assessment of [SOURCE: Page_19]

large bio-pores as this is a lengthy and destructive process, and it is not known what [SOURCE: Page_19]

proportion of them were open to the soil surface. However, Lavelle (1997) discusses how [SOURCE: Page_19]

soil macro-fauna function as ‘system engineers’ [see also Lavelle and Spain (2001, [SOURCE: Page_19]

p^346)]. [SOURCE: Page_19]

The compacted soil crust in the bare soil zone played a significant role in generating [SOURCE: Page_19]

overland flow and minimising local infiltration. Therefore, we can safely assume that the [SOURCE: Page_19]

19 [SOURCE: Page_19]

absence of this crust within the tree belt improved infiltration. Biological crusts, however, [SOURCE: Page_20]

can serve as pioneer species to begin the process of improved surface structure and [SOURCE: Page_20]

surface roughening by accumulating litter (Belnap and Lange, 2001). The absence of a [SOURCE: Page_20]

compact soil crust, the presence and the partial incorporation of tree litter (‘joining’ the [SOURCE: Page_20]

litter layer to the soil surface), greater biological activity and the greater infiltration [SOURCE: Page_20]

capacity of the tree belt appear to all be interrelated. This is consistent with the ecological [SOURCE: Page_20]

processes reported to occur in ‘patch and inter-patch’ patterns of natural arid and semiarid [SOURCE: Page_20]

vegetation systems (e.g. Greene, 1992; Tongway et al., 2001), and govern their source- [SOURCE: Page_20]

sink behaviour (Noy-Meir, 1973). [SOURCE: Page_20]

Planning and management decisions appeared to be important in the establishment of [SOURCE: Page_20]

source-sink landscape processes at the experimental site. For example, exclusion of stock [SOURCE: Page_20]

from the tree belt has reduced mechanical compaction, encouraged the growth of [SOURCE: Page_20]

biological crusts, and helped maintain the litter layer, which protects the surface from [SOURCE: Page_20]

consolidation by raindrop impact. Further, the mixture of Acacia and Callistemon species [SOURCE: Page_20]

appeared to provide an appropriate balance in the leaf litter deposition and decomposition [SOURCE: Page_20]

rates (i.e. the litter degraded at a rate sufficient to encourage biological activity, but also [SOURCE: Page_20]

remained in sufficient quantity to form microterraces in overland flow). The position of [SOURCE: Page_20]

the fence at the site, however, and the creation of a bare soil zone, appears at odds with [SOURCE: Page_20]

the aim of capturing overland flow. Tree belts established more recently by the farmer [SOURCE: Page_20]

have stock exclusion fences further upslope from the tree canopy. [SOURCE: Page_20]

Due to time and resource constraints, this experiment was not replicated spatially. Great [SOURCE: Page_20]

care was therefore taken to select a site with similar conditions in each of the two pasture- [SOURCE: Page_20]

tree belt sequences and to minimise measurement errors. In addition, two tests for [SOURCE: Page_20]

20 [SOURCE: Page_20]

hydrologic similarity suggested that the spatial variation between the sequences was [SOURCE: Page_21]

smaller than the measurement errors. We therefore regard our measurements to be [SOURCE: Page_21]

representative of the runoff, infiltration and capture processes observed during the [SOURCE: Page_21]

experiment. We also emphasise that, here, we were most interested in the redistribution [SOURCE: Page_21]

of water between pasture and trees and that this experiment has addressed these [SOURCE: Page_21]

adequately for at our site. While we have shown that a tree belt can capture overland flow [SOURCE: Page_21]

from a pasture, we accept that these results are not necessarily transferable to other sites [SOURCE: Page_21]

and conditions. [SOURCE: Page_21]

However, our findings build on and confirm some aspects of related studies (McJannet et [SOURCE: Page_21]

al., 2000, 2001; Ticehurst, 2004; Ellis et al., 2005b), and are likely to be relevant to farm [SOURCE: Page_21]

hillslopes in other regions. That is, the runoff generation and capture processes we [SOURCE: Page_21]

observed are likely to occur wherever agricultural land drains onto a tree belt, and the [SOURCE: Page_21]

dominant process of downslope movement of excess water is by Hortonian flow. [SOURCE: Page_21]

The relatively rapid succession (i.e. 30 min apart) of the applied rainfall events is likely to [SOURCE: Page_21]

have affected our results by pre-wetting the soil and possibly reducing the potential for [SOURCE: Page_21]

runoff capture. However, our results show similar ∆S (Table 1) for events 2 and 3, [SOURCE: Page_21]

although CD and ∆PT were much smaller. We would have expected greater capture of the [SOURCE: Page_21]

second and third events if they had not been recently preceded (i.e. within 30 min) by [SOURCE: Page_21]

other events. But very dry surface antecedent conditions could also produce high runoff [SOURCE: Page_21]

rates on some (non-wetting) soils, at least in the early stages of the event and reduce [SOURCE: Page_21]

capture. Despite these uncertainties, applying the events in order of increasing magnitude [SOURCE: Page_21]

would have somewhat reduced the variation in results, relative to those measured with [SOURCE: Page_21]

21 [SOURCE: Page_21]

different antecedent soil water. That is, the larger the event, the larger the effect on [SOURCE: Page_22]

subsequent events, and therefore the smaller the relative effect of antecedent conditions. [SOURCE: Page_22]

Determining responses to a range of soil water antecedent conditions would require [SOURCE: Page_22]

further experimental work. Moreover, defining what are the most likely, or most [SOURCE: Page_22]

interesting, antecedent conditions for each event is a significant exercise, dependent on [SOURCE: Page_22]

soil type, climatic region and design question and outside the brief of this study. It is safe [SOURCE: Page_22]

to assume, however, that the proportion of runoff events that can be captured will depend [SOURCE: Page_22]

on local soil and rainfall conditions and antecedent soil water content. Of equal, if not, [SOURCE: Page_22]

greater importance is the ratio of runoff generation area (hillslope) to runoff capture (tree [SOURCE: Page_22]

belt) areas. Field observations during our experiment have also shown the importance of [SOURCE: Page_22]

tree litter for slowing and dispersing overland flow, and therefore increasing the [SOURCE: Page_22]

opportunity for infiltration. [SOURCE: Page_22]

The capture, and subsequent use, of excess water by trees, however, can be good or bad, [SOURCE: Page_22]

depending on the local management objective. If the excess water would otherwise cause [SOURCE: Page_22]

waterlogging or dryland salinity lower in the landscape, then using it on site would be [SOURCE: Page_22]

good (Silberstein et al., 2002). One could also take advantage of the extra water to [SOURCE: Page_22]

increase tree production as suggested by Cooper et al. (in press) if the trees provide a [SOURCE: Page_22]

commercial product. If these are not potential advantages, then the loss of otherwise [SOURCE: Page_22]

productive land to trees may need to be justified in other ways. [SOURCE: Page_22]

Where tree belts are intended for the management of Hortonian flow, thought should be [SOURCE: Page_22]

given to the magnitude and frequency of runoff and the likely magnitude and subsequent [SOURCE: Page_22]

use of captured water. In this study we simulated 3 rainfall events with local return [SOURCE: Page_22]

periods of 2, 10 and 50 years, applied in relatively rapid succession. The capture of these [SOURCE: Page_23]

or other ‘design storms’ or ‘storm sequences’ may be critical design parameters for some [SOURCE: Page_23]
