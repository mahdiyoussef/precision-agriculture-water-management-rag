Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_12]

This article introduced a flatness based controller for an open channel hydraulic canal. [SOURCE: Page_12]

The controller was tested by computer simulation (SIC) and real experimentation (Gignac canal, [SOURCE: Page_12]

France) using a SCADA system for the implementation. The initial model which assumed [SOURCE: Page_12]

constant lateral outflows has been improved to take into account the variation of lateral outflow [SOURCE: Page_12]

with the water level. This new model has shown to accurately represent the system dynamics. [SOURCE: Page_12]

This paper has described a realistic application of a flatness based open-loop controller that is [SOURCE: Page_12]

able to compute the upstream flow corresponding to a desired outflow, taking into account the [SOURCE: Page_12]

gravitational offtakes along the canal reach. [SOURCE: Page_13]

Sidebar 1: What is Differential Flatness? [SOURCE: Page_27]

The theory of differential flatness, which was first developed in [8], consists in a [SOURCE: Page_27]

parametrization of the trajectories of a system by one of its outputs, called the “flat output” [SOURCE: Page_27]

and its derivatives. Let us consider a system ẋ = f (x, u), where the state x is in Rn , and the [SOURCE: Page_27]

control input u ranges in Rm . The system is said to be flat, and admits z (where dim(z) = dim(u)) [SOURCE: Page_27]

for flat output if all the quantities can be parametrized by z and its derivatives. More specifically: [SOURCE: Page_27]

•   The state x can be written as x = h(z, ż, · · · , z (n) ). [SOURCE: Page_27]

•   The equivalent dynamics can be written as u = g(z, ż, · · · , z (n+1) ). [SOURCE: Page_27]

In the context of partial differential equations, the vector x can be thought of as “infinite [SOURCE: Page_27]

dimensional”. The notion of differential flatness extends to this case, and for a differentially [SOURCE: Page_27]

flat system of this type, the evolution of x can be parametrized using an input u which often [SOURCE: Page_27]

is the value of x at a given point. A system with a flat output can then be parametrized as [SOURCE: Page_27]

a function of this output. This enables the solution of open loop control problems, if this flat [SOURCE: Page_27]

output is the one that needs to be controlled. The open loop control input can then directly be [SOURCE: Page_27]

expressed as a function of the flat output. This also enables the solution of motion planning [SOURCE: Page_27]

problems, where one wants to steer the system from one state to another. Differential flatness [SOURCE: Page_27]

was used to investigate the related problem of heavy chains motion planning [9], as well as [SOURCE: Page_27]

the Burgers equation [10], the telegraph equation [11], the Stefan equation [12] and the heat [SOURCE: Page_27]

equation [13]. [SOURCE: Page_27]

Methods for parametrization can be achieved in different ways depending on the type [SOURCE: Page_27]

of the problem. Laplace transform is widely used [9], [10], [11] to invert the system. The [SOURCE: Page_27]

27 [SOURCE: Page_27]

equations can be transformed back from the Laplace domain to the time domain, thus resulting [SOURCE: Page_28]

in the flatness parametrization. Other methods can be used to compute the parametrization in [SOURCE: Page_28]

the time domain directly. For example, the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya form [13], [14] can be used [SOURCE: Page_28]

to parametrize the trajectories of a distributed parameter system X(ζ, t) in ζ ∈ [0, 1] as a power +∞          i ai (t) ζi! where X(ζ, t) is the state P series in space multiplied by a time function, i.e. X(ζ, t) = i=0 [SOURCE: Page_28]

Kovalevskaya form in the governing partial differential equation and boundary conditions; a [SOURCE: Page_28]

relation between ai (t) and the flat output y(t) or its derivatives can then be found (for example, [SOURCE: Page_28]

ai (t) = y (i) (t) where y (i) (t) is the ith derivative of y(t)) which leads to the final parametrization. [SOURCE: Page_28]

Sidebar 2: How to Impose a Discharge at a Gate? [SOURCE: Page_29]

Once a desired open-loop discharge is computed, it needs to be imposed at the upstream [SOURCE: Page_29]

end of the canal. In open channel flow, it is not easy to impose a discharge at a gate. Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_29]

once the gate is opened or closed, the upstream and downstream gate water levels react instantly, [SOURCE: Page_29]

and modify the discharge: this discharge is among other a function of the water levels on both [SOURCE: Page_29]

sides of the gate. One possibility would be to use a local slave controller that operates the gate [SOURCE: Page_29]

in order to deliver a given discharge. But due to operational constraints, it is usually not possible [SOURCE: Page_29]

to operate the gate at a very high sampling period. As an example, some large gates may not [SOURCE: Page_29]

be operated more than a few times an hour, which directly limits the operation of the local [SOURCE: Page_29]

controller. [SOURCE: Page_29]

Several methods have been developed by hydraulic engineers to perform this control [SOURCE: Page_29]

input based on the gate equation, which provides a good model for the flow through the gate [SOURCE: Page_29]

[15]. The problem can be described as depicted in figure S$_1$. Two pools are interconnected [SOURCE: Page_29]

with a hydraulic structure, in our case a submerged orifice (also applicable for more complex [SOURCE: Page_29]

structures). The gate opening is to be controlled to deliver a required flow from pool 1 to pool [SOURCE: Page_29]

2. [SOURCE: Page_29]

The hydraulic cross-structure is assumed to be modeled by a static relation between the [SOURCE: Page_29]

discharge flowing through the gate Q, the water levels upstream and downstream of the gate Y$_1$ , [SOURCE: Page_29]

Y$_2$ , respectively, and the gate opening W : [SOURCE: Page_29]
