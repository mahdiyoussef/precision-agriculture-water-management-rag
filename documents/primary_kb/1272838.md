Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

This paper proposes and analyses three policy instruments which can be used to enhance [SOURCE: Page_2]

farmers’ compliance with individual water allocations in a decentralized management context. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Three regulation strategies are proposed for the case of groundwater allocations for irrigation: [SOURCE: Page_2]

the first relies on economic instruments; the second is based on tools designed to promote pro- [SOURCE: Page_2]

social behaviors; and the third combines assumptions from the first two approaches. They are [SOURCE: Page_2]

evaluated through 16 scenario workshops involving 124 stakeholders and farmers in five French [SOURCE: Page_2]

groundwater basins. Stakeholders’ perceptions are analyzed, disentangling the ethical, economic, [SOURCE: Page_2]

institutional, social and technical perspectives underlying the stakeholders’ arguments for or [SOURCE: Page_2]

against the proposed instruments for groundwater-use regulation. The analysis reveals a [SOURCE: Page_2]

preference for the strategy that combines economic and social incentives. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Groundwater resources are increasingly being overexploited worldwide, owing to [SOURCE: Page_4]

demographic growth, rising demand for agricultural products, technological progress in [SOURCE: Page_4]

irrigated agriculture and the flexibility that groundwater use provides (Giordano and [SOURCE: Page_4]

Villholth, 2007; Llamas and Martinez-Santos, 2005). Although intensive groundwater [SOURCE: Page_4]

exploitation supported the development of a flourishing agricultural economy (Kuper et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al., in press; Shah, 2008), it also generated significant negative impacts such as [SOURCE: Page_4]

declining water tables, reduction of in-stream flows, the drying-up of springs, wetlands [SOURCE: Page_4]

deterioration, land subsidence and sea-water intrusion (Llamas and Martinez-Santos, [SOURCE: Page_4]

2005; Schlager, 2006; Shah et al., 2003). Groundwater-management policies have been [SOURCE: Page_4]

progressively developed to mitigate these impacts through a better control of [SOURCE: Page_4]

groundwater abstraction. This requires shifting from an open-access regime to one of [SOURCE: Page_4]

regulated abstraction. The total volume of water that can be abstracted is first ‘capped’: [SOURCE: Page_4]

an overall water allocation is defined based on hydrogeological studies which estimate a [SOURCE: Page_4]

sustainable yield, defined as the maximum level of abstraction that can be maintained [SOURCE: Page_4]

over the long term without generating any significant ecological impact on the aquifer [SOURCE: Page_4]

and its dependent ecosystems. This global allocation is then shared among a limited [SOURCE: Page_4]

number of authorized water users. Individual users receive a specific volume that must [SOURCE: Page_4]

not be exceeded, and which, depending on the national legal and regulatory framework, [SOURCE: Page_4]

is associated with a licence, a concession or a use right. Such groundwater-management [SOURCE: Page_4]

policies have already been implemented in a number of countries including several [SOURCE: Page_4]

western states of the USA (Blomquist et al., 2004; Schlager, 2006), Australia (Ross and [SOURCE: Page_4]

Martinez-Santos, 2009), Spain (Garrido et al., 2005), Chile (Hearne and Donoso, 2005) [SOURCE: Page_4]

to mention only a few. More recently, similar policies have been advocated in European [SOURCE: Page_4]

countries, in particular France (Figureau and al., 2012) and the UK (DEFRA, 2013). [SOURCE: Page_4]

2      Three regulation strategies for managing groundwater-based abstraction for irrigation [SOURCE: Page_7]

Three contrasted policy approaches are proposed and discussed in this paper: the [SOURCE: Page_7]

first relies entirely on economic instruments; the second is based on tools designed to [SOURCE: Page_7]

promote pro-social behaviors; and the third combines various assumptions from the first [SOURCE: Page_7]

two approaches. These three strategies were then used as the basis for constructing [SOURCE: Page_7]

policy scenarios, which were debated with stakeholders. [SOURCE: Page_7]

2.1      Coupling economic incentives [SOURCE: Page_7]

The first strategy discussed in this paper assumes that the most efficient way to [SOURCE: Page_7]

influence individual behavior consists of changing the economic incentives through [SOURCE: Page_7]

interventions which alter the costs and benefits of certain targeted actions. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

underlying assumption is that individuals respond by adapting their decisions to [SOURCE: Page_7]

maximize their individual pay-offs. Following scholars like Rapoport and Au (2001) [SOURCE: Page_7]

and Sefton et al (2007), we propose to combine the use of a penalty with a reward. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

penalty consists of a tax charged to farmers who exceed their allocation and is [SOURCE: Page_7]

proportional to the over-pumping; it aims to discourage free-riding (excess pumping). [SOURCE: Page_7]

The revenues from this penalty system are then redistributed among farmers who [SOURCE: Page_7]

withdraw less than their entitlement, each one receiving a share proportional to their [SOURCE: Page_7]

water saving. The reward encourages voluntary contributions to the public good [SOURCE: Page_7]

(reduced abstraction). This combination is expected to lead to an optimal level of [SOURCE: Page_8]

individual contribution (Sefton et al., 2007). Rewards are funded by revenues from [SOURCE: Page_8]

penalties as suggested by Rapoport and Au (2001). The system is expected to meet both [SOURCE: Page_8]

the flexibility objective (since farmers may exceed their allocation by paying the tax) [SOURCE: Page_8]

and the overall compliance objective. By design, the amount of reward is not known in [SOURCE: Page_8]

advance, since it depends on the choice of all other farmers. This uncertainty does not [SOURCE: Page_8]

prevent farmers from taking sound decisions based on a comparison of the expected [SOURCE: Page_8]

utility associated to different strategic choices. Given farmers’ heterogeneity in terms of [SOURCE: Page_8]

production functions, risk aversion and capacity to anticipate strategic behaviors, all [SOURCE: Page_8]

farmers will not take the same decision in a similar context. If the analyst has perfect [SOURCE: Page_8]

information on users, he can theoretically calculate the exact level of penalty that will [SOURCE: Page_8]

ensure budget and water equilibrium. In practice, the level of penalty would probably be [SOURCE: Page_8]

adjusted by trial and errors. [SOURCE: Page_8]

2.2      Promoting pro-social behaviours [SOURCE: Page_8]

An abundant literature, mainly drawing upon experimental economics, has shown [SOURCE: Page_8]

the limitations of incentive-based approaches. It recognizes that individual behaviors are [SOURCE: Page_8]

often influenced by non-economic motives, including adherence to social norms, ethical [SOURCE: Page_8]

commitment, altruism, reciprocity and inequity aversion (d'Adda, 2011; Fehr and [SOURCE: Page_8]

Gächter, 2000; Masclet et al., 2003; del Pilar Moreno-Sánchez and Maldonado, 2010; [SOURCE: Page_8]

Rapoport and Au, 2001; Sefton et al., 2007; Travers et al., 2011). Such social [SOURCE: Page_8]

preferences can be enhanced by policies designed to strengthen reputation effects, [SOURCE: Page_8]

fairness, accountability, trust or moral inclusion. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The second strategy considered in this paper fundamentally seeks to exploit social [SOURCE: Page_8]

preferences to promote pro-social behaviours, so as to meet the objectives of flexibility [SOURCE: Page_8]

and overall compliance. The overarching objective is to “empower public-spirited [SOURCE: Page_8]

motives” rather than only selfish preferences (Bowles, 2008) and to enhance social [SOURCE: Page_8]

norms that can promote cooperative behaviours (Vatn, 2009). This strategy relies on [SOURCE: Page_8]

two main pillars. The first consists of developing cooperation among farmers through [SOURCE: Page_9]

reinforcing values such as reciprocity, solidarity and moral inclusion. The underlying [SOURCE: Page_9]

assumption is that, each year, some farmers are likely to accept to relinquish part of [SOURCE: Page_9]

their individual water allocation to help other farmers confronted by unusual situations. [SOURCE: Page_9]

The volume they give back is made available to the GWUA, which in turn redistributes [SOURCE: Page_9]

it to farmers who have an exceptional need for extra water. The internal redistribution [SOURCE: Page_9]

follows general principles and rules which have been validated by the farm community; [SOURCE: Page_9]

their practical implementation can however be modified each year to take into account [SOURCE: Page_9]

as an interface between the giving and the receiving farmers, who do not interact [SOURCE: Page_9]

directly, since interpersonal relationships may act as an impediment to solidarity. [SOURCE: Page_9]

Another reason for preventing bilateral interaction and agreements is to avoid informal [SOURCE: Page_9]

water trading, and the development of the accompanying “market mentality” (Titmuss, [SOURCE: Page_9]

1971). This internally anonymous reallocation should be facilitated by feelings of [SOURCE: Page_9]

solidarity, reciprocity and trust, which are likely to exist among irrigators since they [SOURCE: Page_9]

increased communication between farmers, as shown by economic experiments [SOURCE: Page_9]

2011). [SOURCE: Page_9]

The second pillar of this strategy is the control of free-rider behaviours, i.e., preventing [SOURCE: Page_9]

farmers from breaking the agreed rules and unilaterally taking the decision to exceed [SOURCE: Page_9]

their allocation. This can be done by disclosing information regarding individuals’ [SOURCE: Page_9]

decisions or actions to all members of the concerned community. The underlying [SOURCE: Page_9]

hypothesis is that transparency increases moral incentives for users to comply with the [SOURCE: Page_9]

allocation rules, since their individual behaviours are being observed by their peers: [SOURCE: Page_9]

“any person can, through open contempt or intimidation, withhold another person from [SOURCE: Page_9]

2.3      Combining economic and social incentives [SOURCE: Page_10]

The third strategy discussed in this paper combines the two sources of the human [SOURCE: Page_10]

motivation to cooperate: the maximization of individual economic gains and of social [SOURCE: Page_10]

utility (Kerr et al., 2012; Vatn, 2009). It consists of offering groups of farmers the [SOURCE: Page_10]

opportunity to sign a contract (which we called a joint liability contract in our [SOURCE: Page_10]

workshops) through which they mutualize their individual water allocations. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Participation is voluntary. Farmers gain two benefits from entering a group: reduced [SOURCE: Page_10]

GWUA fees (compared to remaining as individuals) and increased flexibility (since [SOURCE: Page_10]

they can make any arrangements they wish within the group, whereas individuals must [SOURCE: Page_10]

strictly comply with their individual allocations). However, if the group exceeds its [SOURCE: Page_10]

water entitlement a collective fine is charged to each individual farmer, in proportion to [SOURCE: Page_10]

keep their individual water allocations); (iii) it includes a flat-rate payment as an [SOURCE: Page_11]

incentive to join; and (iv) it includes a performance-related incentive to remain a [SOURCE: Page_11]

member. [SOURCE: Page_11]

3.2   The French context [SOURCE: Page_15]

The study was conducted in France, in a context of the on-going water policy reform [SOURCE: Page_15]

initiated by the 2006 water act. In the 1980 and 1990s, groundwater development has [SOURCE: Page_15]

taken place in an institutional setting that imposed few if any limits on groundwater use, [SOURCE: Page_15]

groundwater-use licenses being almost systematically granted to requesting farmers. As [SOURCE: Page_15]

abstractions rose and environmental problems emerged, the State reacted by enforcing [SOURCE: Page_15]

temporary pumping restrictions. The increasing frequency of such restrictions led to a [SOURCE: Page_15]

new approach, embodied in the 2006 water law^1, which consists of imposing a ceiling [SOURCE: Page_15]

on groundwater abstraction in overexploited aquifers. This ceiling, expressed as an [SOURCE: Page_15]

authorized volume per year, is defined in such a way that groundwater-dependent [SOURCE: Page_15]

ecosystems can remain in good ecological condition, under normal climatic conditions, [SOURCE: Page_15]

i.e., in four out of every five years. The authorized volume is then apportioned by the [SOURCE: Page_15]

State among economic sectors (urban supply, industry, agriculture). The volume [SOURCE: Page_15]

allocated to agriculture is officially assigned to the newly formed Groundwater Users’ [SOURCE: Page_15]

Associations (GWUA- Organisme Unique de Gestion Collective in French) which are [SOURCE: Page_15]

made responsible for apportioning it among farmers. GWUAs must develop their own [SOURCE: Page_15]

rules for defining individual water entitlements. [SOURCE: Page_15]

At the time of the workshops (2013), GWUAs had just been established and national [SOURCE: Page_15]

farmers’ organizations were starting to explore possible ways for establishing water [SOURCE: Page_15]

allocation rules within GWUAs. There was an emerging consensus around several [SOURCE: Page_15]

assumptions which were incorporated in all our scenarios. The first one is that each [SOURCE: Page_15]

99         Most participants express a negative judgment concerning the P&P strategy (Figure [SOURCE: Page_23]

196   5    Discussion and conclusion [SOURCE: Page_28]

197       In a context of water-policy reform, our work was aimed at exploring and evaluating [SOURCE: Page_28]

198   contrasted strategies of decentralized groundwater management. We explored three [SOURCE: Page_28]

199   strategies that GWUA might consider developing in order to prevent farmers from [SOURCE: Page_28]

200   exceeding their allocations while trying to cope with climate variability and other [SOURCE: Page_28]

201   sources of environmental and economic uncertainties. The first is based only on social [SOURCE: Page_28]

202   incentives, the second only on economic incentives and the third strategy combines [SOURCE: Page_28]

203   both. [SOURCE: Page_28]

204       From a policy perspective, the discussions which took place during workshops [SOURCE: Page_28]

205   stressed that shifting from an open-access to a regulated groundwater exploitation [SOURCE: Page_28]

206   regime will generate significant discontent in the farming community. Identifying the [SOURCE: Page_28]

207   factors which determine the acceptability of alternative groundwater regulation [SOURCE: Page_28]

208   strategies is therefore of very high importance. The method and the case study presented [SOURCE: Page_28]

209   in this paper represent a first contribution to this issue. The results obtained also show [SOURCE: Page_28]

210   the preference of French stakeholders for policy instruments aiming at strengthening [SOURCE: Page_28]

211   social incentives rather than economic ones. The “Payment and Penalty” strategy is [SOURCE: Page_28]

212   generally rejected, while the “Community Ties” and the “Joint Liability Contract” [SOURCE: Page_28]

213   scenarios obtain greater support. However, there is some contradiction between the [SOURCE: Page_28]

214   dominant philosophical stance in favor of pro-social behaviors and the repeated [SOURCE: Page_28]

215   assertions that, in real life, farmers think and act in a very individualistic manner, [SOURCE: Page_28]

216   following their self-interest rather than ethical principles and objectives. There is a need [SOURCE: Page_28]

217   to assess the relative weight of these two sources of individual motivations in order to [SOURCE: Page_28]

218   predict what the resulting behaviors are likely to be. Other methods, such as field [SOURCE: Page_28]

219   experiments, should be deployed to reach this scientific objective. Another open [SOURCE: Page_28]

220   question is whether these results can be extrapolated to other similar national contexts, [SOURCE: Page_28]

221   such as Italy or Spain. Answering this question is crucial to providing sound [SOURCE: Page_28]

222   recommendations to EU policy makers but it requires additional field work in different [SOURCE: Page_29]

224      From a methodological perspective, our research shows that the added value of [SOURCE: Page_29]

225   participatory approaches resides in allowing the evaluation of policy options which [SOURCE: Page_29]

226   consider a wide range of viewpoints. In our five case studies, the farmers demonstrated [SOURCE: Page_29]

227   their ability to scrutinize the proposed strategies in terms of effectiveness, acceptability [SOURCE: Page_29]

228   and feasibility of implementation. Their arguments were diverse, referring to the social, [SOURCE: Page_29]

229   institutional, technical, and ethical dimensions of the problem. The value of comparing [SOURCE: Page_29]

230   theoretical instruments with stakeholders’ knowledge also lies in the fact that they are [SOURCE: Page_29]

231   able to anticipate operational constraints, and identify conditions and factors that would [SOURCE: Page_29]

232   foster or hinder their implementation. There are of course a number of limitations [SOURCE: Page_29]

233   associated to the methodology: the opinions expressed can be characterized by cognitive [SOURCE: Page_29]

234   and strategic biases. Many findings are contingent to the design of the theoretical [SOURCE: Page_29]

235   instrument presented and they may be affected by the incomplete information provided [SOURCE: Page_29]

239   behavior may determine the outcome of the groundwater abstraction game, they cannot [SOURCE: Page_29]

240   reliably predict what the outcome is likely to be. Results obtained with such [SOURCE: Page_29]

241   participatory approaches should therefore be considered as complementary to those of [SOURCE: Page_29]

242   economic modeling and experimental approaches. They cannot replace these more [SOURCE: Page_29]

243   standard and quantitative approaches, such as framed field experiments which are [SOURCE: Page_29]

244   considered to be a relevant approach for deepening the analysis presented in this paper. [SOURCE: Page_29]
