Overview of sprinkler testing and evaluation [SOURCE: Page_4]

Typically, field evaluations of sprinkler performance are undertaken by laying catch-cans around a [SOURCE: Page_4]

selected number of sprinklers, or full grid (rectangular or square) arrangement. The sprinkler is [SOURCE: Page_4]

located at the centre of the grid and the distortion caused by wind or sprinkler’s asymmetric coverage [SOURCE: Page_4]

pattern can easily be detected. On the other hand, for research or developmental work in which more [SOURCE: Page_4]

precise measurements are required, the characterisation of water distribution is often undertaken [SOURCE: Page_4]

indoors because it is relatively easier to attain no-wind conditions. Depending on the wetted radius, [SOURCE: Page_4]

full grid catch arrangement may also be used in laboratory testing, but more often catch-cans are [SOURCE: Page_4]

arranged along one or more radial distance of a single sprinkler, technically referred to as single leg [SOURCE: Page_4]

test (Svubure et al. 2010). The single radial grid catch-can arrangement is widely used because of [SOURCE: Page_4]

constraints of space in many laboratories. This arrangement is mostly applied when a symmetrical [SOURCE: Page_4]

watering pattern is expected and in no-wind conditions (ISO 15886-3, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_4]

The design and size of the sprinkler, number and configurations of nozzles, and the operating pressure [SOURCE: Page_4]

are the key factors determining its water distribution profile (Tarjuelo et al. 1999). Ideally, the water [SOURCE: Page_4]

distribution patterns of a sprinkler of the same model and size operating under the same conditions [SOURCE: Page_4]

would be expected to be the same. However, it has been demonstrated that they vary in some cases, [SOURCE: Page_4]

mainly because of manufacturing inaccuracies (Bean 1965). Measurement uncertainties, which are [SOURCE: Page_4]

related to procedures and equipment used for testing, may also cause disparities in the data. Normally, [SOURCE: Page_4]

a sprinkler with a full rotation should have a circular wetting pattern. However, if factors such as [SOURCE: Page_4]

manufacturing imperfections cause the impact-type sprinkler to have a variable rotational speed, an [SOURCE: Page_4]

asymmetric coverage pattern may be expected (Christiansen, 1942). An asymmetric coverage pattern [SOURCE: Page_4]

may also be caused by the sprinkler riser not being perfectly vertical. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Measurement uncertainty [SOURCE: Page_20]

Uncertainty is a parameter that characterises the dispersion of values caused by random and [SOURCE: Page_20]

systematic errors associated with the measurement. Practically, this means that the true value of a [SOURCE: Page_20]

measurement is the measured value plus or minus the uncertainty for that measurement. The [SOURCE: Page_20]

procedure commonly used by metrological facilities to determine uncertainties in their measurements [SOURCE: Page_20]

is described by Bentley (2005). [SOURCE: Page_20]

As mentioned earlier, the uncertainty in the measurement process is a probable reason why some of [SOURCE: Page_20]

the results in Table 3 could not be validated in accordance with ISO 15886-3 (2012). This could be as [SOURCE: Page_20]

a result of inaccuracies of the equipment used to measure flow rate and pressure (for instance pressure [SOURCE: Page_20]

gauge, water meter, stop watch and measuring scale). This intercomparison exercise thus provided the [SOURCE: Page_20]

participating laboratories with data necessary to further audit their measurement uncertainties and [SOURCE: Page_20]

potentially improve the quality of testing. [SOURCE: Page_20]
