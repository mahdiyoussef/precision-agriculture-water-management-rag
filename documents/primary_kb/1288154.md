## 1. INTRODUCTION

The Seine River is a highly artificialised catchment in Europe. The upper Seine drains [SOURCE: Page_3]

extensive, productive agricultural areas, while the lower part flows across the Paris [SOURCE: Page_3]

conurbation, with over 10 million inhabitants, and the Rouen–Le Havre axis, which hosts [SOURCE: Page_3]

intense industrial and commercial activity (Meybeck et al., 1998; Billen et al., 2001). The river [SOURCE: Page_3]

raw materials) and transportation, and these strategic advantages account for the presence of [SOURCE: Page_3]

therein). [SOURCE: Page_3]

The importance of the river and its related resources has sparked monitoring activities [SOURCE: Page_3]

since the 1950s, which evolved into regular surveys with a bi-­‐monthly frequency after 1970. [SOURCE: Page_3]

As a result, the Seine River offers a unique and very complete database that allows a thorough [SOURCE: Page_3]

examination of the river’s evolution during the past half-­‐century, coupling changes in water [SOURCE: Page_3]

quality with major socio-­‐economic and technological changes. Billen et al. (2001, 2007) used [SOURCE: Page_3]

the Riverstrahler model to assess the biogeochemical functioning of the basin and presented [SOURCE: Page_3]

an overview on the dynamics of the river system, although none or very few data were [SOURCE: Page_3]

Ac [SOURCE: Page_3]

scrutinise the actual quality data set with a number of statistical techniques, including not [SOURCE: Page_3]

only the riverine section downstream of Paris, but also the estuary. We aim to detect trends, [SOURCE: Page_3]

turning points and shifting patterns, providing a complete description of the lower reach of [SOURCE: Page_3]

the river. We track concentrations over a long period of time (1970–2014) and establish [SOURCE: Page_3]

trajectories that can be paralleled to the trajectories observed in human systems. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Furthermore, we have updated the analyses until the most recent years and pay special [SOURCE: Page_3]

attention to the evolution after the year 2000, when the application of the Water Framework [SOURCE: Page_3]

The temporal patterns observed in the Seine basin are common in many other European [SOURCE: Page_12]

catchments (Bouraoui & Grizzetti, 2011; Romero et al., 2013), yet the information available in [SOURCE: Page_12]

most other rivers is remarkably less abundant. This is why the Seine offers a precious [SOURCE: Page_12]

opportunity to explore the effectiveness of water policies and to envisage future prospects. [SOURCE: Page_12]

From a management viewpoint, EQSs are efficient at setting up a basic common framework [SOURCE: Page_12]

and at providing an idea on how severely human activities have impacted the ecosystem. [SOURCE: Page_12]

Current regulations, however, fail to manage and prevent diffuse pollution (e.g. nitrate) that [SOURCE: Page_12]

presents irregular patterns and sluggish changes extending over long time periods. Great [SOURCE: Page_12]

inter-­‐annual variability such as that related to dry and wet years, for instance, can mask [SOURCE: Page_12]

subtle, continuing long-­‐term trends. Very long data records, even longer than the record [SOURCE: Page_12]

presented in this study, are required to disentangle these variations, but lengthy time series [SOURCE: Page_12]

are seldom available. [SOURCE: Page_12]

Biased nutrient ratios have been related to increased occurrences of harmful algal blooms, [SOURCE: Page_14]

Glibert & Burkholder 2006; Justić et al. 1995; Officer & Ryther, 1980; Smayda, 1990; among [SOURCE: Page_14]

many others). The annual flux of N to the Seine Bight is at present twice the value that would [SOURCE: Page_14]

correspond to the Redfield ratios of N:P, N:Si, and given the strong correlation between nitrate [SOURCE: Page_14]

and flow, the positive long-­‐term flow anomaly could make the situation more severe. [SOURCE: Page_14]

Finally, the decrease of nitrate inputs observed across stations after 2010 remains [SOURCE: Page_14]

questionable. It is not yet clear whether mitigation practices in agriculture – the [SOURCE: Page_14]

rationalisation of fertiliser use, the expansion of cover crops and organic farming – are slowly [SOURCE: Page_14]

giving results or if the nitrate decrease in the lower Seine results from recent wastewater [SOURCE: Page_14]

treatment improvements. Nonetheless, because the wastewater treatment technology has [SOURCE: Page_14]

reached its optimum (the 70% denitrification in the Achères WWTP already requires the [SOURCE: Page_14]

addition of methanol, and achieving a greater reduction would become counterproductive), [SOURCE: Page_14]

current agri-­‐environmental measures may not be enough to counteract the inertia of past [SOURCE: Page_14]

years, given that the response time to decrease values from >50 to <15 mg·L-­‐1 is several [SOURCE: Page_14]

decades (Grizzetti et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_14]

This uncertainty in the response of freshwater systems is aggravated by the ill-­‐defined [SOURCE: Page_14]

effects of climate change, which could affect nutrient, oxygen, and phytoplankton [SOURCE: Page_14]

concentrations through a number of processes, including increases in temperature, sea-­‐level [SOURCE: Page_14]

rise, changes in precipitation and runoff from land, wind patterns, and storminess. Changes in [SOURCE: Page_14]

required to flush nutrients and contaminants from the system; on the contrary, increased [SOURCE: Page_14]

rainfall and freshwater runoff would enhance diffuse nutrient fluxes (Scavia et al., 2002; [SOURCE: Page_14]

ed Statham, 2012). Warmer conditions and possible lower summer discharges would affect [SOURCE: Page_14]

stratification and oxygen concentration, and could support faster growth or a longer growing [SOURCE: Page_14]

season for phytoplankton; further, temperatures might exceed the optimal growth [SOURCE: Page_14]

temperature range of some organisms and induce drastic shifts in community composition. [SOURCE: Page_14]
