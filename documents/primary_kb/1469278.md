18   A role-playing approach to better understand the challenges of using monthly probabilistic [SOURCE: Page_4]

19   forecasts in sequential decision-making in water management. [SOURCE: Page_4]

20   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_4]

21   The use of probabilistic forecasts is necessary to take into account uncertainties and allow for [SOURCE: Page_4]

22   optimal risk-based decisions in streamﬂow forecasting at monthly to seasonal lead times. [SOURCE: Page_4]

23   Such probabilistic forecasts have long been used by practitioners in the operation of water [SOURCE: Page_4]

24   reservoirs, in water allocation and management, and more recently in drought preparedness [SOURCE: Page_4]

25   activities. Various studies assert the potential value of hydro-meteorological forecasting [SOURCE: Page_4]

26   efforts, but few investigate how these forecasts are used in the decision-making process. [SOURCE: Page_4]

27   Role-play games can help scientists, managers and decision-makers understand the extremely [SOURCE: Page_4]

28   complex process behind risk-based decision. In this paper, we present an experiment focusing [SOURCE: Page_4]

29   on the use of probabilistic forecasts to make decisions on reservoir outflows. The setup was a [SOURCE: Page_4]

30   risk-based decision-making game, during which participants acted as water managers. [SOURCE: Page_4]

31   Participants determined monthly reservoir releases based on a sequence of probabilistic [SOURCE: Page_4]

32   inﬂow forecasts, reservoir volume objectives and release constraints. After each decision, [SOURCE: Page_4]

33   consequences were evaluated based on the actual inﬂow. The analysis of 162 game sheets [SOURCE: Page_4]

34   collected after eight applications of the game illustrates the importance of leveraging not only [SOURCE: Page_4]

35   the probabilistic information in the forecasts but also predictions for a range of lead times. [SOURCE: Page_4]

36   Winning strategies tended to gradually empty the reservoir in the months before the peak [SOURCE: Page_4]

37   inflow period to accommodate its volume and avoid overtopping. Twenty percent of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

38   participants managed to do so and finished the management period without having exceeded [SOURCE: Page_4]

39   the maximum reservoir capacity or violating downstream release constraints. The role- [SOURCE: Page_4]

40   playing approach successfully created an open atmosphere to discuss the challenges of using [SOURCE: Page_4]

41   probabilistic forecasts in sequential decision-making. [SOURCE: Page_4]

2 [SOURCE: Page_4]

42   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_5]

43   Seasonal climate forecasts are used in a large number of water resources applications ranging [SOURCE: Page_5]

44   from droughts (Anderson et al. 2000), urban water (Chiew et al. 2000), hydro-power [SOURCE: Page_5]

45   operations (Block 2011), water supply (Bracken et al. 2010), water allocation (Mushtaq et al. [SOURCE: Page_5]

46   2012), agriculture (Ghile and Schulze 2008) and ground water levels (Guo et al. 2009). [SOURCE: Page_5]

47   Without exception, seasonal forecasting systems are probabilistic. They incorporate [SOURCE: Page_5]

48   uncertainties about the future state of the climate (Brown and Ward 2013), which is [SOURCE: Page_5]

49   especially useful for risk assessment. [SOURCE: Page_5]

50   Coelho and Costa (2010) defined several challenges for integrating seasonal climate forecasts [SOURCE: Page_5]

51   in operational management, ranging from the production of the forecasts to the effective [SOURCE: Page_5]

52   implementation into user applications (e.g. Hartmann et al. 2002; Lemos et al. 2002). In [SOURCE: Page_5]

53   water resource applications, this often requires translating predictions of precipitation and [SOURCE: Page_5]

54   temperature into predictions of streamflow or inflows to reservoirs (Regonda et al. 2011). [SOURCE: Page_5]

55   Practitioners can then make management decisions that are informed by the hydrologic [SOURCE: Page_5]

56   forecasts. [SOURCE: Page_5]

57   Coelho and Costa (2010) particularly emphasise end-user decision-making as a key challenge [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   when implementing seasonal forecasts for water management, even given the common [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   existence of quantitative water decision support systems (e.g. Dutta et al. 2013; Regonda et [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   al. 2011). The decision-making process and the policies for water management are extremely [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   complex, for they have to satisfy a large range of possibly conflicting objectives, comprising [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   technical, socio-economic and environmental issues, and also cover a considerable range of [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   hydrologic conditions (Simonović and Marino 1982; Welsh et al. 2013). A number of studies [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   connect forecast performance to decision-making (e.g. Golembesky et al. 2009), but few [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   recognize that a skilful forecast does not necessarily lead to the forecast actually used by [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   decision makers (Chiew et al. 2003; Kiem and Verdon-Kidd 2011; Ritchie et al. 2004). [SOURCE: Page_5]

3 [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   In addition, almost all studies are unable to incorporate all operating rules or key decisions [SOURCE: Page_6]

68   due to the complexity of the task. Technical complexities together with intricate governance [SOURCE: Page_6]

69   settings contribute to barriers which lower the uptake of seasonal climate forecasts in water [SOURCE: Page_6]

70   resource management (for a detailed review see Kirchhoff et al. 2013; Lemos 2008). [SOURCE: Page_6]

71   Amongst others, these barriers include the challenge in incorporating information in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

72   decision-making process, and the often insufficient human and institutional capacities. [SOURCE: Page_6]

73   Kirchoff et al. (2013) highlight individual water manager behaviour and risk perception as [SOURCE: Page_6]

74   important areas which need to be addressed to realise the value of probabilistic seasonal [SOURCE: Page_6]

75   forecasts (Block and Goddard 2012). [SOURCE: Page_6]

76   These issues can be addressed through an improved decision-making process, using [SOURCE: Page_6]

77   structured decision-making models, and training. It is however extremely complex to [SOURCE: Page_6]

78   replicate a full reality, with all possible consequences. The use of role-play games can aid this [SOURCE: Page_6]

79   process, whilst allowing the investigation of key research questions, such as how decision [SOURCE: Page_6]

80   rules can be formulated or whether probabilistic forecasts lead to better decisions (Ramos et [SOURCE: Page_6]

81   al. 2013). The decision process includes a range of potential actions, a number of possible [SOURCE: Page_6]

82   events, various consequences for each combination of action and event, and a set of [SOURCE: Page_6]

83   probabilities for each combination (Faber and Stedinger 2001; Sankarasubramanian et al. [SOURCE: Page_6]

84   2009). These can be controlled in a game setting, whilst providing an experience which can [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   be close to reality (Cannon-Bowers and Bell 1997) and helpful to enhance understanding. [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   This paper presents a game experiment that focuses on a realistic decision sequence for [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   managing a reservoir – one based on actual hydrology, seasonal forecasts, and typical [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   reservoir management objectives from a setting in the western United States (US). The game [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   was played with different groups of students, researchers, operational hydrologists, [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   forecasters, decision-makers and water managers during conferences and meetings. Players [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   were asked to manage a reservoir used for flood control and water supply, for a four-month [SOURCE: Page_6]

4 [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   period (referred to as the management period). They were presented with a monthly series of [SOURCE: Page_7]

93   probabilistic inflow forecasts for runoff in the coming 1 to 4 months: such forecasts are [SOURCE: Page_7]

94   referred to as seasonal forecasts in practice due to their monthly to seasonal lead times. [SOURCE: Page_7]

95   Participants were required to plan outflows at each decision step while complying with [SOURCE: Page_7]

96   reservoir capacity and release constraints. The objectives of this paper are to present the [SOURCE: Page_7]

97   results obtained and to analyse the decision making process of the participants. [SOURCE: Page_7]

98   In the following sections, we present the game setup, the way it unfolded and how [SOURCE: Page_7]

99   participants managed their reservoirs. The last section is dedicated to discussion and [SOURCE: Page_7]

100   conclusions. [SOURCE: Page_7]

101   2. Material [SOURCE: Page_7]

102   a.     Game setup [SOURCE: Page_7]

103   The setting of the game is a reservoir in a watershed with a pronounced annual runoff cycle [SOURCE: Page_7]

104   defined by a winter-spring snow accumulation and spring-summer melt period, followed by a [SOURCE: Page_7]

105   low runoff regime in the summer and fall. The reservoir management objectives are typical of [SOURCE: Page_7]

106   many managed systems: water supply, minimum environmental releases and flood control. [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   The game was adapted from training material for a course given at the 91st Annual Meeting [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   of the American Meteorological Society. It was modified to be played in an auditorium, in [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   20-25 minutes, and to be accessible for audiences ranging from students, researchers, [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   operational hydrologists, forecasters, decision-makers and water managers. [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   b.     Playing the game [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   To collect the results of each participant, a worksheet was distributed at the beginning of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   game. It allowed participants to record their releases and update their reservoir volume. It [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   also provided information on the long-term flow climatology for each month (the median [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   inflow in Mm$_3$ over the past 30 years). Before starting the game, an example was given for [SOURCE: Page_7]

5 [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   the month of March. Participants were guided on how to fill in the worksheet. Lastly, we [SOURCE: Page_8]

118   asked for a volunteer to play the game in front of the group, bringing a more lively [SOURCE: Page_8]

119   atmosphere to the experience. Since computations for the volunteer could not be executed in [SOURCE: Page_8]

120   real-time, the volunteer was presented with three pre-defined options of releases. They were [SOURCE: Page_8]

121   designed after several test-plays with small groups. For the volunteer, all possible [SOURCE: Page_8]

122   combinations of sequential decisions were pre-calculated, but only the chosen sequence was [SOURCE: Page_8]

123   displayed. Furthermore, the volunteer’s choice and play did not interfere with the play of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

124   other participants, because participants had to define their releases before the choices [SOURCE: Page_8]

125   available for the volunteer were presented. [SOURCE: Page_8]

126   To illustrate the forecast-decision procedure, Figure 1 shows the slides presented for the first [SOURCE: Page_8]

127   month. First the inflow forecasts are displayed with the help of boxplots showing the 5% [SOURCE: Page_8]

128   (min), 25%, 50%, 75%, and the 95% (max) percentiles (Fig. 1a) and participants are given a [SOURCE: Page_8]

129   few minutes to decide on their reservoir releases. On the next slide (Fig. 1b), it is the [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   volunteer’s turn to make a decision, choosing among options A, B and C. The next slide (Fig. [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   1c) displays the actual inflow of the month and participants update their reservoir levels [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   accordingly, balancing the inflow with the release they had decided on for that month. In the [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   last slide of the round (Fig. 1d), we assess the volunteer’s decision, by showing the [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   calculation of reservoir volume at the end of the month for the release option chosen by the [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   volunteer. It also indicates whether the volunteer still has a job. This marks the end of a [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   forecast-decision-update sequence and the game moves on to the next decision (i.e., next [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   month). [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   The game is a repetition of this sequence of steps for forecasts issued once a month from [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   April 1st to July 1st until we reach August 1st. If participants are fired before the last round, [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   they are encouraged to keep playing and try to recover their jobs by lowering the reservoir [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   volume below its maximum capacity. [SOURCE: Page_8]

245   was possible, albeit with a low forecast probability of occurring. The flow climatology [SOURCE: Page_13]

247   and also provided a warning for participants to be cautious and prepare for the coming event [SOURCE: Page_13]

248   with an appropriate release strategy. [SOURCE: Page_13]

249   d.     Evolution of release schedules [SOURCE: Page_13]

250   The way participants were planning their releases months ahead, and how they changed their [SOURCE: Page_13]

251   planning or not as the June high inflow approached, was investigated with the help of the 126 [SOURCE: Page_13]

252   worksheets (out of 162) that had release schedules fully filled. In both groups, i.e., [SOURCE: Page_13]

253   participants who lost (102 worksheets) and participants who won (24 worksheets), we had [SOURCE: Page_13]

254   approximately the same proportion of players that fully filled in their release schedules (78% [SOURCE: Page_13]

255   and 75%, respectively). [SOURCE: Page_13]

256   We observe that participants who won had basically planned their releases for the month of [SOURCE: Page_13]

257   May and June already in the first decision on April 1st. When May 1st and later June 1st [SOURCE: Page_13]

258   arrived, the majority confirmed their previous decisions or just increased their releases of [SOURCE: Page_13]

259   approximately 5 to 10 Mm$_3$ more to accommodate the high inflows in the reservoir without [SOURCE: Page_13]

260   overtopping. [SOURCE: Page_13]

261   On the other hand, participants who lost were, in general, planning very low releases on the [SOURCE: Page_13]

262   first months and already, since the first decision in April, planning to release the maximum in [SOURCE: Page_13]

263   June. What they had not anticipated was that this would not be enough to accommodate the [SOURCE: Page_13]

264   high inflow event and that a better strategy would have been to gradually empty the reservoir [SOURCE: Page_13]

265   already in April and May. On June 1st, players from this group may have been frustrated by [SOURCE: Page_13]

11 [SOURCE: Page_13]

266   the fact that they had their reservoir volumes too high but could not release more than the [SOURCE: Page_14]

267   maximum allowed. [SOURCE: Page_14]

268   In general, the planned releases for July were progressively increased when moving from [SOURCE: Page_14]

269   April to June in both groups. On July 1st, however, when the only release they had to plan [SOURCE: Page_14]

270   was for the coming month and the highest inflow had already passed, the majority in the [SOURCE: Page_14]

271   group of participants who won was able to decrease the values they had planned to release [SOURCE: Page_14]

272   previously on June 1st and, therefore, better target the final goal of having the reservoir [SOURCE: Page_14]

273   volume as close as possible to 500 Mm$_3$ on August 1st. On the other hand, in the group of [SOURCE: Page_14]

274   participants who lost, half of the players had to decide on releasing more than what they had [SOURCE: Page_14]

275   scheduled in the previous decisions to have a chance of getting their reservoir below 500 [SOURCE: Page_14]

276   Mm$_3$ and, consequently, their jobs back. [SOURCE: Page_14]

277   e.     How might participants have used the probabilistic forecasts and the flow [SOURCE: Page_14]

278   climatology when making decisions? [SOURCE: Page_14]

279   It was left to participants to choose how they would take into account forecast and [SOURCE: Page_14]

280   climatology information in their decisions. We could not, unfortunately, follow this process [SOURCE: Page_14]

281   within each participant’s mind. Nevertheless, using the worksheets only, we tried to identify [SOURCE: Page_14]

282   which forecast quantile participants based their releases on. To do so, we calculated the [SOURCE: Page_14]

283   August 1st reservoir volumes they would have obtained if the observed inflows had [SOURCE: Page_14]

284   consistently matched one of the forecast quantiles, or, alternatively, the flow climatology. [SOURCE: Page_14]

285   Volumes obtained with the observed inflows were also estimated. This was done for each [SOURCE: Page_14]

286   decision step (month), so that we could also evaluate the release planning strategy. Figure 5 [SOURCE: Page_14]

287   shows the results. [SOURCE: Page_14]

288   The players who took proactive actions as early as April and May to balance the game [SOURCE: Page_14]

289   objective with the overtopping risk likely focused on upper quantiles of the forecasts (Q75 [SOURCE: Page_14]

290   and Q95), with maybe also some support from the flow climatology. Indeed, most of these [SOURCE: Page_14]

12 [SOURCE: Page_14]

291   participants would not have overtopped their reservoirs had the Q75 and even the Q95 [SOURCE: Page_15]

292   forecast quantiles been verified as observed inflows throughout the game, as early as in the [SOURCE: Page_15]

293   first decision step. On the third and fourth decisions, they seemed to have set aside flow [SOURCE: Page_15]

294   climatology and rather used an ‘adjusted’ upper forecast quantile (Q95) to evaluate the [SOURCE: Page_15]

295   possible observed inflow and optimise their releases to the goals of the game. This might [SOURCE: Page_15]

296   have been the result of having previously noted that the forecasts were, in general, under- [SOURCE: Page_15]

297   predicting the observed inflows. The fact that part of the distribution for August volumes [SOURCE: Page_15]

298   given actual observed flows is above the reservoir maximum during the first two decisions [SOURCE: Page_15]

299   (April, May) means that participants tolerated or were not able to eliminate the overtopping [SOURCE: Page_15]

300   risk early in the management period, but took steps to eliminate it when the potentially high [SOURCE: Page_15]

301   inflow month was imminent. [SOURCE: Page_15]

302   In the group of participants who lost, most participants might have been guided by the [SOURCE: Page_15]

303   medium to lower quantiles (Q50 and Q25) in the first decisions. Figure 5 shows that if the [SOURCE: Page_15]

304   upper quantile forecasts or even the flow climatology values had verified as observed [SOURCE: Page_15]

305   inflows, these participants would have reached reservoir volumes much higher than the [SOURCE: Page_15]

306   allowed 500 Mm$_3$ in August. In contrast, if lower quantiles had verified as observed inflows, [SOURCE: Page_15]

307   most would have been safe from overtopping their reservoirs. Later on, on the third and [SOURCE: Page_15]

308   fourth decisions, these participants might have acted based on the upper forecast quantiles [SOURCE: Page_15]

309   (Q75 and Q90) and, eventually, on the flow climatology to plan their releases. [SOURCE: Page_15]

310   4. Discussion and conclusions [SOURCE: Page_15]

311   This paper presented the results of a game experiment on risk-based decision-making in [SOURCE: Page_15]

312   water management using probabilistic forecasts of inflows to a reservoir. From the analysis of [SOURCE: Page_15]

313   the worksheets collected during the application of the game in eight different contexts, we [SOURCE: Page_15]

314   were able to illustrate key issues on the use of probabilistic forecasts in sequential decision [SOURCE: Page_15]

315   making. [SOURCE: Page_15]

13 [SOURCE: Page_15]

316   In the game setup, seasonal forecasts had to be viewed as a whole and not as independent [SOURCE: Page_16]

317   monthly forecasts. Even though the forecast time evolution warned the participants about a [SOURCE: Page_16]

318   high inflow event, monthly 50% percentile forecast values were under-estimating flows. [SOURCE: Page_16]

319   Given the reservoir constraints, as expressed in the rules of the game, and the goals of the [SOURCE: Page_16]

320   management, it was necessary to look at forecasts months ahead before deciding on the [SOURCE: Page_16]

321   reservoir releases. A winning strategy would be the one that would gradually empty the [SOURCE: Page_16]

322   reservoir two months ahead of the expected high inflow to accommodate its potential volume [SOURCE: Page_16]

323   without overtopping. Although the worksheets were designed to invite participants to adopt [SOURCE: Page_16]

324   this long-term approach, notably by asking them to schedule their releases for the whole [SOURCE: Page_16]

325   management period, still about 25% of the participants only filled their releases for the [SOURCE: Page_16]

326   coming month. [SOURCE: Page_16]

327   Approximately 20% of the participants to the game were able to finish the management [SOURCE: Page_16]

328   period without exceeding the maximum reservoir capacity at any time during the sequenced [SOURCE: Page_16]

329   decisions, and approximately 17% of the participants not only caused the reservoir to [SOURCE: Page_16]

330   overtop, but also were unable to bring its volume back below the threshold. Winners were [SOURCE: Page_16]

331   those who had programmed the releases in a way that they succeeded to adequately decrease [SOURCE: Page_16]

332   their reservoir volumes in the first two months, anticipating the potential high inflows. Losers [SOURCE: Page_16]

333   of the game were those who did not recognize early enough the significance of the high [SOURCE: Page_16]

334   inflow risk, or did not comprehend its potential impact on the reservoir volume. [SOURCE: Page_16]

335   In this paper, answers that showed signs of miscomprehension of the balance equation were [SOURCE: Page_16]

336   not used in the interpretation. Therefore, it has been implicitly assumed in the analysis of the [SOURCE: Page_16]

337   remaining results that the participants were able to take full advantage of the proposed [SOURCE: Page_16]

338   information, once given the necessary tools to read the probabilistic graphs and understand [SOURCE: Page_16]

339   the climatological values at the beginning of the game. The results based on the remaining [SOURCE: Page_16]

340   answers may however still be influenced by the comprehension of the proposed tools and the [SOURCE: Page_16]

14 [SOURCE: Page_16]

341   way they were presented. It is possible that given the timed nature of the game (participants [SOURCE: Page_17]

342   had to decide within a limited amount of time), some participants could understand the way [SOURCE: Page_17]

343   forecasts were displayed, but were not fast enough in applying their understanding. [SOURCE: Page_17]

344   Communicating forecasts and, more generally, probabilities is still a major challenge in [SOURCE: Page_17]

345   hydrometeorology, and can be a barrier to the widespread use of ensemble forecasts in [SOURCE: Page_17]

346   operational contexts. [SOURCE: Page_17]

347   The information collected from the worksheets shows which decisions were taken, but not [SOURCE: Page_17]

348   why they were taken. Therefore, care has to be taken in the interpretation of the results of this [SOURCE: Page_17]

349   game experiment. We can only speculate on what could have been the reasons for many [SOURCE: Page_17]

350   participants to take a certain sequence of release decisions, and on what strategy winners had [SOURCE: Page_17]

351   with respect to the forecast inflows. Therefore, even if the results indicate that the participants [SOURCE: Page_17]

352   who won the game might have considered the upper quantiles of the forecasts, at least in their [SOURCE: Page_17]

353   first decisions, information on why they followed this procedure is not available through the [SOURCE: Page_17]

354   game setup. A possible solution to this limitation could be to expand the game by asking [SOURCE: Page_17]

355   these relevant questions orally or directly in the worksheets. Recording whether participants [SOURCE: Page_17]

356   had prior water resource management or forecasting experience could also help indicate the [SOURCE: Page_17]

357   value of training toward improving the application of probabilistic information. Collecting [SOURCE: Page_17]

358   specific information on the main occupation and background of each participant could also be [SOURCE: Page_17]

359   helpful to evaluate how strategies may vary between different groups (e.g., students, [SOURCE: Page_17]

360   managers or forecasters). [SOURCE: Page_17]

361   Finally, this game is a simplified representation of reality and does not intend to reproduce [SOURCE: Page_17]

362   the full context of operational environments in reservoir management. Indeed, a participant [SOURCE: Page_17]

363   who had real-life experience managing reservoirs noted during one game session that the [SOURCE: Page_17]

364   winning strategy (described in Section 3.2) could have raised alarms in practice for having [SOURCE: Page_17]

365   allowed too much flooding risk en route to achieving a near-perfect target level. Despite the [SOURCE: Page_17]

15 [SOURCE: Page_17]

366   simplifications, however, we received positive feedback after the different applications, even [SOURCE: Page_18]

367   though howls of indignation were often heard in the rooms when the observed June inflow [SOURCE: Page_18]

368   was revealed. The role-playing approach, and the penalty experienced by participants of [SOURCE: Page_18]

369   being fired from their jobs, added a light touch to the experience and created a pleasant [SOURCE: Page_18]

370   atmosphere to discuss the challenges of using probabilistic seasonal forecasts in sequential [SOURCE: Page_18]

371   decision-making, where choices have delayed consequences. Notably, the game has been [SOURCE: Page_18]

372   successfully used as material to teaching and training activities. [SOURCE: Page_18]

382   Sidebar^1: Game setting [SOURCE: Page_19]

383   Each participant plays the role of a water manager for ‘Lake Dual’, which is a reservoir with [SOURCE: Page_19]

384   a capacity of 500 Mm$_3$ that serves two primary functions: water supply for ‘Swof Town’ and [SOURCE: Page_19]

385   flood control for ‘Safe Town’. The residents of ‘Swof Town’ would like to see the reservoir [SOURCE: Page_19]

386   full (500 Mm$_3$) on August 1st to ensure drinking water supply until the end of summer, while [SOURCE: Page_19]

387   the residents of ‘Safe Town’ are interested in keeping monthly releases below 60 Mm 3 to [SOURCE: Page_19]

388   prevent flood damage to their homes. Probabilistic forecasts of monthly inflows are available [SOURCE: Page_19]

389   and updated on the first day of each month during the management period running from April [SOURCE: Page_19]

390   to August. At the beginning of each month, participants have to decide on the monthly [SOURCE: Page_19]

391   reservoir releases for the remaining months in this period. [SOURCE: Page_19]

392 [SOURCE: Page_19]

17 [SOURCE: Page_19]

393   Sidebar^2: Management objectives [SOURCE: Page_20]

394   The goal of each participant is to have the reservoir volume as close as possible to 500 Mm 3 [SOURCE: Page_20]

395   on August 1st without ever exceeding this maximum capacity. Participants also have to [SOURCE: Page_20]

396   maintain a minimum release of 15 Mm$_3$ for environmental flow and their maximum release [SOURCE: Page_20]

397   cannot exceed 60 Mm$_3$. As a penalty, participants are fired from their management role in the [SOURCE: Page_20]

398   game if they fail to meet the constraint of maximum capacity. At the end of the game, the [SOURCE: Page_20]

399   ‘winner’ is the manager that has the highest reservoir volume on August 1st without having [SOURCE: Page_20]

400   exceeded its maximum capacity during the management period. [SOURCE: Page_20]
