### 311. Introduction

32     Water uptake by plant roots is a key element in the process of water transfer in [SOURCE: Page_3]

33the soil-plant-atmosphere continuum (Feddes et al., 2001). In croplands, it is estimated [SOURCE: Page_3]

34that 65% of the precipitation is returned to the atmosphere by evapotranspiration ( Oki [SOURCE: Page_3]

35and Kanae, 2006). Hence, pertinent simulation of the root water uptake (RWU) process [SOURCE: Page_3]

36is of major importance for an efficient agricultural water management. However, the [SOURCE: Page_3]

37RWU process is complex, related to endogenous factors (i.e., genetic control), and to [SOURCE: Page_3]

38exogenous factors such as soil water content, nutrient content, temperature, aeration [SOURCE: Page_3]

39and microbial activity (e.g. Kramer and Boyer, 1995; Hodge et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_3]

40     Early experimental research to understand root behavior dates back to the end of [SOURCE: Page_3]

41the XIXth century, credited to the pioneer works of Charles and Francis Darwin (The [SOURCE: Page_3]

42Power of Movement in Plants, (Darwin, 1880), as has recently been recalled by Baluska [SOURCE: Page_3]

43et al. (2009). However, the first mathematical representations of RWU were [SOURCE: Page_3]

44undertaken some decades later by van den Honert (1948). Since then, RWU modeling [SOURCE: Page_3]

45is typically performed according to one of two approaches: the so-called microscopic [SOURCE: Page_3]

46and macroscopic approaches (e.g. Molz, 1981; Hopmans and Bristow, 2002; Feddes [SOURCE: Page_3]

47and Raats, 2004). [SOURCE: Page_3]

48     The microscopic models are physically-based. They consider water potential of [SOURCE: Page_3]

49both the root system and the soil in the immediate vicinity of roots, and describe thus [SOURCE: Page_3]

50water flow to and through individual roots analogously to Ohm's law. In contrast, the [SOURCE: Page_3]

51macroscopic approaches consider a lumped representation of both the roots and the [SOURCE: Page_3]

52soil. Although physically-based macroscopic RWU models exist in literature, which [SOURCE: Page_3]

Author-produced version of the article published in Agricultural Water Management, 2015, N°155, p. 22-39. The original publication is available at http://www.sciencedirect.com Doi: 10.1016/j.agwat.2015.03.010 [SOURCE: Page_4]

53consider root water potential (e.g., Heinen, 2001; de Jong van Lier et al., 2008; [SOURCE: Page_4]

54Schneider et al., 2010; Couvreur et al., 2012), the macroscopic RWU models used in [SOURCE: Page_4]

55literature are typically empirical, neglecting the hydraulic properties of roots (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_4]

56Feddes et al., 1978; van Genuchten, 1987). [SOURCE: Page_4]

57      The choice of one modeling approach instead of another is context-dependent [SOURCE: Page_4]

58and still subject to debate (de Willigen et al., 2012): although physically based models [SOURCE: Page_4]

59are insightful for the comprehension of water and nutrient uptake processes at the [SOURCE: Page_4]

60root scale (Raats, 2007; Subbaiah, 2011) and require less calibration (Homaee et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

612002), their use is still limited in the domain of crop management due to the rich [SOURCE: Page_4]

62parameterization and computational requirements of such models (Feddes and Raats, [SOURCE: Page_4]

632004; Subbaiah, 2011), compared to the less demanding empirical macroscopic [SOURCE: Page_4]

64models (Feddes and Raats, 2004; Raats, 2007; Subbaiah, 2011), designated as more [SOURCE: Page_4]

65“Hydrologically-oriented” (Feddes and Raats, 2004). [SOURCE: Page_4]

66      When integrated in a greater physically-based soil water transfer model, the [SOURCE: Page_4]

67macroscopic RWU models conceptualize RWU by a sink term in the Richards equation: [SOURCE: Page_4]

68       ∂ θ/∂ t =∇ [k (h) ∇ H ]−S                                                                                      (1) [SOURCE: Page_4]

69where θ denotes the volumetric soil water content [L 3L-3], t the time [T], h the soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

70pressure head [L], H the soil total head [L], k the hydraulic conductivity of the soil [L T - [SOURCE: Page_4]

711] and S the sink term representing RWU [L3L-3T-1]. The sink term S represents herein [SOURCE: Page_4]

72the actual RWU, associating the potential transpiration (T p) to a potential root uptake [SOURCE: Page_4]

73distribution function (β) and to an uptake reduction function (γ) in a product formula: [SOURCE: Page_4]

75      The function β is typically taken in literature as the bulk root density distribution [SOURCE: Page_4]

76(Hopmans and Bristow, 2002), and will be considered as such in this study. [SOURCE: Page_4]

77      Due to their simplifying assumptions, the empirical models have often been said [SOURCE: Page_4]

78to have little biophysical basis (Skaggs et al., 2006, Javaux et al., 2008, Schneider et [SOURCE: Page_4]

79al., 2010). Probably, the most important shortcoming in this type of model is the [SOURCE: Page_4]

Author-produced version of the article published in Agricultural Water Management, 2015, N°155, p. 22-39. The original publication is available at http://www.sciencedirect.com Doi: 10.1016/j.agwat.2015.03.010 [SOURCE: Page_5]

80assumption that root activity is proportional to root density and to local water-content [SOURCE: Page_5]

81status through the aforementioned product formula. When described as such, RWU is [SOURCE: Page_5]

82represented as a passive process, i.e. uptake rates are controlled solely by climatic [SOURCE: Page_5]

83demand, the spatial distribution of soil water availability and root density. [SOURCE: Page_5]

84         In fact, it has been shown experimentally and numerically that the spatial [SOURCE: Page_5]

85distribution of instantaneous RWU rates may differ strongly from that of root density [SOURCE: Page_5]

86(Bruckler et al., 2004; Hodge, 2004, Faria et al., 2010). Such differences are expected [SOURCE: Page_5]

87to be greater in heterogeneous soil structures (Kuhlmann et al., 2012) and to further [SOURCE: Page_5]

88increase with time (Schneider et al., 2010). In addition, it has widely been shown [SOURCE: Page_5]

89experimentally that plants adjust their water uptake patterns to cope with soil water [SOURCE: Page_5]

90content distribution by an enhanced “compensatory” uptake from wetter soil regions [SOURCE: Page_5]

91(e.g., Green and Clothier, 1995; Hodge, 2004; Leib et al., 2006). Skaggs et al. (2006) [SOURCE: Page_5]

92suggested that the compensatory RWU mechanism plays a major role in simulations of [SOURCE: Page_5]

93soil water transfer where irrigation methods impose non-uniform water deficits in the [SOURCE: Page_5]

94root     zone.    Moreover,         Kuhlmann           et    al.     (2012)      suggested           that     omitting   the [SOURCE: Page_5]

95compensatory uptake may lead to underestimate plant transpiration in heterogeneous [SOURCE: Page_5]

96soils. [SOURCE: Page_5]

97         Attempts to conceptualize compensatory RWU in the empirical macroscopic [SOURCE: Page_5]

98models      were      first    undertaken           by      Jarvis    (1989)       who      explicitly       considered   a [SOURCE: Page_5]

99compensatory RWU function, multiplied by both γ and β functions. The author related [SOURCE: Page_5]

100the compensatory uptake mechanism to the plant stress index, expressed by the ratio [SOURCE: Page_5]

101of the actual to potential transpiration (T a/Tp). Compensatory uptake is thus triggered [SOURCE: Page_5]

102in a manner that transpiration is maintained at its potential level as long as T a/Tp is [SOURCE: Page_5]

103greater than a predefined threshold (ω c). Pang and Letey (1998) also explicitly [SOURCE: Page_5]

104accounted for the compensatory RWU, where plant transpiration is maintained at its [SOURCE: Page_5]

105potential level as long as there is at least one soil region where water content is [SOURCE: Page_5]

106greater than a given stress threshold. [SOURCE: Page_5]

107      Other compensatory RWU models in literature do not involve the T a/Tp ratio [SOURCE: Page_6]

108threshold, i.e., continuous. Bouten et al. (1992), Lai and Katul. (2000) and Li et al. [SOURCE: Page_6]

109(2001) proposed that water uptake is proportional to both β and a weighted stress [SOURCE: Page_6]

110index relating the local (considered soil element) to the bulk average (entire root zone) [SOURCE: Page_6]

111water condition, regardless of the ratio T a/Tp. Adiku et al. (2000) and van Wijk and [SOURCE: Page_6]

112Bouten (2001) considered that RWU pattern from the soil is automatically adjusted to [SOURCE: Page_6]

113minimize energy expenditure by the plant. Finally, water-tracking RWU models (Coelho [SOURCE: Page_6]

114and Or, 1996; 1999), which attribute higher uptake intensity to wetter soil regions, [SOURCE: Page_6]

115may provide an alternative method to implicitly account for the compensatory RWU [SOURCE: Page_6]

116process as proposed by Mailhol et al. (2011). However, the latter method has not been [SOURCE: Page_6]

117fully investigated in literature, and most studies account for the compensatory RWU [SOURCE: Page_6]

118via explicit functions. [SOURCE: Page_6]

119      The Jarvis's explicit compensatory RWU function has lately been integrated in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

1202-dimensional (2D) version of the water and heat transfer model in porous media [SOURCE: Page_6]

121Hydrus (Simunek et al., 2008) as discussed by Simunek and Hopmans (2009). The [SOURCE: Page_6]

122authors suggested that the effect of the spatial root distribution on RWU may be [SOURCE: Page_6]

123reduced when compensatory RWU is considered, and concluded thus that a priori [SOURCE: Page_6]

124knowledge of the spatial root distribution may only be effective for non compensatory [SOURCE: Page_6]

125RWU simulations. [SOURCE: Page_6]

126      Despite all the attention, the Jarvis's function is perceived oversimplifying [SOURCE: Page_6]

127compared to microscopic modeling approach (Schneider et al., 2010; Javaux et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

1282013). Moreover, few information exists in literature on the values the ω c threshold [SOURCE: Page_6]

129one should take (Skaggs et al., 2006), which often leads to use of arbitrary values [SOURCE: Page_6]

130(Shouse et al., 2011) or, in some cases, to abandon the use of the compensatory [SOURCE: Page_6]

131function (Oster et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_6]

132      Therefore, with such uncertainties in Jarvis's function parameterization, the effect [SOURCE: Page_6]

133of the latter on RWU pattern as evoked by Simunek and Hopmans (2009) is [SOURCE: Page_6]

134questionable, especially since root density distribution is well known to highly [SOURCE: Page_6]

135determine RWU pattern and rates (e.g., Beudez et al., 2013). One may wonder [SOURCE: Page_7]

136whether a compensatory RWU is even needed when an adequate description of root [SOURCE: Page_7]

138      The aim of this study is to (i) examine the effects of the compensatory RWU [SOURCE: Page_7]

142explore the possibility of the use of root density profiles specific to the watering [SOURCE: Page_7]

143method, water-tracking RWU models, as an approach to replace the need for [SOURCE: Page_7]

144compensatory uptake functions. [SOURCE: Page_7]

145      The model used for the numerical analysis is the well documented Hydrus [SOURCE: Page_7]

146(2D/3D) model (Simunek et al., 2008), which includes an adapted form of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

147Jarvis (1989) function. The simulations were performed to predict water flow in the soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

149maize    plots.    The      compensatory             uptake        levels      (T a/Tp)     ranged        from        1.0   (no [SOURCE: Page_7]

150compensatory uptake) to 0.5 (maximum compensatory level considered). Root profiles [SOURCE: Page_7]

151used were either hypothetical or obtained from in-situ root density observations. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

152hypothetical RDD profiles were presumed to correspond to the real root activity [SOURCE: Page_7]

153pattern depending of the watering method, as water-tracking RWU models. [SOURCE: Page_7]
