Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

The problem of sensor placement for early-warning detection system is a topical issue for industry and [SOURCE: Page_2]

utilities who want to equip their networks with such technology. It consists of finding the best sensor [SOURCE: Page_2]

locations that optimize a criterion such as detection rate or time to detection. Few methods exist [SOURCE: Page_2]

concerning the sensor placement that optimizes the result of a source identification method. This paper [SOURCE: Page_2]

fills the gap by coupling an adjoint source identification method and a Monte-Carlo sensor placement [SOURCE: Page_2]

algorithm. The first one is treated through the use of a backtracking algorithm. It uses binary responses at [SOURCE: Page_2]

sensors to calculate the ranked list of potential contamination location nodes and contamination times. A [SOURCE: Page_2]

criterion is then defined based on the source identification accuracy and specificity. Finally, two [SOURCE: Page_2]

optimizing methods that maximize this criterion are proposed: a greedy algorithm and a local search [SOURCE: Page_2]

algorithm which are both coupled with a Monte Carlo method to give the locations of sensors that are the [SOURCE: Page_2]

best suited for allocating the source of a contamination. These methods are tested on a 2,500 node network [SOURCE: Page_2]

to evaluate their efficiency. [SOURCE: Page_2]

The objective of this paper is to determine optimal sensor placement methods that give the highest [SOURCE: Page_22]

analyzed (with simple manipulation such as aggregation) to evaluate the number of positive sensor [SOURCE: Page_22]

responses that each potential source of contamination can explain. This score allows the creation of a [SOURCE: Page_22]

21 [SOURCE: Page_22]

ranking list among the potential source of contamination. The method uses positive answers to find [SOURCE: Page_23]

potential nodes but also processes negative answers to eliminate candidates. It can also be extended to [SOURCE: Page_23]

multi-contamination but is quite costly in time; extended covering methods may be developed to [SOURCE: Page_23]

overcome this drawback. [SOURCE: Page_23]

The methods have been tested on a real French network with around 2,500 pipes. The influence of [SOURCE: Page_23]

the backtracking time (BT) as well as the initial sensor placement for the local search method has been [SOURCE: Page_23]

carried out. Eight parameter/method combinations have been defined: Two come from the application of [SOURCE: Page_23]

the Piller et al. (2015) method each based on average time to detection (ATDOP) and detection likelihood [SOURCE: Page_23]

(DLOP) maximization through a greedy algorithm. Six method/parameter scenarios have been added to [SOURCE: Page_23]

ATDOP and DLOP. The following two come from a greedy algorithm but with objective being the [SOURCE: Page_23]

maximization of the Contribution criteria in the two parameter cases BT being long, 24h, or short, 4h, and [SOURCE: Page_23]

observation time (OT) being short set at 2h. The fourth last sensor placements assessed are results of a [SOURCE: Page_23]

local search algorithm in the same configuration than for the greedy algorithm but with two different [SOURCE: Page_23]

initializations. The first two begin with ATDOP, the other two launched from DLOP. [SOURCE: Page_23]

It was found that the criteria are conflicting. When comparing ATDOP and DLOP, the first one is [SOURCE: Page_23]

performing better with average time to detection, accuracy and specificity, however it performs worse [SOURCE: Page_23]

concerning detection likelihood than the second one. It can be concluded that ATDOP performs better the [SOURCE: Page_23]

source identification but on less coverage of the network. Also, adding new sensors to ATDOP and DLOP [SOURCE: Page_23]

improves the results for the criteria, however each new added sensor brings fewer result improvements [SOURCE: Page_23]

each time. Particularly ATDOP gets less detection likelihood improvement than the other placement, [SOURCE: Page_23]

impacting the overall score for the source identification performance. [SOURCE: Page_23]

Concerning the backtracking time BT parameter, a small value (e.g. 4h) induces a small average [SOURCE: Page_23]

time to detection score. This parameter is important, because the results show that a small average time to [SOURCE: Page_23]

detection is performing better but on a smaller scale than with a big detection likelihood. For the [SOURCE: Page_23]

22 [SOURCE: Page_23]

observation time OT, 2h period seems to be enough to have an ability to identify the contamination, still a [SOURCE: Page_24]

longer observation time improves the results. [SOURCE: Page_24]

The placements based on the first method presented in this paper, greedy algorithm with [SOURCE: Page_24]

Contribution objective, perform the best overall, however they are the longest with an increasing iteration [SOURCE: Page_24]

execution time going from half an hour to three hours on 120 processors. The solutions given by the local [SOURCE: Page_24]

search are second and the method is much faster than the previous one taking three hours overall on 60 [SOURCE: Page_24]

processors. Finally ATDOP and DLOP are less suited for source identification than the two previous [SOURCE: Page_24]

methods but are fast to compute. [SOURCE: Page_24]

The choice of the sensor placement method for source identification will depend on the objectives [SOURCE: Page_24]

and the network properties as well as the number of sensors available. More research work should focus [SOURCE: Page_24]

on evaluating the impact of hydraulic and transport model error in the final optimal designs. In the first [SOURCE: Page_24]

case the velocity may not be accurate or not the same as in the simulations used to place the sensors. In the [SOURCE: Page_24]

second case, transport model, reaction coefficients and sensor thresholds need careful attention. Results [SOURCE: Page_24]

from the SMaRT-OnlineWDN project have shown that imperfect mixing at cross and double T-junction may [SOURCE: Page_24]

have an impact on the contamination spreading, the same applies when adding the dispersion effect to the [SOURCE: Page_24]

transport model. The two modifications can influence the simulation of the contamination scenarios, [SOURCE: Page_24]

therefore also change the sensor responses, and finally influence the sensor placement optimization. [SOURCE: Page_24]
