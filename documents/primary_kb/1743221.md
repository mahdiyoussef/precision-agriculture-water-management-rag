## 23   ABSTRACT

24           The need to understand and simulate hydrological phenomena and their interactions, and the [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   impact of anthropogenic and climate changes on natural environments have promoted the study of [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   evaporation from bare soils in arid climates. In closed Altiplano basins, such as those encountered in arid [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   and hyper arid basins in northern Chile, evaporation from shallow groundwater is the main source of [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   aquifer depletion and thus, its study is crucial for water resources management. The objective of this work [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   is to understand the mechanisms of evaporation in saline soils with shallow water tables, in order to better [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   quantify evaporation fluxes and improve our understanding of the water balance in these regions. To [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   achieve this objective, a model that couples fluid flow with heat transfer was developed and calibrated [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   using column experiments with saline soils from the Huasco salt flat basin, Chile. The model enables [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   determination of both liquid and water vapor fluxes, as well as the location of the evaporation front. [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   Experimental results showed that salt transport inside the soil profile modified the water retention curve, [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   highlighting the importance of including salt transport when modeling the evaporation processes in these [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   soils. Indeed, model simulations only agreed with the experimental data when the effect of salt transport [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   on water retention curves was taken into account. Model results also showed that the evaporation front is [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   closer to the soil surface as the water table depth reduces. Therefore, the model allows determining the [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   groundwater level depth that results in disconnection of liquid fluxes in the vadose zone. A sensitivity [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   analysis allowed understanding the effect of water-flux enhancements mechanisms on soil evaporation. [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   The results presented in this study are important as they allow quantifying the evaporation that occurs in [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   bare soils from Altiplano basins, which is typically the main water discharge in these closed basins. [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   Keywords: Soil evaporation, Altiplano basins, saline soil column, vapor flow, SiSPAT [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

46          The need to understand and simulate hydrological phenomena and their interactions, and [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   the impact of anthropogenic and climate changes on natural environments have promoted the [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   study of evaporation from bare soils in arid climates (Alazard et al., 2015). However, current [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   knowledge of evaporation remains incomplete because the complex and strongly coupled [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   interaction of transport mechanisms (phase change, capillary flow, film flow, vapor diffusion), [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   soil properties (hydrodynamic and thermal), and atmospheric forcings (air temperature, relative [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   humidity, radiation, atmospheric stability) are not well understood (Trautz et al., 2015). In closed [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   Altiplano basins such as those encountered in arid and hyper arid basins in the northern Chile, [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   evaporation is the main source of aquifer depletion, and thus its study is crucial for water [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   resources management. Understanding evaporation is also important for the study of sensitive [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   soil systems such as wetlands (de la Fuente and Niño, 2010; Johnson et al., 2010). Nonetheless, [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   understanding the evaporation processes in these environments is complex because evaporation [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   occurs under non-isothermal conditions, where water can be mobilized as liquid water or water [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   vapor (Nassar and Horton, 1989; Grifoll et al., 2005; Hernández-López, 2014). In addition, [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   quantifying evaporation in Altiplano basins is difficult due to the harsh conditions, the spatial [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   variability of the evaporation rates, and also due to the difficulty in accessing this region (Kampf [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   et al., 2005; Johnson et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_4]

63          Many studies have been carried out using laboratory soil columns to improve the [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   knowledge on the evaporation mechanisms, focusing on the combined transport of water, heat [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   and solutes (Nassar and Horton, 1989; Cahill and Parlange, 1998; Konukcu et al., 2004; Braud et [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   al., 2005b; 2009a; Gran et al., 2011; Nachshon et al., 2011; Schulz et al., 2015). These studies [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   have been performed in homogeneous soils of sand or silt under saturated conditions at the [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   beginning of the experiments, and have used saline solutions or deionized water. These [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   investigations have enabled the calibration and validation of numerical models to solve the [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   coupled equations of liquid water, water vapor, heat and solute flows, which can be used as [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   predictive tools to quantify evaporation fluxes in arid regions (Boulet et al., 1997; Grifoll et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   2005; Braud et al., 2009b; Saito et al., 2006; Novak, 2010). Nonetheless, few studies have tried to [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   understand the evaporation processes in a homogeneous natural saline medium from Altiplano [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   basins (Johnson et al., 2010; Hernández-López et al., 2014). Moreover, Lictevout et al. (2013), [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   who performed an extensive assessment of the water resources in the north of Chile, highlights [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   that the main problem in the Altiplanic closed basins is the lack of evaporation data. Although [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   studies that involve field data collection have been carried out in these regions, e.g., see Johnson [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   et al. (2010), numerical modelling has not been used to quantify the different evaporation [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   processes that occur in the soils from these environments. In a recent study, Hernández-López et [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   al. (2014) measured the temporal and spatial evolution of soil water content, electrical [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   conductivity and temperature under different groundwater levels in a laboratory column filled [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   with natural saline soil from the Huasco salt flats basin, Chile. They estimated the thermal and [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   isothermal liquid and vapor fluxes and tried to determine the location of the evaporation front, [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   which could not be precisely determined due to their sensors’ poor spatial resolution. [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   Nonetheless, these experiments provided a valuable data set that can be used to better understand [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   evaporation processes in saline soils with shallow water tables using numerical modelling. [SOURCE: Page_5]

87          The objective of this work is to complement the measurements performed by Hernández- [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   López et al. (2014) by conducting a modeling study that can improve the understanding of [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   evaporation processes from bare soils in the Chilean Altiplano basins. The specific objectives of [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   this study are to: (1) develop and calibrate a numerical model that can represent the soil water [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   content and thermal profiles, as well as the evaporation fluxes; (2) simulate liquid water and [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   water vapor fluxes to locate the evaporation front; and (3) perform a sensitivity analysis on the [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   calibrated parameters to ensure their validity based on physical constrains. To achieve these [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   objectives, we used the bare soil version of the SiSPAT (Simple Soil Plant Atmosphere Transfer) [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   model (Braud et al., 1995) and optimized the soil hydrodynamic and thermal properties to [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   adequately represent the experiments reported by Hernández-López et al. (2014). Different water [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   fluxes were analyzed in detail using the optimized model results for two water table levels, and [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   the location of the evaporation front was found. A sensitivity analysis was carried out to better [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   understand the uncertainty that calibration parameters (e.g., tortuosity, enhancement factor) have [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   on evaporation, and finally a discussion of the limitations of our approach is presented. [SOURCE: Page_6]

343   and here we only present the main findings that are relevant for this modelling study. Because the [SOURCE: Page_19]

344   column was homogeneously packed before the evaporation experiments, the zone of increased [SOURCE: Page_19]

345   electrical conductivity and moisture shown in Figure 1 is likely to be the result of salt movement [SOURCE: Page_19]

346   towards the soil surface as evaporation occurred (Nachshon et al., 2011), and also due to salt [SOURCE: Page_19]

347   transport as the water level rose. We believe that these salt-transport processes resulted in soil [SOURCE: Page_19]

348   stratification (in terms of salt deposition) and soil hydraulic properties that are no more vertically [SOURCE: Page_19]

349   homogeneous. Note also that there was no evidence of spatial heterogeneity across the soil [SOURCE: Page_19]

350   surface or preferential flow along the sidewalls of the soil column. For the 0.4 m deep water table [SOURCE: Page_19]

521   4.3 Model limitations [SOURCE: Page_27]

522          Our approach has some limitations that need to be understood in order to perform a [SOURCE: Page_27]

523   correct interpretation of the results. One important aspect that the SiSPAT model does not [SOURCE: Page_27]

524   represent is fluid flow due to solute concentration effects (Barbour and Fredlund, 1989; [SOURCE: Page_27]

525   Nachshon et al., 2011). Indeed, as shown by Nassar et al. (1989), the osmotic gradient can be an [SOURCE: Page_27]

526   important driving force for water movement in unsaturated soils with high clay content. We used [SOURCE: Page_27]

527   the approach proposed by Barbour and Fredlund (1989) to determine the order of magnitude of [SOURCE: Page_27]

528   the liquid and water vapor fluxes due to solute concentration gradients. In this analysis, we [SOURCE: Page_27]

529   assumed that sodium chloride (NaCl) was the main component that contributes to the osmotic 26 [SOURCE: Page_27]

Author-produced version of the article published in Hydrological Processes (2016), 30 25: 4704-4719 The original publication is available at http://onlinelibrary.wiley.com doi:10.1002/hyp.10987 [SOURCE: Page_28]

530   pressure and that the maximum solute concentration occurred at saturated conditions (~25 wt% [SOURCE: Page_28]

531   for NaCl). Also, the observed experimental conditions were utilized to evaluate the temperature [SOURCE: Page_28]

532   in the Van’t Hoff equation (Barbour and Fredlund, 1989), and the experimental soil water content [SOURCE: Page_28]

533   was used to determine the osmotic flow of water within the soil. This analysis resulted in osmotic [SOURCE: Page_28]

534   fluid flows on the order of 10-11 m/s, which are at least one order of magnitude smaller than those [SOURCE: Page_28]

535   obtained using SiSPAT. Thus, in our experiments we expect a small contribution of the osmotic [SOURCE: Page_28]

536   flow of water in the liquid and the gaseous phase. [SOURCE: Page_28]

537          It is also important to discuss that discrepancy between experimental data and model [SOURCE: Page_28]

538   predictions has led to the development of vapor-flux enhancement factors, such as  (Philip and [SOURCE: Page_28]

539   de Vries, 1957; Cass et al., 1984). The inclusion of these enhancement factors in numerical [SOURCE: Page_28]

540   models, such as that used in this work, is due to a lack of sufficiently accurate description of [SOURCE: Page_28]

541   vapor dynamics to represent correctly the soil physics processes that occur during evaporation [SOURCE: Page_28]

542   (Assouline et al., 2013). There are many processes that explain this vapor-flux enhancement [SOURCE: Page_28]

543   (Philip and de Vries, 1957; Cass et al., 1984; Bachmann et al., 2001; Grifoll et al., 2005; Shokri [SOURCE: Page_28]

544   et al., 2009; Shahraeeni and Or, 2012; Assouline et al., 2013; Trautz et al., 2015). For instance, [SOURCE: Page_28]

545   investigations have shown that with proper account of capillary flow, continuity and pathways, [SOURCE: Page_28]

546   no vapor-flux enhancement factors are required (Grifoll et al., 2005; Shokri et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_28]

547   Shahraeeni and Or (2012) demonstrated that water transport can be enhanced by ~10% when [SOURCE: Page_28]

548   isolated liquid-phase bridges are present due to a reduction in the gaseous diffusion path length, [SOURCE: Page_28]

549   and that thermal gradients can enhance water vapor diffusion. Also, vapor flux from within the [SOURCE: Page_28]

550   soil profile can be enhanced by thermally driven convective transport mechanisms (Bachmann et [SOURCE: Page_28]

27 [SOURCE: Page_28]

Author-produced version of the article published in Hydrological Processes (2016), 30 25: 4704-4719 The original publication is available at http://onlinelibrary.wiley.com doi:10.1002/hyp.10987 [SOURCE: Page_29]

551   al., 2001). Recently, Trautz et al. (2015) argued that non-equilibrium phase change is also [SOURCE: Page_29]

552   responsible for vapor-flux enhancement. [SOURCE: Page_29]

553          From the vapor-flux enhancement mechanisms that have been reported in the scientific [SOURCE: Page_29]

554   literature, we hypothesize that a combination between non-equilibrium phase change and cyclic [SOURCE: Page_29]

555   thermal conditions typically found between day and night, could be responsible for the vapor-flux [SOURCE: Page_29]

556   enhancement observed in our experiments. When cyclic thermal conditions are present, two [SOURCE: Page_29]

557   processes can drive soil evaporation: evaporation from the soil surface into the atmosphere during [SOURCE: Page_29]

558   early morning and subsurface evaporation limited by Fickian Diffusion until late afternoon. As [SOURCE: Page_29]

559   explained by Assouline et al. (2013), evaporation from the soil surface depletes the water [SOURCE: Page_29]

560   condensed and redistributed during nighttime. After the depletion of this water, Fickian diffusion [SOURCE: Page_29]

561   becomes the governing process and increases the thickness of the dry layer of soil observed at the [SOURCE: Page_29]

562   soil surface. In addition, as discussed by Trautz et al. (2015), non-equilibrium processes are [SOURCE: Page_29]

563   relevant when cyclic thermal conditions occur. [SOURCE: Page_29]

564          Many studies have used laboratory columns, but very few of them have focused on [SOURCE: Page_29]

565   understanding natural highly saline dry soils. Moreover, typical column experiments do not deal [SOURCE: Page_29]

566   with natural highly salty soils and low water content. Our results show that evaporation in [SOURCE: Page_29]

567   initially dry soils produce moisture content and conductivity distributions that are atypical. This [SOURCE: Page_29]

568   behavior has not been reported in the literature, which typically reports experiments in which an [SOURCE: Page_29]

569   initially saturated soil column is subsequently dried out. [SOURCE: Page_29]

575   calibrated using laboratory observations performed in a homogeneous natural soil column that [SOURCE: Page_30]

576   was filled with soil from the Huasco salt flat, Chile. Modeled and experimental results only [SOURCE: Page_30]

577   agreed when the soil hydrodynamic properties (water retention and hydraulic conductivity curve) [SOURCE: Page_30]

578   were calibrated. The change in the soil hydrodynamic properties can be explained by [SOURCE: Page_30]

582   water content and thermal profiles, and also reproduced the experimental cumulative evaporation [SOURCE: Page_30]

585   (region 1) is located near the surface, where the total flux is directed upwards and the water [SOURCE: Page_30]

586   movement is mainly due to vapor fluxes driven by pressure gradients. The second zone (region 2) [SOURCE: Page_30]

587   is located below region 1 and is where the liquid water flux dominates. The third zone (region 3) [SOURCE: Page_30]

588   is where the liquid water flux is very small. Model results indicate that evaporation occurs in the [SOURCE: Page_30]

589   upper soil profile and that the position of the evaporation front depends on the water table depth. [SOURCE: Page_30]
