11                                                                Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   Accurate modeling of water distribution systems is fundamental for the planning and operating [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   decisions in any water network. One important component that directly affects model accuracy is [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   the knowledge of nodal demands. Conventional models simulate flows and pressures of a water [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   distribution network either assuming constant demands at nodes or using a short-term sample of [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   demand data. Due to the stochastic behavior of the water demands, this assumption usually leads [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   to an inadequate understanding of the full range of operational states in the water system. [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   Installation of sensor devices in a network can provide information about some components in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   system. However, the requirement for a reliable water distribution model that can assist with [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   understanding of real-time events in the entire water distribution system is still an objective for [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   hydraulic engineers. [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   This paper proposes a methodology for the estimation of online (near real-time) demand [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   multipliers. A predictor-corrector approach is developed which predicts the hydraulic behaviors of [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   the water network based on a nonlinear demand prediction model, and corrects the prediction by [SOURCE: Page_2]

Author-produced version of the article published in : Journal of Water Resources Planning and Management, vol 143, n°11, 2017 [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   integrating online observation data. The standard particle filter and an improved particle filter [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   method, which incorporates the evolutionary scheme from genetic algorithms into the resampling [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   process to prevent particle degeneracy, impoverishment and convergence problems, are [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   investigated to implement the predictor-corrector approach. Uncertainties of model outputs are [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   also quantified and evaluated in terms of confidence intervals. Two case studies are presented to [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed particle filter model. Results show that the model [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   can provide a reliable estimate of demand multipliers in near real-time contexts. [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   Water distribution systems (WDS) are constructed to supply water for domestic, industrial and [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   commercial consumers. The design, operation and management of these distribution systems is [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   usually supported by the application of hydraulic models, which are built to replicate the behavior [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   of real systems. These conventional models simulate flows and pressures of a WDS either under [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   steady state conditions (constant demands and operational conditions) or under a short term [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   extended period simulation (time-varying demands and operational conditions), for example a day [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   or a week (USEPA 2005). The outputs from hydraulic models, therefore, usually represent the [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   distribution system behavior during the sampling period (Preis et al. 2009). This leads to an [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   inadequate understanding of the full range of operational states in the water system. [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   The installation of sensor devices as well as the Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   (SCADA) systems within the WDS can provide information on the status of some components in [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   the system. However, the use of this additional data is currently limited to computing gross [SOURCE: Page_3]

Author-produced version of the article published in : Journal of Water Resources Planning and Management, vol 143, n°11, 2017 [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   differences between the model outputs and reality (Kang & Lansey 2009). Modification of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   hydraulic models to maintain the consistency between observed data and simulated data is still a [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   challenge that needs to be dealt with. Estimation of the model states/parameters, hence, is required [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   so that the model is able to represent the real system. [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   Estimation is the process of fitting the outputs from the computer model, usually the pressures and [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   flow rates at particular locations in the water network, with the field measurements, in order to [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   calculate unknown variables of interest. Initial estimation studies in WDSs were pioneered by [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   Rahal et al. (1980), Walski (1983) and Bhave (1988) with the proposal of the ad hoc (trial-and- [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   error) calibration schemes, in which an iterative process to update unknown model parameters was [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   implemented. Due to the slow convergence rate, this method is only applicable to small water [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   networks. Later, explicit calibration methods were introduced (Ormsbee & Wood 1986; Boulos & [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   Wood 1990; Boulos & Ormsbee 1991). These methods solved an even-determined set of water [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   network equations where the number of unknown parameters is grouped to be equal to the number [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   of measurements. As the measurement errors were also neglected, these methods usually did not [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   represent real-world practical outputs. Therefore, explicit calibration models were often used to [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   analyse historic events in water systems (Savic et al. 2009). Subsequently, implicit methods were [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   developed using either mathematical techniques or evolutionary optimization techniques, for [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   example: Complex Method (Ormsbee 1989), Weighted Least Squares approaches (Lansey & [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   Basnet 1991; Datta & Sridharan 1994), Singular value decomposition (SVD) method (Sanz & [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   Pérez 2015) or Genetic Algorithms (GA) (Preis et al. 2009; Abe & Peter 2010; Do et al. 2016). [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   These methods have drawn a high degree of attention from researchers. However, these models [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   are mostly impractical due to either a requirement for a large quantity of ‘good’ observation data [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   (Savic et al. 2009) or ignoring model uncertainties. Furthermore, few approaches have attempted [SOURCE: Page_4]

Author-produced version of the article published in : Journal of Water Resources Planning and Management, vol 143, n°11, 2017 [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   to estimate model parameters and model states in conjunction with model uncertainties. Bargiela [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   and Hainsworth (1989) found that a good approximation of pressure uncertainty bounds can be [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   obtained by a linearization of the mathematical network model. Piller (1995) and Bush and Uber [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   (1998) used a sampling design method to estimate the model parameters and approximate the [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   uncertainties. Lansey et al. (2001) applied a first-order approximation method to identify pipe [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   roughness uncertainty. Nagar and Powell (2002) applied a linear fractional transformation and [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   semi-definite programming method to estimate the pressure heads and their confidence bounds. In [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   addition, some probabilistic methods (Xu & Goulter 1998; Kapelan et al. 2007; Hutton et al. 2013) [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   have also been investigated for the estimation of model parameters. Due to the complexity of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   uncertainties, estimation methods associated with uncertainty quantification are still a continuing [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   research area, especially for real-time estimation purposes. [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   The complexity of uncertainties in WDS modeling has been addressed in Hutton et al. (2012b), in [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   which the uncertainty is divided into three categories: (1) structural uncertainty, (2) parameter [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   uncertainty and (3) measurement/data uncertainty. Structural uncertainty derives from the [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   mathematical representation of the real system, such as network skeletonization and model [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   aggregation. Skeletonized and/or aggregated models are predominantly used instead of all-pipes [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   models to reduce the complexity of the network being analysed as well as to increase [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   computational speed. It has been shown that skeletonized/aggregated network models can closely [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   resemble the behaviour of full sized systems under steady state conditions (e.g. Perelman et al. [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   (2008) and Preis et al. (2011)). The second category, parameter uncertainty, refers to the errors of [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   the parameters used to represent system components (e.g. pipe roughnesses, pipe diameters). [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   According to Kang and Lansey (2009), these parameters are time invariant or vary slowly over [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   time. Hence, this source of uncertainty can be neglected for real-time estimation problems. Finally, [SOURCE: Page_5]

Author-produced version of the article published in : Journal of Water Resources Planning and Management, vol 143, n°11, 2017 [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   measurement/data uncertainty is the uncertainty from measurement devices and, more importantly, [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   uncertainty from the inability to capture the temporal and spatial variation of consumer demands. [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   Because of their high impact on model uncertainty during short periods of time (or in real-time), [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   nodal demands are therefore usually selected as the time varying parameters to be estimated. [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   The issue of short term demand forecasting and real-time demand estimation under uncertainties [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   can be found in some recent studies. Note that the short-term demand forecasting and demand [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   estimation are two different problems. The former focuses on predicting future demands (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   Cutore et al. (2008), Hutton and Kapelan (2015) and Alvisi and Franchini (2017)). The latter [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   focuses on estimation of the current demands, which is also the main interest of this paper. This is [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   useful, as demand estimation can be used at regular time steps to verify the accuracy of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   predicted value and update the system operations. The problem of near real-time demand [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   estimation has been studied using different approaches. Shang et al. (2006) applied an extended [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   Kalman filter, an iterative linear algorithm for nonlinear state estimation, to approximate water [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   demand patterns. In that paper, water demand patterns were predicted by an ARIMA time series [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   model and were refined using real-time observations. Similarly, Hutton et al. (2012a) introduced [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   a particle filter method and an ensemble Kalman filter for the estimation of a single district meter [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   area, which was assumed to follow a linear time series model. The particle filter model was [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   implemented with and without measurement error to show its effect on the demand prediction [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   uncertainty. An alternative for the demand estimates can be found in Kang and Lansey (2009). In [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   their paper, two comprehensive methods for the demand estimation problem were introduced, the [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   Kalman filter and the tracking state estimator (TSE). For the Kalman filter model, the water [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   demand patterns were also assumed to follow a linear time series model, while the TSE model [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   involved recursively computing the sensitivity matrix (i.e. the Jacobian matrix of the measurement [SOURCE: Page_6]

Author-produced version of the article published in : Journal of Water Resources Planning and Management, vol 143, n°11, 2017 [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   vector with regards to the change in the state vector). The uncertainties of the demand estimates [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   were suggested to be quantified by applying the first-order second moment formula. The two [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   models were then tested on a case study (116 pipes, 90 nodes, 1 source and 1 tank) with an [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   assumption that 19 flow measurement sites and 5 pressure measurement sites were available. It [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   should be noted that the demand estimation problem is sensitive to the locations and types of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   measurements (Do et al. 2016). Demand estimation models usually perform better with flow [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   measurements rather than pressure/head measurements. However, due to the cost and difficulty of [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   installing flow measurement devices compared to pressure measurement devices, flow [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   measurement devices are usually not as commonly used as pressure measurement devices in real [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   WDS networks. [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   In summary, water demands in WDS studies are usually assumed to be known and varied based [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   on a diurnal curve. However, this assumption might lead to large approximations of WDS states [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   in real-time due to the unpredictable variation of the water demands. Some efforts have been [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   focused on the real-time demand estimation. By assuming that the water demand follows a linear [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   time series prediction model, these models approximated the water demand patterns with some [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   linear algorithms such as the Kalman filter or extended Kalman filter. Given the nonlinear [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   stochastic nature of the water demands as well as the need for practical applicability, real-time [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   estimation modeling of WDS still requires much research effort. [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   This paper presents a model framework for the online (near real-time) demand estimation of a [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   WDS, which is named the DMFLive model. A predictor-corrector methodology is adopted in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   DMFLive model to predict the hydraulic behaviors of the water network based on a nonlinear [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   demand prediction sub-model, and to correct the prediction by using online pressure observation [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   data. A particle filter method is applied to implement the predictor-corrector approach. The typical [SOURCE: Page_7]

Author-produced version of the article published in : Journal of Water Resources Planning and Management, vol 143, n°11, 2017 [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   problems of the particle filter approach (particle degeneracy, impoverishment and particle [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   convergence) are investigated by two different resampling schemes: systematic resampling (SR) [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   algorithm and systematic resampling integrated with a genetic algorithm process (SRGA). [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   Uncertainties of model outputs are quantified and evaluated in terms of confidence intervals. [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   The paper is structured as follows. First, an explanation of the state estimation problem and its [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   conceptual solution is introduced. Second, the basic concepts of particle filter methods to solve the [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   estimation problem are explained. This is followed by a detailed description of the particle filter [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   methodology applied for water demand state estimation in WDS. Two case studies are then used [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   to evaluate the model. Finally, conclusions and suggestions for future work are given. [SOURCE: Page_8]

343   Several assumptions are made for this study: (1) the model of the water distribution network [SOURCE: Page_17]

344   perfectly represents the real system with known network characteristics (e.g. pipe roughness [SOURCE: Page_17]

430   In extended period simulations of most hydraulic solvers (including EPANET), the nodal demands [SOURCE: Page_21]

431   are considered to be constant during the time step. The levels of the tanks in the network at the end [SOURCE: Page_21]

432   of the time step are consequently computed based on this assumption and are used as the initial [SOURCE: Page_21]

433   tank level for the next step. Due to continuously unpredictable change of the water demand in [SOURCE: Page_21]

434   practice, the actual tank level at the end of the time step is usually different to the tank level [SOURCE: Page_21]

435   computed by the model. As a result, the estimated total volume of water used during the time step [SOURCE: Page_21]

436   is also different from the actual volume of water used in practice. This issue can be overcome by [SOURCE: Page_21]

440   available. In other words, the model outputs will be the estimates of the demand multiplier at the [SOURCE: Page_22]

441   previous time step. [SOURCE: Page_22]

534   Effect of the locations of measurements on the quantification of demand uncertainty [SOURCE: Page_26]

535   As discussed in a number of studies such as in Piller (1995) and Do et al. (2016), the locations of [SOURCE: Page_26]

536   the measurements have a strong impact on the results of the demand estimation models. [SOURCE: Page_26]

537   Furthermore, the selection of measurement locations also affects the confidence intervals of the [SOURCE: Page_26]

538   estimation outputs. [SOURCE: Page_26]

539   From the mathematical point of view, the uncertainty of estimated demands depends on the [SOURCE: Page_26]

540   sensitivity of the flows/heads at measurement locations in relation to the change in the water nodal [SOURCE: Page_26]

541   demands. This sensitivity is represented by the sensitivity matrix J (Eq. (18)), which is, in this case [SOURCE: Page_26]

542   study, the Jacobian matrix of the heads with respect to the demand multipliers. The sensitivity of [SOURCE: Page_26]

543   the heads with respect to the change of the demand multipliers depends on two factors: (1) the [SOURCE: Page_26]

544   position of the nodes in the network and (2) the base demands at the nodes. In fact, the nodes close [SOURCE: Page_26]

545   to fixed-head nodes (tanks or reservoirs) are less sensitive than the ones far from the fixed-head [SOURCE: Page_26]

546   nodes. This is because of a change in nodal demands will result in a smaller change in the pressures [SOURCE: Page_26]

547   of the closer nodes than the farther nodes. In a similar way, small base demands in the same pattern [SOURCE: Page_26]

548   will result in small friction losses and consequently small changes in pressures. Therefore, nodes [SOURCE: Page_26]

549   selected in these regions may cause large uncertainty in demand multiplier estimation. The [SOURCE: Page_26]

550   sensitivity matrix takes into account these two factors. Small values in the sensitivity matrix values [SOURCE: Page_26]

Author-produced version of the article published in : Journal of Water Resources Planning and Management, vol 143, n°11, 2017 [SOURCE: Page_27]

551   mean that the nodes are less sensitive to the demands and the estimation might have large [SOURCE: Page_27]

552   uncertainty. Therefore, the uncertainty of the estimated DMFs can be reduced by selecting the [SOURCE: Page_27]

553   more sensitive locations in the network. [SOURCE: Page_27]

589   Conclusions and recommendations [SOURCE: Page_28]

590   Real-time demand estimation under uncertainties is exceptionally difficult due to the unpredictable [SOURCE: Page_28]

591   stochastic behavior of the water demand as well as the nonlinearities of hydraulic systems. In this [SOURCE: Page_28]

592   paper, the DMFLive model framework has been introduced, which can be used to estimate the [SOURCE: Page_28]

593   demand multipliers of a WDS in near real-time. A predictor-corrector approach has been adopted [SOURCE: Page_28]

594   and solved by a particle filter method. A nonlinear demand prediction model is applied to predict [SOURCE: Page_28]

595   water demand multipliers at each time step, while the online pressure observations are used to [SOURCE: Page_28]

596   correct the prediction. Output uncertainty caused by the measurement errors has also been [SOURCE: Page_28]

Author-produced version of the article published in : Journal of Water Resources Planning and Management, vol 143, n°11, 2017 [SOURCE: Page_29]

597   quantified by the first-order approximation formula. The performance of the DMFLive model is [SOURCE: Page_29]

598   evaluated by two WDS case studies. The results showed that the nonlinear demand prediction [SOURCE: Page_29]

599   model combined with the particle filter method used in the paper are well suited for the near real- [SOURCE: Page_29]

600   time demand estimation problem. [SOURCE: Page_29]

601   Within the first case study, the benefits of having additional information about the tank level of [SOURCE: Page_29]

602   the next time step have been explored. If the estimation of the demand multipliers can be delayed [SOURCE: Page_29]

603   one time step, the tank level at the beginning of the next time step can be used by the model to [SOURCE: Page_29]

604   improve the estimation of the total volume of water used. [SOURCE: Page_29]

605   Within the second case study, three versions of the DMFLive model were developed to be used in [SOURCE: Page_29]

606   large networks with multiple demand patterns. All versions provided good results, showing that [SOURCE: Page_29]

607   the models are capable to be used in large networks. Finally, the effect of the measurement [SOURCE: Page_29]

608   locations on the uncertainty of the estimated demand multipliers has been explored. Results [SOURCE: Page_29]

609   showed that the uncertainty can be used to identify which measurement locations need to be [SOURCE: Page_29]

610   improved. Future work involves considering adding additional uncertainties into the DMFLive [SOURCE: Page_29]

611   model. Moreover, testing the model for non-geographically distributed demand networks is also [SOURCE: Page_29]

612   necessary to show its capability when applied in practice. [SOURCE: Page_29]
