Abstract: [SOURCE: Page_2]

This paper considers the sectorization of a large water distribution network into DMAs and [SOURCE: Page_2]

simultaneously optimizes rehabilitation of the network with new pipes, control valves, and storage [SOURCE: Page_2]

tanks. Since the available water resources are much smaller in the dry season, both the design and [SOURCE: Page_2]

operational settings are optimized to satisfy water demand, water quality and pressure constraints, and [SOURCE: Page_2]

efficiency indices under stringent conditions. Because of the heterogeneity of the multiple decision [SOURCE: Page_2]

variables and the complicated way they interact through the multiple objectives (some complimentary [SOURCE: Page_2]

and some conflicting), it is not possible to fully automate the simultaneous sectorization, rehabilitation [SOURCE: Page_2]

and operational optimization. Therefore, we employ a multi-stage approach where engineering [SOURCE: Page_2]

judgment and network graph simplification and visualization tools are employed to find a good feasible [SOURCE: Page_2]

solution that is used as a first guess for further optimization of sectors and operational settings, to [SOURCE: Page_2]

achieve feasible solutions with better cost of implementation, demand similarity among DMAs and [SOURCE: Page_2]

better pressure uniformity in operations. A multi-objective Agent Swarm Optimization (ASO) [SOURCE: Page_2]

framework is used to iteratively change the sectors at the boundaries. For the final configuration, [SOURCE: Page_2]

sequential linear programming is used to find optimal valve and pump settings. [SOURCE: Page_2]

## INTRODUCTION

This manuscript presents our work on the optimal sectorization of the E-town network, which was part [SOURCE: Page_3]

of the Battle of Water Networks DMAs (BWNDMA 2016), a special contest with 14 participants that [SOURCE: Page_3]

was presented at WDSA 2016 in Cartagena, Colombia, the sixth one in a series of contests since 1985 [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Giustolisi et al., 2015). Firstly, we describe the network and the problem objectives and constraints. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Then, the methodology employed is explained, which consists of some engineering judgment and [SOURCE: Page_3]

network analysis tools to simplify the problem, and the use of mathematical optimization and agent [SOURCE: Page_3]

swarm optimization for design and operational decisions. We subsequently present our results and [SOURCE: Page_3]

discuss lessons of general validity that have been learned from this exercise. Finally, we conclude with [SOURCE: Page_3]

a summary. [SOURCE: Page_3]

This design problem is a very difficult since there are multiple decision variables that are not easy to [SOURCE: Page_6]

There are also approaches that try to tackle the problem directly with heuristic optimization algorithms [SOURCE: Page_6]

A reference to a plethora of other related work on sectorization approaches and specific objectives for [SOURCE: Page_7]

sectorization can be found in these references and literature cited therein. [SOURCE: Page_7]

The approach followed in our work is not exclusively based on any one method presented in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

literature but a combination of several approaches together with engineering judgement to solve the [SOURCE: Page_7]

overall problem in multiple stages. As previously demonstrated in Khedr & Tolson (2015) for a water [SOURCE: Page_7]

are determined using convex optimization tools. In the following section, we describe the [SOURCE: Page_7]

methodologies in detail. [SOURCE: Page_7]

## METHODOLOGY

Engineering principles and Porteau [SOURCE: Page_7]

The Porteau software solution (2017) is a hydraulic toolkit for Water Distribution Analysis that is [SOURCE: Page_7]

developed by IRSTEA research institute in France. It was designed with the help of a Computer- [SOURCE: Page_7]

Aided Software Engineering tool and object oriented programing. This software is an alternative to [SOURCE: Page_7]

the well-known EPANET solution (Rossman, 2000) and it provides different additional tools for [SOURCE: Page_7]

analyzing complex WDNs such as simplification of the network, detection of isolated parts and [SOURCE: Page_7]

duplicated pipes, and it owns a stochastic module for assessing the hydraulic state at peak period [SOURCE: Page_7]

(Piller and Bremond, 2002). The technologic choice and the software possibilities are described in [SOURCE: Page_7]

Piller et al. (2011). [SOURCE: Page_7]

Porteau was first used in the model validation step to identify parts that are isolated from water [SOURCE: Page_8]

resources and to check errors in the data file itself. In the original network file provided for the [SOURCE: Page_8]

competition, a set of pipes was discovered to be disconnected from the rest of the network. This may [SOURCE: Page_8]

cause problems for a hydraulic solver with the presence of customer nodes with no reference of head. [SOURCE: Page_8]

One pipe, originally closed (i.e.: Pipe ‘8872’ in the INP file provided), was opened to avoid problems [SOURCE: Page_8]

in hydraulic analysis and force the hydraulic solver to compute a correct rather than arbitrary [SOURCE: Page_8]

piezometric head. Another issue we identified was regarding the two tanks ‘Tank_3’ and ‘Tank_4’ [SOURCE: Page_8]

connected by a short pipe. This caused mass oscillation between the two tanks at each iteration and [SOURCE: Page_8]

extended period simulation (EPS) hydraulic equations like in Porteau and EPANET were not able to [SOURCE: Page_8]

find a correct physical solution since they ignore pipe inertia; such problems would not be an issue if [SOURCE: Page_8]

a rigid water-column model was used (Piller & Propato, 2006; Nault and Karney, 2016). We also [SOURCE: Page_8]

found that this modeling flaw increased considerably the executable time of the EPS. Since the supply [SOURCE: Page_8]

flow direction is from ‘Tank_3’ to ‘Tank_4’, both in rainy and dry seasons, we placed check valves [SOURCE: Page_8]

on the outgoing pipes from both tanks. This prevention of two-way flow in the numerical model [SOURCE: Page_8]

prevented the mass oscillation, both decreasing the computational time and giving correct and [SOURCE: Page_8]

physically meaningful results. In valve and pump setting optimization problems (see last subsection), [SOURCE: Page_8]

the same set of issues were resolved by putting a unidirectional constraint on the flow of the links [SOURCE: Page_8]

originating from ‘Tank_3’ and ‘Tank_4’. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Several parallel pipes (with the same positions) were found in the original network data. An attentive [SOURCE: Page_8]

check was made to ensure it was not an error coming from the GIS. In Porteau, a connectivity analysis [SOURCE: Page_8]

highlights parallel pipes and visualizes them. Then they are manually checked to see if they are [SOURCE: Page_8]

different pipes. Since we found no duplicated pipes, the parallel pipes were kept in the model. Five [SOURCE: Page_8]

out of service tanks were transformed into junction nodes. Practically, this allows water to cross the [SOURCE: Page_8]

valve chamber and bypass the tank. Another abandoned tank (‘Tank_7’) was isolated because its [SOURCE: Page_8]

elevation was too high to be supplied with sufficient pressure coming from another tank or through [SOURCE: Page_8]

nodes around it. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The second step was to analyze the supply routes (Figure 2) and elevation map (Figure 3) to [SOURCE: Page_9]

understand the supply network (see Figure 1). In the rainy season, the water has three origins. The [SOURCE: Page_9]

source in the south, Cuza WTP, constitutes more than half of the supply and provides water to a large [SOURCE: Page_9]

part of the city. The source at the central east of the network, Bachue WTP, is at a low elevation and [SOURCE: Page_9]

can only supply the low elevation parts close to it, including Tank_14. Bochica WTP, although it has [SOURCE: Page_9]

half the capacity of the south supply, it has the highest elevation and so supplies areas with a large [SOURCE: Page_9]

variation in elevation across the north of the network. [SOURCE: Page_9]

To reduce energy losses in distribution, one of the objectives to be minimized in the new sectorization [SOURCE: Page_9]

is pressure uniformity (i.e. the 𝑃𝑈%'< function in the BWNDMA Problem Description and Rules [SOURCE: Page_9]

(BWNDMA, 2016)). This was taken into account in forming a first feasible DMA configuration using [SOURCE: Page_9]

engineering know how and Porteau Software for graph sectorization and hydraulic and water quality [SOURCE: Page_9]

simulation tool. Initially, the elevation information of the network data was used to visualize a digital [SOURCE: Page_10]

elevation model (DEM). The contours of homogeneous elevations were then used to form the sector [SOURCE: Page_10]

frontiers, where each sector would have a small variance in the elevation. In this way 15 DMAs were [SOURCE: Page_10]

chosen as first guess based on elevation similarity, since the other objective specified in the battle was [SOURCE: Page_10]

also minimizing the number of DMAs with a minimum number of 15 DMAs. The contours were then [SOURCE: Page_10]

moved sufficiently to make sure the DMAs have similar volumes of demand. Closed valves were placed [SOURCE: Page_10]

for isolation from the rest of the network, and one main entry point was chosen for each DMA and [SOURCE: Page_10]

tagged “meters/compteurs”. A graph theoretic tool was used to check that each DMA was connected [SOURCE: Page_10]

only through the tagged inlets: using the network incidence matrix of the connected components and [SOURCE: Page_10]

the PRIM algorithm (a greedy depth-first-search algorithm) the inlets to each DMA were confirmed [SOURCE: Page_10]

(Bartnik & Minoux, 1986). For double-checking the results, a modified version of the Lee algorithm [SOURCE: Page_10]

(Lee, 1961) was used for identifying existing sectors in the network model. The algorithm uses graph [SOURCE: Page_10]

decomposition and the information of the network elements for traversing in depth search until the [SOURCE: Page_10]

border of a potential sector is reached. [SOURCE: Page_10]

The third step is iterative and includes the use of two different steady state hydraulic models to check [SOURCE: Page_10]

the pressure at demand nodes during the peak period. The conventional deterministic models predict [SOURCE: Page_10]

too large flow rates in main pipes, which are improbable to occur simultaneously. On the contrary, [SOURCE: Page_10]

flow rates in branched pipes supplying few consumers are underestimated by the latter. More [SOURCE: Page_10]

representative values can be derived by considering stochastic demands and upper limits of [SOURCE: Page_10]

different confidence intervals; we use the stochastic model Opointe in Porteau that exploits [SOURCE: Page_10]

peak demand diversity/simultaneity curves (Piller and Brémond, 2002) to more accurately [SOURCE: Page_10]

approximate the flow rate at pipes as a function (𝑎𝑛 + 𝑏 𝑛 ) of the number of domestic [SOURCE: Page_10]

consumers, n, served. This way a less pessimistic pressure is calculated in the core network [SOURCE: Page_10]

that corresponds to a satisfaction risk. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Additionally, EPANET was used for simulation from within a c-code with conventional analysis of low [SOURCE: Page_11]

and peak periods. If the lower constraint limit of 15m for pressure was not satisfied it was necessary to [SOURCE: Page_11]

adjust the DMA boundary and specify a new DMA with a more homogenous pressure. Following this, [SOURCE: Page_11]

another steady state for low demand consumption was run to check for the pressure upper limit of 60m. [SOURCE: Page_11]

Among the main results, it was necessary to reinforce the network with two new pipes of length [SOURCE: Page_11]

The fourth step was to tune the setting of the flow control valves (FCVs) and of the pressure regulating [SOURCE: Page_11]

devices (PRVs) that are added at entry points of DMAs. The main role of the FCV is to control the [SOURCE: Page_11]

setting of the FCV. [SOURCE: Page_11]

The solution of the rainy season with pumps working and reduced flows from the water treatment plants [SOURCE: Page_11]

was adapted for the dry season. It was necessary to make two kinds of change: closing and opening [SOURCE: Page_11]

valves to introduce new water routes (shortcuts) and to supply some tanks with insufficient water inflow [SOURCE: Page_11]

## SUMMARY AND PERSPECTIVES

The objective of this paper is to share our experience with the civil engineering and academic [SOURCE: Page_22]

community in facing the challenging Battle of Water Networks District Meter Areas problem. [SOURCE: Page_22]

We have adopted three main strategies: [SOURCE: Page_22]
