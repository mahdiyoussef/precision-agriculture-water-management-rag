Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

Participatory approaches are now increasingly recognized and used as an essential element of policies and [SOURCE: Page_3]

programs, especially in regards to natural resource management (NRM). Most practitioners, decision‐makers [SOURCE: Page_3]

and researchers having adopted participatory approaches also acknowledge the need to monitor and evaluate [SOURCE: Page_3]

such approaches in order to audit their effectiveness, support decision‐making or improve learning. Many [SOURCE: Page_3]

manuals and frameworks exist on how to carry out monitoring and evaluation (M&E) for participatory [SOURCE: Page_3]

processes. However, few provide guidelines on the selection and implementation of M&E methods, an aspect [SOURCE: Page_3]

which is also often obscure in published studies, at the expense of the transparency, reliability and validity of [SOURCE: Page_3]

the study. In this paper, we argue that the selection and implementation of M&E methods are particularly [SOURCE: Page_3]

strategic when monitoring and evaluating a participatory process. We demonstrate that evaluators of [SOURCE: Page_3]

participatory processes have to tackle a quadruple challenge when selecting and implementing methods: using [SOURCE: Page_3]

mixed‐methods, both qualitative and quantitative; assessing the participatory process, its outcomes, and its [SOURCE: Page_3]

context; taking into account both the theory and participants’ views; and being both rigorous and adaptive. [SOURCE: Page_3]

The M&E of a participatory planning process in the Rwenzori Region, Uganda, is used as an example to show [SOURCE: Page_3]

how these challenges unfold on the ground and how they can be tackled. Based on this example, we conclude [SOURCE: Page_3]

by providing tools and strategies that can be used by evaluators to ensure that they make utile, feasible, [SOURCE: Page_3]

coherent, transparent and adaptive methodological choices when monitoring and evaluating participatory [SOURCE: Page_3]

processes for NRM. [SOURCE: Page_3]

### 1. Introduction

Participatory approaches are now increasingly recognized and used as an essential element of policies and [SOURCE: Page_4]

programs, especially related to environmental or natural resource management (NRM) (Dyer et al. 2014; Vacik [SOURCE: Page_4]

et al. 2014). Participatory processes for NRM can be defined as the involvement of members of the public in [SOURCE: Page_4]

agenda‐setting, decision‐making, and policy‐forming activities of organizations or institutions responsible for [SOURCE: Page_4]

NRM (based on Rowe and Frewer 2004). In the remainder of this paper, the term “participatory processes” [SOURCE: Page_4]

refers to participatory processes in the field of NRM. Most practitioners, decision‐makers and researchers [SOURCE: Page_4]

having adopted participatory processes also acknowledge the need to monitor and evaluate such processes [SOURCE: Page_4]

(e.g. Conrad et al. 2011). Monitoring and evaluation (M&E) is usually undertaken to audit the efficiency and [SOURCE: Page_4]

effectiveness of the participatory process, support decisions about the process and learning and documenting [SOURCE: Page_4]

experiences (Forss 2005). Evaluators may be independent judges, participants in a process, evaluation experts [SOURCE: Page_4]

or researchers. [SOURCE: Page_4]

M&E of NRM participatory processes pose specific challenges compared to both M&E of “non‐participatory” [SOURCE: Page_4]

processes and M&E of participatory processes in other fields. For example, participation of a wide range of [SOURCE: Page_4]

stakeholders generates a multiplicity of perspectives and objectives in terms of what the M&E should entail, [SOURCE: Page_4]

and how and when it should be carried out. In parallel, the complexity of social–ecological systems, which [SOURCE: Page_4]

involve diverse actors and sectors, variable stressors, ambiguous cause‐effect relationships, and continuous [SOURCE: Page_4]

and non‐linear changes, preclude the use of traditional approaches to evaluation (Faber and Alkemade 2011). [SOURCE: Page_4]

Many of the challenges faced by evaluators when M&E participatory processes relate to the selection and [SOURCE: Page_4]

implementation of M&E methods. M&E methods are defined here as the techniques or procedures used to [SOURCE: Page_4]

obtain and collate raw data on the participatory process. These include, among others, document reviews, [SOURCE: Page_4]

interviews, participant observation, questionnaires or modelling. The choice and implementation of methods [SOURCE: Page_4]

are particularly strategic when monitoring and evaluating a participatory process. This is for various reasons: [SOURCE: Page_4]

    Methods chosen may impact the results of the study and its quality, validity, and credibility (Patton [SOURCE: Page_4]

1999), [SOURCE: Page_4]

    There are no agreed‐upon evaluation methods (Rosener 1981) as evaluation of a participatory [SOURCE: Page_4]

process is very context specific and therefore methods have to be context‐sensitive (Blackstock et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

2007), [SOURCE: Page_4]

    M&E methods reflect the values and norms within the evaluation practice, they are the direct mirror [SOURCE: Page_5]

of whether the evaluation is ethical (Laitinen 2005). [SOURCE: Page_5]

It is therefore essential for evaluators to make informed choice when selecting and implementing M&E [SOURCE: Page_5]

methods. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Many manuals exist on how to carry out M&E (Fitz‐Gibbon and Lyons Morris 1987; IDRC 1997; The World Bank [SOURCE: Page_5]

2004; UNDP 2009). Many frameworks also exist to guide the M&E of participatory processes in general [SOURCE: Page_5]

(Abelson et al. 2003; Rosener 1981; Rowe and Frewer 2000) and related to NRM specifically (Beierle and [SOURCE: Page_5]

Konisky 2000; Bellamy et al. 2001; Dyer et al. 2014; Webler 1995). This literature is useful in terms of providing [SOURCE: Page_5]

lists of variables to assess the effectiveness of participatory processes and guidelines on the various steps to [SOURCE: Page_5]

follow. However, these manuals and frameworks are not helpful when it comes to choosing between M&E [SOURCE: Page_5]

methods and implementing them (Forss, 2005 Annex 4.A$_1$). As a result, many studies on participatory [SOURCE: Page_5]

processes do not make the M&E methods used transparent (Frewer and Rowe 2005) at the expense of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

transparency, reliability and validity of the study. [SOURCE: Page_5]

The aim of our paper is to identify challenges that evaluators have to tackle when selecting and implementing [SOURCE: Page_5]

methods to monitor and evaluate participatory processes (section two) and to provide tools and strategies to [SOURCE: Page_5]

address these challenges (section four). The M&E of a participatory planning process in the Rwenzori Region, [SOURCE: Page_5]

Uganda, is used as an example to show how these challenges unfold on the ground and how they can be [SOURCE: Page_5]

tackled (section three). [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 2. A quadruple challenge for M&E methods

Four main debates relate to the M&E of participatory processes. They pose a quadruple challenge to the [SOURCE: Page_5]

selection and implementation of M&E methods. These debates are between: 1/ qualitative and quantitative [SOURCE: Page_5]

methods, 2/ process and outcome‐oriented M&E, 3/ theory‐based and participant‐based M&E and 4/ static [SOURCE: Page_5]

and adaptive M&E. By selecting and implementing M&E methods, practitioners, decision‐makers and [SOURCE: Page_5]

researchers take a position among these debates. This position can impact the consideration given to the M&E [SOURCE: Page_5]

results by the different stakeholders. Evaluators need to be aware of these debates and make their position [SOURCE: Page_6]

transparent. [SOURCE: Page_6]

2.1 Qualitative and quantitative M&E methods [SOURCE: Page_6]

When selecting M&E methods, practitioners can choose among a range of possible methods. These methods [SOURCE: Page_6]

are often categorized in two clusters: methods which are more quantitative in nature such as surveys or [SOURCE: Page_6]

questionnaires and methods which are more qualitative in nature such as interviews or participant [SOURCE: Page_6]

observation. A plethora of manuals or books exist which explain in detail how to implement qualitative or [SOURCE: Page_6]

quantitative methods (e.g. Hennink et al. 2010; Mack et al. 2005; Maxim 1999; Taylor 2005). Even though [SOURCE: Page_6]

some authors, like Blackstock et al. (2007) or Forss (2005 p.54) underline that “there is a trend towards [SOURCE: Page_6]

qualitative methods as evaluation tasks become more complex”, other authors suggest that the distinction [SOURCE: Page_6]

between the two “seems of limited relevance, as the qualitative and quantitative nature of data tends to [SOURCE: Page_6]

merge in the course of a practical evaluation”(De Vaus, 2001 in Forss, 2005 p.59). Other authors still, suggest [SOURCE: Page_6]

to build on this distinction and to use both qualitative and quantitative methods. This “methodological [SOURCE: Page_6]

pluralism” is advocated, among others, by authors in mixed‐methods research (e.g. Brannen 1992; Brewer and [SOURCE: Page_6]

Hunter 1989; Creswell 2003; Teddlie and Tashakkori 2009), public participation evaluation (e.g. Bamberger, [SOURCE: Page_6]

1990; Chess, 2000; Cook, 1997) and systems thinking (e.g. Cabrera et al. 2008). Rationale for this [SOURCE: Page_6]

“methodological pluralism” is that multiple methods and triangulation of observation can contribute to [SOURCE: Page_6]

methodological rigor in evaluation (Patton, 1987). It is especially relevant when neither qualitative nor [SOURCE: Page_6]

quantitative methods alone are sufficient to monitor and evaluate the object under consideration (Teddlie and [SOURCE: Page_6]

Tashakkori 2009), as is the case for participatory processes. Based on these considerations, we too, suggest to [SOURCE: Page_6]

use mixed‐methods when evaluating participatory processes. Creswell (2009) highlights that methodological [SOURCE: Page_6]

challenges in using mixed‐methods, particularly in interventions and action research, has only started being [SOURCE: Page_6]

addressed recently. Methodological challenges identified so far include, among others, validity aspects, ethical [SOURCE: Page_6]

issues, prevalence of one type of method over the other and timing of integration (qualitative before [SOURCE: Page_6]

quantitative, vice‐versa or simultaneity) (Creswell 2009; Greene et al. 2001). Our study aims to contribute to [SOURCE: Page_6]

this endeavour. [SOURCE: Page_6]

2.2 Process and outcome‐oriented M&E [SOURCE: Page_7]

A second dichotomy is between monitoring and evaluating the outcomes of a participatory process and the [SOURCE: Page_7]

process itself (Chess and Purcell 1999; Rowe and Frewer 2000). The former focuses on monitoring and [SOURCE: Page_7]

evaluating the results in order to determine whether the participatory means are successful. Results include, [SOURCE: Page_7]

for example, better accepted decisions, consensus or education depending on the targeted objectives of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

participatory process. The latter emphasizes the importance of the means – rather than the results – and looks [SOURCE: Page_7]

at aspects such as fairness, information exchange, group process, and procedures (Chess and Purcell 1999). [SOURCE: Page_7]

While some authors advocate for the assessment of outcomes when monitoring and evaluating a participatory [SOURCE: Page_7]

process (Beierle 1999; Frewer and Rowe 2005), many recognize that the process, along with other external [SOURCE: Page_7]

factors, may have an influence on the outcomes and therefore needs to be considered (Chess and Purcell [SOURCE: Page_7]

1999). Analysts who believe that both process and outcomes should be considered when monitoring and [SOURCE: Page_7]

evaluating a participatory process belong to the “middle ground” (Carnes et al. 1996; IAP$_2$ 2007; Rowe and [SOURCE: Page_7]

Frewer 2000). We adopt this “middle ground” approach. In order to take into account other possible external [SOURCE: Page_7]

factors potentially impacting the process and outcomes, we also include a third cluster on the “context”. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Extreme weather events, like droughts or floods for instance, can impact the process and its outcomes by [SOURCE: Page_7]

modifying participants’ priorities and redefining their objectives. The importance of the “context” is often [SOURCE: Page_7]

emphasized in the literature (e.g. Beierle and Cayford 2002; McAllister 1999; Midgley 2007), but few authors [SOURCE: Page_7]

detail how to assess it (for an example see Blackstock et al., 2007). Our study aims at bridging this gap. [SOURCE: Page_7]

2.3 Theory‐based and participant‐based M&E [SOURCE: Page_7]

Another division which often appears when it comes to monitor and evaluate participatory processes is [SOURCE: Page_7]

between those who define evaluation criteria a priori, based on the literature, and those who consider that [SOURCE: Page_7]

participants should have a say in the M&E design. Both approaches have advantages. Theory‐based evaluation [SOURCE: Page_7]

makes the M&E generally more consistent and eases the comparability of results through more systematic [SOURCE: Page_7]

criteria and methods (Fiorino 1990; Frewer and Rowe 2005; Webler 1995). Participant‐based evaluation, on [SOURCE: Page_7]

the other hand, may allow 1/ for greater trust between the evaluator and the respondent (Conrad et al. 2011), [SOURCE: Page_8]

2/ to build on participants’ knowledge of the context and therefore have methods which are more context‐ [SOURCE: Page_8]

sensitive (Dietz and Stern 2008), 3/ for greater learning since “those who learn most in the process of [SOURCE: Page_8]

evaluation are those who actually do the job – who interview, process surveys, etc.” (Forss, 2005 p.48), and 4/ [SOURCE: Page_8]

to capture the diversity of views about the M&E design, including the methods (Bellamy et al. 2001; Rowe et [SOURCE: Page_8]

al. 2004). A “middle ground” exists in this division as well: certain authors recognize the possibility to combine [SOURCE: Page_8]

both theoretical and participatory elements, for example by “deriving criteria from theory, and subsequently [SOURCE: Page_8]

prioritizing these with the involvement of stakeholders” (Conrad et al. 2011 p.763; see also Chess 2000). If [SOURCE: Page_8]

employed carefully, this approach allows to build on advantages of both approaches. However, we argue that [SOURCE: Page_8]

this might be difficult to implement in practice. Indeed, if participatory M&E in itself can be challenging [SOURCE: Page_8]

(Opondo et al. 2006), deconstructing theoretical assumptions and creatively designing and adapting new [SOURCE: Page_8]

methods to each local situation with stakeholders can be even more difficult (Matthews 2004; Daniell 2012). [SOURCE: Page_8]

Some practical and theoretical insights exist on how to combine both, such as Midgley’s “creative design of [SOURCE: Page_8]

methods” (2000) or Scriven’s “goal‐free evaluation”(1982), but they are few. In the former, Midgley suggests [SOURCE: Page_8]

that different methods may be used to address people's purposes for the intervention. These are determined [SOURCE: Page_8]

based on a series of systemically interrelated questions defined jointly. Goal free evaluation, just as its name [SOURCE: Page_8]

states, involves carrying out an evaluation without predetermined goal. Methods are chosen based on their [SOURCE: Page_8]

ability to uncover the actual effects or outcomes of a process which are then compared with the actual needs [SOURCE: Page_8]

of the participants. Our paper provides a concrete example of how both theoretical and participatory elements [SOURCE: Page_8]

can be combined. In order to account for the multiplicity of viewpoints related to the M&E, our framework [SOURCE: Page_8]

encourages to clarify the “M&E viewpoints”, including what the M&E objectives are, who the evaluators are [SOURCE: Page_8]

and what methods are employed. [SOURCE: Page_8]

2.4 Static and adaptive M&E [SOURCE: Page_8]

Finally, a fourth debate is between an “old‐fashioned” or static model of evaluation prevalent in the 1980’s [SOURCE: Page_8]

and more adaptive, pluralist, collaborative models of evaluation which developed strongly since the 1990’s [SOURCE: Page_8]

(Pollitt 1999). In the static model, M&E is seen as independent and scientific and requires the strict [SOURCE: Page_8]

comparison between the characteristics of an object (in our case a participatory process and its participants) in [SOURCE: Page_9]

a point A in time (ex‐ante) and the same characteristics of the same object in a point B in time (ex‐post). An [SOURCE: Page_9]

example of method to carry out a “static” M&E is Before After Control Impact (BACI) analysis (Smith et al. [SOURCE: Page_9]

1993). This model does not cater for any modification of the variables or the methods used while the M&E is [SOURCE: Page_9]

running. The adaptive evaluation model encompasses various approaches: empowerment evaluation [SOURCE: Page_9]

(Fetterman et al. 1996), fourth generation evaluation (Lincoln and Guba 1989), critical evaluation (Everitt [SOURCE: Page_9]

1996), utilization‐focused evaluation (Patton, 1997), pluralist evaluation (Duran et al. 1995), systemic [SOURCE: Page_9]

evaluation (Boyd et al. 2007), systematic evaluation (Rossi et al. 1999) and democratic evaluation (Floc’hlay [SOURCE: Page_9]

and Plottu 1998). One common element among these approaches and a determinant of the adaptive model is [SOURCE: Page_9]

“an agreement that active participation by major stakeholders is fundamental to good evaluation practice” [SOURCE: Page_9]

(Pollitt 1999 p.79) and that the M&E design and methods can, or should, to a certain extent, be adaptive “on [SOURCE: Page_9]

the way”. The latter element is based on the assumption that surprises may emerge in the course of the [SOURCE: Page_9]

participatory process which the evaluators may not have expected and which might be “more interesting than [SOURCE: Page_9]

ascertaining whether targets were met or analyzing how a project was implemented” (Forss 2005 p.48). [SOURCE: Page_9]

Evaluators must then be equipped with adequate methods to capture or discover the “unknowable”. Following [SOURCE: Page_9]

Chess (2000), we suggest that monitoring and evaluating change requires a rigorous comparison of the same [SOURCE: Page_9]

object at two points in time but that the methods used should at the same time be adaptive to be able to [SOURCE: Page_9]

capture the unexpected. Yet few theoretical or empirical sources explain how to do that. Our study aims at [SOURCE: Page_9]

providing guidelines in that respect. [SOURCE: Page_9]

In summary, we argue that evaluators of participatory processes have to tackle a quadruple challenge when [SOURCE: Page_9]

selecting and implementing M&E methods: [SOURCE: Page_9]

    Using mixed‐methods, both qualitative and quantitative, [SOURCE: Page_9]

    Assessing the participatory process, its outcomes, and its context, [SOURCE: Page_9]

    Taking into account both theory and participants’ views, [SOURCE: Page_9]

    Being rigorous and adaptive. [SOURCE: Page_9]

These four challenges are strongly interconnected. For example, theory‐based M&E is often associated with [SOURCE: Page_9]

static M&E or outcome‐oriented M&E with adaptive M&E. As highlighted by Chess (2000 p.779), “as the field [SOURCE: Page_9]

of social evaluation has matured, many of the dichotomies have become blurred”. While acknowledging this [SOURCE: Page_10]

interconnectedness and blurredness, we argue that practitioners, decision‐makers and researchers need to [SOURCE: Page_10]

### 4. Discussion

In this section, we use the example of the Ugandan case to discuss how the four methodological challenges [SOURCE: Page_22]

highlighted in section two can be tackled. We provide tools and strategies that can be used by evaluators to [SOURCE: Page_22]

ensure that they make utile, feasible, coherent, transparent and adaptive methodological choices when [SOURCE: Page_22]

monitoring and evaluating participatory processes for NRM. [SOURCE: Page_22]

4.1 Qualitative and quantitative M&E methods [SOURCE: Page_22]

Some of the M&E methods used were more quantitative in nature, such as questionnaires, attendance lists [SOURCE: Page_22]

and Likert scales, while others were more qualitative in nature, such as interviews or participant observation. [SOURCE: Page_22]

The multiplicity of methods used allowed triangulation of the data collected as each variable had to be [SOURCE: Page_22]

populated by at least two different methods, as illustrated in Table 1. [SOURCE: Page_22]

Using both qualitative and quantitative methods allowed a wider collection of data over content, time and [SOURCE: Page_22]

space as well as a complementarity in the data collected (McLaughlin et al. 2001; Patton, 1999). For instance, [SOURCE: Page_22]

using questionnaires allowed collecting information from all participants and regularly throughout the process [SOURCE: Page_22]

when interviewing all of them regularly was not possible due to a lack of time or budget. However, these [SOURCE: Page_22]

methods did not allow “surprises”, or elements that were not included in the initial M&E framework, to be [SOURCE: Page_22]

easily captured. They were therefore usefully complemented with interviews and participant observation. [SOURCE: Page_22]

These allowed more in‐depth information ‐ such as participants’ values, concerns and perceptions ‐ and [SOURCE: Page_22]

additional observations ‐ such as the environment in which the interview takes place, the behaviour of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

respondent, his/her hesitations or the presence of other people around ‐ to be collected (Morgan et al. 1992). [SOURCE: Page_22]

### 5. Conclusion

This paper is based on the acknowledgement that participatory processes are increasingly used in [SOURCE: Page_28]

NRM policies and programs and that these processes need to be monitored and evaluated in order to audit [SOURCE: Page_28]

their effectiveness, make more informed decisions in the future or improve our learning based on their [SOURCE: Page_28]

experience. We suggested that the M&E methods’ selection and implementation is particularly strategic and [SOURCE: Page_28]

yet often obscure in published studies. The example of the M&E of a participatory planning process in the [SOURCE: Page_28]

Rwenzori Region, Uganda, was used as an example to illustrate how the four challenges in methods’ selection [SOURCE: Page_28]

and implementation could be tackled. For the qualitative/quantitative challenge, we suggested to use a table [SOURCE: Page_28]

to check the complementarity and coherence among the M&E methods used (e.g. Table 1). For the [SOURCE: Page_28]

process/outcomes challenge, we recommended the adoption of a causal cluster approach (Young 2008) and [SOURCE: Page_28]

the process‐tracing method (George and Bennett 2005) in order to cater for the complexity of the system [SOURCE: Page_28]

under consideration and to identify the causal relationships among the context, process and outcome [SOURCE: Page_29]

variables. For the theory/participant‐based challenge, we identified that clarifying the roles and responsibilities [SOURCE: Page_29]

of the various stakeholders in the six phases of the M&E process was beneficial and that it could be made [SOURCE: Page_29]

transparent through a simple table (e.g. Table 2). For the static/adaptive challenge, we suggested that an initial [SOURCE: Page_29]

list of variables be evaluated before, during and after the participatory process to allow for a “static” ex‐post [SOURCE: Page_29]

comparison, while keeping some of the M&E methods adaptive to required changes “on the way” and open to [SOURCE: Page_29]

“surprises”, that is to variables which may emerge from the ongoing data analysis. We demonstrated that the [SOURCE: Page_29]

implementation of a framework for monitoring and evaluating a participatory process is not linear but is rather [SOURCE: Page_29]

an iterative dynamic among the six M&E phases to adapt to the complexity and uncertainty of the system [SOURCE: Page_29]

under study. Our final thought relates to the time and effort required to monitor and evaluate a participatory [SOURCE: Page_29]

process. Undeniably, a well‐thought M&E requires more time and resources than simply applying a ready‐ [SOURCE: Page_29]

made formula. However, the extent of the M&E, illustrated by the number of variables and methods, should [SOURCE: Page_29]

always be adapted to the M&E objectives and to the resources available, not the other way around. In the [SOURCE: Page_29]

outcomes. But the Rwenzori should by no means be considered as illustrative of the extent of participatory [SOURCE: Page_29]

processes M&Es. It is up to evaluators of each process to find their own balance between comprehensiveness [SOURCE: Page_29]
