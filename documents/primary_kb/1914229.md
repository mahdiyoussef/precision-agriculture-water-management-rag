## ABSTRACT

The FAO-56 dual crop coefficient (FAO-2Kc) model has been extensively used at [SOURCE: Page_2]

the field scale to estimate the crop water requirements by means of the simulated [SOURCE: Page_2]

evapotranspiration (ET) and its two components evaporation (E) and [SOURCE: Page_2]

transpiration (T). Given that the main limitation of FAO-2Kc for operational [SOURCE: Page_2]

irrigation management over large areas is the unavailability (over most irrigated [SOURCE: Page_2]

areas) of irrigation data, this study investigates the feasibility 1) to constrain the [SOURCE: Page_2]

FAO-2Kc ET from LST and VI data, 2) to retrieve irrigation amounts and dates [SOURCE: Page_2]

from LST and VI data and 3) to estimate the root-zone soil moisture (RZSM) at the [SOURCE: Page_2]

daily scale. In practice, the vegetation and soil temperatures retrieved from [SOURCE: Page_2]

LST/VI data are used to estimate the FAO-2Kc vegetation stress coefficient (Ks) [SOURCE: Page_2]

and soil evaporation reduction coefficient (Kr), respectively. The modeling and [SOURCE: Page_2]

remote sensing combined approach is tested over a wheat crop field in central [SOURCE: Page_2]

Morocco, and results are evaluated in terms of ET, irrigation and RZSM estimates. [SOURCE: Page_2]

ET is estimated with a RMSE of 0.68 mm day-1 compared to 0.84 mm day-1 for the [SOURCE: Page_3]

standard (without using LST data) FAO-2Kc based on tabulated values for the [SOURCE: Page_3]

parameters. The total irrigation depth (67 mm) is correctly estimated and is very [SOURCE: Page_3]

close to the actual effective irrigation (69.8 mm) applied by the farmer. Daily [SOURCE: Page_3]

RZSM is estimated with an R$_2$ value of 0.68 (0.42) and a RMSE value of 0.034 [SOURCE: Page_3]

(0.061) m^3 m-3 by forcing FAO-2Kc using the retrieved irrigation (from LST- [SOURCE: Page_3]

derived estimates and precipitation only). Since spaceborne LST data are [SOURCE: Page_3]

currently not available at both high-spatial and high-temporal resolution, a [SOURCE: Page_3]

sensitivity analysis is finally undertaken to assess the potential and applicability [SOURCE: Page_3]

of the proposed methodology to temporally-sparse thermal data. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Keywords: Evapotranspiration, Root-Zone Soil Moisture, Irrigation, FAO-56, [SOURCE: Page_3]

Surface Temperature. [SOURCE: Page_3]

### 1. Introduction

Agriculture is an important pressure on water resources, especially in arid and semi-arid [SOURCE: Page_3]

regions where irrigation can consume more than 80% of the available water (Chehbouni [SOURCE: Page_3]

et al., 2008; Jarlan et al., 2015). Accurate estimation of evapotranspiration (ET), which [SOURCE: Page_3]

critically depends upon the root-zone soil moisture (RZSM), is hence paramount to [SOURCE: Page_3]

determine the crop water requirements and consequently to optimize the on-farm [SOURCE: Page_3]

irrigation management. [SOURCE: Page_3]

The FAO-56 dual crop coefficient (FAO-2Kc, Allen et al., 1998) model has been extensively [SOURCE: Page_3]

used at the field scale to estimate the crop water requirements by means of the simulated [SOURCE: Page_3]

ET. In FAO-2Kc, the total ET is partitioned between the soil evaporation (E) and the plant [SOURCE: Page_3]

transpiration (T) by using a daily water balance for the topsoil layer and the root-zone, [SOURCE: Page_3]

respectively. This model is often chosen for its simplicity and operational basis as it [SOURCE: Page_4]

requires few input data comprised of phenological, standard meteorological and [SOURCE: Page_4]

irrigation data. In addition, FAO-2Kc provides quite acceptable ET estimates when [SOURCE: Page_4]

compared to more physically based -but often over-parameterized models (Allen, 2000; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Er-Raki et al., 2008; Kite and Droogers, 2000). To better constrain the phenological stages [SOURCE: Page_4]

in the FAO model, the basal crop coefficient (Kcb) has been related to satellite based [SOURCE: Page_4]

vegetation index (VI) (Er-Raki et al., 2010, 2007; González-Dugo and Mateos, 2008; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Hunsaker et al., 2005), showing a significant improvement. However, its operational [SOURCE: Page_4]

application to large scales (e.g. irrigation perimeter) still faces two critical issues: 1) the [SOURCE: Page_4]

unavailability (over most irrigated areas) of real- or near-real time irrigation data at the [SOURCE: Page_4]

field scale, and 2) the difficulty in modeling RZSM from meteorological data alone. [SOURCE: Page_4]

In other hand, land surface temperature (LST) derived in the thermal infrared has been [SOURCE: Page_4]

widely used for estimating ET and water stress indices (e.g. Kalma et al., 2008; Li et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

2009). LST has been also assimilated into the FAO method (Er-Raki et al., 2008), and more [SOURCE: Page_4]

recently, used in FAO-2Kc to retrieve the water stress coefficient (Ks) (Dejonge et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

2015; Ihuoma and Madramootoo, 2017; Kullberg et al., 2016). Among the variety of [SOURCE: Page_4]

available approaches, the so-called contextual approach is quite attractive for operational [SOURCE: Page_4]

applications, as it requires few input data. Contextual ET models estimate the ratio of [SOURCE: Page_4]

actual ET to either potential ET (Moran et al., 1994) or available energy by using the [SOURCE: Page_4]

remotely sensed LST – VI (Long and Singh, 2012) and/or LST – albedo space (Merlin, [SOURCE: Page_4]

2013; Roerink et al., 2000). In addition to the demonstrated utility of LST for estimating [SOURCE: Page_4]

ET, its use has been extended to the retrieval of other components of the water budget, [SOURCE: Page_4]

including RZSM (Calvet et al., 1998; Crow et al., 2008). [SOURCE: Page_4]

The relationship between RZSM and LST is explained by the link of the canopy [SOURCE: Page_4]

temperature to the T rate under water-stress conditions, that is when RZSM is not [SOURCE: Page_4]

sufficient to maintain a potential T rate (Boulet et al., 2007; Hain et al., 2009; Moran et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

1994). Several studies have hence derived RZSM through the assimilation of LST or [SOURCE: Page_5]

thermal-based proxy variables into land surface models (Calvet et al., 1998; Crow et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

2008; Hain et al., 2012; Li et al., 2010). Moreover, with Landsat and ASTER thermal data, [SOURCE: Page_5]

the spatial resolution that is potentially achievable for RZSM retrievals is 100 m. Note [SOURCE: Page_5]

however that one key step in the estimation of thermal-based RZSM estimates over [SOURCE: Page_5]

partially vegetated surfaces is the partitioning of the observed LST into soil and canopy [SOURCE: Page_5]

temperatures (Merlin et al., 2014, 2012; Moran et al., 1994). Moran et al. (1994) proposed [SOURCE: Page_5]

the water deficit index (WDI) to estimate a most probable range of crop water stress over [SOURCE: Page_5]

partially vegetated pixels, which is obtained from the aforementioned LST – VI space [SOURCE: Page_5]

(contextual method). This crop water stress index is equivalent to the RZSM normalized [SOURCE: Page_5]

by the soil moisture at field capacity and by the soil moisture at wilting point [SOURCE: Page_5]

(Bastiaanssen et al., 2000). In the FAO formalism, the same thresholds are set for Ks equal [SOURCE: Page_5]

to 1 (soil moisture at field capacity) and for a Ks equal to 0 (soil moisture at wilting point). [SOURCE: Page_5]

In order to take advantage of: i) the simplicity and robustness of the thermal-based [SOURCE: Page_5]

contextual ET models, ii) the utility of LST/VI data for water budget components (E/T, [SOURCE: Page_5]

RZSM) and iii) the availability of LST/VI data at a spatial resolution suitable for [SOURCE: Page_5]

monitoring crops; this study proposes an original approach to better constrain the water [SOURCE: Page_5]

budget components of FAO-2Kc from LST and VI data. In practice, the approach seeks to [SOURCE: Page_5]

retrieve the irrigation volumes and dates from first-guess (LST-derived) ET and RZSM, [SOURCE: Page_5]

and to re-analyze all water-budget components (including ET and RZSM) from the [SOURCE: Page_5]

retrieved irrigation data. In this study, the new methodology is tested by using ground- [SOURCE: Page_5]

based observations of LST/VI, evaluated against ET, RZSM and irrigation observations. A [SOURCE: Page_5]

sensitivity analysis is carried out in order to assess the applicability of the approach to [SOURCE: Page_5]

remote sensing data. [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 3.1. Overview of FAO-56 dual crop coefficient method

The FAO-2Kc is a water balance model driven by 1) meteorological forcing variables to [SOURCE: Page_9]

calculate reference evapotranspiration ET0 and 2) precipitation and irrigation that jointly [SOURCE: Page_9]

determine the water supply to simulate the soil water availability for soil evaporation and [SOURCE: Page_10]

plant transpiration. In practice, FAO-2Kc estimates ET by multiplying ET$_0$ by a two [SOURCE: Page_10]

separate crop coefficients: [SOURCE: Page_10]

𝐸𝑇 = (𝐾𝑠𝐾𝑐𝑏 + 𝐾𝑒)𝐸𝑇0                                                              (4) [SOURCE: Page_10]

where Kcb is the basal crop transpiration, Ks the stress coefficient (0-1) that represents [SOURCE: Page_10]

the vegetation water status and a reduction factor of T (Kcb ET$_0$) and Ke the evaporation [SOURCE: Page_10]

coefficient. ET$_0$ is calculated according to the FAO Penman–Monteith equation (Allen et [SOURCE: Page_10]

al., 1998) at daily scale. The values used for Kcb (Kcbini, Kcbmid and Kcbend) at the three [SOURCE: Page_10]

crop growth stages (initial, mid-season and maturity respectively) were taken from Allen [SOURCE: Page_10]

et al. (1998). Ks (unitless) is calculated based on daily computation of the water balance [SOURCE: Page_10]

for the root-zone layer Zr (m) as follows: [SOURCE: Page_10]

where Dr (mm) is root zone depletion, TAW (mm) is total available soil water in the root [SOURCE: Page_10]

balance. TAW is estimated as the difference between the water content at field capacity [SOURCE: Page_10]

### 3.2. LST-integrated FAO-2Kc: new approach in the calculation of water budget components

Given that the main limitation of FAO-2Kc for operational irrigation management over [SOURCE: Page_11]

large areas is the unavailability (over most irrigated areas) of irrigation data at the field [SOURCE: Page_11]

scale, a new approach (named LST-integrated FAO-2Kc) is proposed to derive the water [SOURCE: Page_11]

budget components from LST and VI data. An overview of the methodology is represented [SOURCE: Page_11]

in Fig. 2 and is explained below. [SOURCE: Page_11]

Basically, LST is integrated in the standard FAO-2Kc at two levels: the ET and SM modeling [SOURCE: Page_11]

components. LST is first partitioned into its soil and vegetation components to force E and [SOURCE: Page_11]

T separately via thermal-derived estimates of Ks and Kr, respectively (ET modeling [SOURCE: Page_11]

component). Note that the thermal-derived Ks is also used to derive a first-guess (LST- [SOURCE: Page_11]

derived) RZSM estimate, based on the FAO-2Kc relationship between TAW and Ks (SM [SOURCE: Page_11]

modeling component). The dynamic of first-guess RZSM is then analyzed to retrieve the [SOURCE: Page_11]

irrigation amounts and dates. The FAO-2Kc is next forced by the previously retrieved [SOURCE: Page_11]

irrigation and re-analyzed estimates of RZSM (RZSMFAO+LST) and ET (ETFAO+LST) are finally [SOURCE: Page_11]

provided. The different components of LST-integrated FAO-2Kc (namely LST partitioning, [SOURCE: Page_11]

thermal-derived Ks and Kr, first-guess ET and RZSM, irrigation retrieval, and re-analyzed [SOURCE: Page_11]

ET and RZSM) are described in the following sections. [SOURCE: Page_11]

3.2.1. Partitioning LST [SOURCE: Page_11]

The method used for partitioning LST into vegetation and soil components relies on the [SOURCE: Page_12]

combination between the hourglass approach (Moran et al., 1994) and the procedure to [SOURCE: Page_12]

obtain the Temperature Vegetation Dryness Index (Sandholt et al., 2002). These two [SOURCE: Page_12]

methods are based on the polygon defined in the LST – VI space. Tsmax is the temperature [SOURCE: Page_12]

of a fully dry bare soil. Tsmin is the temperature of a fully wet bare soil. Tvmax is the [SOURCE: Page_12]

maximum vegetation temperature corresponding to fully stressed (non-transpiring) [SOURCE: Page_12]

vegetation. Tvmin is the minimum vegetation temperature corresponding to well-watered [SOURCE: Page_12]

unstressed vegetation (transpiring at potential rate). Since this study tests the feasibility [SOURCE: Page_12]

of the proposed methodology from in situ measurements, the image-based polygon [SOURCE: Page_12]

cannot be plotted to constrain the temperature endmembers (Tsmax, Tsmin, Tvmax, Tvmin). [SOURCE: Page_12]

Therefore, these temperatures are simulated by using the energy balance model proposed [SOURCE: Page_12]

by Stefan et al. (2015). Tsmin and Tsmax are simulated by a soil energy balance model, while [SOURCE: Page_12]

Tvmin is set to the air temperature and Tvmax is defined according to the assumptions that [SOURCE: Page_12]

the difference between Tsmax and Tsmin is the same that between Tvmax and Tvmin (Stefan [SOURCE: Page_12]

### 3.2.5. Irrigation retrieval

Irrigation events are detected based on a significant increase in first-guess (LST-derived) [SOURCE: Page_15]

RZSM, which cannot be attributed to precipitation. Only significant increases are [SOURCE: Page_15]

represents a water supply greater than 10 mm for a 0.5 m root-zone depth. Note that such [SOURCE: Page_15]

the periods with steady increase in RZSM, the amount of retrieved/inverted irrigation [SOURCE: Page_15]

probable dates for the retrieved irrigation events. If an irrigation is effectively detected [SOURCE: Page_16]

for this period (with a minimum threshold of 10 mm), then the estimated date of irrigation [SOURCE: Page_16]

is set as the last date of the period, in order to agree the maximum LST-based RZSM and [SOURCE: Page_16]

the maximum RZSM simulated from FAO-56. [SOURCE: Page_16]

### 5.2. Applicability to temporally sparse thermal data

As mentioned in Section 2, this study was undertaken by using ground-based radiometric [SOURCE: Page_24]

LST. Therefore, the uncertainty and temporal sampling of remotely sensed LST are not [SOURCE: Page_24]

taken into account. Regarding the uncertainty, many studies have demonstrated that [SOURCE: Page_24]

contextual models, such as the LST/VI-based method used herein to partition LST, allow [SOURCE: Page_24]

us to avoid accurate estimates of surface variables, since the extreme water conditions [SOURCE: Page_24]

(stressed – well-watered) used as boundaries to estimate thermal-based evaporative [SOURCE: Page_24]

indices are estimated from the variability captured within thermal imagery (Kalma et al., [SOURCE: Page_24]

2008; Li et al., 2009). With regard to temporal sampling, this issue becomes a key [SOURCE: Page_24]

limitation of spaceborne thermal sensors due to the restriction of surface retrievals to [SOURCE: Page_24]

sufficiently cloud-free days (Crow et al., 2008). In addition to the thermal data currently [SOURCE: Page_24]

available at high spatial (100 m) resolution have a repeat cycle of 16 days only, and up to [SOURCE: Page_24]

8 days by combining Landsat-7 and -8. To assess the impact of the observation frequency [SOURCE: Page_24]

on the proposed approach, a sensitivity analysis is carried out by decreasing the LST [SOURCE: Page_24]

observation frequency. It should be noted that the smoothing of KsLST (Eq. (9)) to reduce [SOURCE: Page_24]

the day-to-day variability is only applied for a daily revisit of LST observations. For a [SOURCE: Page_24]

frequency between 2 and 16 days the KsLST from Eq. (8) is directly used without [SOURCE: Page_24]

smoothing to LST-derived estimates. The assessment is undertaken in terms of RZSM, ET [SOURCE: Page_24]

and total irrigation water supply simulated by FAO-2Kc. [SOURCE: Page_24]

Increasing the duration between LST observations, naturally leads to a decreasing the [SOURCE: Page_24]

number of thermal-derived ET and RZSM retrievals (from Eq. (4) and (11), respectively) [SOURCE: Page_24]

available to constrain the irrigation from FAO-2Kc. However, given that irrigation can be [SOURCE: Page_24]

estimated, it allows us to run FAO-2Kc for estimating RZSM and ET every day along the [SOURCE: Page_24]

season. Fig. 10 shows the impact of the observation frequency every 2, 4, 8 and 16 days [SOURCE: Page_24]

on estimating RZSM. One can observe the decreasing number of LST-derived RZSM [SOURCE: Page_24]

estimates (RZSMLST), its errors and the significant improvement after running FAO-2Kc [SOURCE: Page_25]

model by using the retrieved irrigation. Such approach allows estimating the RZSM for all [SOURCE: Page_25]

days during the growing season (RZSMFAO+LST) irrespective of the observation frequency [SOURCE: Page_25]

used. [SOURCE: Page_25]

Fig. 11 shows the impact on RZSM and ET estimates of the availability of LST observations [SOURCE: Page_25]

according to the time revisit frequency ranging from 1 to 16 days. Although even the [SOURCE: Page_25]

errors are gradually increasing, the results demonstrate a relatively good performance [SOURCE: Page_25]

and acceptable errors by increasing the revisit period. Fig. 12 shows the impact of the [SOURCE: Page_25]

availability of LST observations on the retrieved total irrigation water amount and [SOURCE: Page_25]

number of irrigation events. Acceptable errors in the total water supply are observed. The [SOURCE: Page_25]

number of simulated irrigation events decreases as the time revisit frequency decreases, [SOURCE: Page_25]

falling below 3 events with a revisit longer than 8 days. Overall, it might be noted that up [SOURCE: Page_25]

to a 10-day revisit of LST observations, a good agreement is obtained with R$_2$ higher than [SOURCE: Page_25]

0.5 and 0.6 for RZSM and ET respectively, and a mean absolute error (MAE) of total [SOURCE: Page_25]

irrigation water supply lower than 15 mm (corresponding to a relative MAE of 21%). [SOURCE: Page_25]

According to these results, it could be considered the use of LST products with time revisit [SOURCE: Page_25]

of 8 days such as i) the combination of Landsat-7 and -8 LST on cloud-free days and/or ii) [SOURCE: Page_25]

the 1 km resolution MODIS LST product downscaled to 100 m resolution by using the [SOURCE: Page_25]

Landsat LST (e.g. Anderson et al., 2012; Cammalleri et al., 2014; Olivera-Guerra et al., [SOURCE: Page_25]

2017; Weng et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_25]

The results show clearly the applicability to remote sensing data and the utility to the [SOURCE: Page_25]

irrigation scheduling at regional scale. Given that KsLST and irrigation volumes and dates [SOURCE: Page_25]

can be fully obtained from remotely sensed LST/VI data, this methodology could be [SOURCE: Page_25]

implemented in an irrigation index to characterize the irrigation distribution, such as the [SOURCE: Page_25]

irrigation index priority proposed by Belaqziz et al. (2013). This index takes into account [SOURCE: Page_25]

the Ks and the irrigation volumes and dates and by using remote sensed-derived Ks and [SOURCE: Page_26]

irrigation would allow evaluate the irrigation scheduling over broad irrigated agricultural [SOURCE: Page_26]

areas poorly monitored. [SOURCE: Page_26]

### 6. Conclusions

A new approach in the calculation of water budget components and for irrigation [SOURCE: Page_26]

scheduling (when and how much to irrigate) is developed by integrating LST data into the [SOURCE: Page_26]

FAO-2Kc model. It relies on: 1) the estimation of first-guess (LST-derived) RZSM from [SOURCE: Page_26]

KsLST (KsLST < 1) during stressed periods and its correction for both cumulated [SOURCE: Page_26]

precipitation and cumulated ET during unstressed periods (KsLST = 1); 2) the estimation [SOURCE: Page_26]

of irrigation amounts and dates along the season from (first-guess) LST-derived RZSM [SOURCE: Page_26]

and ET estimates; and 3) the use of retrieved irrigations to force FAO-2Kc to simulate [SOURCE: Page_26]

RZSM and ET on a daily basis. Statistical results indicate that first-guess (LST-derived) ET [SOURCE: Page_26]

(ETLST) is more accurate than the ET simulated by the standard version of FAO-2Kc while [SOURCE: Page_26]

the first-guess RZSM is significantly improved when FAO-2Kc is implemented by using [SOURCE: Page_26]

retrieved irrigation. Results show that the new methodology combining FAO-2Kc and [SOURCE: Page_26]

LST/VI data is able to 1) accurately estimate the crop ET using the default (non- [SOURCE: Page_26]

calibrated) parameters given by Allen et al. (1998), 2) to estimate the irrigation amounts [SOURCE: Page_26]

and dates and 3) to accurately simulate RZSM. [SOURCE: Page_26]

The impact of temporal sampling in LST observation is assessed by carrying out by [SOURCE: Page_26]

decreasing the LST observation frequency from 1 to 16 days. It is demonstrated that the [SOURCE: Page_26]

irrigation amounts and dates can be estimated, allowing us to run FAO-2Kc for estimating [SOURCE: Page_26]

RZSM and ET along the season on a daily basis. Although errors are gradually increasing [SOURCE: Page_26]

with the observation period, results demonstrate a relatively good performance and [SOURCE: Page_26]

acceptable errors for an observation frequency of 1 per 8 days so it is recommended to [SOURCE: Page_26]

use LST observations at a temporal resolution finer than 10 days. In order to take [SOURCE: Page_27]

advantage of the high temporal resolution of MODIS LST and the high spatial resolution [SOURCE: Page_27]

of Landsat LST, downscaling method could be included in the future for monitoring the [SOURCE: Page_27]

RZSM at the field and daily scale. However, further research will be required to assess the [SOURCE: Page_27]

impact of downscaling uncertainties in the proposed methodology. [SOURCE: Page_27]
