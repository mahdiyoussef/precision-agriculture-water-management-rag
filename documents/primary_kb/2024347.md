29   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   The mobility patterns of two native species, the barbel, Barbus barbus (L.) and the chub [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   Squalius cephalus (L.) and of one non-native fish species, the catfish Silurus glanis (L.) were [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   assessed on a 35.5-km reach of the Upper Rhône River, a strong flowing river with notable [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   thermal regime alterations. An active acoustic tracking technique adapted to large rivers allowed [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   (1) the identification of longitudinal home ranges, movements and preferred habitat at large [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   scale; and (2) the analysis of the influence of discharge and water temperature on the movement [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   patterns of the fish. The active fish-tracking system recorded 1572 fish localisations over seven [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   months on a weekly basis for 80% of the tagged fish (37 barbel, 23 chub and 13 catfish). [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   Compared with the catfish, barbel and chub showed wider longitudinal home ranges, more [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   movements > 1 km and higher inter-individual variability. The catfish preferred artificially [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   heated habitats with less morphological diversity. The three species were more often localised in [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   river sections with high density of woody debris. The results suggest that habitat degradation is [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   more damaging for cyprinids, while the catfish seemed less, if not unimpacted, in large modified [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   rivers. [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

48 [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   Many rivers of the northern hemisphere are heavily affected by human activity causing major [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   changes to the rivers’ continuity, morphology, flow and thermal regimes. Habitat fragmentation [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   and artificial flow variability are known to modify availability and access to functional habitats [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   for fish (Capra et al., 2017; Fullerton et al., 2010; Nilsson, Reidy, Dynesius & Revenga, 2005). [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   Impassable obstacles limit the possible movements in both upstream and downstream directions [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   (Crook et al., 2015; Jansson, Nilsson & Renöfält, 2000) and artificial high flow variability (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   hydropeaking) forces fish to select not the most suitable but the “least-constraining” habitats [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   (Capra et al., 2017). The mobility, spatial distribution and temporal variations of fish are major [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   elements of fish biology that influence their population dynamics and productivity (Benitez, [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   Nzau Matondo, Dierckx & Ovidio, 2015; Fredrich, Ohmann, Curio & Kirschbaum, 2003; [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   Gardner, Deeming & Eady, 2015; Lucas & Baras, 2001; Ovidio, 1999; Radinger & Wolter, [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   2014). The range, frequency and intensity of movements of most holobiotic species remain [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   poorly known or unidentified in large rivers (Booth, Hairston & Flecker, 2013), even though a [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   wide variety of movement behaviours exist in fish at different life stages (adults and juveniles) in [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   different seasons (Benitez et al., 2015; Lucas & Baras, 2001). These studies have shown that [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   flow and temperature variability are significant factors of fish movement behaviour, but this has [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   never been studied in a large hydropeaking river. Although carrying out such behavioural studies [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   in large rivers remains a technical challenge, they are fundamental to understanding the [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   responses of holobiotic fish species to anthropogenic pressures (Benitez et al., 2015; Gardner et [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   al., 2015). In highly regulated and channelized large European rivers, the endemic (i.e. native) [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   rheophilic cyprinid species (e.g. barbel, Barbus barbus (L.) and chub, Squalius cephalus (L.)) are [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   relevant indicators of the ecological status of the rivers (e.g. Maire, Buisson, Biau, Canal & [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   Laffaille, 2013 (for conservation regulation); Morina et al., 2016 (for sediment contamination); [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   Oberdorff, Pont, Hugueny & Chessel, 2001 (for fish-based index)). Indeed, their high sensitivity [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   to fragmentation, flow change and thermal regime variations is due to specific ecological [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   requirements for the reproduction substrate in certain spawning sites or water quality and leads to [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   dramatic declines of their populations (De Leeuw & Winter, 2008; Ovidio & Philippart, 2008; [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   Poulet, Beaulaton & Dembski, 2011). Barbel and chub could also be considered as relevant [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   indicators to study the impact of river fragmentation (Benitez & Ovidio, 2018). It appears on the [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   other hand, that the non-native species, catfish (Silurus glanis (L.)), successfully adapts and [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   seems to take advantage of the general deterioration of river ecosystems (Britton, Cucherousset, [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   Davies, Godard & Copp, 2010; Castaldelli et al., 2013; Guillerault et al., 2015; Poulet et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   2011). Despite these observations, knowledge on native rheophilic species and non-native catfish [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   movement behaviour in large rivers is lacking. The behavioural ecology and particularly [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   seasonal movement patterns of barbel are well documented but exclusively in fourth order rivers [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   or lower (Baras, 1992; Baras, 1995; Le Pichon, Tales, Gorges, Baudry & Boët, 2016; Ovidio & [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   Philippart, 2008). Benitez and Ovidio (2018) showed that within the same river basin, barbel [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   demonstrate flexibility in movement periodicity and optimise the start date of migration towards [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   spawning grounds in accordance with their local environment and individual experiences. This [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   suggests that movement behaviour of a single species may differ depending on the size of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   river. These studies have widely demonstrated frequent seasonal movements between functional [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   habitats and high habitat stringency (substrate, water quality). The documentation of chub [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   mobility in large rivers (i.e. order ≥ 5) is limited to one study with a short tracking duration (5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   to^17 days) within a 4-km long study site that showed significant occupation of backwaters and [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   cover such as woody debris and boulder clusters (Allouche, Thévenet & Gaudin, 1999). [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   Cucherousset et al. (2017) highlighted a knowledge gap on catfish adaptation to river [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   modification and on their mobility and dispersal patterns and Brevé et al. (2014) showed explicit [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   site fidelity and limited movements for adults in a large river (Meuse River, Netherlands). Capra [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   et al. (2017) showed contrasted habitat selection behaviours among barbel, chub and catfish, on a [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   local scale (i.e. microhabitat) and in a hydropeaking flow regime. However, Capra et al. (2017) [SOURCE: Page_5]

107          Telemetry studies in a broad variety of aquatic environments can now be performed [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   thanks to the technological progress achieved over the last two decades, including the use of [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   radio and acoustic bio-telemetry with manual or automated localisation. Large river ecosystems [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   are somewhat restrictive in terms of the logistical deployment of bio-telemetry techniques mainly [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   because of their size and harsh hydraulic conditions, preventing, for example, the possibility of [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   tracking a large number of individuals in a limited period of time. Therefore, telemetry research [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   in large rivers has been essentially based on passive listening methods to analyse fish trajectories [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   and movement speeds between passive listening stations located several kilometres away from [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   each other (Béguer-Pon et al., 2015; Burke & Jepson, 2006; De Leeuw & Winter, 2008; Gardner, [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   Deeming & Eady, 2013; Verbiest, Breukelaar, Ovidio, Philippart & Belpaire, 2012; Brevé et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   2014; Wang, Wei, Kynard & Zhang, 2012). Notable exceptions are Daugherty and Sutton [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   (2005), Wang et al. (2012) and Alexandre et al. (2016), who tracked fish over long distances (20 [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   to 38 km) during 9 to 14 months on a weekly basis using boats. [SOURCE: Page_6]

120          This study assessed the movement patterns of two native rheophilic cyprinid species [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   (barbel and chub) and the non-native catfish in the Rhône River (France) over a seven-month [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   period from spring to autumn. The Rhône River in the Bugey area is characterised by huge flow [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   disruptions due to peak flow management measures and water temperature locally warmed by [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   the cooling system of a nuclear power plant. To overcome the limitations of the active tracking [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   of fish in large rivers (Herrala, Kroboth, Kuntz, & Schramm, 2014), an acoustic tracking [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   protocol adapted from the homing protocol presented in Eiler (2012) using scanning acoustic [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   native rheophilic cyprinids, barbel and the chub, (2) if their movements are influenced differently [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   by discharge and water temperature and (3) if the catfish take advantage of the river degradation [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   unlike the native rheophilic cyprinids. [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   Study site [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   The study reach is a 35.5-km undiverted section of the French Upper Rhône River located north- [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   Compagnie Nationale du Rhône - CNR) and the Jons-Cusset hydroelectric facility (located [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   downstream; Électricité De France - EDF). The upstream limit (river kilometer Km 0; [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   45°51'19.74"N; 5°24'23.40"E) of the study reach is distinguished by an artificial riprap weir [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   (which cannot be crossed by boat). The downstream limit (Km 35.5; 45°46'4.23"N; [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   4°54'49.90"E) is identified by the dam of Jons. Sault-Brénaz’s and Jons-Cusset’s hydroelectric [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   facilities cannot be crossed by fish in an upstream direction but downstream passages remains [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   possible through the turbines or spillways. A nuclear power plant located on the right bank of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   water (between 7 and 10 °C warmer than the upstream water). The warm water discharge creates [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   a strong transversal temperature difference between the left bank and the right bank (Ginot, [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   Souchon & Roger, 1996; Capra et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   Capture and tagging [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   The fish capturing and tagging took place between 15 and 19 March 2010. Close to 20% of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   study reach was sampled per day during the capture and tagging period in the upstream direction. [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   The fish were captured by boat using electrofishing or net fishing techniques (gillnets were left [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   for a maximum of two hours) and the capture location of each individual was recorded. The fish [SOURCE: Page_8]

164   were placed in containers, which were set-up on the riverside and supplied with water using [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   pumps immersed in the Rhône River, for a minimum duration of one hour before and after being [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   tagged. The fish were then selected for tagging according to their weight (minimum = 600 g). [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   Acoustic transmitters were implanted in the fish's intraperitoneal cavity, in accordance with the [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   technique described by Ovidio and Philippart (2008). The fish were anaesthetised with a 0.08 [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   mL/L AQUIS-S® (Aquis-S, NZ Ltd.) solution before the tagging and an antibacterial medication [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   (Marbocyl 2%) was injected in the dorsal muscles to prevent post-operative infections. One hour [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   after the surgery, the fish’s recovery was verified (correct resting position and respiration, no [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   bleeding) before release at their capture site to avoid post-tagging artificial movements (Gardner, [SOURCE: Page_8]

173   Deeming, Wellby, Soulsbury & Eady, 2015). [SOURCE: Page_8]

365   Discussion [SOURCE: Page_16]

366 [SOURCE: Page_16]

367   By tracking 91 adult fish over seven months, the movement metrics and habitat use of two native [SOURCE: Page_16]

368   rheophilic species (barbel and chub) and a non-native species (catfish) were quantified in the [SOURCE: Page_16]

369   Upper Rhône River. The results showed the movements of cyprinids and catfish recorded [SOURCE: Page_16]

370   simultaneously in a large fast-flowing river (the Rhône River). Unlike the catfish, barbel and [SOURCE: Page_16]

371   chub demonstrated (1) wider home ranges (HR), (2) a higher number of net distances travelled > [SOURCE: Page_16]

372   1 km (Dn^1k), (3) a higher inter-individual variability and (4) a higher rate of roaming [SOURCE: Page_16]

373   individuals. Catfish were more often localised in artificially heated habitats (downstream from [SOURCE: Page_16]

374   the nuclear power plant), intensively exploited their habitat and have no preference for available [SOURCE: Page_16]

375   morphological habitat types. [SOURCE: Page_16]

376       The portable active-scanning acoustic system proved a relevant technique to locate fish in a [SOURCE: Page_16]

377   large-size and fast flowing river. It could be used to investigate the complexity and the variability [SOURCE: Page_16]

378   of fish individual movements more accurately, as well as habitat and home range exploitation at [SOURCE: Page_16]

379   high temporal resolution. Furthermore, it allowed high detection rates of the tagged individuals [SOURCE: Page_16]

380   (on average 74% per tracking campaign) without data collisions of dozens of tagged fish [SOURCE: Page_16]

Author-produced version of the article published in Fisheries Management and Ecology, 2018; 25(2) : 136-149 The original publication is available at https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/fme.12272 doi : 10.1111/fme.12272 16 [SOURCE: Page_17]

381   simultaneously and without increasing the search duration. Such a high-performance of a [SOURCE: Page_17]

382   telemetry system has not been achieved with more traditional active telemetry or fixed detection [SOURCE: Page_17]

383   stations at the scale of a large river and thus could contribute to filling gaps in knowledge on the [SOURCE: Page_17]

384   behavioural ecology of fish in such environments. However, determining why 20 % (n=18) of [SOURCE: Page_17]

385   the 91 examined tagged fish were never or poorly detected or disappeared early from the study [SOURCE: Page_17]

386   reach remained problematic. Authors often suggested reasons such as mortality, predation or [SOURCE: Page_17]

387   movements outside of the study area, but their results generally showed higher rates (29-43 %) [SOURCE: Page_17]

388   lost or poorly detected individuals (Béguer-Pon et al., 2015; De Leeuw & Winter, 2008; Verbiest [SOURCE: Page_17]

389   et al., 2012). The mobile active scanning used in this study allowed a high number of contacts [SOURCE: Page_17]

390   with fish in comparison to fixed listening stations and consequently increased the precision of the [SOURCE: Page_17]

391   movement metrics quantified (i.e. HR, Dn, Dn^1k, IE; Figure 4). [SOURCE: Page_17]

392       The evaluation of HR depended mainly on the number of individual localisations recorded, [SOURCE: Page_17]

393   the duration of the study and the telemetry equipment (Alexandre et al., 2016). As in most [SOURCE: Page_17]

394   telemetry studies, seasonal HR and the estimated Dn only partially reflected reality, as fish [SOURCE: Page_17]

395   inevitably travelled outside the tracking periods during other moments of the daily cycle [SOURCE: Page_17]

396   (evening and night-time for example; Horký, Slavík, Bartoš, Kolářová, & Randák, 2007). [SOURCE: Page_17]

398   interval for the three species over seven months was an appropriate way to compare relative [SOURCE: Page_17]

399   mobility patterns among species on a seasonal scale, with acceptable losses of accuracy [SOURCE: Page_17]

400   (Alexandre et al., 2016 [monthly basis; in a stream]; Baras, 1998 [test for successive locations [SOURCE: Page_17]

401   from 1 to 28 days; best cost-effectiveness for barbel = once a week; in a stream]; Hann & [SOURCE: Page_17]

402   Schramm, 2018 [once a week for five months; in a river]; Herrala et al., 2014 [once a month for [SOURCE: Page_17]

404   stream]). HR for barbel and chub (median > 2300 m; Table 1 and Figure 4) were larger than [SOURCE: Page_17]

405   catfish (median < 1300 m; Table 1 and Figure 4), with considerable individual differences. In [SOURCE: Page_17]

406   smaller rivers (median flow < 30 m3/s), the HR of barbel exceeded 10 km on a regular basis and [SOURCE: Page_17]
