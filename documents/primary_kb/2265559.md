## 3                                         ABSTRACT

4    Physiological pea (Pisum sativum L.) seed quality depends on various factors affecting [SOURCE: Page_2]

5   the plant, including water supply, the true effect of which has not been characterized. [SOURCE: Page_2]

6   The effects of different periods of water stress during reproductive stages on the seed [SOURCE: Page_2]

7   yield and seed physiological quality were investigated in field studies over three years [SOURCE: Page_2]

8   (1991, 1992 and 1993) in Brain-sur-l’Authion, France, on a Mollisol, Rendoll, [SOURCE: Page_2]

9   eutrochreptic soil. Irrigation during seed filling (IDSF) and irrigation during the period [SOURCE: Page_2]

10   from the start of flowering to the start of seed filling (IBSF) were compared to a non- [SOURCE: Page_2]

11   irrigated (NI) and a well-irrigated (WI) treatments. The physiological quality of pea [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   seeds was assessed by the germination percentage, conductivity test, and cold test. The [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   germination percentage averaged 98% and was not modified by water stress. In 1993, [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   rainfall during the reproductive stages was high, and there were no differences in seed [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   yield or quality between treatments. In 1991 and 1992 the rainfall was much lower, and [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   the NI treatment showed the lowest seed yield and quality as far as the conductivity test [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   and cold test were concerned. The WI treatment showed the best yields. Water stress [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   during the flowering period (IDSF) did not reduce seed quality compared to WI when it [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   was followed by an optimal water supply during seed filling, and reduced seed yield [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   slightly. Water stress during seed filling (IBSF) decreased seed yield but the effect on [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   seed quality was not significant. Mean seed weight was higher and less variable in IDSF [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   treatments than in WI treatments. Changing irrigation strategies for pea seed production [SOURCE: Page_2]

1   towards irrigation during seed filling may improve the physiological quality of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

2   seedlots without decreasing the seed yield. [SOURCE: Page_3]

1    Seed germination quality in pea depends on the pathological, physical and physiological state [SOURCE: Page_4]

2   of the seed (Powell et al., 1984). While the pathological state (Powell et al., 1984) and physical [SOURCE: Page_4]

3   properties (Deneufbourg, 1992) of the seed are increasingly well known, little is known about [SOURCE: Page_4]

4   the factors that are responsible for differences in physiological quality between seed lots at [SOURCE: Page_4]

5   harvest. At present, the physiological quality of the seed is not really taken into account by pea [SOURCE: Page_4]

6   seed growers. Moreover, the water supply to pea crops for seed production varies greatly in [SOURCE: Page_4]

7   France according to the cropping area and how irrigation is managed. But the consequences of [SOURCE: Page_4]

8   water supply on the physiological quality of the seed are not known. [SOURCE: Page_4]

9    The effect of the water supply of the plant on seed quality has been investigated in some [SOURCE: Page_4]

10   large-seeded legumes, especially in soybean. In field experiments, Smiciklas et al. (1989) [SOURCE: Page_4]

11   observed that water stress during the period of seed filling in soybean induced a reduction in [SOURCE: Page_4]

12   seed quality as assessed by germination and conductivity results, compared with those where [SOURCE: Page_4]

13   there was no or earlier water stress. In contrast, Vieira et al. (1992) working on greenhouse and [SOURCE: Page_4]

14   field experiments on soybean observed no or little effect of drought stress on seed quality. [SOURCE: Page_4]

15   Dornbos and Mullen (1991) observed a negative effect of drought stress which was enhanced [SOURCE: Page_4]

16   by high air temperatures. Heatherly (1993), in a three-year field study, observed that drought [SOURCE: Page_4]

17   stress resulted in reduced germination of harvested soybean, but the daytime temperature [SOURCE: Page_4]

18   during the seed filling period exceeded 30 °C for all experiments. [SOURCE: Page_4]

19    In pea, literature about the effect of drought stress is less abundant. Nichols et al. (1978), [SOURCE: Page_4]

20   working with potted plants, observed no effect of drought stress on seed conductivity or [SOURCE: Page_4]

21   germination. However, in a two-year field experiment, Raymond et al. (1988) found reduced [SOURCE: Page_4]

22   pea seed quality when water was withheld during seed filling. They concluded applying the [SOURCE: Page_4]

23   final irrigation at 500 degree-days after blooming (about two weeks) was necessary to produce [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

1   viable seed yields similar to those obtained with continuous irrigation through early [SOURCE: Page_5]

2   senescence. [SOURCE: Page_5]

3    The effect of the maturity level of the seed at harvest has been reported in soybean (TeKrony [SOURCE: Page_5]

4   et al., 1980) and in peas (Bedford and Matthews, 1976; Rachidian and Le Deunff, 1986) to be [SOURCE: Page_5]

5   an important factor affecting seed quality. For pea, a harvest moisture content of 40 % appears [SOURCE: Page_5]

6   to be the optimum maturity stage for the best expression of the physiological quality of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

7   seed (Fougereux et al., 1995). Periods of drought stress, climatic conditions, and maturity [SOURCE: Page_5]

8   levels are not always specified in the reported experiments. This could explain why results do [SOURCE: Page_5]

9   not all agree. Although a tendency for a deleterious effect of drought stress during the seed [SOURCE: Page_5]

10   filling period on seed quality is becoming apparent, further experiments are needed for more [SOURCE: Page_5]

11   reliable conclusions about the effect of drought stress at different reproductive stages of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

12   crop. The objective of this work was to study the effects of plant water stress at different stages [SOURCE: Page_5]

13   on pea seed quality, distinct from the pathological and mechanical aspects of seed quality. [SOURCE: Page_5]

## 1                                 MATERIALS AND METHODS

2                                         Crop procedures [SOURCE: Page_6]

3    Field studies were conducted in 1991, 1992 and 1993 at Brain sur l’Authion (Anjou, France), [SOURCE: Page_6]

4   on a clay limestone soil (Mollisol, Rendoll, eutrochreptic soil using the US [SOURCE: Page_6]

5   classification). ‘Solara’, a semi-leafless pea cultivar, was used in all experiments. Seeds were [SOURCE: Page_6]

6   sown on 14, 12 and 11 March, respectively for the three years. Inter-row spacing was 0.175 m [SOURCE: Page_6]

7   in 1991 and 0.35 m in 1992 and 1993. Individual plots were 3 x 10 m in 1991 and 5 x 10 m in [SOURCE: Page_6]

8   1992 and 1993. Buffer areas (2 m wide) separated individual plots. Weeds were well [SOURCE: Page_6]

9   controlled by applications of preemergence pendimethalin plus neburon and postemergence [SOURCE: Page_6]

10   pendimethalin plus bentazone. Fungi and insects were well controlled by applications of [SOURCE: Page_6]

11   carbendazim plus chlorotalonil mixed with endosulfan plus thiomethon, every ten days from [SOURCE: Page_6]

12   the beginning of flowering to the end of grain filling. Plant densities were 60, 78 and 88 [SOURCE: Page_6]

13   plant.m-², for 1991, 1992 and 1993 respectively. [SOURCE: Page_6]

14                                      Irrigation management [SOURCE: Page_6]

15    Water was applied by drip lines placed on the soil surface with emitters at 30-cm intervals. [SOURCE: Page_6]

16   There were two rows of peas between lines in 1991 and one row in 1992 and 1993. The [SOURCE: Page_6]

17   amount of water applied was controlled with volumetric counters, one per plot. Drought stress [SOURCE: Page_6]

18   was imposed by withholding water at the start of drought stress periods. In 1991, irrigation [SOURCE: Page_6]

19   during unstressed periods was managed according to the climatic demand. Each day, the water [SOURCE: Page_6]

20   balance was calculated from emergence, taking into account the inputs and outputs of water. [SOURCE: Page_6]

21   The inputs included soil water reserve, rainfall and irrigation. Outputs included the [SOURCE: Page_6]

22   evapotranspiration (ETP, Penman method, Penman, 1948), corrected by a crop coefficient k [SOURCE: Page_6]

23   (Deumier et al., 1991). The values used were 0.5 from emergence to the 5-leaf stage, 0.7 from [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

1   5-leaf to 7- leaf stage, 0.9 from 7-leaf to 9-leaf stage, 1 from 9-leaf stage to the beginning [SOURCE: Page_7]

2   of flowering, and 1.2 after the beginning of flowering. Twenty mm of water were applied when [SOURCE: Page_7]

3   the water deficit exceeded 20 mm. [SOURCE: Page_7]

4    In 1992 and 1993, irrigation during unstressed periods was managed according to [SOURCE: Page_7]

5   tensiometric measurements. In each plot, three tensiometers were installed at a depth of 0.2, [SOURCE: Page_7]

6   0.4 and 0.6 m. Twenty mm of water were applied when the mean value of the three [SOURCE: Page_7]

7   tensiometer readings at 0.4 m was below - 40 MPa. [SOURCE: Page_7]

8                                   Water treatments and rainfall [SOURCE: Page_7]

9    In 1991, three irrigation treatments were imposed, and four in 1992 and 1993. All treatments [SOURCE: Page_7]

10   included optimal irrigation before flowering. In 1991, treatments consisted of a non-irrigated [SOURCE: Page_7]

11   (NI) control (irrigation stopped at the beginning of flowering), and two treatments irrigated [SOURCE: Page_7]

12   until the initiation of seed filling (Ney and Turc, 1993), or the end of seed filling which is [SOURCE: Page_7]

13   defined as physiological maturity according to Ney and Turc (1993). These last two treatments [SOURCE: Page_7]

14   are denoted as IBSF for « irrigated before seed filling » and WI for « well-irrigated » [SOURCE: Page_7]

15   respectively. In 1992 and 1993, the 4 treatments consisted of a non-irrigated (NI) control, two [SOURCE: Page_7]

16   treatments irrigated until the beginning (IBSF) or the end (WI) of seed filling, and a treatment [SOURCE: Page_7]

17   where irrigation was stopped at the beginning of flowering and began again at the initiation of [SOURCE: Page_7]

18   seed filling until the end of seed filling (IDSF for « irrigated during seed filling »). Treatments [SOURCE: Page_7]

19   were arranged in a randomized complete block with three replications in 1992 and 1993. In [SOURCE: Page_7]

20   1991 each treatment was replicated three times but not in a block design due to the irrgation [SOURCE: Page_7]

21   system used. Analysis of variance was conducted separately on each year’s data (significant [SOURCE: Page_7]

22   result if p<0.05, highly significant if p<0.01) and means were separated by Newman-Keuls test [SOURCE: Page_7]

23   at the 5% level. [SOURCE: Page_7]

6                                     Crop development evaluation [SOURCE: Page_8]

7    For irrigation management, the « beginning of flowering » stage was visually determined. [SOURCE: Page_8]

8   The « initiation of seed filling » and « end of seed filling » stages were evaluated with a [SOURCE: Page_8]

9   method based on monitoring seed moisture content. Fifty plants per plot were collected every [SOURCE: Page_8]

10   two or three days between the beginning of pod setting and harvest. Seeds were extracted [SOURCE: Page_8]

11   manually from pods and assembled according to their node number. Seed moisture content [SOURCE: Page_8]

12   was determined for each node according to the method described below. On the basis of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

13   observations of Rachidian and Le Deunff (1986), a node was considered to be at the initiation [SOURCE: Page_8]

14   or the end of seed filling when the moisture content of the seeds was 80 or 55 %, respectively. [SOURCE: Page_8]

15   Thus, the « initiation of seed filling » stage, at the crop level, was defined as the time when [SOURCE: Page_8]

16   seed moisture content at the first node reached 80 %, while the stage « end of seed filling » [SOURCE: Page_8]

17   stage was defined as the time when seeds of the upper node reached 55 % moisture content. [SOURCE: Page_8]

18                                         Harvesting methods [SOURCE: Page_8]

19    Harvests were performed when moisture content of the first node reached 15 %. For each [SOURCE: Page_8]

20   plot, 50 consecutive plants were collected manually well within the edges. Seeds were [SOURCE: Page_8]

21   extracted manually from the pods to constitute a seedlot, and stored in an air-conditioned [SOURCE: Page_8]

22   chamber (15 °C - 72 % relative humidity) until quality assessment. [SOURCE: Page_8]

2    For each plot of each treatment the mean number of flowering nodes per plant, yield and [SOURCE: Page_9]

3   yield components (seed number and mean seed weight) were measured on a sample of 80 [SOURCE: Page_9]

6                                  Physiological quality assessment [SOURCE: Page_9]

7    Two tests were added to the germination percentage, which is the most common measure of [SOURCE: Page_9]

8   seed quality. The first one was a conductivity test, whose relevance for predicting field [SOURCE: Page_9]

9   emergence and identifying low-quality seedlots in peas has been shown in many reports (for [SOURCE: Page_9]

10   example Bedford, 1974; Ladonne, 1992). As some external factors may strongly increase the [SOURCE: Page_9]

11   susceptibility of the seeds to imbibition damage, particularly cold and wet sowing conditions [SOURCE: Page_9]

12   (Simon and Wiebe, 1975; Ladonne, 1992), a cold test was added to assess physiological [SOURCE: Page_9]

13   quality. Ladonne (1992) showed that such a test makes it possible to identify low vigor [SOURCE: Page_9]

14   seedlots which had acceptable germination levels in the laboratory, but resulted in poor field [SOURCE: Page_9]

15   emergence. Germination tests were performed according to ISTA recommendations (ISTA, [SOURCE: Page_9]

16   1985). Two hundred seeds randomly chosen from each seedlot, in four replicates of 50 seeds, [SOURCE: Page_9]

17   were sown in boxes containing sand moistened to 10 % versus dry weight with deionized [SOURCE: Page_9]

18   water. The boxes were then covered with polyethylene films to avoid evaporation and placed [SOURCE: Page_9]

19   for 8 days in a germination room (20°C - photoperiod of 9h/24h). Normal and abnormal [SOURCE: Page_9]

20   seedlings were counted at the end of the germination period and the germination percentage [SOURCE: Page_9]

21   (percent normal seedling) of the four boxes from each seedlot were averaged. Conductivity [SOURCE: Page_9]

22   tests were performed on 100 seeds per seedlot with an automatic seed analyzer (ASAC 1000, [SOURCE: Page_9]

23   Neogen Corporation, Lansing, Michigan, U.S.A.). Before the test, seed moisture content was [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

1   equilibrated at about 15 % to avoid the presence of non-imbibed seeds. To take into account [SOURCE: Page_10]

2   variations in seed size between lots, results are expressed per gram of seed, as recommended [SOURCE: Page_10]

3   by ISTA (ISTA, 1987). Cold test procedures were the same as those of the germination test, [SOURCE: Page_10]

4   except that boxes were placed at 1°C for 24 h before being placed for 8 days at 20° C. For this [SOURCE: Page_10]

5   test, seed moisture content for all seed lots was equilibrated to about 11% by placing seeds for [SOURCE: Page_10]

6   two weeks at ambient air conditions. Linear regression was used to determine the relationship [SOURCE: Page_10]

7   between conductivity test and cold test results. [SOURCE: Page_10]

## 1                                           DISCUSSION

2   As differences in the conductivity test and cold test results occurred between years for WI [SOURCE: Page_13]

3   treatment, the effects of drought stress on pea seed physiological quality must first be [SOURCE: Page_13]

4   discussed year by year. Results from 1991 showed that drought stress starting at the beginning [SOURCE: Page_13]

5   of flowering and maintained during seed filling was sufficient to decrease seed physiological [SOURCE: Page_13]

6   quality. Water stress during seed filling only seemed to decrease seed quality compared to that [SOURCE: Page_13]

7   of well-irrigated plants, but this statement can not be made with definity because the values [SOURCE: Page_13]

8   did not differ significantly. Experiments from 1992 confirmed these results and showed that [SOURCE: Page_13]

9   physiological quality was maintained compared to the WI treatment if drought stress was [SOURCE: Page_13]

10   limited to the period from the start of flowering to the start of seed filling (IDSF treatment), [SOURCE: Page_13]

11   and plants were kept well-watered during the seed filling period. Experiments from 1993 could [SOURCE: Page_13]

12   not confirm these results due to high rainfall. The IBSF treatment from this year did not show [SOURCE: Page_13]

13   any decrease in physiological quality and this could be explained by a lower stress intensity or [SOURCE: Page_13]

14   a later stress period during seed filling compared to the same treatment from 1991 and 1992 [SOURCE: Page_13]

15   (Fig. 1). The results for the three years show that the whole reproductive period is a period of [SOURCE: Page_13]

16   sensitivity of pea seed physiological quality to drought stress, and that the decrease in quality [SOURCE: Page_13]

17   is lower when the stress does not occur during seed filling. These results are in accordance [SOURCE: Page_13]

18   with the results of authors (Raymond et al., 1988 in pea, Smiciklas et al., 1989 in soybean) [SOURCE: Page_13]

19   who observed a deleterious effect of drought stress on pea seed quality when water was [SOURCE: Page_13]

20   withheld during seed filling. Our observations may also explain why drought stress during the [SOURCE: Page_13]

21   reproductive period might have been reported as non effective on pea seed quality, if the stress [SOURCE: Page_13]

22   preceded the seed filling period. [SOURCE: Page_13]

13 [SOURCE: Page_13]

1    Differences in seed quality assessed by the conductivity test and cold test for the WI [SOURCE: Page_14]

2   treatments from 1992 and 1993 managed with the same irrigation system showed that drought [SOURCE: Page_14]

3   stress was not the only factor responsible for the seed physiological quality during our [SOURCE: Page_14]

4   experiments. The effects of the environment during seed development on the germination [SOURCE: Page_14]

5   quality of seeds have been investigated by several authors for some large-seeded leguminous [SOURCE: Page_14]

6   species. In beans (Phaseolus vulgaris L.), Siddique and Goodwin (1980) found a deleterious [SOURCE: Page_14]

7   effect of high maturation temperatures on the percentage of normal seedlings. In soybean [SOURCE: Page_14]

8   (Glycine max (L.) Merr.), Keigley and Mullen (1986) found that exposure to increasing periods [SOURCE: Page_14]

9   of high temperature during the seed filling period resulted in a linear decline in seed [SOURCE: Page_14]

10   germination and vigor. In peas, Nichols et al. (1978) observed no differences in seed quality [SOURCE: Page_14]

11   when the plants were cultivated at 21 or 24°C. However, these authors concluded that there is [SOURCE: Page_14]

12   a deleterious effect of high drying temperatures on seed quality, especially for immature seeds. [SOURCE: Page_14]

13    The effect of mineral nutrition on the germination quality of pea seeds has also been reported [SOURCE: Page_14]

14   in a few papers. Hadavizadeh and George (1988), working on plants grown in pots under glass, [SOURCE: Page_14]

15   observed no effect of potassium, but they found an increase in seed vigor (assessed by the [SOURCE: Page_14]

16   conductivity test) for the situations where high nitrogen was combined with medium [SOURCE: Page_14]

17   phosphorus supply. Keiser and Mullen (1993) showed under hydroponic conditions that poor [SOURCE: Page_14]

18   seed Ca concentration was associated with a low percentage of normal seedlings. Smiciklas et [SOURCE: Page_14]

19   al. (1989) obtained the same results in soybean. In our experiments, no significant differences [SOURCE: Page_14]

20   in mineral nutrition between treatments and between years were observed. But there was a [SOURCE: Page_14]

21   difference (2.5 to 3 °C) in mean temperature during seed dessiccation, especially between [SOURCE: Page_14]

22   1992 and 1993. These differences could be partly responsible for the quality differences among [SOURCE: Page_14]

23   years for the WI treatments. [SOURCE: Page_14]

14 [SOURCE: Page_14]

1     Our results should also be discussed regarding irrigation strategies. The WI treatment [SOURCE: Page_15]

2   showed the best results, as assessed by seed yield, and a high seed physiological quality. The [SOURCE: Page_15]

3   late irrigations needed in this strategy could, however, lead to pathological decreases in quality [SOURCE: Page_15]

4   in field conditions where aspersion is used. But the strategy for pea seed production practiced [SOURCE: Page_15]

5   at present in France is based on irrigation until the beginning of seed filling. This strategy, [SOURCE: Page_15]

6   corresponding to IBSF treatments in our experiments, led to failures in seed yields mainly due [SOURCE: Page_15]

7   to low mean seed weights and to uncertain physiological quality. The strategy involving [SOURCE: Page_15]

8   irrigation limited to the seed filling period (IDSF treatment) gave low seed numbers.m-2, which [SOURCE: Page_15]

9   was consistent with other authors’ results (Silim et al., 1992; Ney et al., 1994), but not low [SOURCE: Page_15]

10   seed yields due to higher mean seed weights. The seed physiological quality with this strategy [SOURCE: Page_15]

11   was as high as in the well-irrigated treatment. Mean seed weight was also less variable, which [SOURCE: Page_15]

12   might lead to higher quality of seedlings at emergence (Deneufbourg and Duchêne, 1994). An [SOURCE: Page_15]

13   adequate water supply during seed filling without irrigation during the flowering period could [SOURCE: Page_15]
