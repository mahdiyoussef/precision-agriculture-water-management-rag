Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

We used a participatory foresight method for assessing if, and how, groundwater markets could be incorporated [SOURCE: Page_1]

into local groundwater management policies. We propose an institutional setup adapted to the French water policy [SOURCE: Page_1]

context, with a cap and trade scenario introducing groundwater markets in the agricultural sector between now and [SOURCE: Page_1]

the 2035 horizon. Considering the local hydrogeological characteristics, we applied this method to five French [SOURCE: Page_1]

groundwater basins, and then analysed the public perception of our scenario by organizing 16 half-day workshops, [SOURCE: Page_1]

involving a total of 44 institutional stakeholders and 80 farmers. Overall, almost half of the participants were [SOURCE: Page_1]

opposed to the introduction of groundwater markets for various ethical, economic and technical reasons. Many of [SOURCE: Page_1]

the preconditions for water trading are still far from being met, and major social and economic risks are anticipated. [SOURCE: Page_1]

However, our results also suggest that there might be scope for developing groundwater markets compatible with [SOURCE: Page_1]

French water policy at a local scale; the preconditions for this are that specific local hydrogeological and [SOURCE: Page_1]

agricultural situations are taken into account, and that a participatory process is developed, involving institutional [SOURCE: Page_1]

stakeholders and farmers. [SOURCE: Page_1]

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Since the mid-1990s, mainstream environmental and resource economists have unfailingly promoted the [SOURCE: Page_2]

development of water markets for reallocating water resources and regulating pollution control (Rosegrant and [SOURCE: Page_2]

Binswanger, 1994; Livingstone, 1995). This approach is based on establishing an aggregate cap on water-resource [SOURCE: Page_2]

abstraction, on allocating individual abstraction rights to users, and on making these rights tradable. The argument, [SOURCE: Page_2]

reflecting standard economic theory, is that markets are more cost-effective than command-and-control regulation [SOURCE: Page_2]

for reaching an optimal allocation of limited renewable stocks, and that they are more flexible for adapting to an [SOURCE: Page_2]

ever-changing economic and environmental context (Young, 2014). The implementation of a system of tradable [SOURCE: Page_2]

water rights was strongly supported by international financial institutions in the 1990s (World Bank, 1993; [SOURCE: Page_2]

Thobani, 1997), and has been adopted and implemented in various developed and developing countries (Grafton [SOURCE: Page_2]

et al., 2011; Easter and Huang, 2014). Historically, water markets were first established for managing surface- [SOURCE: Page_2]

water resources, in regulated river basins with significant infrastructure for storing and transferring water. More [SOURCE: Page_2]

recently, their use has been extended to groundwater resources in several western states of the USA and Australia [SOURCE: Page_2]

(Thompson et al., 2009; Lawson et al., 2010; Skurray et al., 2013; Brozovic and Young, 2014; Wheeler et al., [SOURCE: Page_2]

2016). [SOURCE: Page_2]

Water markets, when applied to groundwater management, do not always function as predicted, because [SOURCE: Page_2]

several barriers may impede their development or operation (Bauer, 1997; 2012; Skurray et al., 2012; 2013; Easter [SOURCE: Page_2]

and Huang, 2014; Wheeler et al., 2016). In many groundwater basins where water markets were introduced, [SOURCE: Page_2]

transactions remain limited and far below expected levels. This may be for different reasons, including an [SOURCE: Page_2]

inadequate institutional framework, high transaction costs, overestimated caps (initial over-allocation), and [SOURCE: Page_2]

problems of compliance and enforcement (Brozovic and Young, 2014). This has led to the need for a new approach [SOURCE: Page_2]

to water markets by policy makers and managers. [SOURCE: Page_2]

It is thus clear that water markets are not a one-fits-all miracle tool that can be used by simply following [SOURCE: Page_2]

a standard recipe from a Resource Economics handbook, and there is a growing recognition that these mechanisms [SOURCE: Page_2]

cannot always be adapted to suit any hydrological and institutional context (Green Nylen et al., 2017; Babbitt et [SOURCE: Page_2]

al., 2017; Holley and Sinclair, 2018). Furthermore, where they are deemed suitable, they must be carefully adapted [SOURCE: Page_2]

to local socio-economic, institutional, environmental and cultural characteristics (Babbitt et al., 2017). As stressed [SOURCE: Page_2]

by Tisdell and Ward (2003), water markets are unlikely to function optimally if social and cultural attitudes to [SOURCE: Page_2]

### 2.1.1. General context

Several basic general conditions should be met before allowing farmers to trade water. First, the resource should [SOURCE: Page_4]

be clearly delimited, its functioning should be understood, and abstraction properly monitored. This is essential [SOURCE: Page_4]

for setting the cap, but also for controlling environmental consequences (Skurray et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_4]

The second condition is that water should be scarce, and considered as such by users and other [SOURCE: Page_4]

stakeholders. Studies have shown that there may be a gap between experts’ and users’ perceptions regarding [SOURCE: Page_4]

resource scarcity (Rinaudo et al., 2016b). [SOURCE: Page_4]

The third condition is that the proposed mechanism and the policymakers promoting it should be regarded [SOURCE: Page_5]

as legitimate, i.e. having the users’ trust and support. Compliance with the newly established allocation mechanism [SOURCE: Page_5]

is clearly dependent upon these conditions. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Finally, trade only makes sense if there is a difference in marginal product value for the different users, [SOURCE: Page_5]

generating opportunities for “win-win” voluntary exchanges of water-use rights. All these conditions must be met [SOURCE: Page_5]

before policymakers can begin to envisage the possibility of implementing a cap and trade water policy. [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 2.1.2. Capping total water use for irrigation, defining water entitlements and initial allocations

Before implementing a cap and trade policy in the agricultural sector, total water use for irrigation (volume or total [SOURCE: Page_5]

pumping rate) must be capped first. Two types of abstraction limits are needed (OECD, 2015): a long-term limit [SOURCE: Page_5]

(the “cap”) defines the maximum amount of water that can be abstracted from the groundwater resource; a short- [SOURCE: Page_5]

term limit (the “adjusted cap”) defines the amount of water that can be abstracted within a specified period (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_5]

a season or a year), depending on meteorological conditions and hydrological status. In parallel, “water [SOURCE: Page_5]

entitlements” should clearly specify individual water-use rights and be aligned with the cap. “Water allocations” [SOURCE: Page_5]

can be used for determining the amount of water that an entitlement holder is permitted to abstract within a [SOURCE: Page_5]

specified period (OECD, 2015). Individual water allocations are aligned with the adjusted cap. The allocated initial [SOURCE: Page_5]

volumes may be based on historical authorized water use, adjusted as a function of the reductions imposed by [SOURCE: Page_5]

capping. An appropriate metering and penalty system should ensure satisfactory enforcement and compliance. [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 2.1.3. Defining water-trading rules

Once caps and individual volumes have been set, policymakers may allow water users to trade their water [SOURCE: Page_5]

entitlements (permanent transfer) or water allocations within a given period (temporary transfer). Trade may be [SOURCE: Page_5]

limited to the agricultural sector or authorized between sectors. Controlling environmental and social externalities [SOURCE: Page_5]

is a major concern for policymakers and managers when water trading occurs, but trade-management rules can [SOURCE: Page_5]

serve to limit the social and environmental effects. Trade regulation is highly dependent upon the hydrological, [SOURCE: Page_5]

economic and social contexts, but it can be organized in diverse ways to ensure that it will be compatible with the [SOURCE: Page_5]

existing institutional framework. For example, trade can be based on bilateral negotiations between buyers and [SOURCE: Page_5]

sellers, which requires minimal supervision and may, or may not, involve brokers. [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 3.1. Policy background

The 2006 law imposed a cap on water use in basins with a structural groundwater deficit. In theory, the cap is [SOURCE: Page_8]

designed to protect the good ecological status of groundwater–dependent ecosystems under normal climatic [SOURCE: Page_8]

conditions, i.e. 4 years out of 5. The State attributes the capped volumes to the different economic sectors (cities, [SOURCE: Page_8]

industries and agriculture). The volume allocated to agriculture is officially assigned to the newly formed [SOURCE: Page_8]

Groundwater User Associations (GWUA), which are responsible for sharing the water among farmers. Under [SOURCE: Page_8]

State supervision, GWUAs must develop their own rules for determining individual entitlements. When we [SOURCE: Page_8]

organized the workshops in 2012-2013, the GWUAs had only just been established. [SOURCE: Page_8]

### 3.3. Adaptation to local hydrogeological characteristics

We then adapted this scenario to fit the specific hydrogeological characteristics of each of the five selected [SOURCE: Page_9]

groundwater basins. Our case studies broadly cover three types of aquifer. The first, illustrated by the Lyonnais [SOURCE: Page_9]

aquifer, consists of three large alluvial systems characterized by high inertia. Given their very significant water [SOURCE: Page_9]

reserves, they can easily buffer inter-annual fluctuations due to recharge (rainfall) or water abstraction by users. [SOURCE: Page_9]

Consequently, the total abstraction limit can be relaxed, and the cap can be based on an average abstraction limit [SOURCE: Page_9]

over a 5-year moving-average period. This allows users to carry over part of their water entitlement from one [SOURCE: Page_9]

season to the next; we assumed a 20% carry-over in the Lyonnais aquifer scenario. [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

The second type of aquifer is characterized by intermediate inertia. Based on observed groundwater levels [SOURCE: Page_10]

at the beginning of the season, managers can make fairly reliable predictions about the volumes that can be pumped [SOURCE: Page_10]

in the next six months, without putting dependent ecosystems at risk. Here, a yearly cap is set, and entitlements [SOURCE: Page_10]

correspond to an annual pumping limit. If necessary, the cap may be reduced before the start of the season and [SOURCE: Page_10]

individual water allocations readjusted accordingly. These assumptions were incorporated in the scenario [SOURCE: Page_10]

discussed with stakeholders in the Tarn-et-Garonne plain and the Serre basin. [SOURCE: Page_10]

The third type of aquifer is characterized by low inertia. Here, it is difficult to predict the volume available [SOURCE: Page_10]

during the cropping season as it depends on rainfall, and we assumed that a low value is set for the yearly cap. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Similarly, the individual allocations granted to farmers reflect this cautious approach. Such uncertainty diminishes [SOURCE: Page_10]

a few months into the growing season, and the available resource can be predicted with greater precision. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Individual allocations can then be adjusted as a function of the available resource. [SOURCE: Page_10]

How the cap is defined has obvious implications for groundwater trading. In the high-inertia case, [SOURCE: Page_10]

uncertainty is minimal because water can be purchased in advance and the volume traded can be fully guaranteed [SOURCE: Page_10]

over a 5-year period. The second case is similar: the volume can only be guaranteed for the season, but it fluctuates [SOURCE: Page_10]

from year to year. In both cases, we assumed that any trade in groundwater occurs before the start of the season. [SOURCE: Page_10]

In the third case, farmers leasing water entitlements before sowing their crop do not know how much water they [SOURCE: Page_10]

will be allocated during the season. Therefore, we proposed that a first round of trading could be conducted before [SOURCE: Page_10]

the growing season. A second round would be optional if additional volumes are allocated later in the season. [SOURCE: Page_10]

We assumed that trade is restricted to existing groundwater management units (GMUs), which vary in [SOURCE: Page_10]

size from 6,000 to 40,000 ha, depending on the hydrogeological nature of the aquifer. [SOURCE: Page_10]

### 4.1. Overview

## 5. DISCUSSION

The results presented above are consistent with the recent literature on water markets. They confirmed that, [SOURCE: Page_20]

whether water trading will be a viable tool for ensuring sustainable groundwater management, will depend on a [SOURCE: Page_20]

host of basin-specific factors (Wheeler et al., 2017; Babbit et al., 2017; Green Nylen et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_20]

Our study confirmed that shifting from a regulatory to a market-based water governance worried many [SOURCE: Page_20]

water users and stakeholders, even when carefully adapting the cap and trade scenario to local conditions. The [SOURCE: Page_20]

institutional arrangements we proposed to French stakeholders incorporate many safeguards against the risk of [SOURCE: Page_20]

market failure. The factors explaining this rejection of cap and trade were globally the same as those reported in [SOURCE: Page_20]

other papers analysing the limits of market-based forms of water governance (Galaz, 2004; Gianocarro et al., 2013; [SOURCE: Page_20]

Hernandez-Mora and del Moral, 2015; Hasselman and Stoker, 2017; Holley and Sinclair, 2018). We review those [SOURCE: Page_20]

factors below. [SOURCE: Page_20]

The views expressed by French stakeholders that preconditions for establishing cap and trade scenarios [SOURCE: Page_20]

are not met, echoed findings in other countries. Several authors (e.g. Boyd and Brumley, 2004; Skurray et al., [SOURCE: Page_20]

2012) identified a good understanding of the groundwater resource and its functioning as one of the key scientific [SOURCE: Page_20]

challenges for establishing groundwater trading. [SOURCE: Page_20]

Our analysis also showed that part of the opposition expressed by French stakeholders concerns the [SOURCE: Page_20]

capping process. Both the cap setting and the initial allocation of water-use rights are major challenges that, if not [SOURCE: Page_20]

properly addressed, can impede the establishment of a water market (McAllister, 2009; Holley and Sinclair, 2018). [SOURCE: Page_20]

The capping process may be at different stages of development, and insufficiently mature for introducing water [SOURCE: Page_20]

trading (Easter et al., 1999). Another major concern was the initial allocation of individual water-use rights where [SOURCE: Page_20]

they do not pre-exist. Stakeholders were particularly concerned by—and had diverging views on—how water-use [SOURCE: Page_20]

rights should be defined and initially allocated among users (Syme and Nancarrow, 1997; 2006; Nancarrow et al., [SOURCE: Page_20]

1998; Rinaudo et al., 2016b). In over-allocated basins, users may advocate different rules for reducing allocations [SOURCE: Page_20]

or entitlements, such as across-the-board reduction, or cancellation of dormant rights. This process raises a debate [SOURCE: Page_20]

that focuses on the issue of distributive justice (Rinaudo et al., 2016b). Poor acceptance of this initial process will [SOURCE: Page_20]

increase the risk of conflicts associated to trading. Moreover, the monitoring of groundwater abstraction remains [SOURCE: Page_20]

a weak link in France (Montginoul et al., forthcoming) and elsewhere (Zhang, 2007, in China; Holley and Sinclair, [SOURCE: Page_20]

2018, for Australia). Compliance problems could increase with water trading as this increases financial incentives [SOURCE: Page_20]

for disobeying the rules. Holley and Sinclair’s (2018) conclusion, which could apply to the French context, is that [SOURCE: Page_20]

## 6. CONCLUSIONS

This paper contributes to the future evolution of the French water policy, highlighting the key challenges of [SOURCE: Page_22]

introducing groundwater trading in the agricultural sector. Our results concern five groundwater basins, showing [SOURCE: Page_22]

that 50% of farmers and 40% of institutional stakeholders are opposed to groundwater trading for various ethical, [SOURCE: Page_22]

measures to limit such negative impact may, in turn, hinder trading and call its very existence into question. [SOURCE: Page_22]

groundwater markets that are compatible with the French water policy context. If groundwater trading is envisaged, [SOURCE: Page_22]

the market regulation mechanisms should be designed at a local scale, within GWUAs. This would ensure that [SOURCE: Page_22]
