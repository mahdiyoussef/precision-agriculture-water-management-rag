Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

The introduction of a living cover crop during a cash crop growth cycle (relay intercropping) [SOURCE: Page_2]

and its maintenance after the cash crop harvest may help to preserve biodiversity, increase soil [SOURCE: Page_2]

organic matter content and carbon sequestration and provide other ecosystem services, such [SOURCE: Page_2]

as increasing useful biotic interactions within the agroecosystem. We studied the impact of [SOURCE: Page_2]

various approaches to managing a red fescue cover crop in a winter wheat crop in terms of [SOURCE: Page_2]

light, water and nitrogen competition, using the STICS crop model adapted for intercropping. [SOURCE: Page_2]

The STICS model for wheat/fescue intercropping was first evaluated on two years of [SOURCE: Page_2]

experimental data obtained in the field. It gave satisfactory statistical results for the prediction [SOURCE: Page_2]

of dry matter, leaf area index (LAI) and nitrogen accumulation in the two species, and for [SOURCE: Page_2]

nitrogen and water dynamics in the soil. By providing access to unmeasured variables, such as [SOURCE: Page_3]

transpiration, the results of simulations with this model improve our understanding of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

performance of the intercrop in the field. For example, we showed that the intercropping [SOURCE: Page_3]

system was more efficient that the wheat crop grown as a monoculture in terms of nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_3]

accumulation and decreasing soil nitrogen levels before the leaching period. However, it also [SOURCE: Page_3]

resulted in lower wheat yields. We then used the STICS model to compare four intercropping [SOURCE: Page_3]

management scenarios differing in terms of the date of red fescue emergence, over 35 climatic [SOURCE: Page_3]

years. We found that, in most climatic scenarios, the emergence of the fescue crop during the [SOURCE: Page_3]

late tillering phase of the wheat crop gave the best compromise between wheat yield overall [SOURCE: Page_3]

nitrogen accumulation and radiation interception. [SOURCE: Page_3]

### 1. Introduction

A cover crop, grown between two main crops, changes the physical (Zibilske and Makus, [SOURCE: Page_3]

2009), chemical (Rinnofner et al., 2008) and biological (Isik et al., 2009) conditions of both [SOURCE: Page_3]

the soil and the crop. The use of cover crops in cropping systems therefore has interesting [SOURCE: Page_3]

agronomic and environmental effects, including protecting the soil against erosion, [SOURCE: Page_3]

contributing to the control of weeds and diseases, providing the next crop with nitrogen and [SOURCE: Page_3]

preventing nitrate leaching (Hartwig and Ammon, 2002). Cover crops are frequently managed [SOURCE: Page_3]

by sowing after the harvest of the main crop, with subsequent destruction by chemical or [SOURCE: Page_3]

mechanical techniques. The introduction of a living cover crop during the cash crop growth [SOURCE: Page_4]

cycle (intercropping) and its maintenance after the cash crop harvest are less frequently [SOURCE: Page_4]

observed. However, this approach has several advantages, including the preservation of [SOURCE: Page_4]

biodiversity, increasing soil organic matter content and carbon sequestration (Scopel et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

2005; Lahmar et al., 2006; Teasdale et al., 2007), and increasing biotic interactions within the [SOURCE: Page_4]

agroecosystem (Hartwig and Ammon, 2002). Direct and indirect interactions between the [SOURCE: Page_4]

cash crop and the cover crop generate opposing facilitative and competitive effects. A [SOURCE: Page_4]

facilitative effect is a positive interspecific interaction between the intercropping species [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Vandermeer, 1989). Such effects occur when one crop increases the availability of resources [SOURCE: Page_4]

to the other crop. For example, a living cover crop can decrease weed growth in the cash crop [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Brandsaeter et al., 1998; den Hollander et al., 2007), improve soil structure regeneration and [SOURCE: Page_4]

provide nitrogen to the following cash crop (if the cover crop is a legume). However, when [SOURCE: Page_4]

the cash crop and the cover crop are intercropped during the cash crop growth cycle, they [SOURCE: Page_4]

generally have to compete for the same resources, which may decrease cash crop yields [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Carof et al., 2007a, b). The success of the intercropping system, which may be combined [SOURCE: Page_4]

with reduced or no-tillage systems (Holland, 2004), therefore depends on maximizing [SOURCE: Page_4]

facilitation and minimizing competition between the two crops, in terms of light, water, and [SOURCE: Page_4]

nutrients. Little is currently known about these aspects, particularly for temperate crops. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Modeling can be used to simulate and to improve our understanding of the partitioning of [SOURCE: Page_4]

resources in these complex systems. It is therefore a useful tool for identifying ways to [SOURCE: Page_4]

increase facilitation or to curb competition. Most of the agronomic intercropping models [SOURCE: Page_4]

studied to date are used as tools for achieving three main objectives. The first one of these [SOURCE: Page_4]

objectives is the analysis of biotic interactions and resource partitioning in an intercropping [SOURCE: Page_4]

system (Berntsen et al., 2004; Tsubo et al., 2005a; Corre-Hellou et al., 2007). In this case, [SOURCE: Page_4]

modeling provides a straightforward quantification of the processes involved in facilitative [SOURCE: Page_5]

and competitive relationships, through the explicit representation of fundamental physical and [SOURCE: Page_5]

biological processes, such as photosynthesis, dry matter partitioning, leaf area growth, root [SOURCE: Page_5]

growth, plant development, the nutrient cycle and energy balance. It is also possible to fine- [SOURCE: Page_5]

tune interactions between light, water, and nitrogen on the basis of analyses of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

relationships between supply and demand and between related indicators, such as NNI or [SOURCE: Page_5]

interception efficiency. The second objective of these models is to assess intercropping [SOURCE: Page_5]

performances (Baumann et al., 2002a; Jensen, 2006; Whitmore and Schröder, 2007). In this [SOURCE: Page_5]

case, modeling provides access to intermediate variables that are of considerable importance [SOURCE: Page_5]

for assessment but difficult to measure, such as nitrogen and water fluxes between different [SOURCE: Page_5]

compartments of the system. The third objective is to use modeling to explore scenarios [SOURCE: Page_5]

defined in terms of climate or management strategy (Tsubo, 2005b; Baumann et al., 2002b). [SOURCE: Page_5]

In this study, we used a modeling approach to investigate facilitation and competition for [SOURCE: Page_5]

resources in terms of light, water and nitrogen, in a cropping system based on the [SOURCE: Page_5]

intercropping of winter wheat and red fescue. We investigated the extent to which the STICS [SOURCE: Page_5]

model (Brisson et al., 2009) accurately reflected the functioning of the intercropping system [SOURCE: Page_5]

over a two-year period of experimentation (Picard et al., submitted), and used this model to [SOURCE: Page_5]

test various cover crop management strategies in terms of competition for light, water and [SOURCE: Page_5]

nitrogen, with the aim of enhancing the agronomic and environmental performances of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

system. [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 2. Materials and methods

2.1. Model overview [SOURCE: Page_5]

The model used was an intercropping extension of the sole crop model STICS (Brisson et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

2009) considering the system to consist of two species rather than one (Brisson et al., 2004). [SOURCE: Page_6]

This model is based on a daily time-step that integrates input variables related to climate, soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

properties and cropping system, to calculate both agricultural and environmental variables [SOURCE: Page_6]

(Brisson et al., 2009). STICS is a generic model that can readily be adapted to various crop [SOURCE: Page_6]

types and is known to be robust, based on parameterizations in various soil and weather [SOURCE: Page_6]

conditions; this model also has a high level of plasticity, based on options in terms of [SOURCE: Page_6]

formalism for both physiology and management (Brisson et al., 2009). The crop is [SOURCE: Page_6]

characterized by its aerial biomass and leaf area index and in terms of the number and [SOURCE: Page_6]

biomass of harvested organs. The soil is divided into a succession of horizontal layers, each of [SOURCE: Page_6]

which is characterized by its water and mineral N content. The soil environment is assumed to [SOURCE: Page_6]

be identical for both crops in the intercropping system. Soil and crop interact through the [SOURCE: Page_6]

roots, via the root density distribution in the soil profile. [SOURCE: Page_6]

STICS simulates daily biomass accumulation in the canopy and the water and nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_6]

balances of the system. Crop development is driven by thermal time and is used principally to [SOURCE: Page_6]

calculate leaf area and to define the filling phase of the harvested organ. The corresponding [SOURCE: Page_6]

phenological stages depend on the species and variety. Crop growth is driven by plant carbon [SOURCE: Page_6]

accumulation, through the interception of solar radiation by the canopy and its transformation [SOURCE: Page_6]

into biomass. This biomass is thus the net result of the processes of photosynthesis, [SOURCE: Page_6]

respiration, and root/shoot partitioning. The STICS intercropping model simulates the sharing [SOURCE: Page_6]

of light between the two crops, by calculating radiation transfer as a function of the volume of [SOURCE: Page_6]

the canopy (height and width) of each species (Brisson et al., 2004). The fraction of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) actually absorbed by the two crops depends greatly [SOURCE: Page_6]

on their respective heights, which depend in turn on both the characteristics of the plants [SOURCE: Page_7]

concerned and the growth conditions. Crop nitrogen content depends on carbon accumulation [SOURCE: Page_7]

and soil nitrogen availability. The amount of nitrogen taken up by each species depends on [SOURCE: Page_7]

root depth penetration, root distribution in the soil layers, and N demand. Daily absorption of [SOURCE: Page_7]

N by each species is determined by the smaller of two quantities: the amount of N available [SOURCE: Page_7]

through the soil-root system or crop N requirements. Crop requirements are calculated with [SOURCE: Page_7]

the N concentration/biomass relationship established from the upper limit of N dilution [SOURCE: Page_7]

curves. The possible existence of water stress and N stress is taken into account with indices [SOURCE: Page_7]

decreasing leaf growth and biomass accumulation in conditions of water or nutrient limitation [SOURCE: Page_7]

(Brisson et al., 2009). Water is transported downwards in the soil when water content exceeds [SOURCE: Page_7]

field capacity in a particular layer. For nitrate, transport within the soil profile is simulated [SOURCE: Page_7]

with the “mixing cells” concept, which accounts for convection and dispersion. [SOURCE: Page_7]

### 2.2. Experimental data

The results used for model parameterization and evaluation were obtained in two field [SOURCE: Page_7]

experiments carried out in the 1999-2000 (referred to as the 1999 experiment) and 2000-2001 [SOURCE: Page_7]

(referred to as 2000) growing seasons at the INRA Grignon experimental station, located in [SOURCE: Page_7]

northern France (48°50’N, 1°56’E). The soil was an orthic luvisol (FAO-UNESCO, 1974) [SOURCE: Page_7]

containing 281 g.kg-1 clay, 599 g.kg-1 silt, and 120 g.kg-1 sand in the 0-90 cm soil layer, in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

1999 experiment, and 261 g.kg-1 clay, 649 g.kg-1 silt, and 90 g.kg-1 sand in the 0-90 cm soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

layer, in the 2000 experiment. Weather conditions during the experimental period are shown [SOURCE: Page_7]

in Fig.1. Cumulative daily mean temperature and cumulative solar radiation were similar in [SOURCE: Page_7]

1999 and 2000 (4895 and 4891 degree days, respectively, for temperature and 4259 and 4319 [SOURCE: Page_7]

Mj.m-², respectively, for solar radiation), whereas cumulative rainfall was greater in 1999 than [SOURCE: Page_7]

in 2000 (1164 and 867 mm, respectively; Fig.1). The preceding crop was barley in 1999 and [SOURCE: Page_8]

rapeseed in 2000. The experimental treatments were winter wheat grown as a monoculture [SOURCE: Page_8]

(Triticum aestivum, cv Isengrain), red fescue grown as a monoculture (Festuca rubra, cv [SOURCE: Page_8]

Sunset) and winter wheat intercropped with red fescue. The intercropped crops and sole crops [SOURCE: Page_8]

were sown at the same time and at the same sites. Plant densities were 18 kg seed.ha-1 for [SOURCE: Page_8]

fescue and 150 plants.m-² for wheat. These crops were grown in mixtures with an additive [SOURCE: Page_8]

design in 1999 and 2000 (identical sowing densities were used for wheat grown as a [SOURCE: Page_8]

monoculture and for intercropped wheat). A split-plot design was used for both experiments, [SOURCE: Page_8]

with four replicates in 1999 and six replicates in 2000. Nitrogen fertilizer applications were [SOURCE: Page_8]

similar for all treatments of the two experiments, including red fescue grown as a [SOURCE: Page_8]

monoculture. The total amount of N fertilizer applied was 166 kg N.ha-1 in 1999 and 160 kg [SOURCE: Page_8]

N.ha-1 in 2000. These amounts were applied in two applications, between tillering and [SOURCE: Page_8]

anthesis, in each year. Weeds, pests and diseases were controlled with appropriate pesticides. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Wheat (both sole-crop and intercropped wheat) was harvested on 18 July 2000 for the 1999 [SOURCE: Page_8]

experiment and on 23 July 2001 for the 2000 experiment. [SOURCE: Page_8]

In each year, measurements were taken during two periods: the intercropping period (referred [SOURCE: Page_8]

to hereafter as ϕ 1) and after the harvest of the wheat crop, when the fescue was growing as a [SOURCE: Page_8]

monoculture (ϕ 2), until the end of the year. For these two periods, we collected the following [SOURCE: Page_8]

data: aerial dry matter, leaf area index (LAI) and nitrogen accumulation in crops, together [SOURCE: Page_8]

with soil mineral nitrogen and soil water content. The experiments are described in detail [SOURCE: Page_8]

elsewhere Picard et al. (submitted). [SOURCE: Page_8]

2.3. Calibration and evaluation of the model [SOURCE: Page_8]

The parameters of STICS defined by Brisson et al. (2009) were used to simulate the wheat- [SOURCE: Page_9]

fescue intercrops. Only varietal parameters, site-specific soil parameters and radiative transfer [SOURCE: Page_9]

parameters for fescue (not available in the paper by Brisson et al., 2009) were calibrated, [SOURCE: Page_9]

using experimental data for sole crops only (Table 1). The model was then evaluated with [SOURCE: Page_9]

intercrop data. [SOURCE: Page_9]

Varietal parameter calibration was carried out in two steps for wheat and fescue. We first [SOURCE: Page_9]

forced leaf area index with the measured values and then calibrated the other modules closely [SOURCE: Page_9]

related to this variable, such as aerial biomass and nitrogen accumulation. In the second step, [SOURCE: Page_9]

we calibrated the parameters determining LAI, without forcing LAI, on the same data set. For [SOURCE: Page_9]

site-specific soil parameters, we calibrated potential soil evaporation (q^0), by optimizing [SOURCE: Page_9]

water content dynamics for sole-crop data. Soil water content was estimated at field capacity [SOURCE: Page_9]

(WC pF$_2$.0) and at wilting point (WC pF$_4$.2), together with bulk density, for each year, on the [SOURCE: Page_9]

basis of observed soil data. For fescue, we also calibrated the “radiative transfers” module in [SOURCE: Page_9]

two steps, using data for fescue grown as a monoculture (1999, 2000). First, leaf area index [SOURCE: Page_9]

(LAI) parameters used to simulate changes in LAI in STICS were optimized on the basis of [SOURCE: Page_9]

observed LAI data. We then used observed LAI and biomass data for fescue grown as a [SOURCE: Page_9]

monoculture to optimize the various parameters of the “radiative transfer” module (Table 1). [SOURCE: Page_9]

We used several criteria to compare simulated and experimental results in intercrops (Wallach [SOURCE: Page_9]

and Goffinet, 1987). The root mean square error (RMSE) was used to evaluate the prediction [SOURCE: Page_9]

error of the model, by heavily weighting large errors. We assessed the predictive ability of the [SOURCE: Page_9]

model by calculating the root mean square error of prediction (RMSEP) and model efficiency [SOURCE: Page_9]

(EF). A detailed description of the estimation of these criteria was provided by Wallach et al. [SOURCE: Page_9]

(2006). [SOURCE: Page_9]

### 2.4. The scenarios simulated

Simulations were used to study several management scenarios, to analyze the impact of the [SOURCE: Page_10]

timing of the fescue cycle on the agronomic and environmental performance of the system. [SOURCE: Page_10]

These simulations were run over 35 years of climatic data (1970-2004) from Versailles [SOURCE: Page_10]

(48°48’N, 2°04’E), which is situated close to the Grignon experimental station, to enable us to [SOURCE: Page_10]

take climate variability into account in the scenario assessment (Fig. 1). We compared four [SOURCE: Page_10]

management scenarios, assessing the performance of the system during the two phases [SOURCE: Page_10]

considered (ϕ 1 and ϕ 2). We stopped the simulation on 31 December, because STICS cannot [SOURCE: Page_10]

run simulations over more than two consecutive years. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Scenario 0 simulated wheat grown as a sole crop and was treated as the reference scenario [SOURCE: Page_10]

against which the performance of the other three scenarios was assessed. In scenario 1, we [SOURCE: Page_10]

simulated fescue emergence one week after the harvest of the wheat crop (in July, precise date [SOURCE: Page_10]

depending on wheat maturity), corresponding to a rotation: wheat monoculture followed by [SOURCE: Page_10]

fescue monoculture. In scenario 2, we simulated fescue emergence in the spring (18 March), [SOURCE: Page_10]

corresponding to relay intercropping: wheat grown as a monoculture from October to March [SOURCE: Page_10]

and then intercropped with fescue. In scenario 3, we simulated the simultaneous emergence of [SOURCE: Page_10]

wheat and fescue (10 October), corresponding to full intercropping. In scenarios 2 and 3, [SOURCE: Page_10]

fescue was not destroyed after wheat harvest, therefore remaining alive during ϕ 2. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Some of the other simulated cropping practices and initial data for state variables were similar [SOURCE: Page_10]

over the 35 years for the four scenarios. Three applications of N fertilizer were carried out, on [SOURCE: Page_10]

25 February, 25 March and 30 April. The amounts of N fertilizer applied during the first and [SOURCE: Page_10]

third applications were fixed at 50 and 40 kg.ha-1, respectively. The amount of N fertilizer [SOURCE: Page_11]

applied during the second application was estimated with the balance-sheet method (Rémy [SOURCE: Page_11]

and Hébert, 1977), with a target yield of 9 t.ha-1 for each climatic year. [SOURCE: Page_11]

### 3.1. Evaluation of the model

Good agreement was found between the observed and simulated data obtained for wheat- [SOURCE: Page_11]

fescue intercrops, for both years (Fig. 2 and 3), as indicated by the RMSE value and the [SOURCE: Page_11]

efficiency (EF) of the model (Table 2). In both years of experimentation, the model [SOURCE: Page_11]

reproduced the marked predominance of wheat over fescue. Dry matter levels were much [SOURCE: Page_11]

lower for fescue than for wheat (Fig. 2a): at harvest, mean observed wheat dry matter content [SOURCE: Page_11]

was about 17.6 t.ha-1 and mean observed fescue dry matter content was about 0.42 t.ha-1, in [SOURCE: Page_11]

both years. The capacity of wheat to develop leaf area more quickly than fescue during ϕ 1 [SOURCE: Page_11]

(Figure 2b) probably played a key role in this predominance. Just before the wheat harvest, [SOURCE: Page_11]

wheat LAI decreased due to senescence, leading to an increase in fescue LAI, which was [SOURCE: Page_11]

overestimated by the model but with no effect on the simulation of dry matter and N [SOURCE: Page_11]

accumulation. [SOURCE: Page_11]

Wheat absorbed much more nitrogen than fescue during ϕ 1 (Fig. 2c). At harvest, the mean [SOURCE: Page_11]

amount of nitrogen absorbed was 190.7 kg N.ha-1 for wheat and 6.5 kg N.ha-1 for fescue. [SOURCE: Page_11]

However, the fescue absorbed the residual nitrogen just after wheat harvest. The simulations [SOURCE: Page_11]

for this variable were of better quality for ϕ 1 than for ϕ 2. The model overestimated nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_11]

accumulation by fescue during this period. However, the differences between the observed [SOURCE: Page_11]

and simulated values remained small. The observed and simulated yield values were 8.47 and [SOURCE: Page_12]

10.7 t.ha-1, respectively, in 1999 and 8.9 and 8.57 t.ha-1, respectively, in 2000, giving a [SOURCE: Page_12]

RMSEP of 2.27 t.ha-1 (n =2). [SOURCE: Page_12]

The agreement between the observed and simulated data for inorganic N in the soil (Fig. 3a) [SOURCE: Page_12]

was less satisfactory than for the results presented above, as indicated by the RMSE and the [SOURCE: Page_12]

EF of the model (Table 2). Nevertheless, the model reproduced the principal changes in soil [SOURCE: Page_12]

inorganic N and water content well until the wheat harvest. The model simulated overall [SOURCE: Page_12]

changes in soil water content well during the two contrasting experimental years (Fig. 3b). [SOURCE: Page_12]

The model also simulated accurately a dry period observed at the end of the summer of 1999 [SOURCE: Page_12]

and at the end of the growth cycle in 2000. However, for 2000, the model tended to [SOURCE: Page_12]

overestimate soil water content just after a dry period (beginning of June until October). [SOURCE: Page_12]

However, the amount of water involved in this overestimate was not very large: the difference [SOURCE: Page_12]

between observed and simulated soil water contents varied between 20 and 40 mm during this [SOURCE: Page_12]

period. After the dry period, the model accurately simulated the reconstitution of soil water [SOURCE: Page_12]

stores. [SOURCE: Page_12]

### 3.2. Performance of wheat-fescue intercropping, as assessed by the model

Simulation results showed that wheat yield was not overly affected by fescue development in [SOURCE: Page_12]

the intercropping system (table 3): yield differences between sole-crop and intercropped [SOURCE: Page_12]

wheat were about 0.43 t.ha-1 in 1999 and 0.39 t.ha-1 in 2000, corresponding to a difference of [SOURCE: Page_12]

less than 5%. The modeling results provide an explanation for this difference, through [SOURCE: Page_12]

analyses of the partitioning of resources between the two crops. The model provides insight [SOURCE: Page_12]

into the contribution of each species to dry matter production, light interception, water [SOURCE: Page_12]

consumption and N acquisition in the intercropping system. The comparison between [SOURCE: Page_13]

simulated aerial dry matter for intercropped wheat and sole-crop wheat showed that dry [SOURCE: Page_13]

matter levels were slightly lower (about 2%) for intercropped than for sole-crop wheat in both [SOURCE: Page_13]

years. However, dry matter levels for wheat plus fescue were 11% and a 9% higher than for [SOURCE: Page_13]

sole-crop wheat, in both 1999 and 2000, due to the growth of fescue during ϕ 1 and, [SOURCE: Page_13]

particularly, ϕ 2. PAR interception by intercropped wheat was similar to that for sole-crop [SOURCE: Page_13]

wheat in both years (Table 3). Nevertheless, over the complete cycle (ϕ 1 and ϕ 2), the model [SOURCE: Page_13]

simulates 27% and a 21% higher levels of total absorbed radiation in 1999 and 2000, [SOURCE: Page_13]

respectively, due to the greater LAI of the fescue. Indeed, fescue intercepted 51 and 35 Mj.m-² [SOURCE: Page_13]

of light, respectively, in 1999 and 2000 during ϕ 1, and about 178 and 119 Mj.m-², [SOURCE: Page_13]

respectively, in 1999 and 2000 during ϕ 2 (Fig. 4). Thus, 19% and 15% less radiation reached [SOURCE: Page_13]

the soil in the intercropped system in 1999 and 2000. [SOURCE: Page_13]

Simulation showed that the intercropped wheat absorbed 2% less nitrogen in 1999 and 2000 [SOURCE: Page_13]

than did sole-crop wheat (Table 3). However, overall nitrogen absorption by wheat plus [SOURCE: Page_13]

fescue in the intercropped system was greater than nitrogen absorption by wheat grown as a [SOURCE: Page_13]

monoculture. Indeed, an additional 43 and 46 kg of nitrogen per hectare was absorbed by the [SOURCE: Page_13]

fescue in 1999 and 2000, respectively, during ϕ 1 and ϕ 2. The nitrogen absorbed by the two [SOURCE: Page_13]

crops reduced residual soil nitrogen levels by 8 and 9 units after harvest in 1999 and 2000, [SOURCE: Page_13]

respectively, and by 39 and 42 units by 31 December, in 1999 and 2000, respectively (Table [SOURCE: Page_13]

3). This decrease in soil nitrogen stores before the end of December decreased the amounts of [SOURCE: Page_13]

nitrogen likely to be leached during January-April leaching period. [SOURCE: Page_13]

The simulated transpiration for intercropped wheat was slightly lower (about 1%) than that [SOURCE: Page_13]

for sole-crop wheat, in both years (Table 3). Nevertheless, the total water transpired by the [SOURCE: Page_13]

two crops over the whole cycle (ϕ 1 and ϕ 2) was 22% in 1999 and 15% higher in 2000 than [SOURCE: Page_14]

that transpired by sole-crop wheat. This had a direct effect on evaporation from the soil: in [SOURCE: Page_14]

### 4. Discussion

4.1) Model performance [SOURCE: Page_18]

Our approach, based on parameterization of the model with experimental data obtained for [SOURCE: Page_18]

monocultures and evaluation of the model with an intercropping system, has been adopted in [SOURCE: Page_18]

various studies modeling intercropping (Baumann et al., 2002b; Berntsen et al., 2004; Corre- [SOURCE: Page_18]

Hellou et al., 2007). This approach gave good results in our study, showing that the model [SOURCE: Page_18]

essentially used the same processes to describe the functioning of the system for both [SOURCE: Page_18]

monocultures and intercropping systems. The changes in state variables observed for the [SOURCE: Page_18]

intercropping system in this study therefore resulted essentially from resource sharing and [SOURCE: Page_18]

changes in growth conditions (simulated by STICS) rather than processes occurring [SOURCE: Page_18]

specifically in the intercropping system and relating to architectural plasticity or specific [SOURCE: Page_18]

biotic constraints. Baumann et al. (2002b) showed that, in certain cases, the model makes [SOURCE: Page_18]

errors, due to different leaf morphology responses in mixtures and in monocultures. In such [SOURCE: Page_18]

cases, models parameterized for monocultures cannot take into account adaptations occurring [SOURCE: Page_18]

in mixtures. In our conditions, any morphological adaptations occurring in intercropped plants [SOURCE: Page_18]

probably had only a very small effect on the simulated processes, due to the very strong [SOURCE: Page_18]

dominance of wheat over fescue. For example, the etiolation of fescue plants would probably [SOURCE: Page_18]

have been insufficient to increase their access to radiation. [SOURCE: Page_18]

Evaluation of the STICS model during the cropping cycle (ϕ1) indicated that this model [SOURCE: Page_19]

accurately simulated biomass production, changes in leaf area and nitrogen accumulation for [SOURCE: Page_19]

the two species. It also accurately simulated nitrogen and water fluxes. In other modeling [SOURCE: Page_19]

studies based on the use of STICS and focusing on pea/barley intercropping systems (Jensen, [SOURCE: Page_19]

2006; Corre-Hellou et al., 2007), the model accurately reproduced the state variables of the [SOURCE: Page_19]

intercropped species, but gave RMSE values slightly lower than those obtained here (Launay [SOURCE: Page_19]

et al., 2009). These previous results were obtained for production levels well below ours (7 [SOURCE: Page_19]

t.ha-1 total biomass, versus 24 t ha-1 in our study), indicating that our results were better. [SOURCE: Page_19]

Corre-Hellou et al. (2007) attributed the differences between observed and simulated data to [SOURCE: Page_19]

poor simulation of the partitioning of radiation between the two species in cases in which the [SOURCE: Page_19]

dominance relationship between the species was inversed at the end of the cropping cycle. For [SOURCE: Page_19]

our intercropping system, no such inversion occurred, with wheat continuing to dominate over [SOURCE: Page_19]

fescue from sowing until harvest. Nitrogen and biotic stresses were also weaker in our [SOURCE: Page_19]

experimental conditions, which were not subject to the constraints of organic farming. This [SOURCE: Page_19]

may account for the differences in the level of production and the better performance of the [SOURCE: Page_19]

model (as STICS models cannot take biotic stresses into account) in our study. [SOURCE: Page_19]

During the intercropping period, the model tended to overestimate the amount of mineral [SOURCE: Page_19]

nitrogen taken up by the fescue after the wheat had been harvested (ϕ2). This decreases the [SOURCE: Page_19]

quality of the model’s predictions for the variable “plant nitrogen” for fescue during this [SOURCE: Page_19]

period. It also implies a possible underestimation of the amount of mineral nitrogen remaining [SOURCE: Page_19]

in the soil during the winter period and therefore likely to be leached. The results obtained for [SOURCE: Page_19]

simulations with the model indicate that the model allowed the uptake of nitrogen by fescue [SOURCE: Page_19]

roots when nitrogen levels in the soil were very low. Dorsainvil (2002) modeled intermediate [SOURCE: Page_19]

crops and attributed the overestimation of nitrogen uptake by crops in the STICS model [SOURCE: Page_20]

during the intercropping period to this factor, which is a generic parameter of the model that [SOURCE: Page_20]

we did not modify (we modified only site-specific parameters). The performance of the [SOURCE: Page_20]

STICS model could be improved by more complete parameterization, but we feel that the [SOURCE: Page_20]

error in the estimation of nitrogen absorption by fescue inherent to the STICS model is [SOURCE: Page_20]

acceptable given the intended use of this model. [SOURCE: Page_20]

### 4.2. Effects of intercropping on plant growth

Although we simulated a strong domination of wheat over fescue, intercropping decreased [SOURCE: Page_20]

wheat yields by about 5%. This yield-decreasing effect of intercropping on wheat has been [SOURCE: Page_20]

reported in studies of other types of mixed cultures involving wheat and legumes, or on [SOURCE: Page_20]

competition between wheat and weeds. In studies of competition between wheat and weeds, [SOURCE: Page_20]

particularly for grass weeds, decreases in wheat yields of more than 20% have been [SOURCE: Page_20]

systematically reported, with yield loss even reaching 90% in some cases, due to competition [SOURCE: Page_20]

for environmental resources (Lemerle et al., 2004; Vandeleur and Gill, 2004; Blackshaw et [SOURCE: Page_20]

al., 2005). In studies of wheat-legume intercropping, yield losses generally vary between 10 [SOURCE: Page_20]

and 30% with respect to wheat sown as a monoculture at the same density (Bulson et al., [SOURCE: Page_20]

1997; Haymes and Lee, 1999; Banik et al., 2006; Thorsted et al., 2006 b; Carof et al., 2007a), [SOURCE: Page_20]

but may reach 70% in some cases in which there is intense competition for resources [SOURCE: Page_20]

(Haymes and Lee, 1999; Hiltbrunner et al., 2007). This variability may be accounted for [SOURCE: Page_20]

principally by the intensity of competition between wheat, the leguminous crop and weeds. In [SOURCE: Page_20]

studies in which wheat yields were little affected by intercropping, the authors generally [SOURCE: Page_20]

attributed this result to a difference in the use of environmental resource niches by the two [SOURCE: Page_20]

crops (Anil et al., 1998) or to the complementary use of these resources by the two species [SOURCE: Page_20]

(Willey, 1979). For our intercropping system involving two members of the grass family, the [SOURCE: Page_21]

small decrease in wheat yields under intercropping cannot be attributed to the use of different [SOURCE: Page_21]

niches. Instead, it is due to the strong dominance of wheat over fescue, resulting from an [SOURCE: Page_21]

earlier onset of the growth cycle, with fescue emerging four months later than wheat. This [SOURCE: Page_21]

difference in emergence times allowed the wheat crop to outcompete the fescue, particularly [SOURCE: Page_21]

for the interception of solar radiation. [SOURCE: Page_21]

Intercropping may facilitate the better use of environmental resources, thereby increasing [SOURCE: Page_21]

productivity (Vandermeer, 1989; Willey, 1990). In the situation studied here, intercropping [SOURCE: Page_21]

did not increase total yields because the fescue was not harvested. By contrast, intercropping [SOURCE: Page_21]

did increase the total amount of biomass produced, consistent with the results of various [SOURCE: Page_21]

studies on intercropping (Hauggaard-Nielsen et al., 2001a; Thorsted et al., 2006c). Teasdale et [SOURCE: Page_21]

al. (2007), in a long-term study of the functioning of an intercropping system involving a [SOURCE: Page_21]

living cover crop, showed that the biomass produced accumulated in the soil, helping to [SOURCE: Page_21]

increase the organic matter content of the soil. We showed that this increase in primary [SOURCE: Page_21]

productivity resulted essentially from improvements in radiation interception when crops [SOURCE: Page_21]

were grown together, particularly after the wheat harvest, consistent with the results obtained [SOURCE: Page_21]

for other combinations of crops (Tsubo et al., 2001; Carof et al., 2007b). Some of these [SOURCE: Page_21]

authors suggested that their results were due to the complementary nature of the aerial [SOURCE: Page_21]

architecture of the two species. In our case, this complementarity results primarily from the [SOURCE: Page_21]

vertical distribution of the leaves, with the taller wheat crop shading the shorter fescue [SOURCE: Page_21]

(simulated data not shown). [SOURCE: Page_21]

The dominance of wheat over fescue results from its genetic characteristics rather than from [SOURCE: Page_21]

an adaptation of its aerial architecture during intercropping. The simulation results, confirmed [SOURCE: Page_21]

by experimental observations (Picard et al., submitted), demonstrate that the LAI and height [SOURCE: Page_22]

of wheat are similar in monoculture and in the intercropping system. This complementarity is [SOURCE: Page_22]

also achieved through the offset of the growth cycles of the two crops, enabling the fescue to [SOURCE: Page_22]

intercept solar radiation during wheat senescence and after the wheat harvest (Fig. 4). This [SOURCE: Page_22]

greater radiation interception efficiency after the wheat harvest may result in the suppression [SOURCE: Page_22]

of weed populations (Bulson et al., 1997; Hauggaard-Nielsen et al., 2001a; Thorsted et al., [SOURCE: Page_22]

2006c; den Hollander et al., 2007; Hilbrunner et al., 2007). However, some studies have [SOURCE: Page_22]

attributed this decrease in weed populations to an allelopathic effect observed in some crop [SOURCE: Page_22]

combinations (White et al., 1989 cited by Banik et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_22]

### 4.3. Effect of intercropping on water and nitrogen fluxes

The presence of a second crop may also affect water balance, by increasing transpiration by [SOURCE: Page_22]

the canopy, as shown by several authors (Thorsted et al., 2006c). Morris and Garrity (1993) [SOURCE: Page_22]

showed that intercropping slightly modified water uptake, which varied between -6 and +7% [SOURCE: Page_22]

of the levels recorded for monocultures, whereas the intercropping system used water much [SOURCE: Page_22]

more efficiently than monocultures. In our intercropping system, the water stress indices [SOURCE: Page_22]

obtained with the STICS model showed that, in the trial conditions, neither of the two crops [SOURCE: Page_22]

experienced water stress during the intercropping trial. This finding may be accounted for in [SOURCE: Page_22]

part by the low levels of biomass production by the fescue during ϕ1. Fescue contributed [SOURCE: Page_22]

2.5% of the total biomass produced during intercropping. It may also be accounted for by the [SOURCE: Page_22]

decrease in evaporation and drainage due to the presence of the fescue, resulting in an absence [SOURCE: Page_22]

of water deficiency in the climatic conditions occurring during the experiments. Consistent [SOURCE: Page_22]

with this finding, Carof et al. (2007b) showed that intercropping did not affect water [SOURCE: Page_22]

availability to cultures in this type of climate. [SOURCE: Page_22]

The dominance of wheat at the start of the growth cycle limited both nitrogen availability to [SOURCE: Page_23]

the fescue and the fescue’s nitrogen demand. Corre-Hellou (2005) showed, for the pea-barley [SOURCE: Page_23]

combination, that a species with a rapid growth rate at the start of the cycle could rapidly [SOURCE: Page_23]

come to dominate in competition for this resource. This is due to a difference in access to [SOURCE: Page_23]

nitrogen, due to the difference in rooting patterns between the two species. In our situation, [SOURCE: Page_23]

the dominance of wheat over fescue was also due to a difference in root architecture. [SOURCE: Page_23]

Simulation results showed that the wheat roots penetrated the soil to a depth of 120 cm, [SOURCE: Page_23]

whereas the fescue roots remained in the first 30 cm of soil. Hauggaard-Nielsen et al. (2001b) [SOURCE: Page_23]

also observed, for a combination of pea and barley, that the barley root system penetrated to [SOURCE: Page_23]

greater depths in intercropping conditions than in monocultures. This difference in rooting [SOURCE: Page_23]

pattern allows the barley crop to use resources more effectively, by exploring the soil more [SOURCE: Page_23]

efficiently than barley grown as a monoculture. In our conditions, the simulation data, [SOURCE: Page_23]

confirmed by experimentation (not shown) showed no difference in root length between [SOURCE: Page_23]

intercropped wheat and wheat monocultures. Maintenance of the living fescue crop [SOURCE: Page_23]

immediately after the wheat harvest significantly decreased nitrogen levels in the soil on [SOURCE: Page_23]

December 31 (Table 4). Fescue is thus an effective catch crop during this period, like other [SOURCE: Page_23]

members of the grass family (Dorsainvil, 2002; Känkänen and Eriksson, 2007). [SOURCE: Page_23]

### 4.4. Impact of the timing of the fescue growth cycle on the performance of the system

Simulation results demonstrated that the system was highly sensitive to the timing of the [SOURCE: Page_23]

fescue growth cycle, particularly in terms of dry matter production, the interception of [SOURCE: Page_23]

radiation and nitrogen capture (Fig. 7). Effects on yield were more limited and there was [SOURCE: Page_23]

almost no effect on water balance in this study. These results are consistent with studies on [SOURCE: Page_23]

the impact of the sowing date of a cover crop after the main crop (Vos and van der Putten, [SOURCE: Page_24]

1997; Dorsainvil, 2002) or of an intercrop (Whitmore and Schöder, 2007; Launay et al., [SOURCE: Page_24]

2009). In the case of a cover crop introduced into the rotation (our scenario 1), Dorsainvil [SOURCE: Page_24]

(2002) showed that the establishment of grasses (ryegrass in the studied concerned) was very [SOURCE: Page_24]

slow if they were sown just after the cereal harvest, resulting in low levels of biomass [SOURCE: Page_24]

production, principally due to water stress. We may have overestimated the emergence rate of [SOURCE: Page_24]

fescue sown after the wheat harvest (scenario 1). Indeed, the simulation conditions for the [SOURCE: Page_24]

emergence of a spring- or summer-sown crop are poorly described in the model: STICS takes [SOURCE: Page_24]

into account only the mean water content of the soil layer containing the seeds, but large [SOURCE: Page_24]

gradients in water content are often observed in the first few centimeters of the soil in [SOURCE: Page_24]

summer. This might lead to the overestimation of fescue growth and resource uptake. In [SOURCE: Page_24]

intercropping situations (our scenarios 2 and 3) Launay et al. (2009) obtained results similar [SOURCE: Page_24]

to ours for a pea-barley system, in which barley yields were 30% higher if barley was sown [SOURCE: Page_24]

two weeks before pea. [SOURCE: Page_24]

Advancing the sowing date of the fescue increases both competition effects (decreasing wheat [SOURCE: Page_24]

biomass and yield) and facilitation effects (increasing total biomass and soil cover, decreasing [SOURCE: Page_24]

the amount of solar radiation reaching the soil). It also increases the efficiency of radiation [SOURCE: Page_24]

and nitrogen use. The sowing date for the fescue is therefore a key technical choice [SOURCE: Page_24]

determining the balance between competition and facilitation. Other canopy management [SOURCE: Page_24]

techniques can also be used to adjust this balance. These techniques include the mechanical [SOURCE: Page_24]

(Thorsted et al., 2006a) or chemical (Carof et al., 2007a) control of the cover crop during the [SOURCE: Page_24]

growth cycle. We studied this balance over only one cropping and intercropping cycle. [SOURCE: Page_24]

However, certain facilitation processes may occur more slowly. For example, the nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_24]

absorbed by the fescue during the intercropping period may be supplied to the next crop, [SOURCE: Page_24]

thereby reducing its nitrogen fertilizer requirements. Similarly, the increase in biomass [SOURCE: Page_25]

production due to the fescue may increase the organic matter content of the soil in the [SOURCE: Page_25]

medium term, thereby improving its fertility. Longer term experimental and modeling studies [SOURCE: Page_25]

are required to determine the consequences of these processes for agronomic and [SOURCE: Page_25]

environmental performance. [SOURCE: Page_25]

### 5. Conclusion

The inclusion of a fescue as a cover crop in a wheat intercropping system may therefore favor [SOURCE: Page_25]

certain biotic processes, such as the production of primary biomass, the interception of [SOURCE: Page_25]

radiation that might otherwise reach weeds and the recycling of nutrient elements. A [SOURCE: Page_25]

comparison of the simulated and observed results showed that the “STICS intercropping” [SOURCE: Page_25]

model accurately simulated these processes and could be used to evaluate their impact on the [SOURCE: Page_25]

agronomic and environmental performance of the system for different climatic and technical [SOURCE: Page_25]

scenarios. The simulated results show that the use of fescue as a cover crop increases the [SOURCE: Page_25]

efficiency of radiation interception by up to 50%, thereby resulting in higher levels of [SOURCE: Page_25]

biomass production and a decrease, by up to 30%, in the amount of radiation reaching the [SOURCE: Page_25]

ground and available to weeds. This makes it possible to recycle mineral nitrogen efficiently [SOURCE: Page_25]

during the intercropping period, with no effect on water balance in the climatic conditions of [SOURCE: Page_25]

the Parisian Basin. Despite the strong dominance of wheat over fescue, the simulation data [SOURCE: Page_25]

nonetheless showed mean yield losses of 2 to 6%, depending on the sowing date for the [SOURCE: Page_25]

fescue. The timing of the fescue growth cycle is thus a key technical choice for control of the [SOURCE: Page_25]

balance between competition and facilitation and for improving the agronomic and [SOURCE: Page_25]

environmental performance of the system. [SOURCE: Page_25]
