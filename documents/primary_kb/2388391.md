10   Abstract: [SOURCE: Page_2]

11   Some portions of the porous rock matrix in the karst unsaturated zone (UZ) can contain large [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   volumes of water and play a major role in water flow regulation. The essential results are presented [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   of a local-scale study conducted in 2011 and 2012 above the Low Noise Underground Laboratory [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   (LSBB – Laboratoire Souterrain à Bas Bruit) at Rustrel, southeastern France. Previous research [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   revealed the geological structure and water-related features of the study site and illustrated the [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   feasibility of specific hydrogeophysical measurements. In this study, the focus is on hydrodynamics at [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   the seasonal and event time scales. Magnetic resonance sounding (MRS) measured a high water [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   content (more than 10 %) in a large volume of rock. This large volume of water cannot be stored in [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   fractures and conduits within the UZ. MRS was also used to measure the seasonal variation of water [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   stored in the karst UZ. A process-based model was developed to simulate the effect of vegetation on [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   groundwater recharge dynamics. In addition, electrical resistivity tomography (ERT) monitoring was [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   used to assess preferential water pathways during a rain event. This study demonstrates the major [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   influence of water flow within the porous rock matrix on the UZ hydrogeological functioning at both [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   the local (LSBB) and regional (Fontaine de Vaucluse) scales. By taking into account the role of the [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   porous matrix in water flow regulation, these findings may significantly improve karst groundwater [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   hydrodynamic modelling, exploitation, and sustainable management. [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   Karst is a complex medium with multi-scale heterogeneity. Water flow pathways are present [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   throughout the entire medium, from rock matrix to fractures and karst features (Fig. 1). The [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   hydrodynamic role of these water pathways is still poorly understood (e.g. Bailly-Comte et al. 2010) [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   and water flow regulation within karst hydrosystems remains an important issue. On one hand, karst [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   hydrosystems have high permeability because a large amount of water moves quickly through karst [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   conduits and rapid pressure transfer occurs. On the other hand, karst hydrosystems provide an [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   important buffer effect because spring discharge remains high even during long dry periods, as at the [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   Fontaine de Vaucluse spring in France. Several hypotheses have been advanced to explain the [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   important water flow regulation capacity of karst hydrosystems: (i) significant water storage within [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   the saturated zone in karst conduits (e.g. Mangin 1975; Marsaud 1996); (ii) water storage in the [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   epikarst, (fractured and weathered near-surface karst) (e.g. Aquilina et al. 2006; Ford and Williams [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   2007); and (iii) delayed infiltration or travel time within the entire unsaturated zone (UZ) (e.g. Celle- [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   Jeanton et al. 2003; Emblanch et al. 2003; Mudarra and Andreo 2010; Mudarra et al. 2012). However, [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   this regulation capacity within the UZ is not related to identified geological features (e.g. fractures, [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   matrix). Finally, few researchers consider water storage in the UZ zone to be significant enough to [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   play a role in water flow regulation and the capacitive function of karst. [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   Due to these complexities, distributed hydrogeological modeling of karst systems remains difficult to [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   implement and is not often done (e.g. Kiraly 1998; Larocque et al. 1999; Scanlon et al. 2003; [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   Worthington 2009; Worthington and Ford 2009). The usual approach to karst hydrosystems is the so- [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   called “black box” model (e.g. Mangin 1975; Marsaud 1996; Labat et al. 2000a; Labat et al. 2000b; [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   Rimmer and Salingar 2006; Fleury et al. 2007; Moussu et al. 2011; Hartmann et al. 2012). However, [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   “black box” models are site-specific. The lack of physics and geology in such models makes difficult their [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   transfer from one karst system to another. Moreover, “black-box” modeling (e.g. Fleury et al. 2007) and [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   hydrochemical estimates (e.g. Batiot et al. 2003) have indicated large residence time differences [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   within a single karst hydrosystem. [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   heterogeneous media such as karst (Bakalowicz 2005). Hydrogeologists often apply techniques [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   commonly used in surface hydrology, such as natural and artificial tracers or rainfall/runoff models. [SOURCE: Page_4]

## 206   3.3.1 MRS

207   MRS is the only ground-based geophysical method that can obtain a signal directly related to the [SOURCE: Page_11]

208   presence of water to a depth of several meters. MRS, which is designed for a quantitative description [SOURCE: Page_11]

209   of aquifer formations, is based on the phenomenon of hydrogen proton magnetic resonance. The [SOURCE: Page_11]

210   MRS array generates a signal with a specific frequency to excite the hydrogen protons in water [SOURCE: Page_11]

211   molecules, and the MRS signal received is specifically related to groundwater (e.g. Legchenko et al. [SOURCE: Page_11]

## 237   3.3.2 ERT

238   The ERT technique has been widely used in karst areas because it is robust and reliable (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_13]

239   Cardarelli et al. 2006; Robert et al. 2012). The sensitivity of electrical resistivity to moisture variations [SOURCE: Page_13]

240   and the rapidity of ERT measurements (approximately one hour per section) led to the selection of [SOURCE: Page_13]

241   this geophysical technique to image rapid resistivity variation during heavy rainfall. During the 30 day [SOURCE: Page_13]

242   campaign, ERT time-lapse acquisition ranged from every three hours during the rain event (17 days) [SOURCE: Page_13]

243   to one section a day after the rain event. [SOURCE: Page_13]

244   The acquisition system used is an ABEM Terrameter SAS 4000 (Dahlin 2001) with four channels and [SOURCE: Page_13]

245   64 electrodes. Implantation of the ERT electrodes, mainly at limestone outcrops, required holes that [SOURCE: Page_13]

246   were mechanically dug into the rock; saltwater and mud were added to ensure a good quality ground [SOURCE: Page_13]

247   contact. The ERT section LSBB03 acquired with Gradient array is presented in this paper (Fig. 4). The [SOURCE: Page_13]

248   section is oriented east-west. This direction is perpendicular to the general slope, is sub- [SOURCE: Page_13]

249   perpendicular to one of the main fault and lineament directions, and is the most heterogeneous [SOURCE: Page_13]

250   direction in terms of apparent resistivity spatial distribution (Carrière et al. 2013). The Gradient array [SOURCE: Page_13]

251   was chosen for this survey because of its robustness and its rapidity (Dahlin and Zhou 2004). [SOURCE: Page_13]

252   A portion of the first ERT results were presented by Carrière et al. (2015) to evaluate the [SOURCE: Page_13]

253   effectiveness and technical limits of ERT to monitor water infiltration via previously recognized karst [SOURCE: Page_13]

254   features under natural conditions. [SOURCE: Page_13]

268   The hydrodynamics of flow points C and D as a function of recharge (rain and effective infiltration) is [SOURCE: Page_14]

271   4.1. Flow dynamics versus rain and effective infiltration [SOURCE: Page_14]

272   Two hydrological periods can be observed: a dry period from 2004 to 2007 and a rainy period from [SOURCE: Page_14]

273   2008 to 2013 (Fig. 5) (Barbel-Perineau et al. 2013). Effective infiltration modeling using CASTANEA [SOURCE: Page_14]

274   (Fig. 5) confirms this alternation of wet and dry periods. One notes that effective infiltration is quite [SOURCE: Page_14]

275   limited between 2004 and 2007. Rigorous modeling of effective infiltration is essential in supporting [SOURCE: Page_14]

276   subsequent interpretations because the difference between rain and effective infiltration can be [SOURCE: Page_14]

277   quite large and can lead to misinterpretation. [SOURCE: Page_14]

278   During the first dry period, the water discharge at point D generally decreases and there is no clear [SOURCE: Page_14]

279   reaction during rain events. On the contrary, during the second rainy period, this water discharge [SOURCE: Page_14]

14 [SOURCE: Page_15]

300   Although these elevated water contents are surprising for a karst hydrosystem UZ, these values [SOURCE: Page_16]

301   agree with the high porosity estimated by other geophysical methods used at the LSBB (Maufroy [SOURCE: Page_16]

302   2010; Bereš 2013) and total matrix porosity measured in plugs in the region (e.g. Masse 1969; [SOURCE: Page_16]

307   It is difficult to conceive that such a large volume of water could be stored in fracture and conduits [SOURCE: Page_16]

308   porosity of karst UZ. For this reason, in this geological context where limestone can be very porous, [SOURCE: Page_16]

309   the majority of this water content can be attributed to the porous matrix. [SOURCE: Page_16]

310   The high water content level detected by MRS near the depth of 15 m corresponds to location of the [SOURCE: Page_16]

311   cross-stratifications and a moderate resistivity zone detected by GPR and ERT (Fig. 3b and c). This [SOURCE: Page_16]

312   feature probably contains a stock of water located above point D, and supplies this water to point D. [SOURCE: Page_16]

313   Its hydrogeological role will be discussed in the next section. [SOURCE: Page_16]

315   4.2.2 Event dynamics [SOURCE: Page_16]

316   This unique large-scale ERT surface based experiment was conducted during a typical Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_16]

317   autumn rain event (17 days). A total of 230 mm of rain were recorded and 120 ERT time-lapse [SOURCE: Page_16]

318   sections were measured over the same profile (LSBB03) during and after the rain event (a total of 30 [SOURCE: Page_16]

319   days). [SOURCE: Page_16]

320   Apparent resistivity values have been arithmetically averaged for each section. Apparent resistivity [SOURCE: Page_16]

321   decreased sharply during the rain event, from 1750 to 1050 Ω.m (Fig. 6b). These variations do not [SOURCE: Page_16]

322   seem to be related to temperature variations because air temperature and water temperature (at [SOURCE: Page_16]

15 [SOURCE: Page_16]

323   point D) remained stable during the experiment (Carrière 2014). Thus, these electrical resistivity [SOURCE: Page_17]

324   variations can be reasonably related to variation in water content in the subsurface. Analysis of this [SOURCE: Page_17]

325   mean apparent resistivity indicator made it possible to select the twelve critical time steps presented [SOURCE: Page_17]

326   in Fig. 6a: before, during, and after the rain event. These results are presented (Fig. 6a) as hourly [SOURCE: Page_17]

327   percentage changes in ρα with a basic representation of vegetation and soil cover around the profile. [SOURCE: Page_17]

328   Previously recognized karst features (Carrière et al. 2013) are also represented. [SOURCE: Page_17]

329   At the beginning of the rain event (Fig. 6a, section 12), ρα decreased moderately and [SOURCE: Page_17]

330   homogeneously along the section. This resistivity decrease could be related to moistening of near [SOURCE: Page_17]

331   surface horizons. After the first rain event (Fig. 6a, section 23), ρα stabilized quickly. During the [SOURCE: Page_17]

332   following heavy rain episode (Fig. 6a, sections 45 to 78), the moistening process appeared to be [SOURCE: Page_17]

333   quite heterogeneous and some zones resembled water pathways or preferential infiltration zones. [SOURCE: Page_17]

334   However, it would be unwise to link observed ρα variations at depth with deep moistening processes. [SOURCE: Page_17]

335   The observed variations can also be directly influenced by near surface variations. Immediately after [SOURCE: Page_17]

336   the rain (Fig. 6a, sections 910 and 1011), ρα increased in some zones. This result may be related [SOURCE: Page_17]

337   to drainage processes. These zones correlate well with zones previously identified as preferential [SOURCE: Page_17]

338   pathways. This second observation reinforced the hypothesis of preferential pathways playing a [SOURCE: Page_17]

339   hydraulic role that can be traced with ERT monitoring. However, it is still not possible to specify [SOURCE: Page_17]

340   properties such as the geometry of these pathways. Other zones where such drainage processes are [SOURCE: Page_17]

341   not identifiable may be related to zones where soil is thicker and remains moist after rainfall. [SOURCE: Page_17]

342   The assumed water pathways identified on the basis of apparent resistivity do not seem to be related [SOURCE: Page_17]

343   to vegetation density or soil cover variations (Fig. 6). However, these zones appear related to [SOURCE: Page_17]

344   fractures or fault zones previously detected by GPR (Carrière et al. 2013). Note also that resistivity [SOURCE: Page_17]

345   variations in fractures are different from those measured in the crushed zone located in the western [SOURCE: Page_17]

346   part of the profile. A crushed zone may provide a larger water pathway than a fracture. It is [SOURCE: Page_17]

347   important to emphasize that the methods used in this study do not image the pathways with [SOURCE: Page_17]

357   5.1 Local scale: study area [SOURCE: Page_19]

358   This integrated hydrogeophysical approach at two different time scales has improved the current [SOURCE: Page_19]

359   understanding knowledge of the hydrogeological functioning of study site. At the seasonal time step, [SOURCE: Page_19]

360   MRS made it possible to identify water storage variation above and below flow point D. These [SOURCE: Page_19]

361   variations are related to slow water dynamics within matrix porosity. This hypothesis is consistent [SOURCE: Page_19]

362   with the regular flow dynamics at point D, which were observed during both dry and recharge [SOURCE: Page_19]

363   periods (Fig. 5). Moreover, it is consistent with the large total matrix porosity within Urgonian [SOURCE: Page_19]

364   limestone, which may reach 20 % (Fig. 2d). [SOURCE: Page_19]

365   At the rain event time scale, ERT monitoring revealed heterogeneity in infiltration (Fig. 6). Some [SOURCE: Page_19]

366   fractures or faulted zones previously detected by GPR (Fig. 3) were identified as probable [SOURCE: Page_19]

367   preferential water pathways (Fig. 7b and d). [SOURCE: Page_19]

368   During wet periods, when water fills these vertical structures, hydraulic head (Δh) increases above [SOURCE: Page_19]

369   flow point D, which induces a prompt increase in flow at D as in October 2011 (Fig. 7d) during ERT [SOURCE: Page_19]

370   monitoring (Fig. 6). During this type of rain event, this increased flow may push forward older, less [SOURCE: Page_19]

371   mobile water according to the water schema proposed by Barbel-Perineau (2013) who studied [SOURCE: Page_19]

372   hydrochemistry within the LSBB. [SOURCE: Page_19]

373   During long dry periods, as for example, between 2004 and 2007 (Fig. 7a), flow point D did not [SOURCE: Page_19]

374   respond to rain pulses even during extreme events (Fig. 7b). Thus, there is probably no significant [SOURCE: Page_19]

412   effective infiltration decreased significantly between 2008 and 2010. Thus, there is a kind of delayed [SOURCE: Page_21]

415           - The annual water discharge of the Fontaine de Vaucluse exhibits dynamics similar to those [SOURCE: Page_21]

451   Without additional measurements, the geophysical survey cannot be used to extend the [SOURCE: Page_23]

452   hydrodynamic properties observed at the LSBB site to the Fontaine de Vaucluse hydrosystem, either [SOURCE: Page_23]

453   wholly or in part. That is why it is not reasonable to try to quantify the consequences of the presence [SOURCE: Page_23]

454   of porous limestone on the Fontaine de Vaucluse spring flows. However, considering that Urgonian [SOURCE: Page_23]

455   limestone covers more than half the surface area of the Fontaine de Vaucluse basin (Fig. 2b) and that [SOURCE: Page_23]

456   Masse (1969) measured high matrix porosity on the entire Urgonian platform, there are necessarily [SOURCE: Page_23]

457   repercussions in Fontaine de Vaucluse outlet flow that may explain the multi-annual dynamics of [SOURCE: Page_23]

458   water flow (Fig. 8). [SOURCE: Page_23]

459   If Urgonian limestones play an important role in Fontaine de Vaucluse hydrodynamics, similar [SOURCE: Page_23]

460   geological formations can similarly impact the hydrodynamics of other karst hydrosystems, including [SOURCE: Page_23]

461   areas far from the Mediterranean Sea where the sedimentary conditions are similar. For example, [SOURCE: Page_23]

463   Schmoker, 1983). Therefore, on the basis of these results presented, it is essential to consider the [SOURCE: Page_24]

464   role of the porous matrix in water flow regulation within a karst UZ. Hydrodynamic modelling should [SOURCE: Page_24]
