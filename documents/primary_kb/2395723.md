Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

Chalk groundwater is an important aquifer resource. It is intensively exploited for human use, with a [SOURCE: Page_3]

large proportion utilized for drinking water. The improvement of the knowledge on Chalk aquifer [SOURCE: Page_3]

hydrogeological functioning is essential for the management of this resource. Here, we developed a [SOURCE: Page_3]

methodology based on a combination of hydrodynamic, hydrochemistry and groundwater dating tools. [SOURCE: Page_3]

A study site with Chalk outcrops was selected in NE France where groundwater geochemistry and [SOURCE: Page_3]

water level were monitored continuously for 2 years from 2017 to 2019 and groundwater residence [SOURCE: Page_3]

time was estimated using CFCs and SF$_6$. Overall, the aquifer has an inertial behaviour with respect to [SOURCE: Page_3]

recharge. Nevertheless, a rapid water level response following rainfall events was observed at one site, [SOURCE: Page_3]

suggesting the presence of highly developed fracture network at the local scale. According to the [SOURCE: Page_3]

mixing process (piston flow, exponential or binary mixing) defined at each sampling site, groundwater [SOURCE: Page_3]

dating indicated rather heterogeneous ages ranging from modern to about 50 years. High spatial and [SOURCE: Page_3]

temporal heterogeneities were observed and interpreted by a combination of hydrogeological setting, [SOURCE: Page_3]

residence time and land use information, highlighting main factors governing the Chalk groundwater [SOURCE: Page_3]

geochemistry including water level fluctuation, thickness of the Unsaturated Zone (UZ), superficial [SOURCE: Page_3]

formations, distribution of fracture network, aquifer-river relations and human activities. A conceptual [SOURCE: Page_3]

1 [SOURCE: Page_3]

model was proposed accordingly to explain the hydrogeological functioning of unconfined Chalk [SOURCE: Page_4]

aquifers. [SOURCE: Page_4]

1    Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

The Chalk formation is widespread in northwestern Europe covering large areas between the Paris [SOURCE: Page_4]

Basin, the London Basin, Denmark and the North West of Germany (Bakalowicz, 2018). Groundwater [SOURCE: Page_4]

of the Chalk Aquifer is intensively exploited for human use, making it one of the most important [SOURCE: Page_4]

aquifers over much of Northern Europe. In France, the Chalk aquifer covers about 20 % of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

metropolitan territory with a total area of 110 000 km^2 (Crampon et al., 1996; Roux, 2018) and [SOURCE: Page_4]

provides approximately 12 billion m^3·year-1 of water, which accounts for 70% of the drinking water [SOURCE: Page_4]

consumed in the north of France (Lallahem, 2002). However, in recent decades, there is a growing [SOURCE: Page_4]

evidence of the deterioration of the European Chalk groundwater quality due to human activities [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Baran et al., 2008; Barhoum, 2014; Chen et al., 2019; Hakoun et al., 2017; Johnson et al., 2001; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Longstaff et al., 1992). Aquifer protection requires a sound knowledge of the Chalk hydrogeology and [SOURCE: Page_4]

groundwater geochemistry. [SOURCE: Page_4]

The Chalk is recognized as possessing possibly triple porosity: matrix, fracture and conduit (karst) [SOURCE: Page_4]

porosity (Headworth et al., 1982; MacDonald et al., 1998; Price et al., 1993; Worthington, 2003), but [SOURCE: Page_4]

the most common models describe this carbonate rocks as a double-porosity medium with a capacious [SOURCE: Page_4]

porous space of low permeability and high permeable fissures of low volume (e.g., Brouyère, 2006; [SOURCE: Page_4]

2 [SOURCE: Page_4]

Fitzpatrick, 2011; Van den Daele et al., 2007). Fracture distribution in the Chalk is highly [SOURCE: Page_5]

heterogeneous. Vertically, fractures are generally only developed towards the top of the aquifer (Allen [SOURCE: Page_5]

et al., 1997). Laterally, fractures are more developed in river valleys than on interfluves as the [SOURCE: Page_5]

structural weaknesses followed by valleys are more fractured and erosion along valleys favors the [SOURCE: Page_5]

opening of horizontal fractures (Allen et al., 1997; Price, 1987; Price et al., 1993). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Recharge through the UZ can occur very rapidly through fractures or slowly within the matrix (Allen [SOURCE: Page_5]

et al., 1997). Most studies consider that in the UZ, recharge is predominately through the Chalk matrix [SOURCE: Page_5]

with a mean downward rate of about 1 m·year-1 (Barraclough et al., 1994; Brouyère et al., 2004; Chen [SOURCE: Page_5]

et al., 2019; Haria et al., 2003; Vachier et al., 1987; Van den Daele et al., 2007; Wellings, 1984). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Recharge through fractures is faster but smaller in volume. It occurs episodically when the infiltration [SOURCE: Page_5]

rate exceeds the hydraulic conductivity of the matrix (Ireson and Butler, 2011; Mathias et al., 2006; [SOURCE: Page_5]

Price et al., 2000). Rapid flow could also temporally or locally be dominant, especially in zones where [SOURCE: Page_5]

karstic systems are developed (Ireson et al., 2006; Mathias et al., 2006; Maurice, 2009). Flow rates [SOURCE: Page_5]

have been estimated with values ranging from 1 to 20 km·day-1 in karstic systems and up to 1m·h-1 in [SOURCE: Page_5]

fissures (Allshorn et al., 2007; Baran et al., 2008; Calba, 1980; Devos et al., 2006; Ireson et al., 2006; [SOURCE: Page_5]

Katz et al., 2009; MacDonald et al., 1998). It should be noted that karst flow has usually a small [SOURCE: Page_5]

contribution to the recharge of Chalk aquifer but can cause punctual problems on groundwater quality [SOURCE: Page_5]

such as nitrate and bacterial contaminations (Barhoum, 2014). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Chemical composition of the Chalk groundwater is mainly controlled by water-rock interactions due [SOURCE: Page_5]

to the dissolution of primary rock minerals and the precipitation of second phases (Gillon et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Most of the chemical characteristics of Chalk groundwater are acquired during initial percolation [SOURCE: Page_5]

through the soil and upper Saturated Zone (SZ). As the Chalk matrix is dominated by carbonate [SOURCE: Page_5]

minerals, a fast homogeneity of the baseline geochemistry is expected (Edmunds et al., 1987). [SOURCE: Page_5]

However, spatial and temporal heterogeneities of the groundwater chemistry can be observed due to [SOURCE: Page_5]

internal and external factors such as local variations of the rock mineralogy (e.g., presence of [SOURCE: Page_5]

dolomitic bodies, clay-rich, phosphatic layers; Deconinck et al., 2005; Jarvis, 1980; Richard et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

2005), the incongruent dissolution of Chalk and dolomitic impurities (Aquilina et al., 2003; Edmunds [SOURCE: Page_5]

3 [SOURCE: Page_5]

et al., 1987; Gillon et al., 2010; Kloppmann et al., 1998), pollution caused by human activities (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_6]

NO$_3$-, Cl-, atrazine), especially agricultural application of fertilizer and pesticide (Barhoum, 2014; [SOURCE: Page_6]

Kloppmann et al., 1994) and the mixing between waters of different residence times and origins [SOURCE: Page_6]

including rapidly percolated water, fissure water and matrix pore water (Gillon et al. 2010). The [SOURCE: Page_6]

mixing process depends on water level fluctuation (Brouyère et al., 2004; Hakoun et al., 2017), [SOURCE: Page_6]

structure of the superficial formation (Barhoum et al., 2014; El Janyani et al., 2014; Valdes et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

2014), climate conditions and topography. [SOURCE: Page_6]

A better understanding of the mixing process and flow mechanisms within aquifers could be achieved [SOURCE: Page_6]

by groundwater dating using natural or anthropogenic age tracers (Cook et al., 1995; Darling et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

2012a; Jaunat et al., 2012; Santoni et al., 2016; Suckow, 2014). In case of young groundwater with [SOURCE: Page_6]

residence times ranging from 0 to 60 years, atmospheric trace gases such as CFCs [SOURCE: Page_6]

(chlorofluorocarbons) and SF$_6$ (sulphur hexafluoride) could be used (e.g., Aeschbach‐Hertig et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

1999; Busenberg and Plummer, 1992; Cook and Solomon, 1995; Dunkle et al., 1993; Plummer et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

2001; Vergnaud-Ayraud et al., 2008). An average age and a distribution of residence times of [SOURCE: Page_6]

groundwater can be estimated considering various mixing models (the most commonly used are the [SOURCE: Page_6]

piston flow, the binary or exponential mixing), providing information on aquifer functioning (Appelo [SOURCE: Page_6]

and Postma, 2004; Cook et al., 2005; Małoszewski and Zuber, 1982). To date, only a few studies have [SOURCE: Page_6]

been undertaken using CFCs and/or SF$_6$ to understand groundwater flow in Chalk aquifers (Cary et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

2014; Darling et al., 2012b, 2005; Gooddy et al., 2006). Furthermore, no studies have combined [SOURCE: Page_6]

geological characterization, hydrodynamic conditions (water level fluctuations and recharge process), [SOURCE: Page_6]

residence time determination and groundwater geochemistry within a Chalk aquifer. A methodology [SOURCE: Page_6]

that combines these different approaches could provide a comprehensive understanding of the Chalk [SOURCE: Page_6]

aquifer functioning. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Indeed, spatial and temporal heterogeneities of hydrogeological and geochemical properties could [SOURCE: Page_6]

exist, resulting in a complex aquifer system even on a small scale. The aim of this work is to establish [SOURCE: Page_6]

a conceptual model of the unconfined Chalk aquifer on a water catchment scale in terms of [SOURCE: Page_6]

4 [SOURCE: Page_6]

groundwater flow paths, mixings and geochemistry for a better understanding of the hydrogeological [SOURCE: Page_7]

functioning of the aquifer. [SOURCE: Page_7]

2.1    Site description [SOURCE: Page_7]

The study area is located at the East of the city of Reims in the Champagne region (NE of France; [SOURCE: Page_7]

Figure 1) and covers approximately 500 km^2 between the Suippe River (as the northern and western [SOURCE: Page_7]

boundary) and the Vesle River (as the southern boundary). Both the two rivers have several tributaries; [SOURCE: Page_7]

the Prosne River (tributary of the Vesle River) is dry for most of the time while others are permanent [SOURCE: Page_7]

watercourses (Figure 1). [SOURCE: Page_7]

The Cretaceous formations outcropping on the surface of the study area are constituted by: Coniacian [SOURCE: Page_7]

(C$_3$), Santonian (C$_4$) and Campanian (C$_5$) Chalks (Figure 1). Despite the different age of formation, [SOURCE: Page_7]

the three Chalks present a similar lithology. The first 10 – 20 meters of the Champagne Chalk are [SOURCE: Page_7]

significantly fractured and the fracturing decreases with depth (Mangeret et al., 2012; Vachier et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

1987). The Chalk formation is partially covered by Tertiary formations (10 – 50 m; Laurain et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

1981) at the Berru Mount and by Quaternary formations including graveluche, colluvium and alluvium [SOURCE: Page_7]

(Figure 1). Graveluche (also called grèze) refers to a periglacial formation with a maximum thickness [SOURCE: Page_7]

of 6 to 10 meters (Rouxel-David et al., 2002b). During the Quaternary, the long period of freeze - thaw [SOURCE: Page_7]

alternation produced movement in the terrain. Graveluches, resulting from this gelification process of [SOURCE: Page_7]

the chalk, are very variable in particle size and composition with more or less content on clay and silt [SOURCE: Page_7]

(Vernhet, 2007). The graveluche formation is more widely spread in the north (the catchment area of [SOURCE: Page_7]

the Suippe River) than in the south of the study area. Colluvium is present mainly in dry valleys, with [SOURCE: Page_7]

a thickness of about 1 to 3 m. They have substantially the same composition as the soils from which [SOURCE: Page_7]

they formed, but a higher silt and clay content. Alluvium deposits are located in river valleys, [SOURCE: Page_7]

composed mainly by sand, clay and gravel (Vernhet, 2007). Their thickness could reach about 10 m in [SOURCE: Page_7]

maximum, with locally accumulated clay layers up to 5 m (Rouxel-David et al., 2002b). [SOURCE: Page_7]

The unconfined Champagne chalk aquifer is a crucial water resource of the region. It is the only [SOURCE: Page_8]

resource used for drinking water and is also largely exploited for agricultural and industrial uses. The [SOURCE: Page_8]

total porosity of the Chalk is about 40% (Crampon et al., 1993), with only 1% related to the effective [SOURCE: Page_8]

porosity (Vachier et al., 1987). The Champagne Mounts constitute the main reliefs where the [SOURCE: Page_8]

transmissivity ranges from 10-6 m²·s-1 to 10-5 m²·s-1, whereas in the valleys these values are much [SOURCE: Page_8]

higher, ranging from 10-2 m²·s-1 to 0.3 m²·s-1 (Rouxel-David et al., 2002b). The study area is divided [SOURCE: Page_8]

into two parts by the groundwater divide line across the summit of Berru Mount and the Champagne [SOURCE: Page_8]

Mounts, which delimits the Vesle River watershed in the south and the Suippe River watershed in the [SOURCE: Page_8]

north. According to water levels, the chalk aquifer of the study area is generally drained by rivers [SOURCE: Page_8]

(Rouxel-David et al., 2002a; Figure^1). The precipitation constitutes the only recharge of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

Champagne chalk aquifer. The climate of the region is temperate oceanic with an average temperature [SOURCE: Page_8]

of 11.2 °C and average annual precipitation of 566 mm (values from MeteoFrance for 2012 - 2018 [SOURCE: Page_8]

period at Reims-Prunay station; Figure 1). The annual effective rainfall ranges generally from 100 to [SOURCE: Page_8]

150 mm (mainly in winter/spring), with the rest of the total precipitation being taken by [SOURCE: Page_8]

evapotranspiration (Baran et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_8]

Land use of the study area can be divided into three major types: 1) agricultural: this type occupies the [SOURCE: Page_8]

majority of the study area with the main crops of wheat, barley, sugar beet and alfalfa (Laurain et al. [SOURCE: Page_8]

1981); 2) forest: this is the second major type of land use which covers in particular the Berru and [SOURCE: Page_8]

Champagne Mounts, representing about 15% of the surface of the study area; 3) anthropogenic: this [SOURCE: Page_8]

category mainly includes cities and villages and represents about only 3% of the area of the sector, [SOURCE: Page_8]

with a landfill situated in middle of the study area (Figure 1). [SOURCE: Page_8]

2.3   Estimation of recharge temperature, excess air and apparent age [SOURCE: Page_11]

The concentrations of noble gases were used to estimate recharge temperature and excess air [SOURCE: Page_11]

contribution (Busenberg and Plummer, 1992; Heaton and Vogel, 1981). The estimation of the recharge [SOURCE: Page_11]

temperature, defined as the temperature in the UZ above the water table where air-water equilibrium is [SOURCE: Page_11]

carried out (Dunkle et al., 1993), is essential for determining the solubility of CFCs and SF$_6$ in Henry’s [SOURCE: Page_11]

law. Excess air refers to dissolution of air bubbles trapped by infiltrating water in the UZ (Heaton and [SOURCE: Page_11]

Vogel, 1981). It is used to correct SF$_6$ data, which could be greatly influenced by this parameter in [SOURCE: Page_11]

contrast to CFCs (Ayraud et al., 2008; Plummer et al., 2001; Vittecoq et al., 2007). The measured [SOURCE: Page_11]

CFCs and SF$_6$ concentrations in water samples (in pmol·L-1) were converted to atmospheric partial [SOURCE: Page_11]

pressures (in pptv) according to the estimated recharge temperature and then compared to the [SOURCE: Page_11]

atmospheric        evolution        curve        (available       on       USGS           web       site: [SOURCE: Page_11]

https://water.usgs.gov/lab/software/air_curve/index.html) to determine the apparent age. [SOURCE: Page_11]

Groundwater is usually a contribution of water from different flow pathways according to aquifer [SOURCE: Page_11]

properties. As a result, conceptual mixing models have to be used to interpret CFCs and SF$_6$ [SOURCE: Page_11]

concentration as groundwater apparent ages. Three models are used in this study for the calculation of [SOURCE: Page_11]

apparent ages: piston flow model (PFM), exponential mixing model (EMM) and binary mixing model [SOURCE: Page_11]

(BMM). The piston flow model assumes that a tracer travels from the inlet position (recharge area) to [SOURCE: Page_11]

the outlet position (e.g., a well or spring) without any dispersion or mixing (Jurgens et al., 2012; [SOURCE: Page_11]

Etcheverry, 2002; Małoszewski and Zuber, 1982). The exponential mixing model describes an [SOURCE: Page_11]

exponential distribution of transit times, with a transit time of zero for the shortest flow line and a [SOURCE: Page_11]

transit time of infinity for the longest line. It is applicable for unconfined aquifers of constant [SOURCE: Page_11]

thickness with uniform areal recharge (Cook et al., 2006). The apparent age calculated from EMM are [SOURCE: Page_11]

expressed by the mean residence time (MRT), which corresponds to the transit time that is longer than [SOURCE: Page_11]

9 [SOURCE: Page_11]

that of 2/3 of the flow lines. Binary mixing model considers a mixing of young and old water masses, [SOURCE: Page_12]

which represents the simplest mixing process. The proportion of the two components and the apparent [SOURCE: Page_12]

age of one of the two components can be calculated. This model can be appropriate for aquifers with [SOURCE: Page_12]

short-circuit pathways, karstic aquifers or watershed with transmissivity contrasts (Jurgens et al., Katz [SOURCE: Page_12]

et al., 2009; Michel, 2004). [SOURCE: Page_12]

3.1    Groundwater level fluctuation [SOURCE: Page_12]

Chalk groundwater level showed seasonal variations (Figure 2). The highest water level was reached [SOURCE: Page_12]

in March/April following the winter/spring recharge, due to excess of rainfall compared to [SOURCE: Page_12]

evapotranspiration. Then the water level declined due to evapotranspiration excess and drainage by [SOURCE: Page_12]

rivers. It reached the lowest level in October/November (Figure 2). Annual variations were also [SOURCE: Page_12]

observed. The water level in March/April 2018 was much higher than that of the same period in 2017 [SOURCE: Page_12]

and 2019, as the winter of 2017 and the spring of 2018 were extremely wet with intensive precipitation. [SOURCE: Page_12]

The water level during high water season of 2018 was the highest since the last ten years, as [SOURCE: Page_12]

demonstrated by piezometric chronicles of two boreholes in Chalk aquifer near the study area [SOURCE: Page_12]

monitored since 2008 (ADES database, 2019; SI 1). [SOURCE: Page_12]

Seasonal water level fluctuations were highly variable on the study area (Table 2). The changes in [SOURCE: Page_12]

water level between high and low water conditions in 2018 were down to < 5 m near river valley (FAP) [SOURCE: Page_12]

but could reach up to 15 m on interfluve (FBN$_1$, FBN$_4$ and FBN$_5$), with the thickness of UZ varying [SOURCE: Page_12]

from 2 m (FAP) to 26 m (FBN$_1$) (Table 2). The rise of water level was not simultaneous in different [SOURCE: Page_12]

boreholes (Figure 2). In 2018, 4 boreholes (FBN$_4$, FEP$_1$, FP$_1$ and FAP) reached their maximum levels [SOURCE: Page_12]

in April, 4 boreholes (FA, FBN$_5$, FVDV and FPM$_3$) reached their maximum levels in March and 1 [SOURCE: Page_12]

borehole (FBN$_1$) reached its maximum level in February. Moreover, the rise of water level during [SOURCE: Page_12]

recharge period was more rapid than the decline of water level during discharge period. This [SOURCE: Page_12]

asymmetry was less obvious for boreholes near river valleys (FAP, FP$_1$; Figure 2). [SOURCE: Page_12]

3.3.2   Groundwater apparent ages [SOURCE: Page_20]

The measured CFCs and SF$_6$ concentrations in water samples (in pmol·L-1) were transferred to [SOURCE: Page_20]

atmospheric partial pressures (in pptv) according to Henry’s law. The data was rejected when [SOURCE: Page_20]

calculated concentrations were greater than atmospheric peak concentrations (marked as [SOURCE: Page_20]

contamination; Table 4). [SOURCE: Page_20]

A frequent contamination of tracer gases was observed on the study area, especially for CFC-12. [SOURCE: Page_20]

Commonly recognized sources of CFCs contamination include seepage from septic tanks, landfills [SOURCE: Page_20]

(Kjeldsen and Christophersen, 2001), release from polyurethane foam waste (Kjeldsen and Jensen, [SOURCE: Page_20]

2001), leaky sewer lines, leakage from underground storage tanks, infiltration or disposal of industrial [SOURCE: Page_20]

wastes and recharge from rivers contaminated with CFCs (Cook et al., 2006). CFCs contamination [SOURCE: Page_20]

could also result from agricultural activities. Agricultural application of pesticides may introduce [SOURCE: Page_20]

CFCs, particularly CFC-11 to unsaturated air, as CFCs are allowed as inert ingredients in pesticide [SOURCE: Page_20]

formulations (Plummer et al., 2000). In addition, CFCs can sorb into rubber and polymers; therefore [SOURCE: Page_20]

water samples can also be contaminated with CFCs from contact with these materials such as [SOURCE: Page_20]

permanently installed submersible pumps in wells with rubber parts (Cook et al., 2006; Dunkle et al., [SOURCE: Page_20]

1993). Although most SF$_6$ concentrations did not exceed atmospheric peak concentrations, apparent [SOURCE: Page_20]

ages estimated by SF$_6$ were generally much younger than all the other tracers, suggesting some [SOURCE: Page_20]

anthropogenic and/or natural addition of SF$_6$. Anthropogenic sources of SF$_6$ include high-voltage [SOURCE: Page_20]

electricity supply equipment, Mg and Al melting and landfills (Darling et al., 2012a; Santella et al., [SOURCE: Page_20]

2008). Unlike CFCs, which come exclusively from anthropogenic sources, SF$_6$ can also be present [SOURCE: Page_20]

naturally in rocks. The major known source of terrigenous SF$_6$ is found in fluorite and granite; [SOURCE: Page_20]

however, significant concentrations of SF$_6$ is also reported in other mineral and rock types such as [SOURCE: Page_20]

hydrothermal mineral deposits, halite and dolomite (Busenberg and Plummer, 2000; Harnisch and [SOURCE: Page_20]

Eisenhauer, 1998). The natural presence of SF$_6$ in Chalk cannot be excluded, as dolomite is commonly [SOURCE: Page_20]

present in Chalk as impurity (Barhoum et al., 2014; Gillon et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_20]

The studied aquifer is unconfined with fractures, making the soil and groundwater very vulnerable to [SOURCE: Page_20]

contamination. The landfill site in middle of the study area (Figure 1) appears to be a major source of [SOURCE: Page_20]

18 [SOURCE: Page_20]

contamination of CFCs and SF$_6$. A small waste deposit site located near the borehole FBN$_1$ (Figure 1) [SOURCE: Page_21]

4.1    Hydrodynamic characteristics [SOURCE: Page_22]

The unconfined Chalk aquifer of the study area possesses highly heterogeneous hydrological [SOURCE: Page_22]

characteristics. A higher variation in water levels was observed on interfluves than in river valleys [SOURCE: Page_22]

(Figure 2) attesting differences in transmissivity and storage yield. In river valleys, high density of [SOURCE: Page_22]

active fractures are consistently developed, resulting in high permeability of the aquifer (Haria et al., [SOURCE: Page_22]

2003; Ineson, 1962; Price, 1987; Price et al., 1993). Water is discharged quickly through the fractures [SOURCE: Page_22]

toward the valleys at times of high recharge, thus not allowing the water levels to rise significantly [SOURCE: Page_22]

(Allen et al., 1997; Whitehead and Lawrence, 2006). Conversely, on interfluves, the low permeability [SOURCE: Page_22]

and storage yield make the Chalk aquifer more sensitive to recharge and discharge, resulting in greater [SOURCE: Page_22]

and faster water level fluctuations between low water and high water periods (Allen et al., 1997). In [SOURCE: Page_22]

general, groundwater reached the maximum level earlier on interfluves than in river valleys, which is [SOURCE: Page_22]

reasonable as on interfluves the distance to the groundwater divide line is closer and the time of lateral [SOURCE: Page_22]

flow from the recharge zones towards the borehole is shorter. [SOURCE: Page_22]

Rapid drawdown of groundwater level observed at FBN$_1$ and FBN$_5$ implied a low productivity of [SOURCE: Page_22]

these two boreholes, which could be explained by the thick UZ (>20 m) and low permeability of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

aquifer. The Chalk aquifer of the study area exhibits a very inertial behavior with respect to recharge, [SOURCE: Page_22]

as evidenced by the slow and progressive temporal variations of the water levels and high stability of [SOURCE: Page_22]

water temperature (standard deviations < 0.1°C; Table 2). No impact of individual rainfall events was [SOURCE: Page_22]

20 [SOURCE: Page_22]

observed, suggesting a dominant recharge through the Chalk matrix, as demonstrated by numerous [SOURCE: Page_23]

studies in Chalk aquifers (e.g., Barhoum, 2014; Haria et al., 2003; Ireson et al., 2006; Mathias et al., [SOURCE: Page_23]

2006; Smith et al., 1970; Wellings and Bell, 1980). However, differences are observed at FAP. Sharp [SOURCE: Page_23]

increases in water level after rainfall events were observed, followed by equally rapid declines. Figure [SOURCE: Page_23]

3 shows an example of this trend in July 2018. Water level started to increase about 1h after the [SOURCE: Page_23]

rainfall event and reached its maximum value about 4h later (85.45 to 85.67 m NGF). Similar rapid [SOURCE: Page_23]

responses within 24h of rainfall were observed in a few previous investigations (Allen et al., 1997; Lee [SOURCE: Page_23]

et al., 2006). Allen et al. (1997) suggested that this would be expected only if the large fractures had [SOURCE: Page_23]

been replenished (rapid transfer initiated) and subsequently drained during recharge period. At FAP, [SOURCE: Page_23]

hourly temperature and EC were also measured and compared with rainfall time series. Water [SOURCE: Page_23]

temperature increased about 2h after the rainfall and reached its maximum value about 7h later (11.6 [SOURCE: Page_23]

to 12.2°C). EC decreased about 2h after precipitation and reached the minimum value about 9h later [SOURCE: Page_23]

(625 to 460 µS.cm-1). In July (summer), the increase in temperature may be easily explained by a [SOURCE: Page_23]

higher atmospheric temperature in July compared with groundwater temperature. EC decreased due to [SOURCE: Page_23]

the dilution by rainwater. The variation of temperature and EC combining with that of water level [SOURCE: Page_23]

confirms that the aquifer at FAP testifies to rapid transfers via a well-developed fracturing network [SOURCE: Page_23]

thus exhibits no longer an inertial behavior. Note that the response of water level was faster than that [SOURCE: Page_23]

of temperature and EC, as the water pressure transfer process was faster than the mixing between [SOURCE: Page_23]

rainwater and groundwater. Rapid flows through fractures are added to matrix flows that are still [SOURCE: Page_23]

effective as evidenced by the evolution of groundwater levels on an annual scale (Figure 2). [SOURCE: Page_23]

Excess air in groundwater provides also useful information on flow pathways and recharge processes. [SOURCE: Page_23]

Higher excess air values were measured in groundwater at FP$_1$, FA, PP and FBN$_4$. These 4 boreholes [SOURCE: Page_23]

have a thin UZ (less than 5 m in 2018 high water period; Table 2). The water level at pumping station [SOURCE: Page_23]

PP is not measured, but historical data showed a UZ thickness of about 5 m (data source: [SOURCE: Page_23]

InfoTerre.brgm.fr). It is suggested that at these 4 shallow groundwater sites, recharge by preferential [SOURCE: Page_23]

fracture flow is favored with more fractures developed near the surface, while the other deeper [SOURCE: Page_23]

groundwater sites are dominated by matrix flow as fracturing of the chalk decreases with depth (Haria [SOURCE: Page_23]

et al., 2003; Mathias et al., 2006). The preferential flow is guaranteed by water-filled small fractures [SOURCE: Page_24]

and the capillary channels above the water table, resulting in a subsequent rapid rise of water table [SOURCE: Page_24]

samples collected at these points. Note that the borehole FAP also has a water table depth of less than [SOURCE: Page_24]

5 m. However, the excess air values in water were relatively low, similar to the mean value of the [SOURCE: Page_24]

study (SI 2). This is consistent with the very developed fracturing network at FAP. Large fractures are [SOURCE: Page_24]

less favorable for the air entrapment, resulting in relatively low excess air values than other shallow [SOURCE: Page_24]

4.2   Origin of major ions [SOURCE: Page_24]

The correlation matrix of major ions (details of the calculation method and results can be found in SI 4 [SOURCE: Page_24]

Also, the correlation is observed between Mg$_2$+, K+ and SO42- ions (SI 5). These correlation relations [SOURCE: Page_24]

impurities and clay minerals in superficial formations (mainly colluvium and alluvium in the study [SOURCE: Page_25]

UZ thickness (< 10m; Table 2). Extremely high major ion concentrations at FBN$_4$ were supposed to [SOURCE: Page_26]

be related to the landfill located upstream, representing an important point source of pollution. FP$_1$ [SOURCE: Page_26]

and FAP have a similar chemical composition with high concentrations on Na+, Mg$_2$+, SO42- and K+ [SOURCE: Page_26]

4.3.2   Temporal variations [SOURCE: Page_27]

The temporal variation of geochemistry was explored by analyzing the EC chronicle compared with [SOURCE: Page_27]

groundwater level time series (Figure 6). A combination of hydrogeological setting, residence time [SOURCE: Page_27]

and land use information was used to explain the temporal variations of Chalk geochemistry on the [SOURCE: Page_27]

study area. [SOURCE: Page_27]

   Area with large fluctuation in water level [SOURCE: Page_27]

Large fluctuation in water level was observed at FBN$_1$ and FBN$_5$ (> 12 m; Table 2). EC time series [SOURCE: Page_27]

were highly correlated with water level fluctuations (Figure 6 and Table 5). During high water level [SOURCE: Page_27]

period in 2018, water level increased substantially following intensive recharge, resulting in an [SOURCE: Page_27]

increase in EC of about 150 and 300 µs∙cm-1 at FBN1and FBN$_5$ respectively. However, no significant [SOURCE: Page_27]

variation of EC was observed during high water level period in 2019, as water level was much lower [SOURCE: Page_27]

under dry climate conditions. According to groundwater dating, the groundwater flow was described [SOURCE: Page_27]

by the exponential mixing model and a MRT of 18 years was estimated at FBN$_1$ (no accurate age was [SOURCE: Page_27]

deduced for FBN$_5$), indicating a spatially uniform recharge and a mixture of different flows. In this [SOURCE: Page_27]

context, contaminants concentrated near the land surface area infiltrated progressively across the UZ [SOURCE: Page_27]

following recharge. Under low water level conditions, the contamination front was disconnected with [SOURCE: Page_27]

the SZ. It transferred slowly downward through the unsaturated Chalk matrix. Meanwhile, solutes in [SOURCE: Page_27]

the SZ were diluted by the mixing process of groundwater, so that a low EC was observed. As water [SOURCE: Page_27]

level rose and reached the contamination front, the contamination source was re-activated and [SOURCE: Page_27]

contaminants concentrations increased rapidly in the SZ so that a high EC was observed. Similar [SOURCE: Page_27]

pollutant remobilization processes were previously suggested by Brouyère et al. (2004), Hakoun et al. [SOURCE: Page_27]

(2017) and Orban et al. (2010). The highly correlation between time series of NO$_3$-, Cl-, SO42-, Ca$_2$+ [SOURCE: Page_27]

concentrations and water level fluctuation (Table 5) confirms this mechanism. [SOURCE: Page_27]

At FBN$_4$, a high correlation between EC and large water level fluctuation (15.5m; Table 2) was also [SOURCE: Page_27]

observed in a context of exponential mixing (MRT = 17 years; Table 4). However, the temporal [SOURCE: Page_27]

variation of EC followed that of water level during the whole monitoring period (2017-2019) while [SOURCE: Page_27]

EC remained stable at low water levels at FBN$_1$ and FBN$_5$. This could be explained by the thin UZ at [SOURCE: Page_27]

25 [SOURCE: Page_27]

FBN$_4$ (2.5m; Table 2), whereby the contaminated area was always partially submerged by [SOURCE: Page_28]

groundwater level even during low water level period. [SOURCE: Page_28]

At FVDV, the water level fluctuation was smaller (8 m; Table 2), resulting in a lower correlation [SOURCE: Page_28]

between EC and water level times series (r = 0.69; Table 5) compared to FBN$_1$, FBN$_4$ and FBN$_5$. [SOURCE: Page_28]

Groundwater consisted mainly of 30-year-old water mixed with 25-30% of modern water, suggesting [SOURCE: Page_28]

small contributions of rapid recharge through fractures. [SOURCE: Page_28]

   Area with developed fracture network and low UZ thickness [SOURCE: Page_28]

EC time series were inversely related to water level fluctuation at FAP and FA (Figure 3 and Figure 6), [SOURCE: Page_28]

suggesting that the dilution effect was the most important factor that influences the geochemistry. At [SOURCE: Page_28]

these two sites, the UZ thickness was low (< 3 m; Table 2) and fractures were supposed to be [SOURCE: Page_28]

developed near surface area. Groundwater dating showed 50-70% and 75-80% of modern water at [SOURCE: Page_28]

FAP and FA respectively by the binary mixing model (Table 4), indicating an aquifer constituted [SOURCE: Page_28]

mainly by freshly percolated rainwater that favors the process of dilution. Larger proportion of modern [SOURCE: Page_28]

water at FA could be explained by its location close to groundwater divide line, receiving few lateral [SOURCE: Page_28]

flow. It should be noted that, even during high water level period, the groundwater at FAP and FA was [SOURCE: Page_28]

composed of 20% to 50% of old water aged over 30 years. This older end-member is linked to the [SOURCE: Page_28]

lateral flow originated from upstream recharge areas. [SOURCE: Page_28]

   Area with superficial formation [SOURCE: Page_28]

EC values were stable in time at FPM$_1$ and FEP$_1$ (Figure 6). Groundwater dating showed a residence [SOURCE: Page_28]

time of > 30 years at FEP$_1$ in a piston flow context, indicating a small recharge area away from the [SOURCE: Page_28]

site (no accurate age was deduced for FPM$_1$; Table 4). These two wells are situated in the north of the [SOURCE: Page_28]

study area, where large surfaces of the Chalk are covered by graveluche (Figure 1). The graveluche [SOURCE: Page_28]

layers rich in clayey minerals are less permeable with an infiltration rate about 3 to 4 times slower than [SOURCE: Page_28]

in the Chalk, therefore could constitute a barrier or buffer zone which limits rapid transport of water [SOURCE: Page_28]

and solute from surface to the SZ (Linoir, 2014). Consequently, the aquifer received recharge mainly [SOURCE: Page_28]

from upstream the graveluche covered area (near the Champagne mounts; Figure 1). Water traveled [SOURCE: Page_28]

26 [SOURCE: Page_28]

laterally in the SZ during more than 30 years, resulting in a stable geochemistry independent on water [SOURCE: Page_29]

level fluctuation. At FPM$_3$ (downstream the borehole FPM$_1$; Figure 1), a time lag was observed [SOURCE: Page_29]

between EC and water level time series, as a result of delayed solute transfer by the discontinuous [SOURCE: Page_29]

graveluche formations. At PP, EC was also stable with a residence time of about 40 years estimated by [SOURCE: Page_29]

a piston flow model (Table 4). With a high proportion of clayey minerals, the alluvium can play the [SOURCE: Page_29]

same role as graveluche as a flow barrier on the Chalk. Moreover, the deep screen position of PP (23- [SOURCE: Page_29]

(e.g., Barhoum et al. 2014; El Janyani et al. 2012) have shown that other superficial formations such as [SOURCE: Page_29]

flint clay layers can have similar impacts on Chalk aquifers. [SOURCE: Page_29]

   Area along riverside [SOURCE: Page_29]

4.4   A conceptual model of aquifer functioning [SOURCE: Page_31]

A conceptual model of unconfined Chalk aquifer (Figure 7) was established in order to explore how [SOURCE: Page_31]

hydrogeological settings including fracture distribution, groundwater level depth, superficial [SOURCE: Page_31]

formations and aquifer-river relationship can influence the hydrogeological functioning of the aquifer [SOURCE: Page_31]

in terms of flow pathways, mixing process, residence time and groundwater geochemistry variation. [SOURCE: Page_31]

In general, under deep groundwater level conditions, the geochemistry of the aquifer tends to be less [SOURCE: Page_31]

influenced by near surface contaminations with relatively low concentrations of agriculture ions. This [SOURCE: Page_31]

situation usually occurs on interfluves, near the groundwater divide line (Figure 7A). If no superficial [SOURCE: Page_31]

formations overlay on the Chalk, the aquifer receives a spatially uniform recharge and an exponential [SOURCE: Page_31]

mixing model could be applied with a relatively short residence time. As transmissivity and storage [SOURCE: Page_31]

yield are low on interfluve with a thick UZ, groundwater level fluctuations are important, leading to [SOURCE: Page_31]

levels of contamination positively correlated to water table dynamics. [SOURCE: Page_31]

At shallow groundwater sites, the thin UZ of Chalk aquifer is usually highly fractured (Figure 7B). [SOURCE: Page_31]

Rapid recharge through fractures is favored, making groundwater a mixture of freshly percolated [SOURCE: Page_31]

water and old water coming from upstream recharge area, as demonstrated by a binary mixing model. [SOURCE: Page_31]

The shallow groundwater sites with high proportion of modern water mainly locate in river valleys [SOURCE: Page_31]

(where fracture networks are well developed) but could also present on interfluves. At these sites, [SOURCE: Page_31]

groundwater geochemistry is mostly influenced by the dilution effects of rainfall, leading to [SOURCE: Page_31]

mineralization degree of groundwater negatively correlated to water table dynamics. [SOURCE: Page_31]

Superficial formations overlaying on the Chalk such as graveluche, colluvium and alluvium have [SOURCE: Page_31]

important effects on groundwater flow and geochemistry. These superficial formations are usually [SOURCE: Page_31]

intermitted and constituted by high content of clayey minerals, presenting a lower permeability than [SOURCE: Page_31]

the Chalk. They could consequently constitute a barrier limiting the rapid recharge of water and solute [SOURCE: Page_31]

into the aquifer. At these sites, a piston flow model better describes the groundwater flow and [SOURCE: Page_31]

relatively old groundwater ages are estimated, which could explain the stable or delayed groundwater [SOURCE: Page_31]

geochemistry with respect to water table fluctuations (Figure 7C and D). [SOURCE: Page_31]

water should be considered (Figure 7E). The recharge of aquifer by rivers could occur, especially [SOURCE: Page_32]

during low water level period, leading to groundwater geochemistry influenced by surface water. [SOURCE: Page_32]

5    Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_32]

The present study brings out interesting implications for the hydrogeological functioning of [SOURCE: Page_32]

unconfined Chalk aquifers, by an approach combining hydrodynamic, geochemical and groundwater [SOURCE: Page_32]

dating tools. Based on an intensive sampling network on a watershed scale, groundwater level [SOURCE: Page_32]

fluctuations and chemical compositions (major ions concentrations) were monitored continuously [SOURCE: Page_32]

during two years (2017-2019). [SOURCE: Page_32]

Seasonal and annual fluctuations of groundwater level were observed on the study area. The hourly [SOURCE: Page_32]

data allowed to study precisely the response of aquifer following exploitation activities (pumping) and [SOURCE: Page_32]

rainfalls. Although the majority of the aquifer has an inertial behavior with respect to recharge, a rapid [SOURCE: Page_32]

response following rainfalls can be allowed locally by highly developed fractured network in the [SOURCE: Page_32]

Chalk. [SOURCE: Page_32]

30 [SOURCE: Page_32]

Groundwater dating using CFCs and SF$_6$ was also realized for the first time in this unconfined Chalk [SOURCE: Page_33]

aquifer. Despite the frequent contaminations of CFCs and SF$_6$ detected on the study area, relevant [SOURCE: Page_33]

information on residence times and mixing processes of the Chalk groundwater was obtained at the [SOURCE: Page_33]

majority of sites. The aquifer is made up of waters of different ages ranging from less than 20 years to [SOURCE: Page_33]

more than 50 years, with piston flow, exponential or binary mixing models defined at each sampling [SOURCE: Page_33]

site implying for different flow pathways and mixing processes in the aquifer. [SOURCE: Page_33]

Despite the Ca$_2$+ - HCO$_3$- chemical facies, a high spatial and temporal heterogeneity of groundwater [SOURCE: Page_33]

geochemistry in the unconfined Chalk aquifer was highlighted on the study area. Different correlation [SOURCE: Page_33]

relationships between the EC time series and groundwater level fluctuations were observed: correlated, [SOURCE: Page_33]

anti-correlated or independent. These variabilities were explained by a combined effect of water level [SOURCE: Page_33]

fluctuation, groundwater residence time, thickness of the UZ, superficial formations, distribution of [SOURCE: Page_33]

fracturing network, aquifer-river relationships and human activities. [SOURCE: Page_33]

The combination of different tools allowed the establishment of a conceptual model of chalk aquifer [SOURCE: Page_33]

functioning. The multi-tool methodology developed in this study and the established conceptual model [SOURCE: Page_33]

might be used in Chalk aquifers or other multi-porosity mediums for the prediction of spatial and [SOURCE: Page_33]

temporal variations of groundwater mineralization and solutes transport (natural or anthropogenic) and [SOURCE: Page_33]

for the evaluation of aquifer vulnerability. However, the used methods still have some limitations. [SOURCE: Page_33]

Firstly, the two-year long time series of groundwater level and geochemical parameters are relatively [SOURCE: Page_33]

short to interpret a general and comprehensive mechanism of aquifer functioning. Then, the age-dating [SOURCE: Page_33]

was disturbed by the contamination of tracers, resulting in the missing of groundwater flow [SOURCE: Page_33]

information at some sites. Also, the methods focus mostly on a qualitative rather than a quantitative [SOURCE: Page_33]

perspective, which could limit its application on contaminant transport prediction. Therefore, further [SOURCE: Page_33]

investigations would be necessary. The monitoring of groundwater level fluctuations and [SOURCE: Page_33]

geochemistry can be continued to obtain longer times series, with the aim to study the influence of [SOURCE: Page_33]

different climate conditions on the Chalk aquifer functioning in a climate change context. With respect [SOURCE: Page_33]

to groundwater dating, tritium analysis could be a good complement for CFCs and SF$_6$, especially for [SOURCE: Page_33]

31 [SOURCE: Page_33]

sites contaminated by several tracers. At last, the hydrodynamic and geochemical numerical modeling [SOURCE: Page_34]

tool, based on the conceptual model established in the study, could be a relevant complement. [SOURCE: Page_34]
