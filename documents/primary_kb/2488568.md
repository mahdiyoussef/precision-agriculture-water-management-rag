## ABSTRACT

Monitoring irrigation is essential for an efficient management of water resources in arid [SOURCE: Page_2]

and semi-arid regions. We propose to estimate the timing and the amount of irrigation [SOURCE: Page_2]

throughout the agricultural season using optical and thermal Landsat-7/8 data. The [SOURCE: Page_2]

approach is implemented in four steps: i) partitioning the Landsat land surface [SOURCE: Page_2]

temperature (LST) to derive the crop water stress coefficient (Ks), ii) estimating the [SOURCE: Page_2]

daily root zone soil moisture (RZSM) from the integration of Landsat-derived Ks into a [SOURCE: Page_2]

crop water balance model, iii) retrieving irrigation at the Landsat pixel scale and iv) [SOURCE: Page_2]

aggregating pixel-scale irrigation estimates at the crop field scale. The new irrigation [SOURCE: Page_2]

retrieval method is tested over three agricultural areas during four seasons and is [SOURCE: Page_2]

evaluated over five winter wheat fields under different irrigation techniques (drip, [SOURCE: Page_2]

flood and no-irrigation). The model is very accurate for the seasonal accumulated [SOURCE: Page_2]

amounts (R ~ 0.95 and RMSE ~ 44 mm). However, lower agreements with observed [SOURCE: Page_2]

irrigations are obtained at the daily scale. To assess the performance of the irrigation [SOURCE: Page_2]

retrieval method over a range of time periods, the daily predicted and observed [SOURCE: Page_2]

1 [SOURCE: Page_2]

irrigations are cumulated from 1 to 90 days. Generally, acceptable errors (R = 0.52 and [SOURCE: Page_3]

RMSE = 27 mm) are obtained for irrigations cumulated over 15 days and the [SOURCE: Page_3]

performance gradually improves by increasing the accumulation period, depicting a [SOURCE: Page_3]

strong link to the frequency of Landsat overpasses (16 days or 8 days by combining [SOURCE: Page_3]

Landsat-7 and -8). Despite the uncertainties in retrieved irrigations at daily to weekly [SOURCE: Page_3]

scales, the daily RZSM and evapotranspiration simulated from the retrieved daily [SOURCE: Page_3]

irrigations are estimated accurately and are very close to those estimated from actual [SOURCE: Page_3]

irrigations. This research demonstrates the utility of high spatial resolution optical and [SOURCE: Page_3]

thermal data for estimating irrigation and consequently for better closing the water [SOURCE: Page_3]

budget over agricultural areas. We also show that significant improvements can be [SOURCE: Page_3]

expected at daily to weekly time scales by reducing the revisit time of high-spatial [SOURCE: Page_3]

resolution thermal data, as included in the TRISHNA future mission requirements. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Keywords: Irrigation, Land surface temperature, FAO-56 model, Landsat, Root-zone [SOURCE: Page_3]

soil moisture, Evapotranspiration. [SOURCE: Page_3]

4    1   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

5    Irrigated agriculture consumes > 70% of freshwater at global scale (Foley et al., 2011) and [SOURCE: Page_3]

6    > 80% in semi-arid and arid regions (Chehbouni et al., 2008; Garrido et al., 2010). The [SOURCE: Page_3]

7    water scarcity issue is particularly acute in the Mediterranean, which is and will continue [SOURCE: Page_3]

8    to be a hot spot of climate change with an observed trend towards warmer conditions and [SOURCE: Page_3]

9    a greater irregularity in seasonal and annual precipitations (Giorgi, 2006; IPCC, 2013). [SOURCE: Page_3]

10   Increasing the water use efficiency in agriculture is essential for the sustainability of [SOURCE: Page_3]

11   water resources and hence has been identified as one key topic related to water scarcity [SOURCE: Page_3]

12   and droughts (Werner et al., 2012). Despite the important pressure of agriculture on [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

13   water resources, information on the amount of irrigated water is often unavailable. [SOURCE: Page_4]

14   Therefore, monitoring and quantifying irrigation over extended areas is critical for an [SOURCE: Page_4]

15   efficient management of water resources. [SOURCE: Page_4]

16 [SOURCE: Page_4]

17   In an attempt to estimate the irrigation volumes from remote sensing data, some recent [SOURCE: Page_4]

18   studies have explored the utility of surface soil moisture estimates from micro-wave [SOURCE: Page_4]

19   sensors (Brocca et al., 2018, 2017; Escorihuela and Quintana-Seguí, 2016; Jalilvand et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

20   2019; Kumar et al., 2015; Lawston et al., 2017; Malbéteau et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_4]

21   In particular, Brocca et al. (2018) developed an approach to quantify the irrigation [SOURCE: Page_4]

22   amounts by combining the currently available coarse resolution satellite soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_4]

23   products (e.g. SMAP, SMOS, ASCAT, AMSR-2) and a soil water balance. This work was [SOURCE: Page_4]

24   applied over various semi-arid and semi-humid regions worldwide but could not be [SOURCE: Page_4]

25   quantitatively assessed due to the unavailability of reliable in situ observations of [SOURCE: Page_4]

26   irrigation over corresponding irrigated perimeters. However, this approach was [SOURCE: Page_4]

27   quantitatively assessed at ~50 km resolution over a semi-arid region (Jalilvand et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

28   2019). Some deficiencies were obtained over periods with sustained rainfalls and the [SOURCE: Page_4]

29   method was not implemented in winter because the method fails in correctly separating [SOURCE: Page_4]

30   irrigation from precipitation (Brocca et al., 2018). This makes the approach unsuitable for [SOURCE: Page_4]

31   winter crops, which are especially important in the Mediterranean. Nevertheless, the [SOURCE: Page_4]

32   ability to quantify monthly irrigations was demonstrated under specific conditions: [SOURCE: Page_4]

33   during prolonged periods of low rainfall and using satellite soil moisture data with a low [SOURCE: Page_4]

34   uncertainty and a frequency higher than 3 days. [SOURCE: Page_4]

35 [SOURCE: Page_4]

36   There are two main issues with the use of microwave-based soil moisture for retrieving [SOURCE: Page_4]

37   irrigation. The first limitation is the very coarse resolution (~40 km) of readily available [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

38   satellite soil moisture data sets. The spatial resolution can be improved to 1 km resolution [SOURCE: Page_5]

39   using disaggregation methods (e.g. Molero et al., 2016; Peng et al., 2017), but this [SOURCE: Page_5]

40   enhanced resolution is still unsuitable for monitoring the water management at the crop [SOURCE: Page_5]

41   field scale, i.e. about 100 m or 1 ha (Anderson et al., 2012). Furthermore, recent methods [SOURCE: Page_5]

42   to obtain soil moisture data at suitable resolution (~100 m) have not reached an [SOURCE: Page_5]

43   operational maturity yet (e.g. Amazirh et al., 2018; Merlin et al., 2013; Peng et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_5]

44   The second limitation is related to the sensing depth (several cm or so) of microwave [SOURCE: Page_5]

45   observations. The dynamics of the top soil moisture is likely to be used to detect irrigation [SOURCE: Page_5]

46   events. However the volume sensed is much smaller than the root zone water storage, [SOURCE: Page_5]

47   which weakens the capability of microwave-based approaches to solve the crop water [SOURCE: Page_5]

48   budget. [SOURCE: Page_5]

49 [SOURCE: Page_5]

50   Alternatively to microwave-based approaches, optical/thermal data have demonstrated [SOURCE: Page_5]

51   to be valuable for monitoring the crop water requirements by means of [SOURCE: Page_5]

52   evapotranspiration (ET) estimates (Gowda et al., 2008; Kalma et al., 2008; Li et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_5]

53   Thermal data have the advantage over microwave data of providing information on the [SOURCE: Page_5]

54   vegetation water status, even within individual fields, in order to improve the water use [SOURCE: Page_5]

55   efficiency (Anderson et al., 2012). In this vein, different methods have been developed in [SOURCE: Page_5]

56   the last decades to estimate ET from LST data (Gowda et al., 2008; Kalma et al., 2008; Li [SOURCE: Page_5]

57   et al., 2009). Despite the large variety of existing approaches to estimate crop water [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   requirements by means of ET estimates, irrigation is generally simulated from the [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   modeled water needs (e.g. Allen et al., 1998; Bastiaanssen et al., 2007; Battude et al., 2017; [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   Corbari et al., 2019; Duchemin et al., 2008). Those models are based either on the water [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   balance or on the coupled energy-water balance, but in both cases, the simulated [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   irrigation may differ considerably from actual irrigation amounts. The reason is that the [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   modeling of soil moisture dynamics and its interaction with the crop consumption [SOURCE: Page_6]

64   through ET is prone to significant uncertainties, especially when no information is [SOURCE: Page_6]

65   available on the actual crop water status over time. Other approaches based on ET [SOURCE: Page_6]

66   estimates from remote sensing surface energy balance (SEB) models (e.g. SEBS, SEBAL, [SOURCE: Page_6]

67   METRIC) have the advantage of estimating the crop water requirement without the [SOURCE: Page_6]

68   calculation of the water balance. This is feasible using daily optical/thermal data. The [SOURCE: Page_6]

69   point is that the remotely sensed variables for operating SEB models at daily scale [SOURCE: Page_6]

70   generally have a spatial resolution of 1 km or more (e.g. Romaguera et al., 2014; van [SOURCE: Page_6]

71   Eekelen et al., 2015), which is unsuitable at crop field scale. When using high-spatial [SOURCE: Page_6]

72   resolution optical/thermal data, the low temporal resolution has to be taken into account. [SOURCE: Page_6]

73   In Droogers et al. (2010), a water balance model was calibrated to minimize the difference [SOURCE: Page_6]

74   between simulated and remotely sensing Landsat-derived ET over an irrigated cotton [SOURCE: Page_6]

75   crop field. The calibration involved adjusting the irrigation amount and a stress threshold [SOURCE: Page_6]

76   below which irrigation is triggered. The stress threshold f^1 was defined as the actual to [SOURCE: Page_6]

77   potential transpiration and ranged from 0.95 to 0.98 in that study. However, due to [SOURCE: Page_6]

78   compensation effects between irrigation amounts and dates, the authors had to further [SOURCE: Page_6]

79   constrain the inverse problem by fixing the irrigation dates during the first half of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

80   season (from March to end of June) and to assume that there is no stress during the second [SOURCE: Page_6]

81   half of the season (from July). Therefore, during the first stage, irrigation events are [SOURCE: Page_6]

82   supposed to be known, while during the second stage, the approach in Droogers et al. [SOURCE: Page_6]

83   (2010) is very similar to the application of the classical FAO-56 model (Allen et al., 1998) [SOURCE: Page_6]

84   that triggers irrigation as soon as the root zone soil moisture gets below 0.95–0.98 times [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   the critical soil moisture below which the crop stress starts. The retrieved irrigation [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   amounts were assessed at the seasonal time scale but, due to the lack of validation data, [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   they were not compared to actual irrigations at shorter time scales. Recently, Corbari et [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

88    al. (2019) developed a system to predict the water needs (irrigation) from the coupling of [SOURCE: Page_7]

89    remote sensing data, soil water-energy hydrological modeling and meteorological [SOURCE: Page_7]

90    forecasts. Landsat-derived vegetation and albedo parameters, as well as land surface [SOURCE: Page_7]

91    temperature (LST) data were used to initialize and calibrate the energy-water balance. [SOURCE: Page_7]

92    However, this approach required observed data of the previous days (especially soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

93    moisture) to simulate the soil moisture and irrigation water needs for up to 3 days, which [SOURCE: Page_7]

94    is not currently possible over large scales because there is no method that allows [SOURCE: Page_7]

95    obtaining operationally soil moisture data at suitable resolution (~100 m). Another [SOURCE: Page_7]

96    approach was proposed by Chen et al. (2018) to detect the timing of irrigation from a [SOURCE: Page_7]

97    vegetation index by using Landsat and MODIS reflectance data. The method was [SOURCE: Page_7]

98    demonstrated to be promising in detecting irrigation events during the first half of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

99    growing season only. Actually, vegetation index presents great fluctuation and is [SOURCE: Page_7]

100   insensitive to water supplement during the second half of the growing season. In addition, [SOURCE: Page_7]

101   the method does not allow retrieving irrigation amounts. [SOURCE: Page_7]

102 [SOURCE: Page_7]

103   Among the thermal-based ET models, the contextual approaches have had an especial [SOURCE: Page_7]

104   interest in the scientific community for its simplicity and operationality over large areas, [SOURCE: Page_7]

105   by estimating ET as a fraction of either potential ET (Moran et al., 1994), or available [SOURCE: Page_7]

106   energy (Long and Singh, 2012; Roerink et al., 2000). The evaporative fraction (EF, defined [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   as the ratio of ET to available energy, i.e, the difference between net radiation and soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   heat flux) can be estimated from the contextual information of remotely sensed optical [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   and thermal images, where dry and wet conditions are identified from the LST – fv (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   Long and Singh, 2012; Moran et al., 1994) space, the LST – albedo (e.g. Roerink et al., 2000) [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   space or even from their combination (Merlin, 2013; Merlin et al., 2014). According to a [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   number of thermal-based methods, LST can be related to the root-zone soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   (RZSM) by means of the canopy temperature and its associated transpiration (Boulet et [SOURCE: Page_8]

114   al., 2007; Hain et al., 2009; Moran et al., 1994). Hence, one key step to estimate thermal- [SOURCE: Page_8]

115   derived RZSM is the partitioning of LST into soil and canopy temperatures (Merlin et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

116   2014, 2012; Moran et al., 1994). In dry and wet regimes where a thermal-based EF (or [SOURCE: Page_8]

117   canopy temperature-based water stress index) is 0 and 1, respectively, LST is no more [SOURCE: Page_8]

118   sensitive to RZSM. LST is hence useful only in a transitional regime where RZSM is [SOURCE: Page_8]

119   strongly related to LST. In the transitional regime, the soil moisture ranges between a [SOURCE: Page_8]

120   given critical soil moisture (SMcrit, below which vegetation is under stress condition) and [SOURCE: Page_8]

121   the soil moisture at permanent wilting point (SMwp, below which water is not accessible [SOURCE: Page_8]

122   to plants). SMcrit is thus defined between SMwp and the soil moisture at field capacity (SMfc, [SOURCE: Page_8]

123   above which water cannot be held against gravitational drainage). Therefore, the [SOURCE: Page_8]

124   nonlinear response of LST for different RZSM levels/regimes is a big issue when trying to [SOURCE: Page_8]

125   develop a RZSM retrieval approach from LST data. Olivera-Guerra et al. (2018) developed [SOURCE: Page_8]

126   an approach to derive a first guess RZSM from a LST-derived water stress coefficient, [SOURCE: Page_8]

127   while under unstressed conditions (i.e. when LST is no more sensitive to RZSM) the RZSM [SOURCE: Page_8]

128   was estimated from a crop water balance model. The temporal dynamics of RZSM were [SOURCE: Page_8]

129   hence obtained along the season under stressed and unstressed condition, by making an [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   optimal use of both the water budget model and sequential LST observations. However, [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   the method in Olivera-Guerra et al. (2018) was not applied to remote sensing data and its [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   application to readily available LST observations requires to account for three major [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   issues that are addressed in the present work. First, a contextual approach should be [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   implemented from Landsat data to partition the LST into canopy and soil temperatures [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   by detecting the wet and dry conditions from the LST – fv space. This would allow for [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   estimating a Landsat-derived crop stress coefficient (Ks) over large scales. Second, a [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   serious complexity is introduced when trying to estimate the daily RZSM from sparsely [SOURCE: Page_8]

7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   available Landsat data. Especially the Landsat-derived Ks should be integrated into a crop [SOURCE: Page_9]

139   water balance model in both recursive and forward modes, in order to provide the [SOURCE: Page_9]

140   temporal dynamics of RZSM along the season at pixel scale over large areas. Third, given [SOURCE: Page_9]

141   that irrigation is usually applied within a single day over the entire crop field, the pixel- [SOURCE: Page_9]

142   scale irrigation estimates can be aggregated (following a strategy to be defined) to provide [SOURCE: Page_9]

143   the irrigation dates and amounts at the crop field scale. [SOURCE: Page_9]

144 [SOURCE: Page_9]

145   Therefore, this study aims, for the first time, to develop an original approach to retrieve [SOURCE: Page_9]

146   the crop field scale irrigation timing and amounts on a daily basis all along the agricultural [SOURCE: Page_9]

147   season from readily available remote sensing data. For this purpose, a key and novel step [SOURCE: Page_9]

148   in the approach is to estimate the daily RZSM by combining a forward and recursive crop [SOURCE: Page_9]

149   water balance initialized by temporally-sparse Landsat data. To our knowledge it is the [SOURCE: Page_9]

150   first remote sensing-based approach to estimate irrigation at such high spatio-temporal [SOURCE: Page_9]

151   resolution from readily available optical/thermal data and without relying on ad hoc [SOURCE: Page_9]

152   assumptions on irrigation regimes (e.g. no stress) and/or dates. The approach is [SOURCE: Page_9]

153   implemented with Landsat-7 and -8 data over three 12 km by 12 km areas in central [SOURCE: Page_9]

154   Morocco and is validated over five sites with different irrigation techniques (drip, flood [SOURCE: Page_9]

155   and no-irrigation) during four agricultural seasons. The paper is presented as follows. [SOURCE: Page_9]

156   Data sets are first described (Section 2). Next, the irrigation retrieval method is presented: [SOURCE: Page_9]

157   i) partitioning the Landsat LST to derive the crop water stress coefficient Ks, ii) estimating [SOURCE: Page_9]

158   the daily RZSM from the integration of Landsat-derived Ks into a crop water balance [SOURCE: Page_9]

159   model, iii) retrieving irrigation at the Landsat pixel scale and iv) aggregating pixel-scale [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   irrigation estimates at the crop field scale (Section 3). Then, the approach is tested over [SOURCE: Page_9]

161   three agricultural areas and validated against in situ measurements in terms of irrigation [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

162   as well as daily RZSM and ET (Section 4). Finally, the conclusions and perspectives are [SOURCE: Page_10]

163   presented (Section 5). [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   2.2.1 Land surface temperature [SOURCE: Page_12]

230   LST is estimated using the single-channel algorithm described in Jiménez-Munoz et al., [SOURCE: Page_12]

231   (2009, 2014), which uses as input the thermal band of Landsat, the atmospheric water [SOURCE: Page_12]

232   vapor content, and the spectral surface emissivity. The thermal data are acquired from [SOURCE: Page_12]

233   bands 6 and 10 of Landsat-7 and -8 Level-1, respectively, while the atmospheric water [SOURCE: Page_12]

234   vapor content is obtained from the daily MODIS MOD05 v^6.0 product. The spectral surface [SOURCE: Page_12]

257   3   Method [SOURCE: Page_13]

258   The method to retrieve irrigation dates and volumes from Landsat LST/NDVI time series [SOURCE: Page_13]

259   is described below. The basic idea behind the retrieval approach is first to determine the [SOURCE: Page_13]

260   irrigation date and then to estimate the (daily) irrigation amount as the difference [SOURCE: Page_13]

12 [SOURCE: Page_13]

261   between the RZSM estimated on the irrigation date and that estimated on the day before. [SOURCE: Page_14]

262   As in Olivera-Guerra et al. (2018), thermal-derived crop stress coefficient (Ks) is [SOURCE: Page_14]

263   translated into RZSM diagnostic by means of the dual crop coefficient FAO (FAO-2Kc) [SOURCE: Page_14]

264   formalism. In this former work, irrigation was estimated from the variability in daily first [SOURCE: Page_14]

265   guess RZSM by using optical/thermal in situ observations. Given that the method [SOURCE: Page_14]

266   proposed herein uses temporally sparse Landsat data, the Landsat-derived RZSM [SOURCE: Page_14]

267   diagnostic is propagated in a recursive and forward water balance mode to estimate the [SOURCE: Page_14]

268   daily RZSM along the season. Therefore, this method significantly differs from the study [SOURCE: Page_14]

269   in Olivera-Guerra et al. (2018) in several major aspects. For clarity, the main assumptions [SOURCE: Page_14]

270   are listed (Section 3.1) and each original component is described separately: the irrigation [SOURCE: Page_14]

271   retrieval at the pixel scale using Landsat-derived Ks (Section 3.2), the use of a contextual [SOURCE: Page_14]

272   method to derive RZSM from Landsat data (Section 3.3), the implementation of a crop [SOURCE: Page_14]

273   water balance model (WB) in recursive and forward modes to estimate the daily RZSM [SOURCE: Page_14]

274   between two successive Landsat overpass dates (separated by 8 to 16 days in clear sky [SOURCE: Page_14]

275   conditions) (Section 3.4), the aggregation of pixel-scale irrigation estimates at the crop [SOURCE: Page_14]

276   field scale (Section 3.5), and the definition of a validation strategy of the field-scale [SOURCE: Page_14]

277   retrieved irrigation dates/volumes (Section 3.6). [SOURCE: Page_14]

279   3.1 Model assumptions [SOURCE: Page_14]

280   The approach is based on several assumptions, some of which relate to the FAO-2Kc [SOURCE: Page_14]

281   modeling approach, while others are specific to the proposed irrigation retrieval method. [SOURCE: Page_14]

282   The assumptions deriving from the FAO-2Kc model are: [SOURCE: Page_14]

283      -   The daily RZSM varies within a range defined by a minimum value set to the soil [SOURCE: Page_14]

284          moisture at wilting point (SMwp) and by a maximum value set to the soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_14]

285          at field capacity (SMfc). Both extreme soil moisture values are estimated using [SOURCE: Page_14]

13 [SOURCE: Page_14]

286          pedo-transfer functions (Wosten et al., 1999). SMwp and SMfc were equal to 0.17 [SOURCE: Page_15]

287          and 0.32 m^3m-3, respectively. Uniform soil parameters were used to test the [SOURCE: Page_15]

288          genericity of the irrigation retrieval approach. [SOURCE: Page_15]

289      -   When RZSM reaches SMfc, any additional water supply is considered as water [SOURCE: Page_15]

290          excess and is therefore drained from the soil bucket by deep percolation [SOURCE: Page_15]

291          (occurring simultaneously to the water excess supply). [SOURCE: Page_15]

292      -   The RZSM is linearly related to Ks between SMwp and the critical RZSM (SMcrit = [SOURCE: Page_15]

293          0.24 m^3m-3), which is estimated as a fraction of the total available water (i.d. [SOURCE: Page_15]

294          difference between SMfc and SMwp) according to the water stress tolerance of crops [SOURCE: Page_15]

295          (Allen et al., 1998). [SOURCE: Page_15]

296      -   The rooting depth is estimated from the vegetation cover and varies linearly [SOURCE: Page_15]

297          between a minimum value (set to 0.1 m) and a maximum value depending on the [SOURCE: Page_15]

298          crop type. [SOURCE: Page_15]

299 [SOURCE: Page_15]

300   The assumptions specific to the irrigation retrieval approach are: [SOURCE: Page_15]

301      -   The retrieved irrigation is the effective irrigation (irrigation minus drainage), [SOURCE: Page_15]

302          meaning that the irrigation excess which triggers deep percolation is not taken into [SOURCE: Page_15]

303          account. [SOURCE: Page_15]

304      -   An irrigation event is detected on the day when the RZSM estimated recursively [SOURCE: Page_15]

305          from the FAO-2Kc water budget reaches SMfc and it is not due to rainfall. [SOURCE: Page_15]

306      -   The field-scale retrieved irrigation occurs on the same day over the entire field [SOURCE: Page_15]

307          crop. [SOURCE: Page_15]

308      -   Due to the saturation of Landsat-derived Ks (equal to 1) for soil moisture values [SOURCE: Page_15]

309          between SMcrit and SMfc, the Landsat-derived RZSM ranges between SMwp and [SOURCE: Page_15]

310          SMcrit. [SOURCE: Page_15]

14 [SOURCE: Page_15]

311      -   If two successive Landsat overpass dates both indicate unstressed conditions [SOURCE: Page_16]

312          (Ks=1), it is assumed that the crop does not undergo water stress during that [SOURCE: Page_16]

313          period. It is also assumed that Ks=1 between a Landsat date indicating unstressed [SOURCE: Page_16]

314          conditions and an irrigation event detected before the next Landsat overpass date. [SOURCE: Page_16]

315      -   In our study, the capillarity rise and runoff are neglected due to flat surfaces and a [SOURCE: Page_16]

316          water table significant deep (>30 m) in the study area (Duchemin et al, 2006 [SOURCE: Page_16]

317 [SOURCE: Page_16]

747   5   Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_34]

748   A new approach to estimate the field-scale irrigation amounts and timing along the [SOURCE: Page_34]

749   agricultural season is developed by integrating the Landsat optical and thermal data into [SOURCE: Page_34]

750   a crop water balance (FAO-based) model. The main idea behind the approach is first to [SOURCE: Page_34]

751   determine the irrigation date and then to estimate the irrigation amount as the difference [SOURCE: Page_34]

752   between the RZSM estimated on the irrigation date and that estimated on the day before. [SOURCE: Page_34]

753   In order to integrate the Landsat data into a crop water balance model and then to retrieve [SOURCE: Page_34]

33 [SOURCE: Page_34]

754   the irrigation at field scale, four general procedures are implemented: i) partitioning the [SOURCE: Page_35]

755   Landsat LST to derive the crop water stress coefficient Ks, ii) estimating the daily RZSM [SOURCE: Page_35]
