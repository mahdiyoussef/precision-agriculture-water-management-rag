31   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

32            The frequency, duration, and severity of drought events are expected to increase in the Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   area as a result of climate change, with strong impacts on forest ecosystems and in particular individual tree growth. [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   Tree growth response to drought is strongly influenced by local site and stand characteristics that can be quantified [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   using competition indices (CIs) and water stress indices (WSIs). These indices have been widely used to predict [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   tree growth; however, they are numerous, and few studies have investigated them jointly. In this context, we [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   investigated the potential of using CIs and WSIs to investigate tree behaviour under drought. The main objective [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   of this study was to quantify P. halepensis Mill. annual radial growth using tree size from the previous year, CIs [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   and WSIs. [SOURCE: Page_2]

40            We studied twelve 50-year-old Pinus halepensis plots located in the South-East of France distributed in [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   different density treatments (light, medium and dense). At each plot, all trees were measured (height, [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   circumference), spatialized and the ring-widths were measured for ~15 trees. We also developed a two-strata (over- [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   and understorey) forest water balance model to simulate soil water content at a daily resolution based on stand [SOURCE: Page_2]

44   characteristics (LAI values in particular) and soil properties. A mixed modelling approach was eventually used to [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   investigate the drivers of P. halepensis annual radial growth and to test the performance of five CIs and four WSIs. [SOURCE: Page_2]

46              The best growth model included tree size, the sum of Basal Area of Larger trees in a 5m-radius (BAL; [SOURCE: Page_2]

47   as CI), and the number of days that trees experienced water stress in a year (as WSI) as predictors. This model [SOURCE: Page_2]

48   explained up to 56 % of the variance in observed pine tree growth, which increased up to 77% when the individual [SOURCE: Page_2]

49   tree was included as a random effect on the intercept. We found that distance-independent CIs can perform as well [SOURCE: Page_2]

50   as distance-dependent CIs in our study site. The duration of drought alone appeared to better predict tree growth [SOURCE: Page_2]

51   than drought intensity and duration, or drought timing. The selected model led us to reaffirm the positive effect of [SOURCE: Page_2]

52   thinning on tree secondary growth when facing long and intense drought. [SOURCE: Page_2]

## 60       1.   INTRODUCTION

61 [SOURCE: Page_3]

62            Drought has been identified as the main concern for the current and future functioning of Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   forest ecosystems (Peñuelas et al., 2017). It strongly impacts the physiological functions of Mediterranean tree [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   and shrub species, limiting their annual growth (Barbeta et al., 2015; Borghetti et al., 1998; Gazol et al., 2018; [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   Ogaya et al., 2003). Drought can also induce individual tree mortality and forest dieback in cases of long-term or [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   extreme drought events (Allen et al., 2010; Carnicer et al., 2011; Greenwood et al., 2017; Hayles et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   Climate models project an increase in temperature – leading to increased potential evapotranspiration – combined [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   with a decrease in precipitation for the Mediterranean area (Giorgi, 2006). This will likely lead to an increase in [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   the duration, intensity and frequency of droughts (Cramer et al., 2018). In this context, understanding the processes [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   underlying the response of trees to drought is not only important for fundamental knowledge, but also for forest [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   management. Some forest management strategies have already been proposed to enhance Mediterranean forests’ [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   growth productivity under dry conditions and to adapt them to climate change, in particular through the reduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

73   of competition among trees by thinning (Aldea et al., 2017; Bréda et al., 1995; Calev et al., 2016; Sohn et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   2016; Vilà-Cabrera et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_3]

75            Ecological competition is defined as a negative interaction between plants, which can be direct (direct [SOURCE: Page_3]

76   contact, allelopathy) or indirect through the use of common resources (Connell, 1990), and can be intraspecific or [SOURCE: Page_3]

77   interspecific. Thus, competition defines the pattern of net resource availability and is largely responsible for [SOURCE: Page_3]

78   differences in individual growth among trees with varying social status and neighbourhoods (Calama et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_3]

79   Competition is considered symmetric when competitors share resources in proportion to their size, while [SOURCE: Page_3]

80   competition is considered to be asymmetric when large competitors capture a disproportionate share of contested [SOURCE: Page_3]

81   resources over smaller competitors (Schwinning & Weiner, 1998). On one hand, trees’ modes of competition are [SOURCE: Page_3]

82   driven by environmental factors and are linked to the most prevailing limiting factor (limitation-caused matter [SOURCE: Page_3]

83   partitioning hypothesis; Pretzsch & Biber, 2010). Competition for belowground resources is often assumed to be [SOURCE: Page_3]

84   symmetric – like in water-limited ecosystems (Pretzsch & Biber, 2010) e.g. Mediterranean ecosystems – while [SOURCE: Page_3]

85   competition for light is asymmetric due to the directional component of light (Schwinning & Weiner, 1998). On [SOURCE: Page_3]

86   the other hand, competition also modulates individual tree response to drought. For example, the reduction of [SOURCE: Page_3]

87   competition by thinning tends to increase stand-level water availability (Bréda et al., 1995) and may alleviate [SOURCE: Page_3]

88   drought-related reductions in tree growth (e.g., Aldea et al., 2017; Gavinet et al., 2015; Olivar et al., 2014; Sohn [SOURCE: Page_3]

89   et al., 2016). In addition, trees can have various responses to drought depending on the species, their size and [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

90   social status. Some studies have demonstrated that large trees are less resilient to drought than smaller trees [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   (Castagneri et al., 2012; Sánchez-Salguero et al., 2015; Zang et al., 2012) while other studies have found the [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   opposite pattern (Calama et al., 2019; Martin-Benito et al., 2011; Trouvé et al., 2017) or no difference between [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   dominant and suppressed trees (Bello et al., 2019; Lebourgeois et al., 2014). These contrasting results can be [SOURCE: Page_4]

94   explained by species-specific differences in shade- and drought-tolerance strategies, and by differences in the [SOURCE: Page_4]

95   population and site characteristics, especially in the stand water balance, whose spatiotemporal dynamics is often [SOURCE: Page_4]

96   not well quantified or considered. [SOURCE: Page_4]

97            Considering the various relationships between competition and drought and their impacts on tree radial [SOURCE: Page_4]

98   growth, models that aim to accurately predict individual tree growth or stand-scale productivity should take into [SOURCE: Page_4]

99   account both competitive and climatic drivers at an annual resolution (Ameztegui et al., 2017). Including these [SOURCE: Page_4]

100   drivers is important to correctly simulate (i) decadal and multi-decadal growth trends, which are strongly [SOURCE: Page_4]

101   influenced by competition, (ii) the impacts of human and natural disturbances on the spatial arrangement of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

102   stand and on the competition intensity experienced by each tree (e.g. after thinning or massive mortality), and (iii) [SOURCE: Page_4]

103   the interannual variability in tree growth, which is mainly controlled by interannual climate variability (Calama et [SOURCE: Page_4]

104   al., 2019; Condés & García-Robredo, 2012; Rathgeber et al., 2005; Sánchez-Salguero et al., 2015). This is [SOURCE: Page_4]

105   especially important to improve empirical forest growth models that statistically link growth data with specific site [SOURCE: Page_4]

106   and climatic conditions. Though empirical models are difficult to extrapolate, they are very precise under their [SOURCE: Page_4]

107   calibration domain and are widely used for forest management planning (e.g. growth and yield models; see [SOURCE: Page_4]

108   Weiskittel Jr et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_4]

109            Several types of indices can be used in such empirical forest growth models to assess the competition and [SOURCE: Page_4]

110   water stress experienced by a tree. Competition indices are often used to investigate the different modes of [SOURCE: Page_4]

111   competition (Biging & Dobbertin, 1995; Prévosto, 2005). For example, asymmetric competition for light can be [SOURCE: Page_4]

112   predicted using competition indices that derive from tree heights and crown sizes, while symmetric competition [SOURCE: Page_4]

113   for belowground resources can be predicted using competition indices based on tree diameters, root mass, or [SOURCE: Page_4]

114   rooting depths (Pretzsch et al., 2017). Many drought indices have been developed for modelling purposes. Speich [SOURCE: Page_4]

115   (2019) classified these drought indices into four levels, from the least to the most integrative: (1) based on [SOURCE: Page_4]

116   precipitation, (2) based on evaporative demand, (3) based on soil moisture storage and stand properties, and (4) [SOURCE: Page_4]

117   based on physiological thresholds. Drought indices that are more mechanistic generally perform better at [SOURCE: Page_4]

118   predicting tree growth than indices including precipitation and evaporative demand, or precipitation alone (Speich, [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

119   2019) as they better represent the actual water available for the plants (3 and 4) and the drought intensity they have [SOURCE: Page_5]

120   experienced (4). [SOURCE: Page_5]

121            In this study, we developed an empirical mixed modelling approach to predict individual Aleppo pine [SOURCE: Page_5]

122   (P.halepensis Mill.) radial growth based on its size, neighbourhood, and climatic factors. We used five competition [SOURCE: Page_5]

123   indices (CIs) that are derived from different types of information in order to investigate the modes of competition [SOURCE: Page_5]

124   of P.halepensis. Several water stress indices (WSIs), with contrasting levels of information were also used to [SOURCE: Page_5]

125   investigate the influence of water stress induced both by soil and climatic factors on P.halepensis radial growth. [SOURCE: Page_5]

126   We especially aimed at evaluating the potential of competition indices (CIs) and water stress indices (WSIs) jointly [SOURCE: Page_5]

127   for predicting annual basal area increment (BAI) in Pinus halepensis under different thinning intensities, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

128   selecting the best CI and WSI indices. Our main hypothesis were, as follows: [SOURCE: Page_5]

129            (i)       P.halepensis’ main mode of competition is symmetric; [SOURCE: Page_5]

130            (ii)      Soil water availability is a better predictor of P.halepensis growth than rainfall alone; [SOURCE: Page_5]

131            (iii)     The best pine growth model includes both competition indices and water stress indices. [SOURCE: Page_5]

## 133       2.   MATERIAL AND METHODS

134       2.1. Study site and experimental design [SOURCE: Page_5]

135 [SOURCE: Page_5]

136            This study was conducted in Southern France in the ‘Saint Mitre’ experimental site, which is located [SOURCE: Page_5]

137   about 30 km west of Marseille (43°27’0”N; 5°2’24”E). The area is flat and at a mean altitude of 130 m above sea [SOURCE: Page_5]

138   level. The climate is Mediterranean, with warm, dry summers and cool, wet winters. The mean annual temperature [SOURCE: Page_5]

139   is 15.3°C and mean annual precipitation is 562 mm (Istres weather station, 1985-2014; Appendix A). However, [SOURCE: Page_5]

140   fluctuations in rainfall are frequently observed between years. For example, 2015 and 2016 received 660 mm and [SOURCE: Page_5]

141   411 mm of rainfall, respectively. Soils are calcareous, with a sandy-loam texture (55% sand, 30% silt and 15% [SOURCE: Page_5]

142   clay) and a mean depth of 60 cm before reaching the calcareous bedrock. The site is composed of a monospecific [SOURCE: Page_5]

143   even-aged (~60 years old) Aleppo pine forest (Pinus halepensis Mill.) that has naturally regenerated after [SOURCE: Page_5]

144   agricultural abandonment ~60 years ago. The understorey is mainly composed of Mediterranean oaks (Quercus [SOURCE: Page_5]

145   ilex and Quercus coccifera), shrubs (e.g. Phillyrea angustiflia, Rosmarinus officinalis) and scarce herbaceous [SOURCE: Page_5]

146   plants (e.g. Brachypodium retusum). [SOURCE: Page_5]

147            Natural pine stands were thinned in 2007, leading to three different pine cover treatments and thus [SOURCE: Page_5]

148   different competition situations: (i) light pine cover (basal area: 10.2 m².ha-1), (ii) moderate pine cover (19.2 [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

149   m²/ha), (iii) dense pine cover (32.0 m²/ha; no thinning). Each treatment was replicated in four 25m × 25m plots [SOURCE: Page_6]

150   (Appendix B). All pines were individually identified, geo-referenced in spring 2017 using a differential GPS [SOURCE: Page_6]

151   (Trimble© TSC$_2$, Trimble Inc, USA) and a laser distance meter (LaserAce® 300, Measurements Devices Ltd, [SOURCE: Page_6]

152   UK). Their circumference at breast height (1.30m) and their height were measured in 2017 using a measuring tape [SOURCE: Page_6]

153   and a rangefinder (Vertex III, Häglof, Sweden), respectively. [SOURCE: Page_6]

229         2.4. Water Balance model [SOURCE: Page_8]

230 [SOURCE: Page_8]

231             The effects of the climate, stand, and soil properties on drought characteristics can be integrated into a [SOURCE: Page_8]

232   forest water balance model. We thus developed a two-strata forest water balance model, i.e. over- and understorey [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

233   are considered (adult tree canopy only composed of Aleppo pines; shrubby mixed understorey), based on Granier [SOURCE: Page_9]

234   et al., (1999), which computes daily variations in soil water content. The model uses daily temperatures and rainfall [SOURCE: Page_9]

235   data as inputs, and some site and stand parameters such as soil depth, maximum and minimum extractable soil [SOURCE: Page_9]

236   water (from soil texture analyses), fine root distribution, soil porosity, and stand Leaf Area Index (LAI). Istres [SOURCE: Page_9]

237   weather station (12 km NW of the site) provided the climatic data over the entire study period and global radiation [SOURCE: Page_9]

238   data came from Marignane station (14km E of the site). The PET was computed using the radiation-based method [SOURCE: Page_9]

239   of Turc (Turc, 1961) in the absence of wind data recorded on-site. Soil samples were collected in 2014 and 2017. [SOURCE: Page_9]

240   In 2014, 2 plots per treatment were sampled; in each of the selected plots 6 soil samples at three soil depths were [SOURCE: Page_9]

241   collected for texture analysis. In 2017, 5 soil pits were dug into the treatments and 30 soil samples of constant [SOURCE: Page_9]

242   volume (3 soil depths and 2 samples per depth) were collected to measure the bulk density, the content in coarse [SOURCE: Page_9]

243   elements and in fine roots (Table 1). The soil properties were considered as constant among the plots, except for [SOURCE: Page_9]

244   soil depth. Water buckets were computed for each soil layer (Jabiol et al., 2009) and aggregated at the plot scale. [SOURCE: Page_9]

245   Transmitted radiation was measured every minute for 48 hours during two successive clear days of April 2017 in [SOURCE: Page_9]

246   9 plots (3 plots/treatment) using 6 solarimeter tubes (PAR/LE Solems) per plot and 2 solarimeter tubes in open [SOURCE: Page_9]

247   conditions, in order to compute transmitted radiation. Based on this transmitted radiation, LAI was then calculated [SOURCE: Page_9]

261       2.5. Water stress indices (WSI) [SOURCE: Page_10]

262 [SOURCE: Page_10]

263               For each plot and each year, we used four different WSIs using different levels of information i.e. soil [SOURCE: Page_10]

264   and/or climatic constraints. The first three WSIs derive from the forest water balance to account for climate and [SOURCE: Page_10]

265   local conditions, while the fourth WSI derives directly from climatic data. We assumed that a drought had occurred [SOURCE: Page_10]

266   when the soil REW dropped below a threshold of 0.4, as proposed by Granier et al. (1999), a threshold that has [SOURCE: Page_10]

267   been successfully applied in other empirical and modelling studies (Bello et al., 2019; Forner et al., 2018; Granier [SOURCE: Page_10]

10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

268   et al., 2007; Speich, 2019). The first WSI cumulates the daily differences between REW and the 0.4 threshold of [SOURCE: Page_11]

269   REW, and indicates both drought intensity and duration (INT, Figure 3): [SOURCE: Page_11]

270 [SOURCE: Page_11]

271   [7]                𝐼𝑁𝑇 = ∑(0.4 − 𝑅𝐸𝑊𝑖 )         for       𝑅𝐸𝑊𝑖 ≤ 0.4 [SOURCE: Page_11]

272 [SOURCE: Page_11]

273   With i the day of the year. [SOURCE: Page_11]

274   The second WSI only represents the drought duration (DUR) and corresponds to the number of days that REW is [SOURCE: Page_11]

275   below 0.4 (Figure 2). The third WSI accounts for drought duration and timing (TIM), and was adapted from the [SOURCE: Page_11]

276   seasonal water stress index developed in Mina et al. (2016). For each season, we produced an intermediate WSI, [SOURCE: Page_11]

277   which is the number of water stress days divided by mean soil water content (𝑆𝑊𝐶) during the given period. We [SOURCE: Page_11]

278   then totalled all of the seasonal WSIs to obtain the annual WSI: [SOURCE: Page_11]

279 𝑁𝑢𝑚𝑏𝑒𝑟 𝑜𝑓 𝑊𝑆 𝑑𝑎𝑦𝑠 280   [8]                𝑇𝐼𝑀 = ∑(           𝑆𝑊𝐶 )𝑠𝑒𝑎𝑠𝑜𝑛 [SOURCE: Page_11]

281 [SOURCE: Page_11]

284   preliminary analyses, we found that for our study site, annual rainfall was a better predictor of growth than seasonal [SOURCE: Page_11]

298 [SOURCE: Page_12]

299             Kruskal-Wallis non-parametric tests followed by Dunn’s test were performed to explore the effect of the [SOURCE: Page_12]

300   cover treatments on BAI, using the {dunn.test} package (Dinno, 2017) from the open-source R statistical software [SOURCE: Page_12]

301   (R Core Team, 2017). [SOURCE: Page_12]

302             Simple linear regressions were used to explore the relationships between tree BAI and the different [SOURCE: Page_12]

303   CIs/WSIs. To jointly test the influence of both competition and water stress on P. halepensis tree growth, we [SOURCE: Page_12]

304   developed linear mixed-effect models using the {lme^4} and {lmerTest} packages (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). Natural [SOURCE: Page_12]

305   logarithm transformations were used to satisfy the assumptions of linearity and normality of the residuals, the [SOURCE: Page_12]

306   ‘LogSt’ function from the {DescTools} package (Signorell et al., 2019) being specifically used to account for null [SOURCE: Page_12]

307   CI values. This led to the following model equation: [SOURCE: Page_12]

313   t, i the random effect estimated for the intercept with tree as a grouping factor, and  is the residual error. BA was [SOURCE: Page_12]

314   included in the models to account for the effect of tree size on current annual growth. We included trees nested [SOURCE: Page_12]

315   into the plot, plots alone, or trees alone as a random effect on the intercept term; however after preliminary analysis [SOURCE: Page_12]

## 423           4.   DISCUSSION

424           4.1. Competition indices [SOURCE: Page_20]

425 [SOURCE: Page_20]

426                In this study, the Hegyi competition index (HEG) was selected as the best predictor of individual annual [SOURCE: Page_20]

427   BAI among the competition indices for the one-variable and the two-variable models, followed by the basal area [SOURCE: Page_20]

428   of larger trees (BAL; BAL), the angles CIs (i.e. HOR and VER), and the relative spacing CI (RS). This suggests [SOURCE: Page_20]

429   that competition would mainly be symmetric, i.e. the system is limited by water. However, the BAL was selected [SOURCE: Page_20]

430   as the best predictor of BAI among the CIs in the three-variable models, followed closely by the Hegyi CI (HEG), [SOURCE: Page_20]

20 [SOURCE: Page_20]

431   and the vertical angles (VER). This suggests that part of the competition explained by certain CIs (i.e. HEG) could [SOURCE: Page_21]

432   be taken into account by WSIs. In other words, this indicates that some CIs do indeed reflect the competition for [SOURCE: Page_21]

433   water. The poor performance of RS can be explained by its generality as it provides a single competition value for [SOURCE: Page_21]

434   all trees within a given plot, which does not accurately represent the actual competition experienced by each [SOURCE: Page_21]

435   individual tree. [SOURCE: Page_21]

436            The three best CIs (BAL, HEG and VER) correspond to three different methods of computing competition; [SOURCE: Page_21]

437   despite this, BAL, HEG and VER have similar BAI predictive power, with a marginal r-squared of about 56%. [SOURCE: Page_21]

438   HEG only relies on circumference and should indicate symmetric competition for belowground resources, while [SOURCE: Page_21]

439   BAL also relies on circumferences but only incorporates trees larger than the subject tree, reflecting both [SOURCE: Page_21]

440   asymmetric and symmetric competition. VER uses tree height, which is more subject to measurement errors, but [SOURCE: Page_21]

441   is more representative of competition for light (e.g. asymmetric competition) than circumference. We investigated [SOURCE: Page_21]

442   the relationship between height and circumference. However, no clear relationship was detected (Appendix G), [SOURCE: Page_21]

443   indicating that both represent different aspects of competition with water stress indices explaining yet another [SOURCE: Page_21]

444   aspect of competition. It also highlights the fact that CIs can share some information, as competition can never be [SOURCE: Page_21]

445   defined as completely size-symmetric or completely size-asymmetric (Schwinning & Weiner, 1998). Hence, our [SOURCE: Page_21]

446   hypothesis (i) is not entirely verified: P.halepensis’ mode of competition seems to be asymmetric, but this is [SOURCE: Page_21]

447   contradicted by the good performance of the HEG index. [SOURCE: Page_21]

448            Moreover, BAL is distance-independent, while HEG and VER are distance-dependent. The fact that a [SOURCE: Page_21]

449   distance-independent CI and distance-dependent CIs have similar predictive power is not straightforward. In most [SOURCE: Page_21]

450   cases and especially in heterogeneous stands, distance-dependent competition indices appear to be more correlated [SOURCE: Page_21]

451   with tree growth, as they consider the spatial arrangement of the trees within a stand (Contreras et al., 2011; [SOURCE: Page_21]

452   Pukkala & Kolström, 1987; Rouvinen & Kuuluvainen, 1997). However, results from other studies suggest that [SOURCE: Page_21]

453   neither distance-independent indices nor distance-dependent indices perform universally better (our study; Biging [SOURCE: Page_21]

454   & Dobbertin, 1995; Prévosto, 2005). These contrasting results suggest that a single best CI for all sites and all [SOURCE: Page_21]

455   species may not exist. In fact, CIs are most likely species-specific, and depend on local site conditions. For [SOURCE: Page_21]

456   example, Contreras et al. (2011) found that the best predictor of BAI of Pinus ponderosa, Pseudotsuga menzisii [SOURCE: Page_21]

457   and Larix occidentalis was the horizontal angles CI. Cattaneo et al. (2018) found that the Hegyi CI was the best [SOURCE: Page_21]

458   predictor of Pinus pinea and Pinus halepensis radial growth, compared to asymmetric competition indices. In our [SOURCE: Page_21]

459   study, the stands are quite homogeneous and major changes in the stand composition and structure did not occur [SOURCE: Page_21]

460   between 2007 and 2017 (Appendix F). Therefore, if we aim to successfully predict tree growth over a long-term, [SOURCE: Page_21]

21 [SOURCE: Page_21]

461   and especially growth release after neighbourhood mortality, the Hegyi competition index may be more suitable. [SOURCE: Page_22]

462   However, to simulate short-term radial growth, testing several different CIs would be a valuable approach as their [SOURCE: Page_22]

463   predictive power could differ between species and stand spatial arrangements. [SOURCE: Page_22]

465       4.2. Water stress indices [SOURCE: Page_22]

466 [SOURCE: Page_22]

467            Our results suggest that the growth of P. halepensis is largely controlled by soil water content rather than [SOURCE: Page_22]

468   annual precipitation alone, which verifies our second hypothesis and is in accordance with Alfaro-Sánchez et al. [SOURCE: Page_22]

469   (2018), Misson et al. (2004), Rathgeber et al., (2005) and Vennetier et al. (2018). Soil water availability is more [SOURCE: Page_22]

470   biologically meaningful than annual precipitation alone, as it not only integrates water inputs (rainfall and [SOURCE: Page_22]

471   interception by the canopy), but also the soil water content (e.g., according to soil depth and texture), and water [SOURCE: Page_22]

472   loss through vegetation (evapotranspiration, which depends on PET, a combination of atmospheric temperature, [SOURCE: Page_22]

473   air relative humidity and solar radiation). Indeed, atmospheric conditions also play an important role in regulating [SOURCE: Page_22]

474   stomatal conductance. For example, Maseyk et al. (2008) found in the case of P.halepensis that, irrespective of [SOURCE: Page_22]

475   soil moisture, leaf vapour pressure deficit greatly influenced stomatal conductance when REW was above 0.2. In [SOURCE: Page_22]

476   general, vapour-pressure deficit was found to limit tree growth and was therefore advised to be considered in forest [SOURCE: Page_22]

477   models (Novick et al., 2016; Sanginés de Cárcer et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_22]

478            Among the water stress indices tested, drought duration alone (DUR) better predicted P. halepensis BAI [SOURCE: Page_22]

479   than an index that combines drought timing (TIM), or duration and intensity (INT). Similarly, preliminary analyses [SOURCE: Page_22]

480   revealed that the annual water stress indices performed better than the seasonal indices tested (Appendix A), which [SOURCE: Page_22]

481   is the reason why we chose to use annual indices over seasonal indices. This would suggest that P. halepensis uses [SOURCE: Page_22]

482   water whenever it is available, with no distinction for the time of the year. These results were not expected as [SOURCE: Page_22]

483   drought timing is known to have a differential impact on cambial activity, and thus ring-width (Campelo et al., [SOURCE: Page_22]

484   2007; Mina et al., 2016, Raventós et al., 2001). In the case of P. halepensis, Pasho et al., (2012) demonstrated that [SOURCE: Page_22]

485   cumulative precipitations from winter to spring drive secondary growth of the same year. However, Rathgeber et [SOURCE: Page_22]

486   al., (2005) found that the duration and intensity of the drought was the main predictive factor of P. halepensis [SOURCE: Page_22]

487   growth, although they did not investigate the effect of drought duration alone. [SOURCE: Page_22]

488            In our study, the fact that drought duration alone better predicted Aleppo pine growth than an index that [SOURCE: Page_22]

489   also integrates drought intensity could suggest that its cambial activity may be more sink-limited than source- [SOURCE: Page_22]

490   limited. In other words, it may be more limited by the drought-induced loss of cell turgor in the cambium than by [SOURCE: Page_22]

22 [SOURCE: Page_22]

491   the carbon availability through photosynthesis and carbon reserves (Fatichi et al., 2013; Lempereur et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_23]

492   In this sink-limited approach, there is no notion of drought intensity: as soon as the REW drops below 0.4, [SOURCE: Page_23]

493   xylogenesis stops, even though the stomata can still be open and allow carbon assimilation and transpiration. [SOURCE: Page_23]

494   Indeed, there is evidence that cambial and leaf growth are inhibited sooner than photosynthesis when water stress [SOURCE: Page_23]

495   increases (Hsiao et al., 1976; Lempereur et al., 2015; Muller et al., 2011; Tardieu et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_23]

496            As P. halepensis is known to strongly control its transpiration through stomata closure, a strategy to [SOURCE: Page_23]

497   reduce water stress (Melzack et al., 1985), we could also hypothesise – as another way of explaining our results – [SOURCE: Page_23]

498   that P. halepensis closes its stomata as soon as there is water stress, and thus stops growing. However, Maseyk et [SOURCE: Page_23]

499   al. (2008) found that P. halepensis transpiration follows the same trend as described in Granier et al. (1999): [SOURCE: Page_23]

500   transpiration decreases linearly after reaching REW = 0.4. This threshold therefore seems accurate when looking [SOURCE: Page_23]

501   at tree transpiration; however, it might not be appropriate when linking it to tree growth. Despite the fact that P. [SOURCE: Page_23]

502   halepensis is well studied (Baquedano & Castillo, 2007; Froux et al., 2005; Hover et al., 2017; Klein et al., 2011; [SOURCE: Page_23]

503   Melzack et al., 1985; Ungar et al., 2013), there is a need for more physiological studies investigating the non- [SOURCE: Page_23]

504   linear, threshold-based relationship between secondary growth and drought for this species. To achieve this, [SOURCE: Page_23]

505   several different approaches could be envisaged, such as using photosynthetic rate as a proxy for carbon [SOURCE: Page_23]

506   assimilation, or by investigating water-use efficiency. A promising approach would be to investigate the link [SOURCE: Page_23]

507   between cambial activity and drought stress experienced by the tree by combining leaf water potential (Ψ pd) and [SOURCE: Page_23]

508   dendrometer data that has been pre-analysed to remove shrinking-expansion phases arising from changes in the [SOURCE: Page_23]

509   water content in the elastic tissue of the stem (Balducci et al., 2019; Zweifel et al., 2006). For instance, Lempereur [SOURCE: Page_23]

510   et al. (2015) found that the summer interruption of Quercus ilex growth was associated with a threshold of -1.1 [SOURCE: Page_23]

511   MPa and remained nil for values of Ψpd ranging from −1.1 to −4 MPa, well before transpiration ceased and [SOURCE: Page_23]

512   cavitation occurred. [SOURCE: Page_23]

513            Finally, our results suggest that water availability is a better predictor of annual tree growth than [SOURCE: Page_23]

514   competition alone, which can be explained by high climatic interannual variability, while CIs remained constant. [SOURCE: Page_23]

515   Our findings also suggest that WSIs and CIs together are the best predictors of Aleppo pine radial growth, [SOURCE: Page_23]

516   confirming our final hypothesis (iii). [SOURCE: Page_23]

521       4.3. Inter-individual variability [SOURCE: Page_24]

522 [SOURCE: Page_24]

523            Finally, as indicated by the large proportion of the variance explained by the random effect of the tree [SOURCE: Page_24]

524   individual on the intercept (22% for the best model), there was a high inter-individual variability in P. halepensis [SOURCE: Page_24]

525   radial growth within our study population. The Western European population of P. halepensis is considered to [SOURCE: Page_24]

526   have very low genetic diversity (Soto et al., 2010). However, it has high phenotypic plasticity, which has been [SOURCE: Page_24]

527   demonstrated many times for various traits at different spatial scales and climatic conditions (Baquedano et al., [SOURCE: Page_24]

528   2008; Choat et al., 2018; de Luis et al., 2013; Rathgeber et al., 2005; Vizcaíno-Palomar et al., 2016; Voltas et al., [SOURCE: Page_24]

529   2015; Voltas et al., 2018). In our study, all P. halepensis individuals were within the same forest area and were [SOURCE: Page_24]

530   approximately the same age; we can therefore assume that they are part of the same genetic population. The high [SOURCE: Page_24]

531   variability in individual tree growth could therefore be explained by phenotypic variations that arose due to the [SOURCE: Page_24]

532   micro-local heterogeneity in abiotic and biotic factors, which could not be investigated in this study (e.g., presence [SOURCE: Page_24]

533   of pathogens, varying water availability at the individual level due to small-scale changes in slope or soil properties [SOURCE: Page_24]

534   and that is not considered in the WSIs). For example, we considered the understorey as a uniform patch ergo the [SOURCE: Page_24]

535   competition applied by the understorey was also considered to be uniform. These results highlight the importance [SOURCE: Page_24]

536   of considering variations in local conditions to accurately represent existing environmental heterogeneity (Ettl & [SOURCE: Page_24]

537   Peterson, 1995). [SOURCE: Page_24]

539       4.4. Management perspectives [SOURCE: Page_24]

540 [SOURCE: Page_24]

541            The importance of thinning has already been supported by many other studies investigating forest [SOURCE: Page_24]

542   management practices for adaptation to climate change (Aldea et al., 2017; Calev et al., 2016; Millar et al., 2007; [SOURCE: Page_24]

543   Olivar et al., 2014; Vilà-Cabrera et al., 2018). In particular, thinning has been found to have a positive effect on [SOURCE: Page_24]

544   biomass accumulation of young Aleppo pines, which is even more marked at dry sites (Alfaro-Sánchez et al., [SOURCE: Page_24]

545   2015). However, the results available in the literature are not as clear when looking at the effects of thinning on [SOURCE: Page_24]

546   microclimatic variables. For instance, forest thinning has been found to have a limited impact on [SOURCE: Page_24]

547   evapotranspiration (Liu et al., 2018), which was explained by the rapid recovery of understorey vegetation in the [SOURCE: Page_24]

548   thinned plots. The importance of accounting for understorey evapotranspiration was also highlighted by Simonin [SOURCE: Page_24]

549   et al. (2007) who found a substantial contribution of understorey evapotranspiration to stand evapotranspiration. [SOURCE: Page_24]

550   Mediterranean forests often present a well-developed shrubby understorey which influences the microclimate [SOURCE: Page_24]

24 [SOURCE: Page_24]

551   (Prévosto et al., 2019) and therefore needs to be taken into account for forest management. Our empirical growth [SOURCE: Page_25]

552   model integrates this understorey layer and all of the variables associated with it (evapotranspiration, transpiration, [SOURCE: Page_25]

553   rainfall interception) to compute the soil water availability at the stand level. Because water-stress was quantified [SOURCE: Page_25]

554   through a soil water budget model based on functional processes, this model can provide useful insights to forest [SOURCE: Page_25]

555   managers despite the fact that it was calibrated on a short time period and at a single site. Firstly, managers should [SOURCE: Page_25]

556   focus on soil water storage rather than precipitations alone to quantify drought situations. This is clearly more [SOURCE: Page_25]

557   complicated, but the use of forest models can be a useful alternative for assessing soil moisture and predicting tree [SOURCE: Page_25]

558   growth. Our empirical pine tree growth model can provide useful information for forest managers of [SOURCE: Page_25]

559   Mediterranean forests. For example, with ongoing climate change, the number of water stress days is expected to [SOURCE: Page_25]

560   increase (IPCC, 2014) and according to our model, this will correspond to an abrupt decline in tree annual growth [SOURCE: Page_25]

561   if the number of water stress days exceeds roughly 200 days (Figure 6). Our results suggest that reducing [SOURCE: Page_25]

562   competition by thinning could alleviate the negative effect of drought (Figure 7). For example, in dense stands [SOURCE: Page_25]

563   with a mean BAL = 3 (corresponding to our dense cover), 150 days of drought in a year is already predicted to [SOURCE: Page_25]

564   lead to an abrupt decline in tree growth in our conditions. Thinning dense stands, leading to moderate to light cover [SOURCE: Page_25]

565   (mean BAL = 1) would reduce the impact of the water stress and increase annual pine growth by 0.7 mm². This [SOURCE: Page_25]

566   study further highlights the positive effects of thinning, especially in regard to alleviating drought-related stress. [SOURCE: Page_25]
