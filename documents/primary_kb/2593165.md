19   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   Sludge from a slaughterhouse wastewater plant, and mixtures of bulking agent (crushed wood [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   pallet) and sludge were studied by Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR). The NMR spin-spin [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   relaxation (T$_2$) and spin-lattice relaxation (T$_1$) signals for sludge, wet crushed wood pallet and [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   mixtures of sludge and bulking agent were decomposed into three relaxation time [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   components. Each relaxation time component was explained by a non-homogeneous water [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   distribution on a microscopic length scale and by the porosity of the material. For all samples, [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   the T$_2$ relaxation time value of each component was directly related to the dry matter content. [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   The addition of wet crushed wood to sludge induced a decrease in the relaxation time, [SOURCE: Page_2]

CemOA : archive ouverte d'Irstea / Cemagref 28   explained by water transfer between the sludge and the wood. [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and respirometric measurements were performed on [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   sludge and wood mixtures. MR images of the mixtures were successfully obtained at different [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   biodegradation states. Based on specific NMR measurements in an identified area located in [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   the MRI cells, the results showed that grey levels of MR images reflected dry matter content. [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   This preliminary study showed that MRI would be a powerful tool to measure water [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   distribution in sludge and bulking agent mixtures and highlights the potential of this technique [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   to increase the understanding of sludge composting. [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   Treatment of wastewater is an important issue in western countries and research is currently [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   being undertaken to investigate the elimination or re-use of residual sludge at better cost, [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   including composting of wastewater sludge. In order to do so, sludge needs to be mixed with [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   a bulking agent which creates a porous matrix. Proportions of sludge and bulking agent are [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   chosen so that sludge covers the bulking agent with a thin layer, and then micro-organisms in [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   the sludge are sufficiently aerated to consume the available biodegradable organic matter. [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   During sludge composting treatment, the active biodegradation takes place in three to four [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   weeks, followed by several months of maturation (Gupta, 2008). Selection of an initial waste [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   recipe with optimal physical and biochemical parameters is essential to create an environment [SOURCE: Page_3]

CemOA : archive ouverte d'Irstea / Cemagref 51   conducive to high microbial growth and activity, and then to shorten and improve the [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   biodegradation in the active phase (Haug, 1993; Agnew, 2003). Some of the important [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   physical parameters for this waste recipe are water content, air porosity and specific surface [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   area. [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   Research on composting is often focused on overall understanding of the biodegradation [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   phenomena, i.e. on a macro-scale. All the parameters of biodegradation such as oxygen [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   consumption, bacteria activity, porosity and moisture content are averaged, and little is known [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   regarding the influence of local behaviour of the physical parameters on overall [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   biodegradation. Since solid wastes are very heterogeneous, better understanding of water [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   content, porosity and specific surface spatial distribution is needed, and Magnetic Resonance [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   Imaging (MRI) could help to fill this gap. Indeed, most of the common techniques to measure [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   physical parameters can not be applied along the composting process because they are [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   invasive or destructive. Moisture measurements need sampling and drying. Classic porosity [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   measurement implies to fill the porous volume with a liquid (Annan, 1998), which is [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   destructive for the sample. Pycnometry is a non destructive method but results directly depend [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   on the applied pressure leading to the measurement of total air space (TAS) or only free air [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   space (FAS) (Su, 2006). Measurement of the specific surface is not easier. The direct [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   measurement technique through gaseous absorption (BET) used by Palmowski et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   (Palmowski, 2003) can only be applied after drying the waste. On the contrary, MRI is non- [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   destructive and non-invasive and thus makes it possible to follow the evolution of samples [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   over time. MRI is well known for its medical applications, but it has been used in other fields [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   including the wood industry (Muller, 2002), study of bacterial chemotaxis (Sherwood, 2003; [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   Olson, 2004), chemical engineering (Mantle, 2003), civil engineering (Faure, 2005), fluid [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   mechanics (Bonn, 2008), pharmaceutical science (Richardson, 2005) and food science (Hills, [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   1998; Butz, 2005). In an MRI experiment, the volume in which the signal is collected is [SOURCE: Page_4]

CemOA : archive ouverte d'Irstea / Cemagref 76   called a voxel, and its typical order of magnitude is the cubic millimetre. Voxel intensity is a [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   function of local proton density and of their respective spin-lattice (T$_1$) and spin-spin (T$_2$) [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   relaxation times. Proton density is proportional to the amount of material containing protons. [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   In most materials, water is the main constituent related to proton density. Consequently, the [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   grey level intensity of a voxel may be related to water content or air content (i.e. porosity). [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   Indeed when the voxel size is larger than the pore size the porosity is directly related to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   intensity of the MRI signal: the greater the quantity of gas, the lower the signal. This method [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   has been used in food science to quantify the distribution of porosity during proving of dough [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   (Grenier, 2003; Lucas, 2005). In the extreme case where the pore volume is much larger than [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   the voxel size, the pore size can be measured. For example, air cavities have been detected [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   and quantified in food products such as cheese (Mariette, 2004; 2006). In addition to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   porous fraction, the specific surface area can be estimated if the resolution of the MR images [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   is good enough. [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   Spin-lattice (T$_1$) and spin-spin (T$_2$) relaxation times are related to water content and also to [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   material microstructure and to molecular structure (Brownstein, 1979; Belton, 1987; Hills, [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   1989b; a; Denisov, 2002). Several authors have used the T$_2$ and T$_1$ relaxation times as tracers [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   of water content for the investigation of draining processes (Mariette, 2006) and rehydration [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   processes (Ziegler, 2003). [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   The aim of this study was to evaluate the potential of the MRI technique in sludge composting [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   to quantify the water distribution. . The first part of this report focuses on the description of [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) signals in sludge and in mixtures of sludge and [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   bulking agent and their sensitivity to water content. The second part describes the preliminary [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   results of MRI images from sludge and bulking agent mixture. The potential for determination [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   of water content and measurement of local porosity by MRI is discussed. [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   2.1 Sludge and bulking agent [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   The sludge came from the biological activated sludge treatment of the wastewater from a [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   slaughter-house. Iron chloride and a polymeric flocculent (PRAESTOL K133) were added to [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   the sludge to induce flocculation and, after a decantation step, the sludge was dewatered using [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   a press band. The collected sludge was stored frozen at -20°C in 1kg batches. Sludge was [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   unfrozen before each experiment, initially in a refrigerator at 4°C and then at room [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   temperature. [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   The dry matter (DM, measured by drying at 105°C until constant weight) in the sludge was [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   13.6% of total weight, with approximately 80% organic matter to DM. The exact quantity of [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   living bacteria present in the sludge was unknown. Flocs of bacteria were observed using [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   optical microscopy (data not shown). Sludges with higher dry matter content were prepared [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   using a low pressure oven (34 Mb and 30°C). Mixtures of sludge and bulking agent were [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   prepared by hand mixing the sludge with crushed wood pallet as bulking agent. The size of [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   the wood chips considered was 8 to 12 mm. Wood was stored after drying at 80°C. Different [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   samples were then studied by NMR, i.e. the sludge without bulking agent, mixtures of wood [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   and sludge, and a rehydrated wood sample (DM 45%). Since there is water transfer between [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   sludge and dried wood after mixing, the saturated wood sample was prepared to mimic the [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   contribution of the re-hydrated wood fraction in the mixture. [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   A combined experiment using MRI and respirometry was set up. Two large respirometric [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   cells were prepared with 4.5 kg of sludge (wet) mixed with 2.5 kg of wood (dry mass ratio [SOURCE: Page_6]

## 200   3.1 NMR

201   Figure 2 shows typical CPMG (a) and SR (b) signals for two wood and sludge mixtures with [SOURCE: Page_9]

202   dry matter of 24% and 70%. CPMG signal intensities decreased throughout the acquisition [SOURCE: Page_9]

203   time t, according to equation 1. An increase in signal intensity was obtained for SR as [SOURCE: Page_9]

204   expected from equation 2. The effects of water content on NMR parameters can be described [SOURCE: Page_9]

205   from the two CPMG signals. Firstly, since the initial signal intensity was proportional to the [SOURCE: Page_9]

206   sample proton content, and because water is one of the most abundant protonic molecules in [SOURCE: Page_9]

207   our system, this explains the reduction in signal intensity from 3.7 to 2.2 a.u. observed [SOURCE: Page_9]

208   between the two samples. Secondly, the water relaxation time is highly sensitive to water [SOURCE: Page_9]

209   content. Indeed, T$_2$ relaxation was directly related to molecular mobility, i.e., the slower the [SOURCE: Page_9]

210   molecular mobility the shorter the relaxation time (Belton, 1990). The relaxation time of a [SOURCE: Page_9]

211   small molecule is thus always higher than the relaxation time of a large molecule. Moreover, [SOURCE: Page_9]

212   the exchange between water protons and exchangeable protons from the molecule contained [SOURCE: Page_9]

213   in the dry matter has to be taken into account for water. This exchange, known as chemical [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

214   exchange, is one of the most efficient mechanisms of water relaxation (Belton, 1987; Hills, [SOURCE: Page_10]

215   1989b; a; Belton, 1990; Denisov, 2002). Consequently, water relaxation is always sensitive to [SOURCE: Page_10]

216   water content and its value decreases with water content, explaining the more rapid decrease [SOURCE: Page_10]

217   in the CPMG signal observed for the driest sample. The same mechanisms are also involved [SOURCE: Page_10]

218   in the T$_1$ relaxation from the Saturation Recovery NMR sequence. The intensity measured at [SOURCE: Page_10]

219   2 s was lower for the driest sample and the recovery of the signal (shorter T$_1$) was faster for [SOURCE: Page_10]

220   the driest sample. [SOURCE: Page_10]

221   The spin-spin relaxation distributions (T$_2$) from MEM obtained from fitting the CPMG curve [SOURCE: Page_10]

222   are presented in Figure 3. For all samples, two or three peaks were extracted from the signal [SOURCE: Page_10]

223   according to the sample dry matter. Two mechanisms can be proposed to explain such multi- [SOURCE: Page_10]

CemOA : archive ouverte d'Irstea / Cemagref 224   exponential relaxation behaviour. The first is based on the assumption of the chemical [SOURCE: Page_10]

225   composition of the sample. For example, in the case of the water and fat mixture in an [SOURCE: Page_10]

226   emulsion, a specific exponential can be attributed to each phase, i.e. water and fat (Duval, [SOURCE: Page_10]

227   2006). However, since our samples were mainly composed of water, this hypothesis did not [SOURCE: Page_10]

228   hold. The second mechanism is based on the sample microstructure which may induce non- [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   homogeneous water distribution on a microscopic length scale. Indeed, when water exchange [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   by diffusion between different compartments is slow compared to the relaxation time value, [SOURCE: Page_10]

231   the averaging effect is not sufficient to yield a single relaxation time value, and several peaks [SOURCE: Page_10]

232   are observed for the water relaxation. This multi-exponential relaxation for water has already [SOURCE: Page_10]

233   been reported for many samples which exhibited restriction to the water diffusion. For [SOURCE: Page_10]

234   example three peaks have been reported from the relaxation decay curve of vegetable tissues. [SOURCE: Page_10]

235   These peaks were attributed to water in the vacuole, in the cytoplasm and in the cell wall, [SOURCE: Page_10]

236   respectively (Snaar, 1992; Van Dusschoten, 1995; Duval, 2005; Musse, 2009). This [SOURCE: Page_10]

237   mechanism therefore seems the most relevant to explain the multi-exponential behaviour of [SOURCE: Page_10]

238   the relaxation. [SOURCE: Page_10]

239   For the sludge, depending on the dry matter, two or three peaks were extracted from the signal [SOURCE: Page_11]

240   (Figure 3-a). Their T$_2$ values covered a range from 5 to 60 ms. The sludge was composed of [SOURCE: Page_11]

241   flocculated bacteria which induced heterogeneity in the water distribution. Indeed, water was [SOURCE: Page_11]

242   always present in bacteria, in and around the flocs. Since water protons are thus in three [SOURCE: Page_11]

243   different compartments, it is tempting to associate each relaxation time with a water [SOURCE: Page_11]

244   compartment. Granular sludges have already been studied by NMR (Lens, 1997; Lens, 1999), [SOURCE: Page_11]

245   and a relaxing agent was used to lower the proton relaxation times in the solution. Relaxation [SOURCE: Page_11]

246   times of 10-15 ms and 30 ms were attributed to methanogenic (Lens, 1999) and sulfidogenic [SOURCE: Page_11]

247   bacteria (Lens, 1997), respectively. Water in the granular matrix had a relaxation time of 40- [SOURCE: Page_11]

248   100 ms (Lens, 1999). According to these results, the first peak could be related to the bacteria [SOURCE: Page_11]

CemOA : archive ouverte d'Irstea / Cemagref 249   and the second to the water around the bacteria (Figure 3-a). However, a water proton from [SOURCE: Page_11]

250   the solution could well contribute to the first or the second peak. Since no peak was found [SOURCE: Page_11]

251   with a value close to the free water value (1.5-3 s), this means that there were some molecules [SOURCE: Page_11]

252   in the sludge solution that lowered the overall relaxation of the proton of this compartment. [SOURCE: Page_11]

253   The polymer added for the flocculation could explain the decrease in the water relaxation [SOURCE: Page_11]

254   time, but the effect of the iron chloride (used to dehydrate the sludge) has also to be taken into [SOURCE: Page_11]

256   Wood relaxation is well documented in the literature (Hsi, 1977; Menon, 1987; Araujo, 1992; [SOURCE: Page_11]
