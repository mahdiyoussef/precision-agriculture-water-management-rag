The growth of fleshy fruits largely depends on the balance of water influx and efflux (Lang, 1990; Lang and Thorpe, 1989). The flux of water into a fruit results from a tight coordination between vascular (xylem and phloem) transport and fruit cell expansion. The former is regulated by vascular conductivity and the water potential gradient between plant and fruit, and the latter by cell wall properties and the turgor of fruit cells (Lockhart, 1965; Matthews and Shackel, 2005). In fleshy fruits such as grape, which accumulate high concentrations of soluble sugars, carbon fluxes may also [SOURCE: Page_4]

t ip influence water flux by altering water potential gradients between the plant and fruit through changes in fruit osmotic potential (Coombe, 1992; Wada et al., 2008; Wada et al., 2009; Keller and [SOURCE: Page_4]

cr Shrestha, 2014; Zhang and Keller, 2017). Therefore, it is essential to investigate the regulation and coordination of water and carbon fluxes during expansive growth as they determine fruit yield and [SOURCE: Page_4]

us their ratio largely determines fruit composition, e.g. sugar concentration (Guichard et al., 2001; Nardozza et al., 2017; Kawasaki and Higashide, 2018). an The growth of a grape berry typically displays a double sigmoidal growth curve in which two Version postprint [SOURCE: Page_4]

phases of rapid growth, stages I and III, are separated by a lag phase, stage II (Coombe, 1992). The M onset of ripening is referred to as véraison and has been associated with the transition from stage II to stage III. At véraison, the resumption of rapid berry growth is accompanied by turgor loss, softening, sugar accumulation, organic acid degradation, cell wall loosening, xylem hydraulic d [SOURCE: Page_4]

changes, and colour accumulation in red cultivars (Coombe, 1992; Nunan et al., 1998; Huang and te [SOURCE: Page_4]

Huang, 2001; Tyerman et al., 2004; Castellarin et al., 2016). At the same time the main water transport pathway changes from xylem to phloem (Lang and Thorpe, 1989; Greenspan et al., 1996; ep [SOURCE: Page_4]

Keller et al., 2015), and sugar transport shifts from the symplastic to apoplastic pathway (Zhang et al., 2006). Similar changes are observed in other fleshy fruits (Morandi et al., 2010; Clearwater et al., c [SOURCE: Page_4]

2012; Gould et al., 2013; Brüggenwirth et al., 2016). Ac [SOURCE: Page_4]

The complex changes in berry physiology that occur at véraison make it difficult to differentiate the importance of each process in controlling the resumption of growth. The rapid growth of post- véraison berries occurs under an extremely low and relatively stable turgor of about 0.01–0.05 MPa (Thomas et al., 2006; Thomas et al., 2008; Matthews et al., 2009; Castellarin et al., 2016), and there is no correlation between growth rate and turgor of fruit cells (Matthews et al., 1987). Therefore, it was postulated that post-véraison growth might be controlled by cell wall extensibility and/or a changing turgor threshold for cell expansion (Matthews et al., 1987; Huang and Huang, 2001; Matthews et al., 2009; Castellarin et al., 2016). Berry growth module te [SOURCE: Page_7]

The berry growth module was an adaptation of a fruit growth model originally developed for peach ep [SOURCE: Page_7]

(Fishman and Génard, 1998; Dai et al., 2008) and simulated the growth of an individual grape berry from the post-véraison developmental stage when cell division had ceased. In this module, berry growth was driven by two environmental variables (temperature and relative humidity), and two c [SOURCE: Page_7]

plant variables (xylem water potential and phloem sucrose concentration). The plant variables were Ac [SOURCE: Page_7]

calculated hourly by the whole-plant model. The berry growth module assumed that: (i) a grape berry can be considered as one compartment (a cell community with a constant number of growing cells) separated by a composite membrane from the parent vine and the outside environments; (ii) the Lockhart equation originally applied to a single cell can describe the effect of hydrostatic pressure on the irreversible cell wall expansion in this average compartment (Lockhart, 1965; Fishman and Génard, 1998). A berry cluster was considered as a collection of berries, assuming all berries are identical. Thus total carbon or water uptake by a berry cluster equal the carbon or water uptake by a single berry multiplied by the number of berries. [SOURCE: Page_7]
