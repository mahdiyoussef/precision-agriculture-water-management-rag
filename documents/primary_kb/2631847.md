Abstract [SOURCE: Page_4]

Neutron intensity measured by the aboveground cosmic-ray neutron intensity probe (CRP) allows estimating soil moisture content at the field scale. In this work, synthetic neutron intensities were used to remove the bias of simulated soil moisture content or update soil hydraulic properties (together with soil moisture) in the Community Land Model (CLM) using the Local Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter. The cosmic-ray forward model COSMIC was used as the non-linear measurement operator which maps between neutron intensity and soil moisture. The novel aspect of this work is that synthetically measured neutron intensity was used for real time updating of soil states and soil properties (or soil moisture bias) and posterior use for the real time scheduling of irrigation (data assimilation based real-time control approach). Uncertainty of model forcing and soil properties (sand fraction, clay fraction and organic matter density) were considered in the ensemble predictions of the soil moisture profiles. Horizontal and vertical weighting of soil moisture was introduced in the data assimilation in order to handle the scale mismatch between the cosmic-ray footprint and the CLM grid cell. The approach was illustrated in a synthetic study with the real-time irrigation scheduling of fields of citrus trees. After adjusting soil moisture content by assimilating neutron intensity, the irrigation requirements were calculated based on the water deficit method. Model bias was introduced by using coarser soil texture in the data assimilation experiments than in reality. A series of experiments was done with different combinations of state, parameter and bias estimation in combination with irrigation scheduling. Assimilation of CRP neutron intensity improved soil moisture characterization. Irrigation requirement was overestimated if biased soil properties were used. The soil moisture bias was reduced by 35% after data assimilation. The scenario of joint state-parameter estimation resulted in the best soil moisture characterization (50% decrease in root mean square error compared to open loop simulations), and the best estimate of needed irrigation amount (86% decrease in Hausdorff distance compared [SOURCE: Page_4]

to open loop). The coarse scale synthetic CRP observation was proven to be useful for the fine scale soil moisture and soil properties estimation for the objective of irrigation scheduling. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Keywords: Data Assimilation; Cosmic ray; Soil Moisture; Parameter Estimation; Bias Estimation; Irrigation Scheduling [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 1.     Introduction

Globally, 70% of fresh water is used by agriculture (FAO - Food and Agriculture [SOURCE: Page_6]

Organization of the United Nations). Therefore, it is necessary to increase the water [SOURCE: Page_6]

use efficiency and reduce the water need for crop production, while maintaining crop [SOURCE: Page_6]

yield. Enough water should be applied to meet the requirement of maximum crop [SOURCE: Page_6]

evapotranspiration (ET). Farmers usually base irrigation scheduling on their own [SOURCE: Page_6]

experience taking into account soil water status and crop growth. However, it is [SOURCE: Page_6]

unlikely that the optimal scheduling of irrigation is acquired without the knowledge of [SOURCE: Page_6]

crop water needs. Low cost sensors that measure soil moisture content can be of [SOURCE: Page_6]

advantage. However, these sensors typically have a very small measurement volume [SOURCE: Page_6]

which is much smaller than the scale of the fields where the crops are grown. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Numerical models like crop growth models (Heng et al., 2009) and land surface [SOURCE: Page_6]

models (Wood et al., 2011) can be used for the quantitative estimation of the irrigation [SOURCE: Page_6]

requirement under specific soil water and crop growth conditions. The estimated [SOURCE: Page_6]

irrigation amount can be applied accurately with new agricultural technology like drip [SOURCE: Page_6]

irrigation (Sampathkumar et al., 2012). However, uncertain model input data and [SOURCE: Page_6]

deficits in the model structure result in biased estimates of soil water status, crop [SOURCE: Page_6]

transpiration and therefore irrigation requirement. [SOURCE: Page_6]

The optimal scheduling of irrigation is complicated given the high heterogeneity [SOURCE: Page_6]

of soil moisture content in drip irrigated fields. An estimate of soil moisture content [SOURCE: Page_6]

for the complete root zone is important in this context. It is difficult to achieve this [SOURCE: Page_6]

with     small-scale   measurements    (e.g.,   TDR-Time     Domain     Reflectometry, [SOURCE: Page_6]

FDR-Frequency Domain Reflectometry or TDT-Time Domain Transmission) as a [SOURCE: Page_7]

prohibitively large number of sensors is needed to cover large irrigated areas. Soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

moisture information from remote sensing on the other hand is limited to the upper [SOURCE: Page_7]

few soil centimeters, and often has a very coarse horizontal resolution (>10 km) [SOURCE: Page_7]

(Entekhabi et al., 2010; Kerr et al., 2010; Montzka et al., 2013). A further limitation [SOURCE: Page_7]

of satellite-derived soil moisture content is that it is not reliable for highly vegetated [SOURCE: Page_7]

areas (Njoku and Chan, 2006) and high uncertainties (Merlin et al., 2009; Montzka et [SOURCE: Page_7]

al., 2013). The spatial variability of soil moisture is controlled by soil hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_7]

properties, meteorological forcing, land cover patterns and topographic features at [SOURCE: Page_7]

different measurement scales. Small scale variability is more driven by soil hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_7]

properties while large scale variability is also more driven by the other factors. Hence, [SOURCE: Page_7]

strengths and weaknesses of each measurement method rely on the additional [SOURCE: Page_7]

uncertainty given by these additional controlling factors (Crow et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_7]

A new promising method which can determine integral root zone soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_7]

from the measured above ground fast neutron intensity (defined as the number of [SOURCE: Page_7]

counted neutrons per unit of time – e.g., counts per hour) has been proposed (Zreda et [SOURCE: Page_7]

al., 2012). This synthetic study focuses on the assimilation of cosmic-ray probe (CRP) [SOURCE: Page_7]

neutron intensity (Bogena et al., 2013; Desilets et al., 2010; Rosolem et al., 2014; [SOURCE: Page_7]

Shuttleworth et al., 2013; Zreda et al., 2008; Zreda et al., 2012). Soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_7]

measurements at the intermediate scale of the cosmic ray probe have the advantage [SOURCE: Page_7]

that they are less affected by small scale variability of soil hydraulic properties. A [SOURCE: Page_7]

further advantage is that soil moisture can be determined for a deeper layer (10-70 cm) [SOURCE: Page_7]

in higher temporal frequency than remote sensing (Rosolem et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_8]

Primary cosmic rays originate from our galaxy and eventually collide with [SOURCE: Page_8]

atmospheric nuclei, generating secondary cosmic rays mainly consisting of neutrons [SOURCE: Page_8]

(Lal and Peters, 1967). Primary cosmic rays create cascades of secondary high-energy [SOURCE: Page_8]

neutrons through colliding with atmospheric nuclei and the high-energy neutrons can [SOURCE: Page_8]

penetrate the atmosphere and collide with nuclei in soils. These collisions in the soil [SOURCE: Page_8]

generate fast neutrons. Some of these fast neutrons are eventually scattered back to [SOURCE: Page_8]

the atmosphere and the fast neutron intensity can be measured with the CRP. The [SOURCE: Page_8]

measured intensity of fast neutrons above the ground depends strongly on soil [SOURCE: Page_8]

moisture content (Hendrick and Edge, 1966; Zreda et al., 2012). CRPs make use of [SOURCE: Page_8]

this principle to estimate soil moisture content for an area of about 600 m diameter [SOURCE: Page_8]

and variable measurement depth (~10-70 cm) depending on the soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_8]

conditions (Zreda et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_8]

Measured neutron intensities above ground need to be corrected for variations in [SOURCE: Page_8]

incoming high-energetic neutrons and atmospheric pressure (Zreda et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_8]

Moreover, as the measured neutron intensity depends on additional sources of [SOURCE: Page_8]

hydrogen (besides of soil moisture), these need to be taken into account in order to [SOURCE: Page_8]

isolate the soil moisture signal. Corrections have been proposed for other hydrogen [SOURCE: Page_8]

sources like atmospheric vapor (Rosolem et al., 2013), lattice water and organic [SOURCE: Page_8]

carbon in the soil (Franz et al., 2013), hydrogen atoms stored in the litter layer [SOURCE: Page_8]

(Bogena et al., 2013) and above-ground biomass (Baatz et al., 2015). Data [SOURCE: Page_8]

assimilation studies have shown the advantage of using measured multi-source soil [SOURCE: Page_8]

moisture observations for improving the soil moisture profile characterization of a [SOURCE: Page_9]

land surface model (Crow et al., 2008; De Lannoy et al., 2007b; Han et al., 2012; [SOURCE: Page_9]

Huang et al., 2008; Reichle et al., 2008; Walker et al., 2001). Measured neutron [SOURCE: Page_9]

intensities have already been used for assimilation in a land surface model to improve [SOURCE: Page_9]

estimates of soil moisture profiles, but the model parameters were calibrated a priori [SOURCE: Page_9]

(Han et al., 2015a; Rosolem et al., 2014; Shuttleworth et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_9]

In this paper we will investigate the benefits of assimilating coarse scale (600 m) [SOURCE: Page_9]

neutron intensity data into the Community Land Model (CLM) for the application of [SOURCE: Page_9]

drip irrigation for citrus trees on a finer scale (100 m) than the CRP scale. The neutron [SOURCE: Page_9]

intensity measured by a synthetic CRP affects a larger area than a typical irrigation [SOURCE: Page_9]

management unit (1 ha in this work). In order to study the impact of soil moisture data [SOURCE: Page_9]

assimilation on irrigation scheduling, the drip irrigation was therefore simulated at a [SOURCE: Page_9]

finer spatial scale than the footprint of a CRP. The drip irrigation was applied at the [SOURCE: Page_9]

vegetated area and resulted in a very heterogeneous soil moisture distribution with the [SOURCE: Page_9]

alternation of patches of wet and dry soil. It is very CPU-intensive to explicitly model [SOURCE: Page_9]

the irrigated patches and the non-irrigated parts, and a simplified implementation was [SOURCE: Page_9]

adopted in this work, which will be further detailed in the methodology section. In the [SOURCE: Page_9]

simulation experiments, CLM was driven by biased soil properties to mimic the [SOURCE: Page_9]

intrinsic model uncertainties. The coarse scale CRP neutron intensity observations [SOURCE: Page_9]

were used to update the field scale heterogeneous soil moisture field through data [SOURCE: Page_9]

assimilation. The joint soil moisture and soil properties (or soil moisture bias) [SOURCE: Page_9]

estimation scheme was evaluated. This is important because soil moisture content and [SOURCE: Page_9]

crop transpiration are sensitive to model parameters (Hou et al., 2012; Rosolem et al., [SOURCE: Page_10]

2012; Schwinger et al., 2010). Typically, field measurements of parameter values are [SOURCE: Page_10]

scarce and very uncertain, especially because of the scale mismatch between a local [SOURCE: Page_10]

measurement and the model scale (Waller et al., 2014). Model parameter estimation [SOURCE: Page_10]

in the context of a data assimilation framework was proven to be successful, using [SOURCE: Page_10]

either an augmented state vector approach (Chen and Zhang, 2006), dual state [SOURCE: Page_10]

parameter estimation (Moradkhani et al., 2005b) or parameter estimation in a loop [SOURCE: Page_10]

external to the data assimilation filter (Vrugt et al., 2005). Successful applications are [SOURCE: Page_10]

reported for such diverse areas as groundwater hydrology (Franssen and Kinzelbach, [SOURCE: Page_10]

2008; Kurtz et al., 2014; Schöniger et al., 2012), rainfall-runoff models (Moradkhani [SOURCE: Page_10]

et al., 2005a; Vrugt et al., 2006), land surface models (Han et al., 2014a; Pauwels et [SOURCE: Page_10]

al., 2009), vadose zone hydrology (Montzka et al., 2011; Wu and Margulis, 2013) and [SOURCE: Page_10]

atmospheric models (Ruiz et al., 2013). A data assimilation framework can consider [SOURCE: Page_10]

uncertain model forcing, model structure and initial conditions, as well as parameter [SOURCE: Page_10]

uncertainties. Data assimilation has become a commonly used method for parameter [SOURCE: Page_10]

estimation, especially for large scale applications (Wanders et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_10]

Joint soil moisture and soil moisture bias estimation has been proven to be helpful [SOURCE: Page_10]

for improving data assimilation results (De Lannoy et al., 2007a; Kumar et al., 2012b) [SOURCE: Page_10]

like soil temperature assimilation with bias correction (Bosilovich et al., 2007; [SOURCE: Page_10]

Reichle et al., 2010). In this study, we also evaluated the impact of the soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_10]

bias estimation method (Dee, 2005) on improving the soil moisture assimilation and [SOURCE: Page_10]

irrigation scheduling and compared it with joint state-parameter estimation. [SOURCE: Page_10]

In Han et al., 2015a, we studied the joint updating of soil moisture, soil [SOURCE: Page_11]

temperature and leaf area index by assimilating CRP neutron intensity and land [SOURCE: Page_11]

surface temperature. In this study however, we considered in addition the joint [SOURCE: Page_11]

updating of soil moisture and soil properties, or soil moisture and soil moisture bias, [SOURCE: Page_11]

and the vertical and horizontal weighting for updating soil moisture in the footprint of [SOURCE: Page_11]

a CRP. This implies that in this work states and parameters for many model grid cells [SOURCE: Page_11]

in the CRP footprint are updated with a single CRP neutron intensity observation. [SOURCE: Page_11]

This is therefore a small multiscale data assimilation experiment with the irrigation [SOURCE: Page_11]

scheduling as one of the objectives. [SOURCE: Page_11]

It is expected that a more accurate characterization of the heterogeneous soil [SOURCE: Page_11]

moisture distribution can be obtained if the coarse scale CRP neutron intensity data [SOURCE: Page_11]

are assimilated using a combination of data assimilation and parameter estimation (or [SOURCE: Page_11]

bias estimation). Based on such results, it is then assumed that the estimated irrigation [SOURCE: Page_11]

requirement could be improved. The objective of this study is to evaluate with help of [SOURCE: Page_11]

a synthetic study: 1) the potential of measured neutron intensity data by the CRP for [SOURCE: Page_11]

improving the characterization of soil moisture content and soil properties (or soil [SOURCE: Page_11]

moisture bias), and 2) the impact of the assimilation of neutron intensity on better [SOURCE: Page_11]

irrigation scheduling and the potential for real-time irrigation optimization. In this [SOURCE: Page_11]

study, the spatial variability of soil properties and crop status will be considered in the [SOURCE: Page_11]

data assimilation. [SOURCE: Page_11]

2.   Methodology [SOURCE: Page_11]

The main components of the methodology are: (i) measurement of above-ground [SOURCE: Page_12]

neutron intensity, which is linked to field scale soil moisture content by a [SOURCE: Page_12]

measurement operator (section 2.1) and horizontal weights (section 2.3); (ii) the land [SOURCE: Page_12]

surface model CLM (version 4.5) which simulates the transport of water and energy [SOURCE: Page_12]

in the soil-plant-atmosphere continuum (section 2.2); (iii) data assimilation according [SOURCE: Page_12]

to the Local Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter (LETKF) methodology (Hunt et al., [SOURCE: Page_12]

2007) which optimally combines measurements and model predictions to update soil [SOURCE: Page_12]

moisture (and possibly soil properties or soil moisture bias), taking into account [SOURCE: Page_12]

uncertain atmospheric forcing and model parameters (or model bias) (section 2.3) and [SOURCE: Page_12]

(iv) an optimization routine which calculates irrigation need for the ensemble of soil [SOURCE: Page_12]

moisture forecasts (section 2.4). [SOURCE: Page_12]

### 2.2. Community Land Model - CLM

The Community Land Model (CLM - version 4.5) developed by the National [SOURCE: Page_14]

Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR) was used to calculate soil moisture content [SOURCE: Page_14]

and evapotranspiration (Oleson et al., 2013). CLM uses the simplified Richards [SOURCE: Page_14]

equation to model water flow in the unsaturated zone and calculation of land surface [SOURCE: Page_14]

energy fluxes is done by invoking the Monin-Obukhov similarity theory. In addition, [SOURCE: Page_14]

the following processes can be simulated by CLM: transfer of solar radiation and [SOURCE: Page_14]

longwave radiation, stomatal physiology and photosynthesis, crop dynamics and [SOURCE: Page_14]

irrigation (Oleson et al., 2013). The land cover can be represented by 17 plant [SOURCE: Page_14]

functional types (PFTs) and the calculation of energy fluxes is based on the PFTs. [SOURCE: Page_14]

Hydraulic and thermal parameters in CLM are derived based on soil properties such [SOURCE: Page_14]

as sand and clay fraction and organic matter density (Oleson et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_14]

### 3.4. Irrigation Scheduling

In Valencia, citrus trees are typically irrigated 3~6 days per week from April to [SOURCE: Page_26]

October. For simplicity, the irrigation duration was assumed to be two hours from [SOURCE: Page_26]

06:00 AM onwards, and the irrigation was applied every three days for reasons of [SOURCE: Page_26]

computational efficiency. CLM was run in prediction mode for three days to estimate [SOURCE: Page_26]

the needed amount of irrigation (at 06:00) using the predefined atmospheric forcing [SOURCE: Page_26]

data. The first assimilation of soil moisture data was done at 23:00, seven hours [SOURCE: Page_26]

before the start of the second irrigation period. [SOURCE: Page_26]

Five irrigation estimation scenarios were designed to assimilate the CRP neutron [SOURCE: Page_26]

intensity in the land surface model and to evaluate the impact of data assimilation, [SOURCE: Page_26]

parameter estimation and bias estimation on the characterization of the soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_26]

profile, irrigation amount and evapotranspiration: [SOURCE: Page_26]

(1) Irrigation estimation with the true soil properties and atmospheric forcing [SOURCE: Page_26]

(Reference). [SOURCE: Page_26]

(2) Irrigation estimation with biased soil properties, without CRP neutron [SOURCE: Page_26]

intensity assimilation (No_DA, i.e. Open loop). [SOURCE: Page_26]

(3) Irrigation estimation with biased soil properties, with the assimilation of CRP [SOURCE: Page_27]

neutron intensity (every three days) (Only_DA_SM). [SOURCE: Page_27]

(4) Irrigation estimation with biased soil properties, with both CRP neutron [SOURCE: Page_27]

intensity assimilation (every three days) and soil moisture bias estimation [SOURCE: Page_27]

(DA_SM_Bias). [SOURCE: Page_27]

(5) Irrigation estimation with biased soil properties, with neutron intensity [SOURCE: Page_27]

assimilation (every three days) including soil properties estimation (DA_SM_Par). [SOURCE: Page_27]

For the scenario of DA_SM_Par, the study period included both 2010 and 2011. In [SOURCE: Page_27]

2010, joint soil moisture and soil properties estimation were carried out in order to [SOURCE: Page_27]

update the biased soil properties (sand fraction, clay fraction and organic matter [SOURCE: Page_27]

assimilation without soil properties updating. For the scenarios without soil properties [SOURCE: Page_27]

estimation, the experiments were only carried out for 2011. [SOURCE: Page_27]

The estimation of soil properties during the year 2010 deteriorated in case of [SOURCE: Page_27]

intensive irrigation. In case of intensive irrigation, the soil column is close to [SOURCE: Page_27]

saturation, and the ensemble members show a limited spread. In addition, the [SOURCE: Page_27]

sensitivity of soil moisture with respect to soil properties becomes small. Parameter [SOURCE: Page_27]

estimation (i.e., updating of soil properties) is not very efficient during these periods. [SOURCE: Page_27]

Therefore, soil properties were not updated if the accumulated irrigation amount [SOURCE: Page_27]

between two data assimilation time steps was larger than 10 mm. [SOURCE: Page_27]

### 5.   Discussion

The proposed data assimilation and parameter estimation (or bias estimation) can [SOURCE: Page_34]

improve the soil moisture and irrigation estimation. The joint state-parameter [SOURCE: Page_34]

estimation is the best scenario, and reduced the RMSE values of soil moisture content [SOURCE: Page_34]

more than 50%, the spatial similarity of irrigation amount was increased and the HD [SOURCE: Page_34]

values were decreased by 86% on average. The novelty of this work was the [SOURCE: Page_34]

assimilation of the new CRP data in combination with irrigation scheduling. In [SOURCE: Page_34]

general, classical parameter estimation tends to focus on uncertainty in the parameter [SOURCE: Page_34]

estimates only, while neglecting partial or all of the other uncertainty sources (Liu and [SOURCE: Page_34]

Gupta, 2007). We did not aim to compare the parameter estimation methodology with [SOURCE: Page_34]

other methodologies in this study. The synthetic CRP neutron intensity observations [SOURCE: Page_35]

were assimilated in CLM and the synthetic study potentially overestimated the [SOURCE: Page_35]

performance of the proposed method. [SOURCE: Page_35]

In a real-world application, the model will show systematical biases and also the [SOURCE: Page_35]

implementation of the project area in the model is a strong simplification which might [SOURCE: Page_35]

generate additional bias. A complication for the application of the data assimilation [SOURCE: Page_35]

system in a real-world application is therefore the presence of model structural bias, [SOURCE: Page_35]

and parameter estimation could compensate for this bias so that the estimated [SOURCE: Page_35]

parameter values are not necessarily closer to the true parameter values. The approach [SOURCE: Page_35]

will try to identify the effective parameter values that maximize model performance at [SOURCE: Page_35]

that scale (Wagener et al., 2007). Therefore, as an alternative, instead of updating [SOURCE: Page_35]

states and parameters jointly, also states and bias could be estimated jointly. It was [SOURCE: Page_35]

shown in this paper that both approaches gave improvements. Although uncertainty of [SOURCE: Page_35]

soil hydraulic parameters is important in the context of irrigation scheduling, it might [SOURCE: Page_35]

be difficult to infer better estimates of soil hydraulic parameters due to other sources [SOURCE: Page_35]

of uncertainty like model structural bias. On the other hand, this does not need to [SOURCE: Page_35]

hamper successful operational implementation of the proposed method. Hendricks [SOURCE: Page_35]

Franssen et al (2011) demonstrated the feasibility of operational prediction of [SOURCE: Page_35]

groundwater levels (Franssen et al., 2011), coupled to operational optimization of [SOURCE: Page_35]

groundwater management at the same site (Bauser et al., 2012). The water works [SOURCE: Page_35]

Zurich applied this methodology (Franssen et al., 2011) now for the period 2009-2015, [SOURCE: Page_35]

with consistent better predictions than for the open loop run. However, in the [SOURCE: Page_35]

operational implementation on-line parameter estimation was avoided and only states [SOURCE: Page_36]

were updated. It is therefore possible that for on-line irrigation scheduling a [SOURCE: Page_36]

conservative, potentially less successful strategy should be followed where only states [SOURCE: Page_36]

are updated. This synthetic study showed that state updating only also would improve [SOURCE: Page_36]

irrigation scheduling considerably. We believe therefore that although in a real-world [SOURCE: Page_36]

case study results will be less favorable than in the synthetic study, data assimilation [SOURCE: Page_36]

with updating states only, or joint updating of states and bias (or parameters) in case [SOURCE: Page_36]

of a systematic bias, will improve irrigation scheduling compared to a scenario [SOURCE: Page_36]

without data assimilation. [SOURCE: Page_36]

An additional challenge for the real world application is the forward modeling of [SOURCE: Page_36]

the CRP neutron intensity. The measured CRP neutron intensity needs to be corrected [SOURCE: Page_36]

for variations in the incoming high-energetic neutrons, the atmospheric pressure and [SOURCE: Page_36]

humidity, lattice water and organic carbon content of the soil, and aboveground [SOURCE: Page_36]

biomass. The aboveground biomass of citrus trees is temporally variable related to the [SOURCE: Page_36]

growth of the oranges (or lemons) over the year. The impact of vegetation water [SOURCE: Page_36]

content on the CRP neutron intensity is still under active study. In principle, an [SOURCE: Page_36]

empirical methodology is suited to correct for the influence of aboveground biomass [SOURCE: Page_36]

on measured neutron count intensity (Baatz et al., 2015). In this study, the [SOURCE: Page_36]

synthetically measured CRP neutron intensity was applied uniformly at the CRP [SOURCE: Page_36]

footprint and a simple multiscale data assimilation scheme was proposed to update the [SOURCE: Page_36]

field scale CLM simulation using coarse scale CRP neutron intensity. This may not be [SOURCE: Page_36]

optimal as all the grid cells within the CRP footprint contribute differently to the [SOURCE: Page_36]

measured CRP neutron intensity. The soil spatial heterogeneity in the CRP footprint [SOURCE: Page_37]

was introduced by adding a random spatially correlated noise. Heterogeneous land [SOURCE: Page_37]

cover was not considered in this study. However, the spatial variability of ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_37]

parameters could be a further confounding parameter influencing the results. [SOURCE: Page_37]

Altogether, accounting for temporally variable biomass in the COSMIC operator does [SOURCE: Page_37]

not seem a large limitation, but spatially variable soil moisture conditions within the [SOURCE: Page_37]

cosmic ray probe footprint, are a serious challenge. [SOURCE: Page_37]

Furthermore, the weather forecast is essential to the irrigation scheduling. This [SOURCE: Page_37]

aspect was not considered in this work. If the precise precipitation forecast cannot be [SOURCE: Page_37]

obtained, the irrigation requirement cannot be estimated accurately. [SOURCE: Page_37]

A further important complication for real-world applications is that farmers want [SOURCE: Page_37]

to irrigate the citrus based on their own experience, and in combination with the low [SOURCE: Page_37]

water prices they might not want to follow the suggested irrigation scheduling. [SOURCE: Page_37]

Altogether, we feel that the methodology is suited for real-world applications and can [SOURCE: Page_37]

improve irrigation scheduling compared to more traditional scheduling, but that the [SOURCE: Page_37]

farmer participation is the most critical factor, besides model structural bias and soil [SOURCE: Page_37]

moisture heterogeneity within the cosmic ray probe footprint. [SOURCE: Page_37]

Therefore, the successful real application of the proposed method needs: a [SOURCE: Page_37]

calibrated land surface model, an improved COSMIC operator in which the measured [SOURCE: Page_37]

cosmic-ray neutron intensity is corrected for above and below ground biomass, not [SOURCE: Page_37]

too large spatial variability of soil moisture content within the cosmic ray probe [SOURCE: Page_37]

footprint and a precise weather forecast including uncertainty characterization and [SOURCE: Page_37]

participation of farmers. [SOURCE: Page_38]

A further possible improvement is the consideration of irrigation below ET [SOURCE: Page_38]

requirement, known as deficit irrigation (DI), which can reduce water demand to meet [SOURCE: Page_38]

the maximum ET (Fereres and Soriano, 2007). [SOURCE: Page_38]

### 6.   Conclusions

This study investigated the assimilation of synthetic measurements of coarse [SOURCE: Page_38]

scale CRP neutron intensity in CLM for updating field scale root zone soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_38]

content. The synthetic study mimicked a drip irrigated citrus farmland near Valencia, [SOURCE: Page_38]

Spain. CLM was driven by biased soil properties and the joint estimation of soil [SOURCE: Page_38]

moisture and soil properties (or soil moisture bias) was evaluated in a data [SOURCE: Page_38]

assimilation framework using the state augmentation method. The non-linear [SOURCE: Page_38]

measurement operator COSMIC was used to simulate the CRP neutron intensity on [SOURCE: Page_38]

the basis of the soil moisture profile estimated by the CLM model. Fast neutron [SOURCE: Page_38]

intensity was assimilated directly, and both soil moisture and soil properties (soil [SOURCE: Page_38]

moisture bias) were updated using the LETKF in combination with the CLM model. [SOURCE: Page_38]

The horizontal and vertical weights for the different CLM grid cells in the CRP [SOURCE: Page_38]

footprint were also considered using a Gaussian window function. [SOURCE: Page_38]

The results show that assimilating CRP neutron intensity can improve joint soil [SOURCE: Page_38]

moisture and soil properties estimation, and irrigation scheduling. Data assimilation [SOURCE: Page_38]

schemes that remove soil moisture bias or update soil properties on the basis of CRP [SOURCE: Page_38]

neutron intensity outperform data assimilation without bias or parameter estimation. [SOURCE: Page_38]

The joint soil moisture and soil parameter estimation with simple multiscale [SOURCE: Page_39]

assimilation strategy of CRP neutron intensity can potentially be used for irrigation [SOURCE: Page_39]

scheduling in the future. The main challenges for the real world application are: [SOURCE: Page_39]

model calibration to remove the bias, forward modeling of cosmic-ray neutron [SOURCE: Page_39]

intensity under high vegetation coverage, precise weather forecasts and cooperation of [SOURCE: Page_39]

farmers. [SOURCE: Page_39]
