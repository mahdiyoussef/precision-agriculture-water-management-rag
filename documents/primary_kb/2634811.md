## 41   ABSTRACT

42   Increasingly severe droughts are expected to negatively impact forest functioning in the 43   future, especially in the Mediterranean region. Favouring mixed species stands has been 44   advocated as a compromise between wood production and biodiversity conservation, but 45   whether such management practices would allow forest ecosystems to acclimate to future 46   climate conditions remains to be addressed. We tested whether the transpiration of Quercus 47   cerris (Qc) and Quercus petraea (Qp) during droughts differ when they grow in pure or mixed 48   forests. We measured sap flux density (FD) and leaf carbon isotope composition (δ13C), as a 49   proxy for intrinsic water use efficiency (WUEint), in pure and mixed Qc and/or Qp forest plots 50   in Italy during the summer 2012. Qc and Qp showed a drop in FD of 41% and 52% 51   respectively during the summer drought independently of the mixtures, but with different 52   seasonal trends. Qp transpiration response to soil drought did not differ between pure and 53   mixed plots. In contrast, Qc transpiration was reduced by 56% in mixed plots at the maximum 54   of the drought and only by 31% in the pure plot. Furthermore, we observed higher WUEint for 55   Qc in the mixed plot and no change for Qp, supporting the higher water stress intensity in the 56   mixed plot for Qc. Our study illustrated the negative impact of Qp on Qc when these species Version postprint [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   compete for water resources. We suggest that managing for mixed stands in the Mediterranean 58   region might, in some cases, increase the detrimental effect of drought on species functioning. [SOURCE: Page_4]

## 74   1. INTRODUCTION

75   Both fundamental and applied questions are emerging about potential changes in the 76   functioning of European forest ecosystems in regions subjected to severe climatic changes. 77   Scenarios predict a global increase in the frequency and intensity of atmospheric and soil 78   drought during the growing season throughout Europe (IPCC, 2007) and whether today’s 79   forest ecosystems will acclimate and survive in the upcoming decades remains to be seen. The 80   Mediterranean zone will be one of the regions most affected during the next decades, with a 81   considerable reduction in rainfall during the growing season accompanied by more frequent 82   and more intense heat-waves (Giorgi 2006; Somot et al. 2008). Ecological stability and 83   productivity of Mediterranean forests is particularly sensitive to climatic conditions 84   (Scarascia-Mugnozza et al. 2000). In this context, forest management practices adapted to 85   future climatic change must be implemented (Fabbio et al. 2003). Specifically, these practices 86   should be able to counteract the likely decrease in productivity and increase of catastrophic 87   events such as fires and pests outbreaks so that acceptable levels of goods and services can 88    continue to be provided. [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   Mixed forests have been advocated as a potential compromise between high wood production Version postprint [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   and biodiversity conservation (Kelty 2006) and seem to be an interesting management option 91   to help forest ecosystems acclimate to future climatic conditions. The two mechanisms 92   underlying the positive effects of biodiversity on ecosystem services in mixed forests are: 93   complementarity of resource use arising from facilitation and/or niche stratification between 94   species with different functional traits, and selection effects explained by a higher probability 95   of including one or several highly productive species in more diverse stands (Loreau & 96   Hector 2001). Positive effects of biodiversity on productivity in mixed Mediterranean forests 97   have already been demonstrated (i.e. Vilà et al. 2007); however, species interactions in mixed 98   forests may also strongly influence other ecosystem functions, particularly related to water 99   use under soil water deficit conditions, though studies to date are lacking for these 100   ecosystems. [SOURCE: Page_6]

143   2.2 Drought intensity [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   In order to characterize the drought intensity experienced by the study trees, we estimated the 145   daily relative extractable water in the soil over the whole root zone (REW, unitless) in each 146   plot over 2012. REW is defined as the ratio between available soil water and maximum 147   extractable water. It varies between 1.0 (field capacity) and 0.0 (permanent wilting point). 148   When REW is higher than 0.4, soil water conditions are non-limiting for transpiration of a 149   large range of tree species and stomatal conductance mainly depends on air vapour pressure 150   deficit, irradiance and forest phenology (Granier et al. 1999). ## 244   3. RESULTS

245   3.1 Model simulation of drought intensity [SOURCE: Page_12]

246   On June 1st, i.e. just before the first measurement period (P$_1$), soil water content was close to 247   field capacity (Fig. 1). During P$_1$, REW was well above 0.4 for all plots, suggesting that 248   transpiration was not yet limited by soil water (Fig. 1). From P$_1$ to P$_3$, REW decreased 249   progressively in all three plots (Fig. 1a). During P$_2$, REW was already lower than 0.4 in all 250   plots. P$_3$ revealed even lower REW values in all three plots; furthermore, REW was still 251   decreasing during this period. Over the whole study period, REW reached minimum value in 252   all plots on August 23rd, 2012, i.e. between P$_3$ and P$_4$. REW was lower than 0.4 for a 253   minimum period of 45 days in the mixed plot and a maximum period of 65 days in the Qc 254   pure plot. Finally, rain events between August 23 rd and September 9th 2012 resulted in a sharp 255   increase in REW. During the last period (P$_4$), REW was higher than 0.4 in all plots. Radiation, 256   VPD, and PET were high during P$_1$ and P$_2$ and then decreased in P$_3$ and P$_4$ (Fig. 1b). [SOURCE: Page_12]

257 [SOURCE: Page_12]

258   3.2 Tree transpiration Version postprint [SOURCE: Page_12]

259   As the drought intensified during the summer months, FD Mean of both species and in all plots 260   progressively decreased to a minimum during P$_3$. Thereafter, FD Mean increased from P$_3$ to P$_4$ 261   for both species and in all plots (Fig. 2). [SOURCE: Page_12]

262   In the pure plots, the mixed-effect models revealed significant species (P = 0.001), PET and 263   REW effects (P < 0.001) on FD Mean when considering the four periods individually or 264   combined. For all plots and all periods combined, we observed significant species (P = 0.012) 265   and measurement period (P < 0.001) effects on FD Mean-s, but no effect of the mixture (P = 266   0.081). The interactions between mixture and measurement period or species and 267   measurement period were significant (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3), whereas the interaction between 268   mixture and species was not (P = 0.068). Qc always had significantly higher FD Mean-s values 269   than Qp in all plots. [SOURCE: Page_12]

270   The Tukey contrast tests revealed that FD Mean-s values did not differ from P$_1$ to P$_2$ in the Qc 271   pure plot (Fig. 3), whereas values in P$_3$ were significantly lower (Fig. 3). Conversely, in the 272   Qp pure plot, FD Mean-s was already lower in P$_2$ than in P$_1$ (Fig. 3), and no further decrease in 273   FD Mean-s was observed between P$_2$ and P$_3$. [SOURCE: Page_12]

## 297   4. DISCUSSION

298   4.1 Response to drought of the two oak species in pure conditions [SOURCE: Page_14]

299   Before any soil water deficit occurred (P$_1$), daily sap flux density of Qc was higher than that 300   of Qp in the pure plots (Fig. 2 and 3); this is consistent with previous measurements 301   conducted by Tognetti et al. (1996) on the same species in Italy. [SOURCE: Page_14]

302   Both oak species displayed a marked decrease in PET standardized sap flux density (FD Mean-s) 303   throughout the summer (from P$_1$ to P$_3$) in the pure plots (Fig. 2 and 3), but with different 304   intensities and timing. The decrease in FD Mean-s for Qc occurred between P$_2$ and P$_3$ whereas it 305   occurred between P$_1$ and P$_2$ for Qp (Fig. 2 and 3). The pattern observed for Qc, a “drought 306   tolerant” species sensu Kramer (1983), was consistent with observations by Tognetti et al. 307   (2007) who concluded that this species has a non-conservative water use strategy. Indeed, Qc 308   transpiration regulation occurred late during the dry period. This strategy allows the tree to 309   avoid limiting its carbon assimilation during medium soil water deficit periods, as has 310   previously been observed for other Quercus species (Epron & Dreyer 1993). Conversely, the 311   pattern observed for Qp corresponded to a “water conservative” strategy, which is consistent Version postprint [SOURCE: Page_14]

312   with previous studies conducted on this species (Bréda et al. 1993). Physiological and 313   morphological mechanisms potentially supporting this strategy include rapid stomatal 314   regulation when soil water deficit increases and developing extensive lateral and deep rooting 315   systems (Kramer 1983). It is noteworthy that these different water use strategies actually led 316   to a similar relative decrease in transpiration from P$_1$ to P$_3$ for these species in our study (Fig. 317   4). A differential influence of atmospheric conditions (radiation, VPD) on species 318   transpiration is not a likely explanation for this pattern since we were able to show that these 319   conditions impacted transpiration similarly for the two species; furthermore, we used PET 320   standardized daily sap flux density means in our calculations. Our results indicate that under 321   similar environmental conditions, contrasted water use strategies may not necessarily confer 322   any clear advantage to a given species in terms of transpiration response to drought. [SOURCE: Page_14]

323   Nevertheless, the rainy episodes at the end of August (between P$_3$ and P$_4$) were clearly more 324   beneficial in terms of transpiration for Qc than for Qp (Fig. 3); this can be explained by the 325   shallower rooting system in Qc (Nardini 1999) than for Qp. As evaporative demand (VPD and 326   ETP) was very low during P$_4$ in comparison to P$_1$, the lower transpiration rates of Qc during 327   P1could be explained by stricter stomatal control against atmospheric drought at the 328   beginning of the summer than at the end of the drought. [SOURCE: Page_14]

330   4.2 Impact of species interactions on the response to drought [SOURCE: Page_15]

331   In this study, we revealed the differential impact of competition for water resources between 332   the two species on their response to soil water deficit. Indeed, while tree transpiration rates for 333   Qp did not differ between the mixed and the pure plot, Qc had much lower transpiration rates 334   in the mixed plot (Fig. 3). Furthermore, Qc response to drought was timed differently between 335   pure and mixed conditions (Fig. 3): FD Mean-s of Qc decreased much earlier during the summer 336   in the mixed plot than in the pure plot, even though the decrease in REW was slightly lower in 337   the mixture (Fig. 1). We have thus demonstrated a clearly detrimental effect of interspecific 338   competition for water resources on Qc transpiration under soil drought in a Mediterranean 339   forest. [SOURCE: Page_15]

340   Several morphological and/or physiological adaptations may explain the observed patterns. 341   Sap flow measurements were conducted on dominant and co-dominant trees in each plot so 342   differences in crown exposure to atmospheric conditions can be excluded as a potential cause 343   for the patterns we found for FD Mean-s. However, a different vertical distribution of the Qc root Version postprint [SOURCE: Page_15]

344   system in pure and mixed plots could lead to a different mean depth of water extraction, thus 345   explaining the patterns observed for Qc. The response of stomatal conductance is assumed to 346   be linked to the soil water deficit experienced by the majority of the roots, even if water is 347   being extracted from deeper depths (Irvine et al. 1998). We can therefore assume that the Qc 348   roots are mostly restricted to the upper soil horizons in the mixed plot. We were unable yet to 349   directly evaluate the mean rooting depth of the sampled trees or to estimate it through indirect 350   approaches such as soil water labelling experiments (e.g. Zapater et al. 2011). Contrary to Qc, 351   Qp typically develops a deep root system when competing with other species, thus allowing 352   the tree to maintain high levels of transpiration during soil water deficit periods (Bréda et al. 353   1993; Leuschner et al. 2001; Zapater et al. 2013). We therefore hypothesize that in mixed 354   plots, where the limiting soil water conditions occur every year, Qp roots consistently 355   outcompeted Qc roots in the deeper and wetter soil horizons, which explained the absence of 356   a negative response in terms of transpiration of Qp although mixed with a higher water 357   consumer such as Qc (Fig. 3). Interspecific competition thus resulted in a negative impact on 358   Qc transpiration as compared to the pure plot situation. For Qp, on the other hand, usable 359   water resources were neither reduced nor improved when the species was mixed with Qc. Our 360   results thus show a negative interaction between the two oak species for soil water use in this 361   Mediterranean ecosystem and, contrary to our initial hypothesis, do not support any [SOURCE: Page_15]

376   4.3 Leaf physiological adjustments in pure vs. mixed conditions Version postprint [SOURCE: Page_16]

377   Differences between Qc and Qp in the response of tree-level transpiration to mixture 378   conditions were accompanied by different leaf-level physiological adjustments. Indeed, while 379   no difference in δ13C was found between pure and mixed trees for Qp, higher δ13C values (and 380   thus WUEint) were found for Qc in the mixed plot (Fig. 4). Higher δ13C values for Qc is 381   consistent with the large body of literature showing higher δ13C and WUEint under higher 382   drought conditions (e.g., Galle et al. 2010; Zhang & Marshall 1994). Enhanced δ13C and 383   WUEint for Qc in the mixed plot was consistent with reduced transpiration and supported our 384   hypothesis of reduced water resource availability in the mixed plot for this species. It should 385   be noted that our δ13C values were obtained on leaves sampled in July 2012, i.e. before any 386   intensive soil water deficit occurred (Fig. 1). As Qc is a deciduous species, these data 387   therefore might reflect a strong, long-term physiological acclimation of Qc trees to the 388   different soil water deficit conditions encountered every year in the different plots. For Qp, 389   the similar δ13C values between plots was also consistent with transpiration data and further 390   indicated that this species neither benefited nor suffered from the interactions with Qc in 391   terms of water use. 393   In conclusion, under severe soil water deficit conditions, we highlighted an interesting 394   differential response pattern in terms of transpiration and water use efficiency between 395   Quercus cerris (Qc) and Quercus petraea (Qp) when they are growing in pure plots or when 396   they are competing for water resources in mixed species conditions. Qp sap flux density was 397   neither negatively affected nor favoured by species interactions as would have been expected 398   had complementarity in water resource use occurred. On the contrary, Qc was negatively 399   impacted in its water use when growing in mixed conditions with Qp, an oak species that is 400   known to be able to extract water from deep soil layers. Our results 402   suggest that management practices in the Mediterranean region that favour mixing of Qp and 403   Qc could be detrimental for Qc in terms of water use and survival in the expected context of 404   more frequent and more severe droughts.
