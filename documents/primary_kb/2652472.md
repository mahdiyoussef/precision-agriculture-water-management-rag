33   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_4]

34   In mixed forests, interactions among species influence ecosystem functioning but [SOURCE: Page_4]

35   environmental conditions also play an important role in shaping relationships between [SOURCE: Page_4]

36   biodiversity and ecosystem functioning. In the context of climate change, the carbon and [SOURCE: Page_4]

37   water balance in pure vs. mixed forest stands may be differentially influenced by changing [SOURCE: Page_4]

38   soil water availability. To test this hypothesis, we compared the influence of biodiversity on [SOURCE: Page_4]

39   water use efficiency (WUES) in boreal forests between wet and dry years. We assessed the [SOURCE: Page_4]

40   carbon isotope composition (δ13C) of tree rings in Betula pendula, Pinus sylvestris and Picea [SOURCE: Page_4]

41   abies growing in pure vs. mixed stands. In addition, we tested whether differences in WUES [SOURCE: Page_4]

42   affected patterns of basal area increment (BAIS). No biodiversity effect was found for stand [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   δ13C (δ13CS) during the wet year. However, there was a significant increase in δ13CS between [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   the wet and the dry year and a significant effect of biodiversity on δ13CS in the dry year. The [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   increase in δ13CS in mixed stands was associated with both selection and complementarity [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   effects. Although BAIS decreased significantly in the dry year, changes in δ13CS did not [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   translate into variations in BAIS along the biodiversity gradient. Our results confirmed that the [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   physiological response of boreal forest ecosystems to changing soil water conditions is [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   influenced by species interactions and that during dry growing seasons, species interactions in [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   mixed stands can lead to lower soil moisture availability. This illustrates that biodiversity [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   effects can also be negative in mixed stands in the sense that soil resources can be more [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   intensively exhausted. Overall, our results confirm that in boreal forests, the biodiversity- [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   ecosystem functioning relationship depends on local environmental conditions. [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   Key-words: biodiversity, boreal forest, drought, δ13C, mixed forest, water use efficiency [SOURCE: Page_4]

## 55   INTRODUCTION

56   The biodiversity and ecosystem functioning (B-EF) relationship has received growing interest [SOURCE: Page_5]

57   from ecologists throughout the last two decades due to the rapid loss of diversity observed [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   during the last century (Symstad and others 2003). Several mechanisms have been put [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   forward to explain how community composition and richness can either negatively or [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   positively influence ecosystem functions. A negative influence on biodiversity can result from [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   direct competition for resources among species in a given ecosystem. Negative interactions [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   commonly arise when ecological niches overlap and/or when species share the same [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   functional characteristics and are thus functionally redundant in the ecosystem (Naeem 2008; [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   Vilà and Sardans 1999). In contrast, positive B-EF relationships are commonly attributed to [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   two other mechanisms: complementarity and selection. Complementary use of resources [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   among species refers both to ecological niche partitioning and facilitation (Loreau and Hector [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   2001) and implies species coexistence without major interspecific competition for resources. [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   The selection effect recognises that the probability of occurrence of high-performing species [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   is greater in highly diverse ecosystems (Loreau and Hector 2001). Studies investigating [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   complementarity and selection effects mainly focused on ecosystem productivity (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   Bradford 2011; Fargione and others 2007; Isbell and others 2009; Morin and others 2011; [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   Zhang and others 2012), while only a few were interested in other ecosystem functional traits [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   (e.g. Forrester and others 2010; Kunert and others 2012; Meinen and others 2009). [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   In addition to competition, complementarity and selection effects, local environmental [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   conditions also play an important role in shaping B-EF relationships (Belote and others 2011; [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   Hooper and Dukes, 2004). Under the assumptions of the “stress-gradient” hypothesis, which [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   predicts that the net outcome of biotic interactions (competition and facilitation) shifts from [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   negative to positive along gradients of limiting physical conditions (Bertness and Callaway [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   1994), positive biodiversity effects are expected to be more common in severely resource- [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

80    limited conditions while negative effects should prevail in richer and milder environments. [SOURCE: Page_6]

81    This general conceptual model has recently been refined (Maestre and others 2009) and is [SOURCE: Page_6]

82    widely supported in the literature (Herbert and others 2004; Jucker and Coomes 2012; Steudel [SOURCE: Page_6]

83    and others 2012; He and others 2013; Wang and others 2013). In the context of climatic [SOURCE: Page_6]

84    change, most regions around the world are expected to encounter more extreme [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   environmental conditions (IPCC, 2007). The “stress-gradient” hypothesis is therefore of great [SOURCE: Page_6]

86    interest since B-EF relationships are likely to change in the future. Whether or not more [SOURCE: Page_6]

87    diverse ecosystems might be better adapted and/or more resilient to these changes is an [SOURCE: Page_6]

88    important issue to investigate. [SOURCE: Page_6]

89    In the boreal climate zone, simulations predict a general shift from short, cool summers [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   towards longer, warmer summers (IPCC 2007; Jylhä and others 2010). Thus, boreal forest [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   ecosystems are expected to encounter more frequent and intense reduced soil water [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   availability in summer. Contrasted responses of carbon and water fluxes in forest ecosystems [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   to warmer and drier climate have already been observed (reviewed in Boisvenue and Running [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   2006). Few studies so far were conducted on the response of boreal forests to these [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   conditions. Nevertheless, Dulamsuren and others (2010) showed that increasing summer [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   temperatures accompanied by decreasing precipitation lead to reduced productivity in taiga [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   forests in Mongolia. [SOURCE: Page_6]

98    Plants adapt to reduced soil water conditions through numerous physiological and/or [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   morphological processes (review in Kozlowski and Pallardy 2002). At the leaf level, under [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   drought conditions, they must manage the trade-off between optimum carbon gain for growth [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   and loss of water through transpiration (Farquhar and others 1982) which usually results in an [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   increase in intrinsic water use efficiency (WUEint), defined as the ratio between CO$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   assimilation during photosynthesis and stomatal conductance for water vapour (e.g. Zhang [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   and Marshall 1994). At ecosystem level, carbon and water fluxes are influenced by species- [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   specific functional responses to environmental conditions and by intra- and inter-specific [SOURCE: Page_7]

106   interactions both below- and above-ground. Both positive and negative interactions among [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   species may occur in mixed stands and this may lead to differing spatial and temporal [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   resource availability and physiological and morphological adaptations within species. These [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   complex interactions mean that general predictions on stand-level water use efficiency [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   (WUEs) under dry conditions cannot solely be based on individual species responses to these [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   conditions. [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   In this context, little information is available concerning the relationship between tree [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   biodiversity and the regulation of carbon and water fluxes in boreal forest ecosystems. [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   Gamfeldt and others (2013) found positive relationships between tree species richness and [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   multiple ecosystem services (tree biomass, soil carbon storage, berry production and game [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   production potential) in production forests in Sweden and explained the observed positive B- [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   EF relationship by facilitation processes among tree species. In contrast, Grossiord and others [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   (2013a) found no complementary effect for biomass production and WUE S in a young boreal [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   plantation, though they did find a weak selection effect. These studies, however, did not [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   compare the B-EF relationship in contrasted environmental conditions. [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   In this study, we tested the following assumptions: i) in boreal forest ecosystems, the stand- [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   level carbon and water balance under non-limiting soil water conditions (wet year) should not [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   necessarily depend on biodiversity effects, and ii) under limiting soil water conditions (dry [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   year), species identity, species combinations and richness levels should influence ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   functioning. We analyzed the influence of species richness on time-integrated WUE S [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   estimated from the carbon isotope composition (δ13C) (Farquhar and others 1982) of ring [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   whole wood measured in tree rings from two years with contrasting soil water conditions (wet [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   year in 2004 and dry year in 2006). Samples were taken from 26 mature stands in Eastern [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   Finland, which were either pure or mixed with varying percentages of Betula pendula, Pinus [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   sylvestris and Picea abies. We also tested whether changes in WUE S would influence stand [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   basal area increment (BAIS). [SOURCE: Page_8]

## 134   MATERIALS AND METHODS

135   Site description [SOURCE: Page_9]

136   The study was conducted in August 2012 in 26 stands of boreal forest that are distributed over [SOURCE: Page_9]

137   a 2000 km^2 area around Joensuu, Finland (62.60°N, 29.76°E). The stands were 30 m x 30 m [SOURCE: Page_9]

138   and included pure, 2-species mixture, or 3-species mixture of Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris), [SOURCE: Page_9]

139   Norway spruce (Picea abies) and Silver birch (Betula pendula). Four replicates of each [SOURCE: Page_9]

140   combination were selected except for pure birch stands that were sampled only twice. Mean [SOURCE: Page_9]

141   tree age within the stands was 33.9 ± 5.8 years. Tree species characteristics are given in Table [SOURCE: Page_9]

142   1. [SOURCE: Page_9]

143   The stands were selected according to the following specifications: no change in management [SOURCE: Page_9]

144   had occurred in the last 5 years; confounding factors like altitude, topography or soil type [SOURCE: Page_9]

145   were kept to a minimum between selected stands; in a given forest patch, the stands were [SOURCE: Page_9]

146   more than 500 m apart to avoid spatial autocorrelation; stands were mostly even-aged and [SOURCE: Page_9]

147   single-layered. More detailed information on the selection procedure of the study stands can [SOURCE: Page_9]

148   be found at http://www.fundiveurope.eu. The selected stands were situated between 80 and [SOURCE: Page_9]

149   200 m a.s.l. on Podzols soils (FAO classification) above mica schist bedrock. Mean annual [SOURCE: Page_9]

150   rainfall in the region is around 700 mm and mean annual temperature is 2.1°C. [SOURCE: Page_9]

152   Selection of the target years [SOURCE: Page_9]

153   The daily water balance model “BILJOU” (Granier and others 1999) was implemented in [SOURCE: Page_9]

154   order to quantify soil water availability at a daily time-scale in the study region and then to [SOURCE: Page_9]

155   select the driest and the wettest year over the past fifteen years. We used above-canopy [SOURCE: Page_9]

156   measurements of daily meteorological conditions (rainfall, global radiation, air temperature [SOURCE: Page_9]

157   and humidity, wind speed) from the Hyytiälä forestry field station (61.84°N, 24.28°E, 153 m [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

158   a.s.l.) to run the model. Although this meteorological station is situated 300 km south-west of [SOURCE: Page_10]

159   Joensuu, it is the nearest source from which data could be obtained to estimate daily relative [SOURCE: Page_10]

160   extractable soil water (REW) over the 1997-2011 period (Fig. 1). The model was initialized [SOURCE: Page_10]

161   assuming that soil was at field capacity on 1 st January. We used a leaf area index of 5.0 m 2 m-2 [SOURCE: Page_10]

162   and a soil holding capacity of 125 mm as input data for model simulations. We discarded the [SOURCE: Page_10]

163   years 1998 and 2010 because of too many missing data. Soil water deficit was assumed to [SOURCE: Page_10]

164   occur when the REW dropped below 0.4, a threshold value that has been shown to induce [SOURCE: Page_10]

165   stomatal closure and interrupt radial growth in forest trees (Granier and others 1999). For each [SOURCE: Page_10]

166   remaining year from 1997 to 2011, we characterized annual water stress using the starting day [SOURCE: Page_10]

167   and the duration of the period when REW dropped below the threshold limit coupled with a [SOURCE: Page_10]

168   water stress index which cumulates the difference between simulated REW and the 0.4 [SOURCE: Page_10]

## 258   RESULTS

259   Wood carbon isotope composition [SOURCE: Page_15]

260   We observed considerable variability in δ13Ci across species, species mixtures and years, [SOURCE: Page_15]

261   ranging from -28.7 ‰ for birch in a 2-species mixture with spruce to -26.2 ‰ for spruce in a [SOURCE: Page_15]

262   2-species mixture with pine during the wet year, and from -27.4 ‰ for birch in a mixture with [SOURCE: Page_15]

263   spruce to -24.2 ‰ for pine in a 3-species mixture during the dry year. Mixed-effect models [SOURCE: Page_15]

264   revealed a significant species effect (P < 0.001), year effect (P < 0.001) and an interaction [SOURCE: Page_15]

265   between Shannon index and year (P = 0.019), but no effect of the Shannon index alone, the [SOURCE: Page_15]

266   interactions of the Shannon index and the species, or the interactions of the year and the [SOURCE: Page_15]

267   species (P > 0.050). The significant effect of the interaction between Shannon index and year [SOURCE: Page_15]

268   was only observed for pine and spruce (P = 0.012 and P = 0.020, respectively), indicating for [SOURCE: Page_15]

269   these species a differential response of δ13Ci to the mixture in the two years. [SOURCE: Page_15]

270   For each species, whatever the richness level, we found significantly higher values of δ13Ci [SOURCE: Page_15]

271   during the dry year than during the wet year (P = 0.003 for birch, P = 0.017 for pine and P = [SOURCE: Page_15]

272   0.037 for spruce). For the wet year, we did not observe any effect of the Shannon index or of [SOURCE: Page_15]

## 326   DISCUSSION

327   1. Climate effect [SOURCE: Page_18]

328   Our stable isotope analysis revealed a strong physiological response in boreal trees to [SOURCE: Page_18]

329   changing soil water conditions and helped clarify the mechanisms responsible for observed [SOURCE: Page_18]

330   changes both at the leaf and stand level. The increase in both individual species’ δ13Ci and [SOURCE: Page_18]

331   stand level δ13CS from wet to dry years (Fig. 2) reflects an active regulation of carbon and [SOURCE: Page_18]

332   water fluxes in this boreal ecosystem and the strong sensitivity of the three species to limiting [SOURCE: Page_18]

333   soil water conditions. Assuming that the difference in δa between the two selected years was [SOURCE: Page_18]

334   negligible, the magnitude of the increase in δ13CS reflected a mean increase in WUES of 17.0 ± [SOURCE: Page_18]

335   1.8 µmol mol-1 (i.e. +19.2 ± 1.5 %). This increase is consistent with the extensive body of [SOURCE: Page_18]

336   literature documenting the functional response of boreal trees to drought conditions (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_18]

337   Vaganov and others 2009; Zhang and Marshall 1994; Saurer and others 2004). This pattern [SOURCE: Page_18]

338   indicates that in the context of climate change, when more frequent periods of water shortage [SOURCE: Page_18]

339   in north-European regions can be expected (IPCC 2007), the three locally-adapted species [SOURCE: Page_18]

340   studied here will be more efficient in using available water resources for carbon acquisition. [SOURCE: Page_18]

341   However, carbon sequestration and storage rates in wood may well decline; indeed, we found [SOURCE: Page_18]

342   that the increase in stand δ13CS during the dry year was associated with a decrease in BAIS. [SOURCE: Page_18]

343   This is unsurprising as numerous studies conducted in boreal forests have shown lower [SOURCE: Page_18]

344   productivity under water limiting conditions (e.g. Kljun and others 2007). [SOURCE: Page_18]

345 [SOURCE: Page_18]

346   2. Biodiversity effects under wet conditions [SOURCE: Page_18]

347   Our results confirmed our first hypothesis that under non-limiting soil water conditions there [SOURCE: Page_18]

348   would be little or no effect of biodiversity on δ13CS and BAIS (Fig. 2). Interactions among [SOURCE: Page_18]

349   species under non-limiting soil water conditions did not lead to any clear B-EF relationships [SOURCE: Page_18]

350   in our boreal study forests, which is consistent with the expectations of the “stress-gradient” [SOURCE: Page_18]

17 [SOURCE: Page_18]

351   hypothesis (Bertness and Callaway 1994). This result is also consistent with a recent study we [SOURCE: Page_19]

352   conducted over a wet summer on the influence of biodiversity on species- and stand-level [SOURCE: Page_19]

353   carbon and water balance in a young boreal plantation in Finland (Grossiord and others [SOURCE: Page_19]

354   2013a). Therein, we argued that in addition to non-limiting soil water conditions, the young [SOURCE: Page_19]

355   ontogenic stage of the plantation likely caused the absence of any biodiversity effect. [SOURCE: Page_19]

356   However, the present study does not support this assumption. Similar observations made in [SOURCE: Page_19]

357   other ecosystem types also concluded on the absence and/or the decrease of biodiversity [SOURCE: Page_19]

358   effects under non-limiting environmental conditions (e.g. Hughes and Stachowicz 2004; [SOURCE: Page_19]

359   Steudel and others 2012). [SOURCE: Page_19]

360 [SOURCE: Page_19]

361   3. Contrasting biodiversity effects under wet and dry conditions [SOURCE: Page_19]

362   The major result of this study was the positive relationship between biodiversity and the [SOURCE: Page_19]

363   difference in δ13CS between dry and wet years (Δδ13CS) (Fig. 2). Drier conditions globally [SOURCE: Page_19]

364   enhanced δ13CS, and thus WUES, but this influence depended on species interactions. Even [SOURCE: Page_19]

365   though the relationship was rather dispersed, stands with greater diversity showed the greatest [SOURCE: Page_19]

366   increase in δ13CS, pointing to a greater compromise between carbon accumulation and water [SOURCE: Page_19]

367   consumption under limiting soil water conditions. However, those changes in ecosystem-scale [SOURCE: Page_19]

368   physiological acclimatization to drought failed to minimize the decrease in BAI S during the [SOURCE: Page_19]

369   dry year and did not influence the productivity of these plots the following year (Fig. 2). The [SOURCE: Page_19]

370   literature actually mentions this apparent inconsistency (e.g. Vaganov and others 2009). The [SOURCE: Page_19]

371   compromise between carbon acquisition and water loss at leaf or plant level usually has a [SOURCE: Page_19]

372   moderate effect on how much assimilated carbon is invested in secondary growth (Flanagan [SOURCE: Page_19]

373   and Johnsen 1995). Carbon allocation to above- or below-ground components in response to [SOURCE: Page_19]

374   soil water conditions strongly varies among species (e.g. Axelsson and Axelsson 1986) and [SOURCE: Page_19]

375   could partially explain the absence of enhanced BAIs in our study. [SOURCE: Page_19]

18 [SOURCE: Page_19]

376 [SOURCE: Page_20]

377   4. Origin of the biodiversity effects [SOURCE: Page_20]

378   Partitioning the influence of biodiversity on δ13CS into selection and complementarity effects [SOURCE: Page_20]

379   revealed that during the dry year the proportionally greater increase in WUE S observed in the [SOURCE: Page_20]

380   2-species mixtures was a result of both mechanisms (Fig. 3). Selection effects refer to a higher [SOURCE: Page_20]

381   performance of one or several species in mixtures on the cost of others. Therefore, the pattern [SOURCE: Page_20]

382   of increasing δ13Cs we observed was most likely driven by the two conifers rather than by [SOURCE: Page_20]

383   birch, since both conifers showed higher δ13Ci in 2-species mixtures while birch did not (Table [SOURCE: Page_20]

384   1). Higher δ13CS values in the 2-species mixtures with birch were also partially explained by a [SOURCE: Page_20]

385   complementarity effect, probably as a result of different rooting strategies among the species. [SOURCE: Page_20]

386   Birch is known to have deeper rooting systems that explore larger volumes than those of pine [SOURCE: Page_20]

387   and spruce, thus enabling birch to access both shallow and deep soil layers (Kalliokoski and [SOURCE: Page_20]

388   others 2008). The fact that birch δ13Ci values did not respond to increasing diversity during the [SOURCE: Page_20]

389   dry year supports this assumption and suggests that the effects of intra- and inter-specific [SOURCE: Page_20]

390   interactions for water acquisition for this species are similar. In contrast, the complementarity [SOURCE: Page_20]

391   effect for δ13CS was not significant in stands where the two conifers cohabited. Pinus [SOURCE: Page_20]

392   sylvestris and Picea abies are known to present similar rooting system development when [SOURCE: Page_20]

393   cohabiting in mixed stands (Kalliokoski and others 2008). It therefore appears that the two [SOURCE: Page_20]

394   conifers were in part functionally redundant in terms of carbon and water acquisition and use [SOURCE: Page_20]

395   in these mixed stands. [SOURCE: Page_20]

396   Although in the 3-species mixtures none of the three biodiversity effects were significantly [SOURCE: Page_20]

397   different from zero, overall they were generally consistent with results for the 2-species mixed [SOURCE: Page_20]

398   stands (Fig. 3). Numerous B-EF studies have revealed such saturating or even hump-shaped [SOURCE: Page_20]

399   relationships (e.g. Gamfeld and others 2013; Vilà and others 2013). One explanation for this [SOURCE: Page_20]

400   type of pattern is increasing functional redundancy at higher levels of species richness (as [SOURCE: Page_20]

19 [SOURCE: Page_20]

401   observed for the two conifers here), which leads to faster and more complete exploitation of [SOURCE: Page_21]

402   available resources in stands with greater richness. [SOURCE: Page_21]

403 [SOURCE: Page_21]

404   5. Other potential origins of the biodiversity effects [SOURCE: Page_21]

405   Variations in δ13C in tree rings reflect physiological processes of acclimatization to [SOURCE: Page_21]

406   environmental conditions and physical archives of short- or long-term changes in soil [SOURCE: Page_21]

407   moisture and/or air humidity in forests (Saurer and others 2004; Andreu and others 2008; [SOURCE: Page_21]

408   Mölder and others 2011). Therefore, the significantly higher δ13CS values we observed along [SOURCE: Page_21]

409   the diversity gradient in the dry year could be viewed not only as a consequence of species [SOURCE: Page_21]

410   interactions, but also as a result of lower soil water availability in mixed stands as compared [SOURCE: Page_21]

411   to pure ones. High soil moisture exhaustion in mixed stands during dry years was already [SOURCE: Page_21]

412   reported in other hydrological investigations in mixed species forests (Gebauer and others [SOURCE: Page_21]

413   2012). Such a feedback effect could arise because of the particularly high competitiveness of [SOURCE: Page_21]

414   birch for water resources and its high water consumption (Kalliokoski and others 2008). This [SOURCE: Page_21]

415   would lead to a higher drought exposure for the conifers in the mixed stands and thus reduced [SOURCE: Page_21]

416   transpiration through stomatal regulation. This increased drought exposure may have been [SOURCE: Page_21]

417   also the cause for the absence of any increase in BAIs in the mixed stands. Nevertheless, [SOURCE: Page_21]

418   differences in canopy structure inducing different atmospheric humidity and light conditions [SOURCE: Page_21]

419   between pure and mixed stands (Kelty 2006) as well as below-ground competition among [SOURCE: Page_21]

420   these species for nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus...) may also have contributed to the mixture [SOURCE: Page_21]

421   effects we detected; however, this is beyond the scope of the present study. [SOURCE: Page_21]

## 424   CONCLUSION

425   In our boreal study region, we demonstrated a clear interaction between the [SOURCE: Page_22]

426   biodiversity/ecosystem carbon and water flux relationship and environmental conditions, [SOURCE: Page_22]

427   consistent with the “stress gradient” hypothesis. The increase in δ13CS, and thus in ecosystem- [SOURCE: Page_22]

428   level water use efficiency, with drier soil conditions was much higher in mixed stands than in [SOURCE: Page_22]

429   pure ones, but this did not prevent a decrease in above-ground growth rates with drought. At [SOURCE: Page_22]

430   the species level, functional similarities between the two conifers lead to a redundant [SOURCE: Page_22]

431   contribution of these two species to ecosystem-level carbon and water balance. In contrast, the [SOURCE: Page_22]

432   presence of birch in mixed stands strongly impacted the water use efficiency of the conifers [SOURCE: Page_22]

433   under limiting water conditions. Our results suggest that species interactions in mixed stands [SOURCE: Page_22]

434   during dry growing seasons can lead to lower soil moisture availability as compared to pure [SOURCE: Page_22]

435   ones. This illustrates that biodiversity effects can also be negative in mixed stands in the sense [SOURCE: Page_22]

436   that soil resources can be more intensively exhausted. Our study highlights the need to take [SOURCE: Page_22]

437   into account not only species growth characteristics but also species functional traits when [SOURCE: Page_22]

438   managing boreal mixed forest in order to bolster mixed-forests in a context of climate change. [SOURCE: Page_22]
