Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

Microdensity profiles of Douglas-fir’s clones and weather data were used to study phenotypic [SOURCE: Page_2]

plasticity (dendroplasticity). Within-ring microdensity was interpreted as the variation of tree Manuscrit d’auteur / Author manuscript [SOURCE: Page_2]

response to weather-controlled changes of water balance during selected growing-seasons. An [SOURCE: Page_2]

original norm of reaction was obtained by pairing evident points of abrupt changes in the ring [SOURCE: Page_2]

microdensity profiles and in the weather indices. The coefficients of the non-linear models [SOURCE: Page_2]

adjusted to the reaction norms were analysed as dendroplasticity variables. Dendroplasticity [SOURCE: Page_2]

variables were significantly different between geographical locations, years and clones. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Heritability of the dendroplasticity variables ranged from low to high and was similar to [SOURCE: Page_2]

heritability values of microdensity variables such as mean ring density (MRD). Coefficients [SOURCE: Page_2]

of genetic variation of dendroplasticity variables were intermediate between those of wood Manuscrit d’auteur / Author manuscript [SOURCE: Page_2]

density and growth variables. Dendroplasticity variables were phenotypically and genetically Version postprint [SOURCE: Page_2]

related to ring microdensity variables, more strongly to latewood variables. Dendroplasticity [SOURCE: Page_2]

provides retrospective, synthetic and easy-to-interpret information about tree response to the [SOURCE: Page_2]

variation of the balance between water availability and water demand during the growing [SOURCE: Page_2]

season. The proposed model of dendroplasticity is described by a number of parameters that [SOURCE: Page_2]

are linked to a biological meaning. Our results suggest that there is a potential for adaptation [SOURCE: Page_2]

to drought in Douglas-fir, with two mechanisms involved: at individual level, short-term [SOURCE: Page_2]

plastic response during the growing season and, at population level, long-term, between- [SOURCE: Page_2]

generation, evolution process. Manuscrit d’auteur / Author manuscript [SOURCE: Page_2]

Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

Douglas-fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii (Mirb.) Franco) is an important exotic forest species [SOURCE: Page_3]

abundantly planted in Europe, mainly in France (De Champs, 1997; Thivolle-Cazat, 2004). Manuscrit d’auteur / Author manuscript [SOURCE: Page_3]

This species is particularly sensitive to a reduction of water availability during the growing [SOURCE: Page_3]

season (Silen, 1978; Rozenberg and Pâques, 2004; Martinez-Meier et al., 2008a). It has been [SOURCE: Page_3]

recently affected by extreme climate events like the 2003 drought and heat wave in Europe, [SOURCE: Page_3]

with symptoms ranging from partial to complete foliage necrosis (DSF, 2004; Martinez-Meier [SOURCE: Page_3]

et al., 2008b). [SOURCE: Page_3]

A warming climate scenario is predicted (IPCC 2001; 2007), where extreme climate events, [SOURCE: Page_3]

like the one in 2003, are expected to increase in frequency and intensity (Meehl et al., 2004; [SOURCE: Page_3]

Stott et al., 2004; IPCC 2007). This is a challenge for the survival of vulnerable tree species. Manuscrit d’auteur / Author manuscript [SOURCE: Page_3]

Douglas-fir adaptation to the future climate conditions may materialize through different Version postprint [SOURCE: Page_3]

complementary universal processes: exhibiting phenotypic plasticity, through gamete and/or [SOURCE: Page_3]

seed dispersal onto new favorable habitats, and through in situ population evolution (Rehfeldt [SOURCE: Page_3]

et al., 2001; Rehfeldt et al., 2002; St-Clair and Howe, 2007). Climate change appears to be [SOURCE: Page_3]

rapid in the last decades. The rate of change may result too rapid for a single tree generation [SOURCE: Page_3]

to adapt (St-Clair and Howe, 2007). If the celerity of this climatic change is confirmed in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

next years, evolution and migration may play very minor roles in the survival of tree species [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Noss, 2002; Rehfeldt et al., 2002; Savolainen et al., 2004). On the contrary, phenotypic [SOURCE: Page_3]

plasticity, phenotypic by environment dependencies (DeWitt and Scheiner, 2004), may appear Manuscrit d’auteur / Author manuscript [SOURCE: Page_3]

as the most important key process in tree acclimation to unpredictable environmental trends [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Bradshaw, 1965; Bradshaw and Hardwick, 1989) and in the context of the rapid climate [SOURCE: Page_3]

changes (Bréda et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_3]

Wood record across the tree’s life is a measure of individual phenotypic plasticity to climate. [SOURCE: Page_3]

In temperate climate wood is formed during a limited part of the year, the growing season. [SOURCE: Page_3]

cell-walls and smaller lumen diameters closely follow weather variations, from wet to dry [SOURCE: Page_4]

within the growing season (Larson, 1963; 1994). Thus anatomical differences appear as a [SOURCE: Page_4]

plastic reaction to environmental changes. These anatomical changes do not come without a [SOURCE: Page_4]

function, as small capillaries have increased resistance to negative tensions, allowing trees to [SOURCE: Page_4]

maintain the integrity of their hydraulic system under lower water availability (Hacke et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

2001; Domec and Gartner, 2002; Taiz and Zeiger, 2006). [SOURCE: Page_4]

In Douglas-fir, microdensity is strongly related to anatomy (Rathgeber et al., 2006). Wood [SOURCE: Page_4]

microdensity profiles are integrated measures of wood anatomy. Unlike anatomy, Manuscrit d’auteur / Author manuscript [SOURCE: Page_4]

microdensity requires less complex analytical approaches. Recent results show that Douglas- Version postprint [SOURCE: Page_4]

firs surviving to the 2003 drought and heat wave have significantly different microdensity [SOURCE: Page_4]

profiles over time before the event compared to non-surviving neighbors (Martinez-Meier et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al., 2008b), underlining a potential use of microdensity as an adaptation marker. [SOURCE: Page_4]

The evaluation of phenotypic plasticity requires the construction of a norm of reaction (NoR), [SOURCE: Page_4]

a phenotypic response by a given genotype to an environmental gradient (Sarkar, 1999; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Fuller, 2003; Gibert et al., 2004). The NoR obtained from a microdensity profile is denoted [SOURCE: Page_4]

dendroplasticity NoR where the within-ring microdensity variation is related to within- [SOURCE: Page_4]

growing season weather variation. Sanchez-Vargas et al. (2007) fitted linear models for [SOURCE: Page_4]

measuring dendroplasticity in one growing-season (1996) in three clonal sites with different [SOURCE: Page_4]

clone composition, using different drought indices for each site. In this study we improve the [SOURCE: Page_4]

model used by Sanchez-Vargas et al. (2007) by the use of a unique, biologically more [SOURCE: Page_4]

realistic, non-linear model to relate the ring microdensity profile with the corresponding [SOURCE: Page_5]
