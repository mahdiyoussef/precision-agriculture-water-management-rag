Abstract [SOURCE: Page_4]

Water governance occurs at multiple levels, from the local to the supra-national, which are often [SOURCE: Page_4]

highly fragmented. The interconnected nature of water requires interactions among these multiple [SOURCE: Page_4]

governance levels. Public participation may foster such interactions. Thus, many water management [SOURCE: Page_4]

reforms involved decentralization and public participation worldwide over the last decades. Yet, it is [SOURCE: Page_4]

not demonstrated how these reforms may improve water resources sustainability. Their analysis in [SOURCE: Page_4]

the literature does not show concretely how interactions among multiple levels materialize and are [SOURCE: Page_4]

influenced by participation. As such, the question addressed is how interactions among multiple [SOURCE: Page_4]

levels of water governance manifest over time in a participatory intervention. Using a case study in [SOURCE: Page_4]

the Rwenzori region in Uganda, this article compares the multi-level interactions before and during a [SOURCE: Page_4]

participatory process. The latter has been purposely implemented to bridge gaps between local and [SOURCE: Page_4]

provincial levels through a participatory planning process centered on the provincial level. Four types [SOURCE: Page_4]

of flows were analyzed: information and knowledge, hydrosocial, financial and human. Our analysis [SOURCE: Page_4]

shows that using artefacts like the role-playing game and planning matrix fostered bi-directional [SOURCE: Page_4]

information and knowledge flows. Hydrosocial flows did not change in depth but the legitimacy of [SOURCE: Page_4]

the two organizations implementing the participatory process was reinforced. Project financial flows [SOURCE: Page_4]

were injected through a provincial academic institution, who is not a regular budget recipient. They [SOURCE: Page_4]

were therefore superimposed on existing budgeting process. We conclude by providing suggestions [SOURCE: Page_4]

for the engineering of participatory processes in order to foster more collaborative and effective [SOURCE: Page_4]

multi-level water governance. [SOURCE: Page_4]

### 1. Introduction

Both scale and governance have become important areas of study for social scientists engaged with [SOURCE: Page_6]

water issues in the past few decades (Norman, Bakker, & Cook, 2012). Water governance can be [SOURCE: Page_6]

defined as the set of rules, practices, and processes (formal and informal) through which decisions for [SOURCE: Page_6]

the management of water resources and services are taken and implemented, stakeholders articulate [SOURCE: Page_6]

their interest and decision-makers are held accountable (OECD, 2015). Water governance occurs at [SOURCE: Page_6]

multiple levels (from local to supra-national), which are often highly fragmented. In parallel, the very [SOURCE: Page_6]

nature of water, a highly interconnected resource and interdependent with other sectors, requires [SOURCE: Page_6]

interactions among multiple levels (Moss & Newig, 2010; Norman et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_6]

We distinguish here “scale” from “level” as per Daniell & Barreteau (2014) where scale is ‘‘the relative [SOURCE: Page_6]

size or extent of something’’ (Oxford Dictionary) and levels are a graduated range on each scale. Daniell [SOURCE: Page_6]

& Barreteau (2014) identify eight different scales (expanded and adapted from Cash et al., 2006): [SOURCE: Page_6]

spatial, temporal, administrative, institutional, management, networks, knowledge/information and [SOURCE: Page_6]

stakes/issues. According to this definition, the temporal scale, for example, may include the levels of [SOURCE: Page_6]

hours, days, weeks, years, centuries, etc. Many studies and papers tend to use the terms “scale” and [SOURCE: Page_6]

“level” interchangeably. As such, terms used by other authors are altered to match these definitions [SOURCE: Page_6]

where their terms do not fit the operational definitions but their use and intent do. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Daniell & Barreteau (2014) therefore distinguish multi-level from cross-scale interactions (Fig.1). The [SOURCE: Page_6]

former includes interactions that take place on a single scale while the latter includes interactions [SOURCE: Page_6]

which take place from one scale to another. [SOURCE: Page_6]

We focus here on one specific part of governance: planning; and one specific scale: administrative, [SOURCE: Page_7]

including different levels, from local to supra national. We argue that many decisions about water go [SOURCE: Page_7]

through planning processes at different administrative levels, from supra-national to local. Hence, [SOURCE: Page_7]

investigating planning and the administrative scale is relevant for analyzing water governance (Newig [SOURCE: Page_7]

& Koontz, 2013). [SOURCE: Page_7]

It should be noted that Daniell & Barreteau's (2014) framework indicates that there is not just one [SOURCE: Page_7]

administrative scale: there are as many administrative scales as there are issues addressed by public [SOURCE: Page_7]

policies within a specific political organization. For example, one could look at the interactions between [SOURCE: Page_7]

the administrative provincial level dealing with water issues and the administrative provincial level [SOURCE: Page_7]

dealing with food or energy issues. This would bring us closer to nexus research which generates a [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

Author-produced version of the article published in Environmental Management, 2020. The original publication is available at https://link.springer.com Doi:10.1007/s^00267-020-01348-8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

large amount of literature we do not deal with in this paper. Rather than these interactions across [SOURCE: Page_8]

different administrative scales, we are interested here in the interactions among the different levels [SOURCE: Page_8]

on the administrative scale that focuses on water issues in Uganda. We are therefore interested in the [SOURCE: Page_8]

structuring of the organization of public policies and the interactions between their different levels on [SOURCE: Page_8]

a particular issue: water. We argue that this aspect is important as well, in particular because the multi- [SOURCE: Page_8]

level structuring of the organization of public policies is often similar from one issue to another and [SOURCE: Page_8]

potentially generating inefficiencies and biases in public policies. This is at least the case in Uganda, [SOURCE: Page_8]

which is discussed in this paper. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The interconnected nature of water, and the fact that water governance occurs at multiple levels [SOURCE: Page_8]

implies that governance is expected to create interactions among these different levels in order to take [SOURCE: Page_8]

into account decision-making across a range of water issues and dynamics, from climate trends at a [SOURCE: Page_8]

global level to the sharing of water between two neighbors at a local level. [SOURCE: Page_8]

However, both the literature and practice show that there are still a number of operational difficulties [SOURCE: Page_8]

in creating effective interactions among these different levels (Cash et al., 2006; Lovell, Mandondo, & [SOURCE: Page_8]

Moriarty, 2002; OECD, 2011). [SOURCE: Page_8]

One of the solutions that was put forward to foster coordination among multiple levels of water [SOURCE: Page_8]

governance is public participation (Dore & Lebel, 2010; Newig & Fritsch, 2009). Participation can in fact [SOURCE: Page_8]

appear quite logically as a potential coordination mechanism among different levels as soon as actors [SOURCE: Page_8]

from these different levels or their legitimate representatives are gathered in the same arena. Thus [SOURCE: Page_8]

many water management reforms across the world over recent decades entailed devolution of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

implementation of water policies to lower levels of management and increased public participation [SOURCE: Page_8]

(Boelens, Getches, & Guevara-Gil, 2010; UNECA, 2014). This has led, for example, to the creation of [SOURCE: Page_8]

multi-stakeholder and multi-level platforms (e.g. Lebel & Garden, 2007), or to the implementation of [SOURCE: Page_9]

role-playing games in multi-level arenas (e.g. Ducrot, 2009). [SOURCE: Page_9]

Despite the rapidly increasing academic interest in multi-level water governance and how different [SOURCE: Page_9]

forms of participation and stakeholder engagement have been enacted in case studies across the [SOURCE: Page_9]

world, there are relatively few that put a particular focus on the specific interactions created between [SOURCE: Page_9]

multiple levels of governance. This is particularly the case if we look at studies with formal schemes of [SOURCE: Page_9]

representation that go beyond interpersonal relations and power (e.g. actor-networks, coalition- [SOURCE: Page_9]

building and/or levels of participant decision-making control, and concepts such as legitimacy); social [SOURCE: Page_9]

learning (e.g. exchange of and development of collective knowledge); and mapping administrative [SOURCE: Page_9]

levels of actors to participation structures (e.g. Maleki & Bots, 2013; Renn, Berghöfer, Wittmer, & [SOURCE: Page_9]

Rauschmayer, 2010). Those focusing on a range of scales, levels and dynamics concurrently, based on [SOURCE: Page_9]

a number of flow types in the water governance system, such as finances, power, water, [SOURCE: Page_9]

information/knowledge and people are rarer (e.g. Edelenbos & Teisman, 2013). To make a small [SOURCE: Page_9]

contribution to this under-researched area of the participation and multi-level water governance [SOURCE: Page_9]

literature, we will focus on just the water administrative scale and a range of dynamics over the time. [SOURCE: Page_9]

Hence, the research question of this paper is: how interactions among multiple levels of water [SOURCE: Page_9]

governance manifest over time in a participatory intervention? [SOURCE: Page_9]

To answer this question, this article analyses one case study: a participatory process implemented in [SOURCE: Page_9]

the Rwenzori region in Uganda. The participatory process was developed as part of a European Union [SOURCE: Page_9]

funded research project called AfroMaison^1. The Rwenzori case is relevant to our research question [SOURCE: Page_9]

because the participatory process was set up purposefully to catalyze multi-level participatory [SOURCE: Page_9]

governance. The rationale of the intervention was based on the acknowledgement that the [SOURCE: Page_9]

implementation of decentralized water policies in Uganda was hindered by multi-level governance [SOURCE: Page_10]

gaps, notably a lack of resources and skills among district administrations, which are the ones [SOURCE: Page_10]

bridge these gaps through the implementation of a multi-level participatory planning process. This [SOURCE: Page_10]

paper analyses multi-level interactions that existed in the case study before the participatory process [SOURCE: Page_10]

was implemented and those that were generated during the participatory process. The objective is to [SOURCE: Page_10]

compare both in order to analyze the extent to which the participatory process has modified these [SOURCE: Page_10]

Our analysis is based on a critical and reflexive posture. It is critical in that it does not magnify the [SOURCE: Page_10]

results of the participatory process but simply seeks to account for its apparent effects, while taking [SOURCE: Page_10]

into account the power plays and complexity of interactions among actors and levels. Our posture is [SOURCE: Page_10]

also reflexive in that it accounts for the fact that actors in the field, by their very actions, contribute [SOURCE: Page_10]

to the production of knowledge and, conversely, that researchers influenced actions in the field with [SOURCE: Page_10]

the results of their analyses. This posture is at the heart of debates within the sociologists’ [SOURCE: Page_10]

community (Claeys-Mekdade, 2006). Without entering into these debates, our position is in line with [SOURCE: Page_10]

sociological research focusing on action-research and its implications for field activities and the [SOURCE: Page_10]

production of knowledge (Daré & Venot, 2016; Le Goff, 2012; Schwidt, 2017). The aim of the paper [SOURCE: Page_10]

based on this analysis is to present insights that may be applicable for the engineering of [SOURCE: Page_10]

participation processes in order to foster more collaborative and effective multi-level governance. [SOURCE: Page_10]

The limits of this approach are discussed at the end of the paper. [SOURCE: Page_10]

We have therefore decided to use the framework developed by Daniell & Barreteau (2014) in [SOURCE: Page_17]

   Physical or material flows (e.g. water, pollution, food, concrete, ecology). We will focus here [SOURCE: Page_17]
