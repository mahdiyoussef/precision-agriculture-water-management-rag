16 [SOURCE: Page_3]

17   Similar to many southern and southeast Asian regions, the mobilisation of arsenic (As) from [SOURCE: Page_3]

18   sediments has driven a widespread contamination problem for groundwater resources in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   Cambodian Mekong Delta. For the first time, the seasonal changes in As concentrations and [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   potential links to groundwater pumping for irrigation in shallow aquifers of the Cambodian Mekong [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   Delta are investigated. Using environmental tracers (18O, 2H, 3H, major/trace ions and rare earth [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   elements) the natural and pumping-induced changes in hydrogeological processes are identified. [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   Three conceptual models are proposed: Model 1, where there is limited local recharge or low [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   recharge rates (3H mean residence time > 60 years) and groundwater has a large range in As [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   concentrations (0.2 to 393.8 g/L). In this semi-confined aquifer, only one of the six groundwater [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   sites has As concentrations that increase (by 10.9 g/L) potentially due to groundwater pumping and [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   resultant mixing with high-As and low (Pr/Sm)NASC groundwater. However, data on groundwater [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   extraction volumes is required to verify the link with irrigation practices. Model 2, where [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   groundwater is recharged by evaporated surface waters (fractionated 18O and 2H). There are [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   moderate As concentrations (64.1-106.1 g/L) but no significant seasonal changes even though the [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   recharging waters have relatively greater organic carbon contents during the dry season (reduced [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   Ce/Ce*anomaly). Finally model 3, where groundwater is significantly recharged by wet season [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   rainfall (~50 % from 18O data). There is a minor increase in As concentrations with recharge (by 6. [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   g/L). These combined results highlight an aquifer system in the irrigated region of the Cambodian [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   Mekong Delta where As concentrations are largely impacted by natural rather than irrigation [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   processes. Seasonal-scale recharge processes control As processes where the aquifer is not confined [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

42 [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   The impact of irrigation practices on the contamination of groundwater by arsenic (As) is a globally [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   recognised phenomenon. Whilst in many cases As is derived from natural sources, it is well [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   established that groundwater pumping can cause changes to the mixing and biogeochemical [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   processes, and thereby accentuate the As issue in groundwater resources. The extraction of [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   groundwater leading to changes in As concentrations has reportedly occurred in the United States [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   (e.g. Ayotte et al., 2011; Smith et al., 2018), Vietnam (Berg et al., 2008), and Taiwan (Liu et al., 2003). [SOURCE: Page_4]

49 [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   The main aquifer of the Cambodian Mekong Delta is viewed as a groundwater system that remains [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   under relatively ‘natural’ conditions in terms of the extent and timing of groundwater pumped for [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   irrigation (e.g. Polya et al., 2005; Polizzotto et al., 2008; Lawson et al., 2013; 2016). Therefore, the [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   effects of groundwater pumping for irrigation on the As crisis in the Cambodian Mekong Delta has [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   not received the same attention compared to other southern and southeast Asia regions. For [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   example, in Bangladesh where the consequences of As contamination on public health are the most [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   severe worldwide (e.g. Raessler, 2018) there are several models proposed for the As release in [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   groundwater in irrigation regions. Many studies have hypothesised that irrigation withdrawals of [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   groundwater are the cause of elevated As concentrations in aquifers (e.g. Harvey et al., 2002). It is [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   suggested that pumping promoted As mobilisation in groundwater through the increase in hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   gradients that allow the transfer of young surficial organic matter to reach greater depths in the [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   aquifer compared with ‘natural’ conditions (Neumann et al., 2010). Radloff et al. (2017) also found [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   that the sorption and desorption processes within the aquifer during pumping can rapidly affect As [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   mobilisation in groundwater, and for the aquifer sands in the Bengal Basin this was not highly [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   sensitive to changes in the As redox state. In comparison, Mailloux et al. (2013) analysed a site in [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   Bangladesh where reductions in hydraulic heads due to pumping and resultant increases in recharge [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   did not significantly influence groundwater redox, and it was concluded that the As mobilisation was [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   due to natural long-term processes pre-dating human perturbations. [SOURCE: Page_5]

68 [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   Despite lower groundwater extraction rates in the Cambodian Mekong Delta compared with other [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   As-affected regions of southeast Asia (Lawson et al., 2013), many farmers and rural inhabitants are [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   dependent on groundwater resources, particularly during the dry season. Groundwater from shallow [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   aquifers in the upper delta region represents a strategic resource to people who cannot rely on [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   surface water, for both supplementary domestic use or for irrigation. Land irrigated by groundwater [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   in this region has reportedly increased by ~10% per year over the period 2003-2015 (Erban and [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   Gorelick, 2016). In the Vietnam Mekong Delta, a study found that the pumping of deep confined [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   aquifer systems causes inter-bedded clays to compact and release either As into solution, or release [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   dissolved organic carbon and competing ions that help to mobilise As (Erban et al., 2013). However, [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   this was an investigation of a deep aquifer system and to the best of our knowledge, the irrigation [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   pumping impacts on As in shallow aquifers of the Cambodian Mekong Delta have not yet been [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   documented. [SOURCE: Page_5]

81 [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   In the Cambodian Mekong Delta, many previous studies have established that the major As [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   mobilisation processes in the shallow aquifers include the microbial decomposition of organic [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   matter and the reductive dissolution of arsenic-bearing iron minerals (Fe(III) oxyhydroxides) under [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   anaerobic conditions (Pederick et al., 2007; Rowland et al., 2007; Polizzotto et al., 2008; Quicksall et [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   al., 2008; van Dongen et al., 2008; Lawson et al., 2013; 2016; Richards et al., 2017a; 2019). However, [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   there are many different models that describe the environmental context of these processes. For [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   example, since labile organic carbon is essential for the mobilisation of As in aquifers, there have [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   been many studies devoted to analysing the sources and transfers of this carbon. In the Cambodian [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   Mekong Delta, studies have highlighted the multiple sources of organic carbon from both in-aquifer [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   sources (van Dongen et al., 2008) and surficial sources (Lawson et al., 2013; 2016; Richards et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   2019), which is used in the reductive dissolution of arsenic-bearing iron minerals (Fe(III) [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   oxyhydroxides) that mobilises As in groundwater. The release of As has also found to be slower [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   where the source of organic matter is from sediments that are older and deeper, compared with [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   recently recharged near-surface organic carbon (Polizzotto et al., 2008). Therefore, the [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   hydrodynamics of an aquifer, and modifications thereof, can significantly influence the rates of As [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   mobilisation in groundwater. [SOURCE: Page_6]

98 [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   In this study, we build on the extensive As investigations already undertaken in the Cambodian [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   Mekong Delta (see Richards et al., 2019), by providing the first conceptual model of the seasonal- [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   changes in As concentrations in the shallow aquifer of the irrigated region. Groundwater from [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   typical rural sites was analysed where the resource is used for both irrigation and domestic use [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   purposes. In this study we investigate shallow groundwater along a 40 km length of the Bassac River, [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   the river that stems from the Mekong mainstream in Phnom Penh at the apex of the delta. There are [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   currently no groundwater monitoring bores or on-going measurements of hydraulic heads in this [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   region, therefore we use environmental tracers (stable isotopes, major ions, trace elements, rare [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   earth elements and tritium) to help identify key hydro(geo)logical processes. The study objectives [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   are to analyse (i) the origins of groundwater pumped for water supplies; (ii) the seasonal-scale [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   changes in groundwater chemistry; and (iii) whether or how the As concentrations seasonally [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   change during the dry season when groundwater is used for irrigation. [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   2. Study Area [SOURCE: Page_6]

114 [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   The study area is located in the Kandal province of the Cambodian Mekong Delta, situated between [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   Phnom Penh and the border with Vietnam (Fig. 1). The tropical monsoon climate in this delta is [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   characterised by high wet season rainfall volumes derived from southwestly weather systems, and a [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   dry season that is dominated by northeasterly weather systems (Briese, 1996). [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   2.1 Hydro(geo)logy and aquifer geology [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   South of Phnom Penh the Mekong River diverges to form the Bassac River that runs in parallel with [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   the Mekong River until they both discharge to the South China Sea in Vietnam. In the Cambodian [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   Mekong Delta, the rivers have high seasonal variations in water levels due to the large seasonal [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   differences in rainfall levels between the wet and dry seasons. For example, the Bassac River has [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   fluctuations in water levels of up to 6.6 m (between 1990-2018; Fig. 2a). Due to the flat topography [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   of the delta there are large areas that are annually inundated during the wet season rise of the river [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   water levels. Additional influences to the river water levels include tidal changes, groundwater [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   baseflow during the dry season (e.g. Rasmussen and Bradford, 1977; Polizzotto et al., 2008), and [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   river recharge to the aquifer during the wet season (e.g. Richards et al., 2018). In the study area, it [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   remains unclear whether the Bassac River interacts with shallow groundwater along its length, or [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   whether this is a spatially variable phenomenon limited by the thickness and continuity of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   underlying confining clay-rich sediments (as is the case in the Vietnamese section of the Mekong [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   Delta). The Bassac River has also been deepened in recent years due to river-bed mining (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   Bravard et al., 2013), which has caused pools of up to 15 m deeper than the natural level (Brunier et [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   al., 2014; Anthony et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_7]

136 [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   The sediments underlying the Bassac River are comprised of Quaternary alluvial sediments [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   deposited under river and marine environments. These sediments form stratified deltaic layers of [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   sand, gravel, clay and silt, and are regionally divided into two groups: the old (Pleistocene) and [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   young (Holocene) alluvium. The younger Holocene alluvium outcrops across the low elevation region [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   of the delta (<10 m ASL). In the study area this younger alluvium is the clay-rich confining layer that [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   can reach a thickness of up to ~18 m (Rasmussen and Bradford, 1977); the thickness increases [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   towards the Vietnamese coast reaching a maximum of > 100 m (Anderson, 1978). Underlying the [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   younger alluvium is the older Pleistocene alluvium, which in comparison contains less clay, coarser [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   material (sand, gravel, pebbles), and is therefore the major shallow aquifer. In the Cambodian [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   section of the delta the aquifer can be up to 100-120 m deep, whereas in the lower delta in Vietnam [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   the thickness reaches up to 450 m (Briese, 1996). Bedrock underlies and borders these alluvial [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   sediments, and in cases such as the basalt intrusions the bedrock is also interbedded with the old [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   alluvium. The bedrock is comprised of igneous, metamorphic and consolidated sedimentary rocks [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   that range in age from Precambrian to Plio-Pleistocene (Anderson, 1978). Underneath the Bassac [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   River the basement bedrocks are over 160 m from the surface (JICA, 2002). [SOURCE: Page_8]

152 [SOURCE: Page_8]

153   Regionally groundwater flow in the delta is towards the south (Briese, 1996). Although groundwater [SOURCE: Page_8]

154   is an important water resource in the delta there are very few bores monitored for hydraulic head [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   data. Previous studies have identified that groundwater levels are relatively low in June and July, and [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   increase by ~5m in October and November (JICA, 2002). In the northern region of the Kandal [SOURCE: Page_8]

157   Province previous studies have highlighted that groundwater close to the river is heavily influenced [SOURCE: Page_8]

158   by river water level fluctuations, with the river’s influence on groundwater levels decreasing with [SOURCE: Page_8]

159   distance and depth (Polizzotto et al., 2008; Richards et al., 2017a). However, the spatio-temporal [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   variations in groundwater elevations throughout the Cambodian Mekong Delta, particularly due to [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   groundwater pumping, are very poorly documented. The clay-rich sediments of the younger [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   alluvium forms a confining layer at the surface resulting in the underlying old alluvial aquifer acting [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   as a semi-confined system. [SOURCE: Page_8]

164 [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   As in many developing countries, in both semi-arid and wet tropical environments, groundwater [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   resources in the Cambodian Mekong Delta are essential to rural communities (e.g. about 3,000 [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   private wells have been identified in the study area by the Water Sanitation Program (WSP, 2019)). [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   Although groundwater resources are of poorer quality compared with river water in terms of [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   electrical conductivity and arsenic (see below results from this study), it is an unmonitored water [SOURCE: Page_9]

170   resource, and there is a cost associated with installing groundwater bores, many rural inhabitants [SOURCE: Page_9]

171   rely on accessing groundwater for both agricultural and domestic (including drinking) uses. [SOURCE: Page_9]

174   2.2 Irrigation [SOURCE: Page_9]

175   This region of the Mekong Delta is dedicated to farming that is heavily dictated by the wet and dry [SOURCE: Page_9]

176   seasons and the topography. Land along the river banks is slightly elevated then gently slopes down [SOURCE: Page_9]

177   in a perpendicular direction towards low-lying wetlands located further away from the main river [SOURCE: Page_9]

178   streams. Close to river banks, farmers can cultivate yearlong; orchards and vegetables dominate the [SOURCE: Page_9]

179   landscape and are irrigated especially during the dry season (Feb-May). Further away from the river, [SOURCE: Page_9]

180   farmers cultivate up to two crops of short term rice (e.g. 90-95 days crop). The first season starts [SOURCE: Page_9]

181   when the flood recedes (in November/December; this is locally called recession rice) and is mainly [SOURCE: Page_9]

182   rainfed though supplementary irrigation is common. For the second season, the cropping calendar [SOURCE: Page_9]

183   differs based on topography and water availability. Some farmers (low lying, more water available, [SOURCE: Page_9]

184   and higher vulnerability to floods) cultivate and irrigate rice during the dry season (Feb-May; this is [SOURCE: Page_9]

185   locally called dry-season rice), while others (slightly more elevated, less water available but less [SOURCE: Page_9]

186   vulnerable to floods) start cultivating rice in May with the first rains and harvest before the floods [SOURCE: Page_9]

187   come towards the end of July (supplementary irrigation is still needed; this is locally called early wet [SOURCE: Page_9]

188   season rice; Pillot, 2007). [SOURCE: Page_9]

189 [SOURCE: Page_9]

190   In support of the agricultural activities in the study area there is a dense network of earthen artificial [SOURCE: Page_9]

191   channels locally call preks, acting as drainage and irrigation canals (SOFRECO, 2019). The beds of [SOURCE: Page_9]

192   these channels were usually dug several meters below the land surface, perpendicularly to the [SOURCE: Page_9]

193   Bassac, on a length of about 4 km, and are spaced 0.5 to 1 km (Fig. 1b). During high river water flows [SOURCE: Page_9]

194   the channels divert the water and sediments inland, and can either link the Bassac River with other [SOURCE: Page_9]

195   natural river streams in the delta or the water and sediments culminate in the low lying wetlands. [SOURCE: Page_10]

196   The same channels drain water back to the river during low river flow periods. The canals are used [SOURCE: Page_10]

197   for transportation, fishing and are also a major source of irrigation water (with farmers installing [SOURCE: Page_10]

198   small diesel pumps to draw water from the canals to their fields). [SOURCE: Page_10]

199 [SOURCE: Page_10]

200   There has been recent rehabilitation (including deepening) of some irrigation channels in this region [SOURCE: Page_10]

201   of the delta to improve the water control and availability. Some channels were equipped with gates, [SOURCE: Page_10]

202   managed by water user associations to regulate flow from or to the river. In some cases, floating [SOURCE: Page_10]

203   pumping stations were installed on the Bassac and water is pumped in the channel during dry [SOURCE: Page_10]

204   season. Despite these changes, many of the irrigation channels (60% in 2018; SCP, 2018) cannot [SOURCE: Page_10]

205   provide river water inflows all year round, especially during the driest months. Many farmers are [SOURCE: Page_10]

206   obliged to install their own groundwater bores in order to irrigate during the dry season and for [SOURCE: Page_10]

207   domestic purposes. Groundwater is used for the irrigation of vegetables, fruit tree farms and rice [SOURCE: Page_10]

208   fields, and is mostly pumped at the end of the dry season for irrigation (~ May/June, depending on [SOURCE: Page_10]

209   the year). [SOURCE: Page_10]

211   2.3 Arsenic [SOURCE: Page_10]

212   In the Cambodian Mekong Delta, groundwater has been used to irrigate rice fields since ~ 1984 and [SOURCE: Page_10]

213   in areas where groundwater is the primary source of irrigation water the concentration of As in the [SOURCE: Page_10]

214   soils is elevated, albeit with lower health risks compared with the higher toxicity of arsenic present [SOURCE: Page_10]

215   in rice cultivated in Bangladesh (Murphy et al., 2017). For example, in the Kandal province Murphy et [SOURCE: Page_10]

216   al. (2018) reported As concentrations in soils close to wells that were approximately double the [SOURCE: Page_10]

217   concentrations of soils further from the well. Although groundwater is an essential dry season water [SOURCE: Page_10]

218   resource, it is well established (e.g. Phan et al., 2013) that As in groundwater is a health threat to the [SOURCE: Page_10]

219   rural population in the Cambodian Mekong Delta. Accordingly, As has received a lot of national [SOURCE: Page_10]

220   attention (e.g. database of 59000 records of As in groundwater in Cambodia; WSP, 2019). However, [SOURCE: Page_10]

221   in part because of the lack of in-situ groundwater monitoring data, this As database has not been [SOURCE: Page_11]

222   analysed in combination with the modified hydro(geo)logical processes due to irrigation practices. In [SOURCE: Page_11]

223   addition, as discussed previously, the natural processes influencing As concentrations are already [SOURCE: Page_11]

224   numerous and complex; including the non-uniform distribution of the eroded Himalayan sediments, [SOURCE: Page_11]

225   competing electron acceptors, and the reactivity, age and transfers of organic carbon (Papacostas et [SOURCE: Page_11]

226   al., 2008; Quicksall et al., 2008; Lawson et al., 2016; Richards et al., 2017b). An additional potential [SOURCE: Page_11]

227   source of As in the study area is the use of fertilisers. However, a recent study in Kandal by Murphy [SOURCE: Page_11]

228   et al. (2018) calculated that As loads in groundwater used for irrigation were over 3000 times the As [SOURCE: Page_11]

229   loads present in the inorganic fertilisers being used. [SOURCE: Page_11]

232   3. Methods [SOURCE: Page_11]

233 [SOURCE: Page_11]

234   In this study, a new As dataset is analysed in conjunction with hydrochemical tracers to inform us on [SOURCE: Page_11]

235   the hydro(geo)logical processes in areas where groundwater is used for irrigation. Here we analyse [SOURCE: Page_11]

236   river bathymetry data in conjunction with hydrochemical parameters to study the potential [SOURCE: Page_11]

237   interactions between the river and shallow groundwater. The hydrochemical tracers are also used [SOURCE: Page_11]

238   to identify hydrogeological processes affecting As concentrations. Field visits were conducted during [SOURCE: Page_11]

239   June and September in 2017 and 2018 (Fig. 2b). Samples in June represent the end of the dry [SOURCE: Page_11]

240   season/start of the wet season, and it is during June that we expect to see the full impacts of the dry [SOURCE: Page_11]

241   season pumping on shallow groundwater samples. In comparison, samples collected in September [SOURCE: Page_11]

242   represent the peak of the wet season. [SOURCE: Page_11]

243 [SOURCE: Page_11]

244   3.1 River bathymetry and flow [SOURCE: Page_11]

245   River bathymetry data was used to map sections of the river bathymetry and flow velocity from one [SOURCE: Page_11]

246   bank to the other (back and forth). The ADCP was coupled with a differential GPS in order to map [SOURCE: Page_11]

247   the exact profile track perpendicular to the riverbed. Then, the entire river flow through the section [SOURCE: Page_12]

248   was inferred. During the dry season, an acoustic Doppler current profiler (ADCP, RDI™ 600 kHz) was [SOURCE: Page_12]

249   used to map sections of the river bathymetry and measure the river flow at the northern (R10) and [SOURCE: Page_12]

250   the southern (R$_1$) sites. In addition, a hand held pressure sensor was used to record the river depth [SOURCE: Page_12]

251   (surface water level elevation minus water column) at each of the sampling sites along the length of [SOURCE: Page_12]

595   5.1 Hydro(geo)logical processes [SOURCE: Page_25]

596 [SOURCE: Page_25]

597   River-groundwater interactions [SOURCE: Page_25]

598   The hydrochemical results (3H and EC) from this study did not show much evidence of seasonal-scale [SOURCE: Page_25]

599   recharge of the Bassac River to the shallow aquifer, despite in some areas the measured river depths [SOURCE: Page_25]

600   suggest that this would be possible. Previous studies in other sites of the Cambodian Mekong Delta [SOURCE: Page_25]

601   have highlighted a connection between the Bassac and Mekong River and surrounding shallow [SOURCE: Page_25]

602   aquifers that result in linked changes in groundwater quality (e.g. Polizzotto et al., 2008; Lawson et [SOURCE: Page_25]

603   al., 2016; Richards et al., 2017a, b). Therefore, we hypothesise that the bores sampled in this study [SOURCE: Page_25]

604   were located outside of the zone of influence of recharge from the Bassac River (at distances of 0.3- [SOURCE: Page_25]

605   1 km from the river’s edge). Despite the high dry season groundwater pumping rates, the river stable [SOURCE: Page_25]

606   isotope and EC values do indicate potentially dry season inflows to the river from the shallow [SOURCE: Page_25]

607   aquifer. In addition, the stable isotope results suggest an increase in local delta rainfall contributions [SOURCE: Page_26]

608   to the river during the wet season compared with greater surface water inflows from northern [SOURCE: Page_26]

609   catchments to the river in the dry season. This corresponds with previous work that has used both [SOURCE: Page_26]

610   physical models (MRC, 2009) and stable and radiogenic isotope data (Nguyen et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_26]

611 [SOURCE: Page_26]

612   Groundwater recharge [SOURCE: Page_26]

613   One groundwater site (G$_1$) showed evidence of local recharge during the wet season by rainfall or [SOURCE: Page_26]

614   inundation waters. This site therefore is located where this is little or no confining unit at the [SOURCE: Page_26]

615   surface. The combination of lower EC, higher REE concentrations and lower stable isotopes in the [SOURCE: Page_26]

616   wet season compared with the dry season highlights an older laterally flowing groundwater system [SOURCE: Page_26]

617   that mixes with wet season recharging rainfall and/or inundation waters. The mixing calculation [SOURCE: Page_26]

618   using 18O values highlighted a high percentage (~50 %) of rain/inundation waters that mixed with [SOURCE: Page_26]

619   the groundwater system during the wet season. [SOURCE: Page_26]

620 [SOURCE: Page_26]

621   A second groundwater site (G$_7$) also showed evidence of recent recharge but, as highlighted by the [SOURCE: Page_26]

622   fractionation of stable isotope values, this is by evaporated waters. At G$_7$, recharge occurs during [SOURCE: Page_26]

623   both the dry and wet season most likely via the nearby permanent surface waters trapped by a dyke [SOURCE: Page_26]

624   in the low elevation areas. Previous studies to the north of the study area have also found ponds and [SOURCE: Page_26]

625   wetlands to be a recharge source for shallow groundwater (e.g. Lawson et al., 2016; Richards et al., [SOURCE: Page_26]

626   2019). Given the stagnation of these waters at the surface, the underlying sediments must act as a [SOURCE: Page_26]

627   low-infiltration layer. The relatively constant stable isotope and major ion chemistry at G$_7$ suggest [SOURCE: Page_26]

628   that there is constant mixing between the dry and wet season recharge with lateral groundwater [SOURCE: Page_26]

629   flow. [SOURCE: Page_26]

630 [SOURCE: Page_26]

631   For 7 of the 10 groundwater sites there is no detectable 3H contents, therefore the mean residence [SOURCE: Page_26]

632   times are > 60 years old, and these sites represent shallow groundwater from a semi-confined [SOURCE: Page_26]

633   aquifer at ~30 to 62 m depth. The stable isotope and major ion hydrochemistry also indicates that [SOURCE: Page_27]

634   groundwater from this semi-confined aquifer is not locally or recently recharged by rainfall, river, [SOURCE: Page_27]

635   inundation or irrigation waters and changes in EC are controlled by variations in water-rock [SOURCE: Page_27]

636   interactions in the aquifer and in-aquifer mixing. [SOURCE: Page_27]

639   5.2 Seasonal changes in groundwater arsenic concentrations [SOURCE: Page_27]

640   Simplified schematics for each of the three types of groundwater recharge processes (limited recent [SOURCE: Page_27]

641   recharge, evaporated water and rainfall recharge) are presented in figure 8. At each of the [SOURCE: Page_27]

642   groundwater sites there is dry season groundwater pumping that exceeds wet season pumping. The [SOURCE: Page_27]

643   influence of groundwater pumping versus recharge processes impacting on the As concentrations in [SOURCE: Page_27]

644   groundwater is not consistent between the groundwater sites (Fig. 8) and is explored below. [SOURCE: Page_27]

645 [SOURCE: Page_27]

646   Model 1. Limited recent recharge [SOURCE: Page_27]

647   Model 1 is assigned to groundwater sites where the aquifer is semi-confined, there is limited [SOURCE: Page_27]

648   modern recharge (3H  0.8 TU), and there is no recharge from evaporated waters or recent rain [SOURCE: Page_27]

649   water. This includes 5 groundwater sites where the seasonal changes in As concentrations are not [SOURCE: Page_27]

650   significant after accounting for total errors (G$_2$, G$_3$, G$_5$, G10 and G$_4$), and 1 groundwater site where [SOURCE: Page_27]

651   there is a dry season increases in the As concentration (by 10.9 g/L; G$_6$). Further work, including [SOURCE: Page_27]

652   the comparison with groundwater pumping volumes, is required to determine whether the [SOURCE: Page_27]

653   difference in As concentration changes between the sites is related to groundwater pumping [SOURCE: Page_27]

654   practices. [SOURCE: Page_27]

655 [SOURCE: Page_27]

656   At G$_6$, the increase in As concentrations correlate with the period of high pumping rates during the [SOURCE: Page_27]

657   dry season. Unlike some As models proposed from sites in Bangladesh, the groundwater pumping at [SOURCE: Page_27]

658   G6 did not induce mixing with younger waters (e.g. Harvey et al., 2002), nor did it induce the dilution [SOURCE: Page_27]

659   of As concentrations (e.g. Radloff et al., 2017). The groundwater that is drawn into the bore during [SOURCE: Page_28]

660   pumping (during both the dry and wet season) has a mean residence time of > 60 years, and changes [SOURCE: Page_28]

661   in As concentrations are therefore related to seasonal-scale in-aquifer processes that are triggered [SOURCE: Page_28]

662   or accentuated during the relatively high pumping volumes of the dry season. [SOURCE: Page_28]

663 [SOURCE: Page_28]

664   Groundwater bores installed for water supplies often have long screen intervals to increase the [SOURCE: Page_28]

665   pumping volumes, which is the case for the private bores sampled in this study with screens along [SOURCE: Page_28]

666   the length of the casing. On-going pumping can therefore result in the connection of otherwise [SOURCE: Page_28]

667   separate aquifer zones (Ayotte et al., 2011). At the site G$_6$, changes in As concentrations may result [SOURCE: Page_28]

668   from greater extractions of groundwater during the dry season that draws in groundwater from [SOURCE: Page_28]

669   different aquifer zones that are otherwise not accessed during the wet season when pumping rates [SOURCE: Page_28]

670   are lower. Groundwater elevations have been found to decrease by up to 5 m in the Kandal Province [SOURCE: Page_28]

671   during the June/July irrigation period (JICA, 2002). Since groundwater flows to a bore during [SOURCE: Page_28]

672   pumping are firstly accessed from relatively high hydraulic conductivity (K) zones in the aquifer, only [SOURCE: Page_28]

673   after prolonged pumping will regions of lower K contribute to the groundwater volumes extracted. [SOURCE: Page_28]

674   Therefore, particularly towards the end of the dry season, the continued groundwater pumping can [SOURCE: Page_28]

675   cause inflows of waters with distinct chemical properties. In previous work on the shallow [SOURCE: Page_28]

676   groundwater aquifer in the Mekong Delta, at a site 8 km south of our study area, Wang et al. (2018) [SOURCE: Page_28]

677   reported that the sediments with the highest hydraulic conductivities (K = 3.8x^10-6 to 4.6x^10-6 cm/s) [SOURCE: Page_28]

678   were associated with relatively low concentrations of As (11-12 ppm) and total organic carbon (TOC: [SOURCE: Page_28]

679   0.9-1 wt %), compared with the lower K (2.6x^10-7 to 5.9x^10-7 cm/s) sediments that have higher As [SOURCE: Page_28]

680   concentrations (15-28 ppm) and TOC contents (4.8-5.1 wt %). Therefore, pumping-induced inflows [SOURCE: Page_28]

681   from these lower K zones can contribute greater concentrations of As to the groundwater pumped. [SOURCE: Page_28]

682   In comparison, during the wet season, pumped volumes are lower, therefore mostly higher K zones [SOURCE: Page_28]

683   in the aquifer are drawn into the aquifer, thereby reducing the As concentrations. [SOURCE: Page_28]

684 [SOURCE: Page_28]

685   In addition to mixing processes directly affecting As concentrations during pumping, the resultant [SOURCE: Page_29]

686   groundwater chemistry changes can also potentially induce changes in ion sorption/desorption and [SOURCE: Page_29]

687   mineral dissolution/precipitation processes that also affect As concentrations. For example, in near- [SOURCE: Page_29]

688   neutral waters minor increases in pH can result in desorption processes that increase As [SOURCE: Page_29]

689   concentrations (Richards et al., 2019). However, in this study the increased As concentrations during [SOURCE: Page_29]

690   the dry season is associated with a minor decrease in pH compared with the wet season. Therefore [SOURCE: Page_29]

691   seasonal changes in groundwater pH are not driving changes in As concentrations. [SOURCE: Page_29]

692 [SOURCE: Page_29]

693   The results from this study highlight that the increase in in-aquifer interactions between [SOURCE: Page_29]

694   groundwater from higher K/lower K zones produces high As concentrations in groundwater in the [SOURCE: Page_29]

695   dry season, which may result from changes in redox-driven processes over short time-frames [SOURCE: Page_29]

696   (McMahon et al., 2011). Reducing groundwater conditions promote the reduction of As(V) to As(III) [SOURCE: Page_29]

697   that can lead to desorption from ferric (Fe(III)) and Mn (hydr)oxides and/or sedimentary organic [SOURCE: Page_29]

698   matter (e.g. Richards et al., 2019). In addition, the release of high OC groundwater can result in the [SOURCE: Page_29]

699   respiration of DOC producing NH$_4$ and an increase in the reductive dissolution of Fe (hydr)oxides also [SOURCE: Page_29]

700   releasing As into solution (e.g. Lawson et al., 2016). At G$_6$, the changes in groundwater chemistry [SOURCE: Page_29]

701   corresponds with these processes; with dry season increases in Fe concentrations (from 1.23 to [SOURCE: Page_29]

702   21.57 g/L), and high concentrations of HCO$_3$ and NH$_4$ (Table 1). [SOURCE: Page_29]

703 [SOURCE: Page_29]

704   Changes in REE patterns and ratios can indicate changes in the groundwater system that are [SOURCE: Page_29]

705   significant for As. For example, temporal changes in REEs in groundwater can result from changes in [SOURCE: Page_29]

706   the aquifer pH and redox conditions and mixing between waters (e.g. Dia et al., 2000; Seto and [SOURCE: Page_29]

707   Akagi, 2008). For model 1, the REE data confirms that sites G$_3$, G$_5$, G$_2$, and G10 (Figs. 7e-g) show [SOURCE: Page_29]

708   relatively little temporal variation in mixing or redox effects on REE patterns and concentrations, [SOURCE: Page_29]

709   similar to low changes in As concentrations. In comparison there are changes in LREEs [SOURCE: Page_29]

710   concentrations and patterns at sites G4 and G6 in the dry season (Fig. 7d), which are likely to result [SOURCE: Page_29]

711   from mixing with waters that have interacted with different mineralogy and/or organic matter [SOURCE: Page_30]

712   compared with wet season groundwater. This is consistent with the As being derived from a low K [SOURCE: Page_30]

713   zone of the aquifer during pumping. However, this change in groundwater mixing during the dry [SOURCE: Page_30]

714   season only results in a significant increase in As concentrations at site G$_6$ and not at site G$_4$ (Fig. 8). [SOURCE: Page_30]

715 [SOURCE: Page_30]

716   Model 2. Recharge by evaporated surface waters [SOURCE: Page_30]

717   The second As model is based on data at site G$_7$, where the groundwater has been recently [SOURCE: Page_30]

718   recharged and there is mixing with evaporated waters. At site G$_7$ there is no significant change in As [SOURCE: Page_30]

719   between the dry season (67.6 ±3.6 g/L) and wet season (64.1 ±3.5 g/L). Similar to Model 1, this [SOURCE: Page_30]

720   site is located in an area where there are significant increases in groundwater pumping during the [SOURCE: Page_30]

721   dry compared with the wet season. In contrast with model 1, the groundwater from the shallow [SOURCE: Page_30]

722   aquifer is recharged during both the dry and wet seasons by evaporated surface waters. Although [SOURCE: Page_30]

723   recharged by evaporated surface waters, there is no evidence that the As concentrations are high [SOURCE: Page_30]

724   due to affects from evaporation. For example, As molar ratios relative to conservative ions in [SOURCE: Page_30]

725   solution (e.g. Cl) are high at G$_7$ (dry season As/Cl: 0.008) compared to samples with low As [SOURCE: Page_30]

726   concentrations (dry season As/Cl: < 0.00005). [SOURCE: Page_30]

727 [SOURCE: Page_30]

728   As concentrations are likely to be controlled by evaporated water recharge processes, and less so by [SOURCE: Page_30]

729   pumping since there are no changes in As concentrations at the seasonal-scale. This is consistent [SOURCE: Page_30]

730   with models proposed in other sites of the Cambodian Mekong Delta where there is limited [SOURCE: Page_30]

731   groundwater pumping, which showed that for groundwater outside of the influence of river inflows, [SOURCE: Page_30]

732   the high As concentrations are predominantly the result of recharge of evaporated surface waters [SOURCE: Page_30]

733   and the transfer of young organic matter into the aquifer resulting in the reductive dissolution of [SOURCE: Page_30]

734   Fe/Mn (hydr)oxides (Lawson et al., 2013, 2016; Richards et al., 2018). The low vertical transfer times [SOURCE: Page_30]

735   (ie. > 200 years; Lawson et al., 2013) of the shallow clay-rich sediments in the Mekong Delta implies [SOURCE: Page_30]

736   that the confining layer underlying the evaporated surface waters at site G$_7$ is relatively thin, and [SOURCE: Page_31]

737   allows for transfer times to the shallow groundwater system of < 60 years (3H data, this study). [SOURCE: Page_31]

738 [SOURCE: Page_31]

739   Due to the local recharge processes occurring at site G$_7$, there is less likely to be the horizontal [SOURCE: Page_31]

740   stratification of groundwater chemistry that was proposed in model 1. Increases in dry season [SOURCE: Page_31]

741   groundwater pumping may induce greater drawdown rates of the recharging waters, and may result [SOURCE: Page_31]

742   in the observed increase in the Ce/Ce* ratio (Table 2), which in a reducing aquifer can be indicative [SOURCE: Page_31]

743   of an increase in REEs sourced from an organic-rich environment (Pouret et al., 2010; Davranche et [SOURCE: Page_31]

744   al., 2015). This implies that during the dry season the evaporative water recharge transfers more [SOURCE: Page_31]

745   organic-rich waters compared with the wet season. However, this did not result in a significant [SOURCE: Page_31]

746   increase in As concentrations in the groundwater. [SOURCE: Page_31]

747 [SOURCE: Page_31]

748   Model 3. Rainfall recharge [SOURCE: Page_31]

749   Model 3 is based on groundwater at the site G$_1$. This is the only site in this study to show evidence [SOURCE: Page_31]

750   of significant recharge by wet season rainfall, which results in wet season increases in REE [SOURCE: Page_31]

751   concentrations, decreased 18O and 2H values, and the dilution of many major ions. Despite this, [SOURCE: Page_31]

752   there were some ion concentrations that increased during recharge, including As (from 1.3 ±0.1 to [SOURCE: Page_31]

753   8.6 ±0.5 g/L), and Fe (4 to 25 g/L). Therefore, a driver of increases in As at site G$_1$ is linked to wet [SOURCE: Page_31]

754   season recharge processes, and likely due to the transfers of young OC and the resultant reductive [SOURCE: Page_31]

755   dissolution of Fe(III) (hydr)oxides (as reflected in wet season increases in Fe). Similar to the model 2, [SOURCE: Page_31]

756   due to efficient recharge processes there is unlikely to be any significant horizontal stratification of [SOURCE: Page_31]

757   groundwater chemistry, and the seasonal-scale recharge indicates that at this site there is a clay- [SOURCE: Page_31]

758   window and therefore the shallow aquifer is unconfined. [SOURCE: Page_31]

761   5.3 Future work on As models [SOURCE: Page_31]

762   As observed in the 3 models proposed, there are multiple physical and biogeochemical processes [SOURCE: Page_32]

763   impacting groundwater in the Kandal region of the Cambodian Mekong Delta that result in the high [SOURCE: Page_32]

764   spatial heterogeneity of the groundwater As concentrations. In contrast, we observe very few sites [SOURCE: Page_32]

765   with significant seasonal changes in groundwater As concentrations. Temporal variations were only [SOURCE: Page_32]

766   observed at two sites, and the causes of these variations ranged from natural processes (significant [SOURCE: Page_32]

767   seasonal recharge at site G$_1$), to potential anthropogenic impacts (from groundwater pumping at [SOURCE: Page_32]

768   site G$_6$). Further work in the region requires an analysis of the seasonal changes in As concentrations [SOURCE: Page_32]

769   at sites such as G$_6$ in combination with groundwater extraction volumes, analysis of sediments, and [SOURCE: Page_32]

770   changes in hydraulic head data. In addition, monitoring bores should be installed so that an analysis [SOURCE: Page_32]

771   of aquifer processes can be undertaken outside of the bias of pumping influences and the regional [SOURCE: Page_32]

772   extent of irrigation pumping effects on groundwater As concentrations can be assessed. This would [SOURCE: Page_32]

773   allow a more in-depth analysis of the natural versus anthropogenic processes controlling the spatial [SOURCE: Page_32]

774   and seasonal changes in As in groundwater from the irrigated area of the Cambodian Mekong Delta. [SOURCE: Page_32]

775   Finally, the on-going projects devoted to rehabilitating current water resources infrastructure (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_32]

776   deepening of the prek channels) raise questions on the potential impact on groundwater quality. [SOURCE: Page_32]

777   The deepening could result in faster vertical transfer times or clay-windows allowing surface water [SOURCE: Page_32]

778   recharge into the semi-confined aquifer, and thus may result in an increase in As mobilisation in [SOURCE: Page_32]

779   groundwater with the introduction of young organic matter. [SOURCE: Page_32]

782   6. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_32]

783   Managing groundwater As contamination issues is important nationally for the rural community of [SOURCE: Page_32]

784   Cambodia in the delta, but also for the 17.5 million Vietnamese people that live down-gradient in [SOURCE: Page_32]

785   the Mekong Delta. This study presents a first insight into the seasonal variations of As [SOURCE: Page_32]

786   concentrations in groundwater in the irrigation region of the Cambodian Mekong Delta. Where the [SOURCE: Page_32]

787   aquifer is semi-confined, this provided ideal sites to consider groundwater pumping impacts on As [SOURCE: Page_32]

788   concentrations because there were no wet season recharge processes influencing changes in [SOURCE: Page_33]

789   groundwater chemistry. The results from this study show that in the shallow semi-confined aquifer a [SOURCE: Page_33]

790   lack of seasonal variations in As concentrations at most groundwater sites indicates that the wet-dry [SOURCE: Page_33]

791   variations in pumping volumes are not significantly impacting the mobilisation of As in the semi- [SOURCE: Page_33]

792   confined aquifer. There was only one site (G$_6$) that exhibited dry season increases in As [SOURCE: Page_33]

793   concentrations, which is potentially caused by the dry season increases in groundwater pumped for [SOURCE: Page_33]

794   irrigation. In areas where the aquifer is not confined, it was the natural recharge processes (of either [SOURCE: Page_33]

795   evaporated surface waters or wet season rainfall) that controlled As concentrations in groundwater. [SOURCE: Page_33]

796   The results from this study present a conceptual model of the seasonal-scale processes affecting As [SOURCE: Page_33]

797   concentrations in the irrigated area of the Cambodian Mekong Delta, and early findings are similar [SOURCE: Page_33]

798   to other southern and southeast Asia regions such as Bangladesh where models of both natural and [SOURCE: Page_33]

799   anthropogenic processes are required to describe the As processes. In future work, information on [SOURCE: Page_33]

800   pumping volumes is essential to verify (i) whether groundwater extraction is causing the increase in [SOURCE: Page_33]

801   the As concentrations at site G$_6$, and also (ii) whether the lack of seasonal changes in groundwater [SOURCE: Page_33]
