Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

Treated wastewater in micro-irrigation is a promising approach that could be used to decrease [SOURCE: Page_2]

the pressure on water resources. However, the clogging of such systems due to biofilm [SOURCE: Page_2]

development and chemical precipitation constitute a constraint with the use of treated [SOURCE: Page_2]

wastewater (TWW) and lead to lowers irrigation system performance. The objective of this [SOURCE: Page_2]

work is to study the development of biofilm and composition of fouling due to TWW under [SOURCE: Page_2]

shear stresses of 0.7, 2.2 and 4.4 Pa detected along micro-irrigation systems. For this purpose, [SOURCE: Page_2]

a Taylor-Couette reactor (TCR) was specifically calibrated for the cultivation of biofilm. The [SOURCE: Page_2]

analysis of fouling composition samples (organic and inorganic) shows that biofilm tends to [SOURCE: Page_2]

develop under the highest shear stress value (4.4 Pa). Precipitation of calcium carbonate in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

form of calcite was observed in conjunction with biofilm growth using X-ray diffractometry [SOURCE: Page_2]

(XRD) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). These results can be used to ascertain the [SOURCE: Page_2]

origins of chemical and biological clogging of dripper and fouling of pipes related to [SOURCE: Page_2]

reclaimed water- irrigation. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

Reuse of treated wastewater for irrigation and especially in micro-irrigation, would appear to [SOURCE: Page_3]

an efficient way to mitigate the problem of water resource rarefaction, especially in arid and [SOURCE: Page_3]

semi-arid countries (Megersa and Abdullahi 2015). Micro-irrigation, also known as drip [SOURCE: Page_3]

irrigation, is an irrigation technique characterized by low, frequent and localized water [SOURCE: Page_3]

delivery to crops in the form of drops using drippers (Bounoua 2010). However, dripper [SOURCE: Page_3]

clogging is a common problem in micro-irrigation (Liu and Huang 2009; Niu et al. 2012), and [SOURCE: Page_3]

notably when treated wastewater is used (Dazhuang et al. 2009; Gamri et al. 2014). Clogging [SOURCE: Page_3]

can be physical (caused by accumulated suspended solids), organic (linked to algae and [SOURCE: Page_3]

biofilm growth) and / or chemical (due to precipitation of chemical elements) and can be [SOURCE: Page_3]

observed simultaneously (Bucks et al. 1979). There are several factors that can influence [SOURCE: Page_3]

lateral fouling and dripper clogging, hydrodynamic conditions, in particular, influences the [SOURCE: Page_3]

composition, structure, morphology and the activity of the biofilm (Besemer et al. 2007; [SOURCE: Page_3]

Araujo et al. 2016; Thomen et al. 2017). In order to understand how hydrodynamic conditions [SOURCE: Page_3]

can influence biofilm development and dripper clogging, several experimental and numerical [SOURCE: Page_3]

studies on drippers and pipes have been performed. Wei et al. (2006) and Al Muhammad [SOURCE: Page_3]

(2016) used a method based of computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to calculate pressure [SOURCE: Page_3]

distribution and flow velocity inside dripper channels. According to CFD simulations, [SOURCE: Page_3]

changes in pressure gradients mainly occur at the angles formed by channel walls. Gamri et [SOURCE: Page_3]

al. (2014) shows that the lowest velocity inside a dripper occurs in the central circle and at [SOURCE: Page_3]

some angles of the dripper channels, which are the areas where biofilms principally develop. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Velocity vector distribution confirms that these areas are characterized by swirls where water [SOURCE: Page_3]

recirculates, thus facilitating biomass growth and causing detached biofilm fragments to [SOURCE: Page_3]

deposit. In pipes, the liquid velocity at the solid-liquid interface is negligible resulting in a [SOURCE: Page_3]

boundary layer characterized by high shear forces and velocity gradients (Landau and Lifchitz [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

1989). With low flow velocities, the boundary layer becomes thicker and provides favorable [SOURCE: Page_4]

conditions for the slow but constant adhesion of bacteria over time. However, higher flow [SOURCE: Page_4]

velocities amplify the adhesion strength of biofilm (Chen et al. 2005), and this favors the [SOURCE: Page_4]

transport of the substrate through the porous biofilm, which contributes to its development [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Characklis et al. 1982; Wäsche et al. 2002). On the other hand excessively high speeds [SOURCE: Page_4]

determine shear forces which induce a delay in the initiation phase (21 days for 1.2m s-1 [SOURCE: Page_4]

instead of 10 days for 0.8 m s-1) (Gamri 2014) and detachment of bacterial cells (Donlan [SOURCE: Page_4]

2002; Horn et al. 2003). [SOURCE: Page_4]

According to Li et al. (2012) the flow velocity inside pipes transporting reclaimed wastewater [SOURCE: Page_4]

for micro-irrigation influences the growth and surface topography of biofilms. For the [SOURCE: Page_4]

aforementioned work, polyethylene pipes with an outside diameter of 16 mm and inside [SOURCE: Page_4]

diameter of 13.8 mm were used. Different flow velocities were studied from 0.06, to 1.78 m s- 1 (916 < Re < 29340). The average biofilm thickness reached its maximum at velocity 0.45 m [SOURCE: Page_4]

s-1 with a shear stress of 0.81 Pa under a turbulent regime (Re = 7334). Other research studies [SOURCE: Page_4]

have shown that very high velocities determine shear stress on the biofilm, which leads to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

detachment of some or all of the biofilm (Characklis 1981; Melo and Vieira 1999; Horn et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

2003; Derlon et al. 2008). Since the present study is on micro-irrigation, the conditions of [SOURCE: Page_4]

shear stresses selected are those found in irrigation pipes and drippers. As the study of biofilm [SOURCE: Page_4]

development inside drippers and pipes at defined shear stress is difficult, a Taylor-Couette [SOURCE: Page_4]

reactor (TCR) is used as a powerful tool to study the effect of hydrodynamic condition on [SOURCE: Page_4]

biofilm development. TCR is a rotating annular bioreactor (RAB) that provides a constant [SOURCE: Page_4]

shear stress distribution and growth of biofilm in a turbulent flow environment (Characklis [SOURCE: Page_4]

1990; Gomes et al. 2014). Understanding and mastering TCR hydrodynamic conditions can [SOURCE: Page_4]

facilitate the comprehension of biofilm initiation, development and detachment. Previous [SOURCE: Page_4]

TCR studies using wastewater showed that biofilm presents three detachment zones related to [SOURCE: Page_4]

the range of shear stress. Detachment is greater inside the first zone (for shear stress values [SOURCE: Page_5]

below 0.3 Pa) than at the second (for shear stress ranging from 0.3 to 2 Pa), the third zone can [SOURCE: Page_5]

resist shear stress magnitude up to 13 Pa (Derlon et al. 2007). Rochex et al. (2008) observed [SOURCE: Page_5]

that an increase in shear stress from 0.055 Pa to 0.27 Pa in the same TCR: reduces the [SOURCE: Page_5]

diversity of bacterial communities that colonize surfaces, slows down biofilm maturation, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

The objective of this paper is to study the kinetic development of biofilm and associated [SOURCE: Page_5]
