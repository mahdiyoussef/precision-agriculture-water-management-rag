Montpellier, INRAE, Institut Agro, Montpellier, France 14 15 16 17                                                                   Abstract 18                    We consider a simple crop irrigation model and study the optimal control which consists of maximizing the 19                biomass production at harvesting time. A specificity of this work is to impose a quota on the water used for 20                irrigation, in a context of limited resources. The model is written as a 2d non-autonomous dynamical system 21                with a state constraint, and a non-smooth right member given by threshold-based soil and crop water stress Fo [SOURCE: Page_2]

functions. We show that when the water quota is below the threshold giving the largest possible production, 22 the optimal strategy consists of irrigating once. We then show that the optimal solution can have one or several 23 singular arcs, and therefore be better than simple bang-bang controls, as commonly used. The gains over the 24                best bang-bang controls are illustrated on numerical simulations. These new feedback controls that we obtain rP [SOURCE: Page_2]

25                are a promising first step towards the concrete application of control theory to the problem of optimal irrigation 26                scheduling under water scarcity. 27 Key-words. Crop irrigation, water management, optimal control, singular arcs, feedback synthesis. 28 ee [SOURCE: Page_2]

29 30        1       Introduction 31 rR [SOURCE: Page_2]

32        Today, in the context of the climate crisis, tensions around water resources are growing, and agriculture in many 33        countries, particularly in the South, must now be considered as having to be water-saving. One way to cope 34        with this changing context is to impose irrigation quotas. But such quotas could imply yield losses as the best 35 ev [SOURCE: Page_2]

crop water requirements would not necessarily be fulfilled. In these particular but expected situations, optimizing 36        the schedule of irrigation to minimize yield losses becomes crucial. Crop modeling and numerical simulations are 37        handy tools for understanding and adjusting these compromises. More particularly the control theory as irrigation 38 iew [SOURCE: Page_2]

is typically a control variable of a crop model. 39            Numerous texts and articles have developed systematic approaches to tackle the scheduling optimization prob- 40        lem. We refer to [11, 12, 18, 19, 23] and references therein. However, most of the existing approaches do not allow 41        to have analytical descriptions of an optimal solution’s theoretical properties as they are based on the numerical 42        optimization of complex models. Control theoretical tools, such as the Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle, allow, as 43        a rule, to discover structures of optimal strategies in terms of state feedback available for large classes of operating 44        conditions and parameter values. Unlike optimization among open-loop controls that apply for precise require- 45        ments, closed-loop procedures offer adaptability and robustness for concrete applications, when state variables 46        can be measured in real-time. However, when models have too numerous equations, variables, and parameters 47        or do not present a robust structure, the study of optimal strategies with these techniques is most of the time 48        out of reach of analytical characterizations. This is a motivation to consider models with a reasonable simplicity 49        to benefit from these theoretical approaches (see e.g. [13, 17]). Even when these models are imperfect, optimal 50        policies’ derivation is of possible relevance. It generally provides simple but non-intuitive control rules that can 51        be tested in simulation on more realistic detailed models. Sometimes, combinations of analytical description and 52        reduced optimization can be obtained, which still offer advantages over purely numerical solutions in open-loop. 53        This is the spirit in which this work has been conducted. 54            The aim of this work is investigate optimal irrigation strategies in the context of water quotas, with the help 55        of a simplified crop model. The objective is to characterize the maximal biomass production that can be obtained 56        for a given value of the quota (as an intrinsic performance of the system without consideration of water pricing), 57        and for which feedback strategy it can be achieved. 29 30 7        Conclusion 31 rR [SOURCE: Page_18]

We have introduced a simple crop irrigation model in order to study optimal irrigation scheduling using mathe- 32       matical analysis. We have first shown, using a comparison tool, that the state constraint of this model is never 33       activated for the optimal control problem solutions. 34           Moreover, we have demonstrated that, under water scarcity, an optimal trajectory has to reach as fast as possible 35 ev [SOURCE: Page_18]

the domain for which the relative humidity is below or equal to the threshold of maximal crop transpiration, and 36       then to maintain it in this domain until the harvesting time. However, due to water scarcity, it has to be strictly 37       below this threshold at some stage. We have then proved that the optimal strategy consists of irrigating once but 38 iew [SOURCE: Page_18]

with possible multiple steps (”SMS strategy”) and not necessarily with a single shot (”OS strategy”) as commonly 39       used in practice. Moreover, we have shown that when the water-stress functions do not present any corner in 40       between their extreme values, the SMS strategy has at most one step which is necessary at the maximal crop 41       transpiration threshold (that we called ”SOS strategy”). The SOS strategy is simpler to apply than the SMS one 42       (with more than one step) as the soil moisture has simply to be maintained at the transpiration threshold until 43       the water quota is reached. A remarkable feature is that the structure of the optimal strategy does not depend 44       on the radiation interception efficiency function (although optimal trigger threshold and step values do rely on 45       this function). As the considered stress functions are piecewise linear, we have used the non-smooth Maximum 46       Principle to obtain relatively simple strategies, relying on the single determination of singular arcs. Paradoxically, 47       the consideration of smooth stress functions would have led to optimal strategies without a particular structure 48       easy to characterize and to implement as the SMS (and SOS) ones. 49           We have then compared the three control strategies: the open-loop one-shot (OS), commonly used in practice, 50       the feedback saturated one-shot (SOS), that could exhibit a singular arc, and the more sophisticated feedback 51       strategy with multiple increasing shots (SMS), that could exhibit several singular arcs. We have shown numerically 52       the superiority of this last strategy. We guess that the SMS strategy (that coincides with the SOS one for simple 53       water-stress functions) could also be the best one in real situations. This would be a promising result since 54       SMS/SOS irrigation schemes are not such intuitive control strategies, that could also be tested on simulations of 55       more detailed models. This shall be the matter of future work. 56 57 58 59 60 17 [SOURCE: Page_18]
