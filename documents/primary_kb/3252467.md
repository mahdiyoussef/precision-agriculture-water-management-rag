## 46   ABSTRACT : 47

48   Hydrochorous dispersal may play a major role in the propagation of weeds such as Sorghum [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   halepense (Johnsongrass). However, the long-distance dispersal of this weed via agricultural [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   intermittent channel networks, such as irrigation channels, has been poorly studied. In this [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   study we focused on a seed dispersal kernel obtained after an irrigation event in a vegetated [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   channel. The main objectives of the study were to highlight the specificities of irrigation [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   channels in relation to seed transport, and investigate hydraulic factors and microscale [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   channel features associated with the deposition of seeds along the channel. Our results [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   showed that despite very different flow and morphological conditions compared to natural [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   waterways, seeds of Johnsongrass were able to travel hundred meters at the scale of one [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   irrigation event. This is likely due to the floating ability of more than 50% of the Johnsongrass [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   seeds. A Regression Tree algorithm, which explained 72% of seed deposition variability, [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   showed the importance of the channel features such as the width of the section and the [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   hydraulic structures, sluice gates and culvert, to explain the patterns of seed deposition. A [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   complex role of the emergent vegetation was highlighted. The results suggest a threshold of [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   vegetation density should be reached to enhance seed deposition. The results indicate that the [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   spread of existing Johnsongrass populations could be limited by preserving existing hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   structures, or maintaining areas where the density of terrestrial vegetation is sufficient to [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   enhance seed retention downstream. [SOURCE: Page_3]

## 73   1. INTRODUCTION

74 [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   Diaspore dispersal is a major mechanism in plant distribution and propagation (Bolker and [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   Pacala, 1999 ; Bullock et al., 2006, Nilsson et al., 2010). For many species living next to open [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   channels, such as irrigation channels or surface drainage ditches, water is a major vector of [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   transport (Favre-Bac et al., 2014 ; Soomers et al., 2010 ; Van Dijk et al., 2014). Channels [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   have been shown to be important corridors in agricultural landscapes (Van Dijk et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   Rainfall or irrigation events are episodic events that can be crucial for dispersal (Cain et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   2000) in open channels. [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   For weeds, dispersal determines the dynamics of invasion. Sorghum halepense, also called [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   Johnsongrass, is one of the most widespread weeds in the world (Holm et al., 1977). This [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   herbicide-resistant perennial grass is mostly found in Mediterranean and tropical areas [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   (McWhorter, 1989). Warm temperatures and good moisture conditions have been reported to [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   enhance its development (Andújar et al., 2013), which explains why it is commonly found [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   next to irrigation channels (Holm et al., 1977). Johnsongrass has been reported in maize fields [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   (Andújar et al., 2011 ; Ghersa et al., 1993), tomato fields (Andújar et al., 2013), and poplar [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   fields (San-Martín et al., 2016). Its presence causes yield losses due to high competition with [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   crops and consequently lower incomes for farmers. Warwick and Black (1983) showed that [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   Johnsongrass can spread by both rhizomes and seeds. However, the long-distance dispersal of [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   Johnsongrass, mainly associated with seeds, has been poorly studied. [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   Attempts have been made to characterize long-distance dispersal kernels for seeds despite the [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   inherent difficulty of this process (Cain et al., 2000). Long-distance dispersal can be defined [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   as a dispersal distance that is more than 100 m, or the distance of dispersal of the seeds that [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   are in the upper 1% of the total seed dispersal distances (Cain et al., 2000 ; Turchin, 1998). [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   Long-distance dispersal, or, more simply, the tail of the dispersal kernel, is very important [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   because its estimation affects the results of models of population spread at large spatial scales [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   (Bullock et al., 2006) but has been little studied in streams (Nilsson et al., 2010) and, to the [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   best of our knowledge, never in irrigation channels. Some formulas for characterizing [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   dispersal kernels have been proposed over time, that were mainly functions of the distance [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   from the seed release location (Bullock et al., 2006 ; Greene and Calogeropoulos, 2002 ; [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   Nathan et al., 2011). Over the years, some authors have highlighted the fact that these models [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   did not effectively take into account the heterogeneity of the environment crossed by the [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   seeds (Bullock et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   There is a large literature focusing on hydrochory in natural waterways, i.e. rivers and upland [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   streams (Boedeltje et al., 2003, 2004; Riis, 2008; Jansson et al., 2005; Greet et al., 2011; [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   2012, 2013). By comparing seedlots trapped with net and their nearest stands, authors have [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   estimated distance events of several kilometres (Boedeltje et al., 2003). They have also [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   highlighted effects of seasonal flow variation on composition of riverine seed banks [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   dispersing by hydrochory (Greet et al., 2011, 2012, 2013). Compared to natural waterways, [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   irrigation channels have specificities that justify a study on their influence on seed dispersal. [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   Indeed, these channels sometimes contain hydraulic structures for irrigation purposes such as [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   culverts or sluice gates that could impact hydrochory. Due to their restricted hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   capacities and operation rules, flow patterns in irrigation channels present less variations than [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   those encountered in natural streams, and do not experience extreme flow (flood pulse) [SOURCE: Page_5]

117   conditions resulting in erosion of sediments and seeds as can happen in rivers (Riis et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   2008; Boedeltje et al., 2003, 2004). This could limit long-distance hydrochorous dispersal. [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   Also, while main channels can be perennial, lower level channels conveying water to fields [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   are intermittent. Such channels, covering by far the largest cumulated length of irrigation [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   channels, experience long periods without water that allow non-aquatic species to develop [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   inside the channel bed, the latter being submerged during water inflow events of limited [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   duration compared to natural waterways. They also have a smaller width than rivers, which [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   could limit the long distance dispersal of seeds by hydrochory due to increased retention [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   probability on banks (Nilsson et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   Regardless, irrigation channels convey important flows in networks of many kilometres, and [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   the factors that can affect the transport of seeds during an irrigation event are numerous. We [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   differentiate intrinsic factors that correspond to the traits of the seeds from extrinsic factors [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   such as the morphology of the channel (Nilsson et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   Amongst the intrinsic factors shaping seed dispersal kernel figures its buoyancy or its ability [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   in spending time in water (Boedeltje et al., 2003). For example, some authors indicated that [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   dispersal distance of seeds is enhanced by high seed buoyancy (Boedeltje et al., 2004; Riis, [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   2008; van den Broek et al., 2009). When considering the extrinsic factors affecting seed [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   dispersal, we know that flow hydraulics is the first determinant of the pattern of the kernel [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   (Merritt and Wohl, 2002 ; Nilsson et al., 2003), as it has been determined for riverine [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   landscapes. Hydrodynamic phenomena that develop both at the free surface and in the water [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   column are crucial for seed deposition but are very difficult to estimate in real channels [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   because they depend on several factors. Seeds also interact with channel microscale features, [SOURCE: Page_6]

139   such as the density of the living or dead vegetation (Chambert and James, 2009 ; Defina and [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   Peruzzo 2010 ; O’Donnell et al., 2015), the channel morphology (Merritt and Wohl, 2002 ; [SOURCE: Page_6]

141   Soomers et al., 2010), and the presence of hydraulic structures (Merritt and Wohl, 2006). Due [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

142   to the high number of interacting factors, their variability along channels (Groves et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   2009), and the inherent difficulties in assessing some of them for an entire channel, the [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   models for predicting water-dispersed kernels lack accuracy. [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   To calibrate these models, continuous dispersal kernels are needed (Portnoy and Willson, [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   1993). Three main methods have been used to determine dispersal kernels in the field: [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   tracking (Lagrangian method), trapping (Eulerian method) and ‘re-locating’, i.e., the [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   relocation of diaspores after the dispersal event (Eulerian method). Tracking is a very time- [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   consuming method, and the small number of seeds tracked does not generally allow an [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   accurate shape of the kernel to be obtained. Trapping is more efficient, but the choice of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   trap locations can largely affect the results. Re-locating is therefore potentially the best [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   method for water ecosystems but also raises the problem of the rate of seed relocation. For [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   small seeds, such as those of Johnsongrass, relocation is a real challenge (Bullock et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   2006). [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   This study focused on the dispersal kernel of seeds of Johnsongrass obtained at the event scale [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   (a single irrigation event of around one hour) in an irrigation channel. We worked on the tail [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   of the dispersal kernel because we focused on the factors affecting the long-distance dispersal. [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   The objectives of the study were to (i) highlight the specific intrinsic and extrinsic factors [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   affecting the long-distance dispersal of Johnsongrass, (ii) implement an efficient method to [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   reconstitute and characterize a seed dispersal kernel in intermittent channels. Our main [SOURCE: Page_7]

161   hypothesis was that the tail of the dispersal kernel is largely affected by channel microscale [SOURCE: Page_7]

162   features (especially bank and in-channel vegetation). [SOURCE: Page_7]

## 164   2. MATERIAL AND METHODS

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

165   The method employed in this paper followed three main steps: (i) a relocation experiment in a [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   vegetated irrigation channel, (ii) the interpolation of the seed dispersal data using kriging in [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   order to reconstitute the full dispersal kernel, (iii) the study of the factors affecting the tail of [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   the dispersal kernel. [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   2.1. Study site [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   The Crau plain is a 600 km² flat area in southern France. The first channel networks were [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   built between the 12th and 15th century to divert water from the Durance River for different [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   land uses, such as mill functioning and irrigation. Currently, the channel network is still [SOURCE: Page_8]

173   operational and supplies some 14 000 hectares of irrigation command area. [SOURCE: Page_8]

174   The Crau climate is Mediterranean with mean annual rainfall of approximately 560 mm with [SOURCE: Page_8]

175   high interannual variability and high potential evapotranspiration (1060 mm/year) (data issued [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   from the INRA CLIMATIK platform, https://intranet.inra.fr/climatik_v^2). Crau agriculture is [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   mainly high-quality hay produced in permanent grasslands. Border irrigation is a widespread [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   practice in the area. Water is conveyed from primary channels to secondary or tertiary [SOURCE: Page_8]

179   channels that border the grassland plots. Water is then ponded by closed sluice gates and [SOURCE: Page_8]

180   overflows from the channels to irrigate sections of the plots called ‘calans’. The high [SOURCE: Page_8]

181   hydrological connectivity in the system is thought to favour weed diaspore transport into the [SOURCE: Page_8]

182   grasslands. [SOURCE: Page_8]

183   The ‘Domaine du Merle’ is an experimental and educational station (Montpellier SupAgro) in [SOURCE: Page_8]

184   the Crau region. The channel chosen for the experiment (Fig. 1) is straight and flows from [SOURCE: Page_8]

185   north to south (43.644478 N-5,007125 E to 43,640391 N-5,005966 E). The channel depth [SOURCE: Page_8]

186   varies from 0.34 to 0.74 m with a mean upper width of 2.11 m. It is a tertiary channel that [SOURCE: Page_8]

187   irrigates a 4.2 ha plot divided into 6 “calans” (irrigated surface unit). The mean slope is [SOURCE: Page_8]

188   0.31%, and the length is approximately 450 m. The channel has an upstream valve controlling [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

189   the flow of water, and five other hydraulic structures (four sluice gates and one pipe culvert) [SOURCE: Page_9]

190   (Fig. 1) located on the studied section serve to irrigate each calan in the plot. The channel is [SOURCE: Page_9]

191   heterogeneously vegetated with terrestrial vegetation species that were found at different [SOURCE: Page_9]

192   densities according to the location in the channel such as Conyza Canadensis, Paspalum [SOURCE: Page_9]

193   Dilatatum, Plantago spp., Picris spp., Cyperus spp. [SOURCE: Page_9]

194   2.2. Channel experiment: Release of seeds during an irrigation event [SOURCE: Page_9]

195   Seeds from Johnsongrass (Fig. 2) were collected at the end of summer 2016 and their main [SOURCE: Page_9]

196   characteristics are presented in Table 1. The seeds were shaken within sieves of different [SOURCE: Page_9]

197   diameters to separate the seeds from larger and smaller fractions of the plant. We estimated [SOURCE: Page_9]

198   the length and width of the seeds using calipers. The longest axis (length) and the diameter of [SOURCE: Page_9]

199   the seed (width) were measured in millimetres with calipers and averaged over 10 seeds. We [SOURCE: Page_9]

200   estimated the mean weight of the seed by weighting 10 samples of 100 seeds each using a [SOURCE: Page_9]

201   high-precision balance (Precisa XB 160M, precision: 0.001 g and accuracy: 0.01g). [SOURCE: Page_9]

202   As buoyancy could be an important factor affecting seed dispersal (Boedeltje et al., 2003, [SOURCE: Page_9]

203   2004; Carthey et al., 2016), we estimated the potential duration of buoyancy following the [SOURCE: Page_9]

204   protocol from Boedeltje et al. (2003). According to this protocol, we placed ten samples of 50 [SOURCE: Page_9]

205   seeds in polyethylene cylindrical pots filled with tap water. The pots were then shaked for 5 s [SOURCE: Page_9]

206   and the number of floating seeds counted immediately, then every 4 min during the first hour, [SOURCE: Page_9]

207   every 30 min during the four following hours, and daily for two weeks. We determined the t^50, [SOURCE: Page_9]

208   t^90, and tmax periods after which 50%, 90%, and all of the seeds had sunk, respectively (Table [SOURCE: Page_9]

209   1). The complete dataset of seed buoyancy is described in Appendix S$_1$. [SOURCE: Page_9]

210   Tekiela and Barney (2013) proposed the use of fluorescent powders (which does not affect the [SOURCE: Page_9]

211   floating behaviour of the seeds) to efficiently detect the seeds after a dispersal event using UV [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

212   lights. We adapted the method developed in Tekiela and Barney (2013) for a one-dimensional [SOURCE: Page_10]

213   channel. The Johnsongrass seeds were then placed in plastic pots and shaken with an UV [SOURCE: Page_10]

214   powder in order to spread the powder uniformly onto all the seeds and entirely coat them. The [SOURCE: Page_10]

215   powder we used was the Ultraviolet Ultrabright Red Fluorescing Powder (UVXPBR), [SOURCE: Page_10]

216   distributed by LDP-LLC, Carlstadt, USA, as proposed in Tekiela and Barney (2013). The test [SOURCE: Page_10]

217   on the effect of the powder on seed buoyancy is described in Appendix S$_2$, following the [SOURCE: Page_10]

218   protocol described in Tekiela and Barney (2013). [SOURCE: Page_10]

219   The release experiment in the channel occurred in October 2016. Using a high precision [SOURCE: Page_10]

220   balance, we measured 30.47 g of seeds to get a sample of approximately 7000 +/- 250 seeds, [SOURCE: Page_10]

221   according to the mean weight of the seed. The sample was then marked with UVXPBR [SOURCE: Page_10]

222   powder. Thirty minutes before the experiment, the seeds were shaken in a bottle of water to [SOURCE: Page_10]

223   remove any possible additional powder. The irrigation water was released in the channel [SOURCE: Page_10]

224   using the upstream valve with a flow of around 0.07 m^3.s-1. The five sluice gates located [SOURCE: Page_10]

225   downstream in the channel remained open for the duration of the experiment. No overflow [SOURCE: Page_10]

226   into the grassland plot occurred because the purpose of the study was to understand the [SOURCE: Page_10]

227   dispersal of seeds inside the channel. After the valve was opened, the canal gradually filled, [SOURCE: Page_10]

228   and the flow became steady after approximately 20 minutes. After the steady regime had been [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   established, we released the seeds directly into the flow by submerging a pot containing the [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   seeds in the release area (Fig. 1). The upstream valve was closed 50 minutes after the valve [SOURCE: Page_10]

231   opened. [SOURCE: Page_10]

232   After stopping the inflow (by closing the upstream valve), a drainage time of 3 hours was [SOURCE: Page_10]

233   allowed to empty the channel by infiltration. Under dark conditions (at night), we walked [SOURCE: Page_10]

234   along the entirety of the channel with a 50 W UV high-powered rechargeable light (LDP-LLC [SOURCE: Page_10]

235   reference XniteFlashBFLUV) to identify the seeds marked with the fluorescent powder. A [SOURCE: Page_10]

10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

236   series of photos was taken along the channel according to distance intervals described below [SOURCE: Page_11]

237   using a Nikon D3200 camera with an AF DX Fisheye-Nikkor objective set to infinity. A [SOURCE: Page_11]

238   fluorescent wooden frame (80×155 cm) was used to delimit a surface for photographing the [SOURCE: Page_11]

239   deposited seeds. The camera was fixed onto the frame. Considering the dark conditions, the [SOURCE: Page_11]

240   exposure of the camera was set to 10 seconds, and the entire surface of the wooden frame was [SOURCE: Page_11]

241   scanned with a UV light. From the seed release area to the first sluice gate (the first 25 m), the [SOURCE: Page_11]

242   channel was photographed exhaustively, but from the first sluice gate, photographs were [SOURCE: Page_11]

243   taken every 5 m because the density of deposited seeds exponentially decreased. [SOURCE: Page_11]

244   All image analyses were run in batch mode using the software ImageJ (Schneider et al., [SOURCE: Page_11]

245   2012). Every photo in RGB colour mode was cropped to remove the wooden frame. The [SOURCE: Page_11]

246   photos were resized considering the known length and width of the wooden frame. Then, the [SOURCE: Page_11]

247   background was corrected using a rolling ball algorithm to delete the illuminated areas of [SOURCE: Page_11]

248   vegetation or ground. The red channel (because UVXPBR powder is red) was separated into [SOURCE: Page_11]

249   black and white images using a threshold filter. Seed contours were counted on each image [SOURCE: Page_11]

250   using a particle segmentation algorithm to obtain number of seeds per image. [SOURCE: Page_11]

251   During the steady-state flow, the water-surface elevation was measured using an electronic [SOURCE: Page_11]

252   tacheometer every 5 m. In dry conditions, the channel topography, i.e. the Digital Terrain [SOURCE: Page_11]

253   Model (DTM) and Digital Surface Model (DSM), was characterized. The DTM was created [SOURCE: Page_11]

254   from elevation cross-sectional profiles every 10 m from the tacheometer. The DSM, i.e. the [SOURCE: Page_11]

255   channel topography including the vegetation cover, was continuously characterized by a [SOURCE: Page_11]

256   multi-view stereo approach. We obtained approximately 4000 images with a Nikon D3200 [SOURCE: Page_11]

257   camera with a fixed 28 mm AF Nikkor objective along the channel and we reconstructed the [SOURCE: Page_11]

258   DSM using digital image matching with Photoscan Pro® (Gillanet al., 2014) (Fig. 1). The [SOURCE: Page_11]

11 [SOURCE: Page_11]

259   DSM was aligned to the DTM using ground control points distributed every 5 meters along [SOURCE: Page_12]

260   the channel. [SOURCE: Page_12]

261   2.3. Interpolation of the dispersal data via a kriging method [SOURCE: Page_12]

262   The seed deposition dataset along the channel came from each photo location as described [SOURCE: Page_12]

263   above and was thus irregularly sampled along the channel. To characterize the local spatial [SOURCE: Page_12]

264   structure of seed dispersal and perform appropriate interpolation, the spatial variation in seed [SOURCE: Page_12]

265   deposition was first studied using a 1D variogram. First, a log-normal transformation was [SOURCE: Page_12]

266   performed on the data. Then, using an automatic procedure in geoR (Ribeiro and Diggle, [SOURCE: Page_12]

267   2001), we fitted the variogram model assuming a stationary isotropic covariance model, and [SOURCE: Page_12]

268   we determined the nugget, sill and range. The interpolation and extrapolation of the whole [SOURCE: Page_12]

269   dispersal data was conducted using ordinary kriging. The step for the interpolation was 0.8 m [SOURCE: Page_12]

270   because this length corresponded to the width of the wood frame we used to take the photos of [SOURCE: Page_12]

271   the seeds. The kriging was performed using the R RandomFields package (Schlather et al., [SOURCE: Page_12]

272   2015). [SOURCE: Page_12]

273   2.4. Factors affecting the tail of the dispersal kernel [SOURCE: Page_12]

274   We worked on the reconstituted dispersal data from a set distance (15 m) from the seed [SOURCE: Page_12]

275   release area. Although this was not strictly considered to be the tail of the dispersal kernel [SOURCE: Page_12]

276   according to the definitions given by Cain et al. (2000), we considered this distance to be [SOURCE: Page_12]

277   outside the influence of the first peak of the dispersal kernel. The distance was determined [SOURCE: Page_12]

278   observing the shape of the dispersal kernel. [SOURCE: Page_12]

279   The explanatory factors we chose could be differentiated between “hydraulic” factors and [SOURCE: Page_12]

280   factors linked to channel microscale features. The hydraulic factors were: the hydraulic radius [SOURCE: Page_12]

281   (Hr), the mean velocity (V) and the width of the section at the water-surface (WidthSection). [SOURCE: Page_12]

12 [SOURCE: Page_12]

282   Factors linked with channel microscale features were the downstream influence of hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_13]

283   structures (Down_Struct), the percentage of vegetation cover above the level of the water [SOURCE: Page_13]

284   (Pem) and below the level of the water (Pim). [SOURCE: Page_13]

285   Hr and WidthSection could be directly derived from the DTM and DSM, and from the water- [SOURCE: Page_13]

286   surface elevation data measured during the channel experiment at steady flow. The mean [SOURCE: Page_13]

287   velocity V was estimated based on simulation results from SIC software (Baume et al., 2005) [SOURCE: Page_13]

## 337   4. DISCUSSION

338   4.1. Dispersal traits of Johnsongrass [SOURCE: Page_15]

339   Our study demonstrates that Johnsongrass seeds can be transported considerable distances [SOURCE: Page_15]

340   from their sources (hundreds of meters), at the time-scale of a single irrigation event. [SOURCE: Page_15]

341   Dispersal distances of this range have been observed for other hydrochorous seeds in [SOURCE: Page_15]

342   agricultural networks (Soomers et al., 2010), but with very different hydraulic conditions [SOURCE: Page_15]

343   (lower mean velocities) and higher transfer times. This ability to be transported considerable [SOURCE: Page_15]

344   distances from the source has to be analyzed jointly with the ability of effective germination [SOURCE: Page_15]

345   and establishment to make conclusions on the potential spread of this weed via channels (Riis, [SOURCE: Page_15]

346   2008). Note that Johnsongrass seeds have been reported to keep a strong ability to germinate [SOURCE: Page_15]

347   at least two and a half years after soil burial (Warwick and Black, 1983). [SOURCE: Page_15]

348   Amongst species traits, buoyancy could be considered a key factor determining species [SOURCE: Page_15]

349   dispersal abilities (Boedeltje et al., 2004). Johnsongrass seedlots belong to semi-aquatic and [SOURCE: Page_15]

15 [SOURCE: Page_15]

350   terrestrial bank species with relatively low buoyancy (see Appendix S$_1$ in Boedeltje et al. [SOURCE: Page_16]

351   (2003)), because a significant part of the Johnsongrass seedlots sunk immediately after [SOURCE: Page_16]

352   soaking (35%) and had a t^50 lower than the day. This trait is in accordance with the result [SOURCE: Page_16]

353   found by Boedeltje et al. (2004) for species hydrochory on floodplains, namely that t^50 of [SOURCE: Page_16]

354   Johnsongrass lasted the same duration as the irrigation event. [SOURCE: Page_16]

355   The shape of the dispersal kernel suggested that the peak was principally due to non-floating [SOURCE: Page_16]

356   seeds that were trapped by the morphology of the channel bottom, the tail being primarily [SOURCE: Page_16]

357   composed of floating seeds. This assumption of short range dispersal of non-buoyant seeds is [SOURCE: Page_16]

358   in contradiction with Markwith and Leigh (2008) that estimated a potential subaqueous [SOURCE: Page_16]

359   hydrochory over several kilometres for large seeds of Hymenocallis coronaria in rivers during [SOURCE: Page_16]

360   flood events. Long dispersal distances were not observed in this study. On the one hand, the [SOURCE: Page_16]

361   bottom shear stresses and the mean velocities were lower in the studied channels than in rivers [SOURCE: Page_16]

362   during floods. On the other hand, we think that non buoyant seeds are likely to be trapped [SOURCE: Page_16]

363   quickly after release due to vegetation and its role on benthic velocity and filtering effect. In [SOURCE: Page_16]

364   all cases, as buoyancy is a dynamical process, it could be useful to test this assumption using a [SOURCE: Page_16]

365   mechanistic model (for an example of a wind/water dispersal model, see Soomers et al. [SOURCE: Page_16]

366   (2012)). [SOURCE: Page_16]

367 [SOURCE: Page_16]

368   4.2. Structural effects of channel on the tail of the dispersal kernel [SOURCE: Page_16]

369   The results of this study also support the assumption that the deposition of seeds along a [SOURCE: Page_16]

370   channel is both associated with channel microscale features (Levine and Murrell, 2003; [SOURCE: Page_16]

371   Soomers et al., 2010), and hydraulic characteristics (Merritt and Wohl, 2002). Width of the [SOURCE: Page_16]

372   section was an important factor for seed deposition in our study. Based on the results of the [SOURCE: Page_16]

16 [SOURCE: Page_16]

373   regression trees, higher values of section widths were linked with important deposition rates. [SOURCE: Page_17]

374   This result could be related to area of low velocities and probably recirculating zones [SOURCE: Page_17]

375   favouring the seed deposition. It was noted that the major rises in channel widths were located [SOURCE: Page_17]

376   after the hydraulic structures where most seeds deposited. The increase of the channel width [SOURCE: Page_17]

377   at the water-surface can also be linked with the increase of the roughness of the channel (the [SOURCE: Page_17]

378   latter causing an increase of the water level, the channel cross-section being trapezoidal). This [SOURCE: Page_17]

379   would cause a decrease of local mean velocity and consequently enhanced deposition of [SOURCE: Page_17]

380   seeds. [SOURCE: Page_17]

381   The effect of the mean flow velocity was poor compared to other variables. Based on the [SOURCE: Page_17]

382   results of the regression tree 0–80 m, a higher velocity was associated with a higher [SOURCE: Page_17]

383   deposition rate. It appears that the local acceleration of mean velocity in the channel was [SOURCE: Page_17]

384   linked to the presence of the hydraulic structures because they create local narrowing of the [SOURCE: Page_17]

385   channel. Merritt and Wohl (2002) also reported that in such high-energy areas, seed [SOURCE: Page_17]

386   deposition could be enhanced due to macroturbulent waves that could deposit seeds a little [SOURCE: Page_17]

387   above the water level. [SOURCE: Page_17]

388   As in other studies, hydraulic structures, such as the culverts studied in Beltman et al. (2005) [SOURCE: Page_17]

389   and Soomers et al. (2010) and their hydraulic impacts, such as backwater effects (Merritt and [SOURCE: Page_17]

390   Wohl, 2002; Nilsson et al.,1991), were linked with a high rate of deposition, especially for the [SOURCE: Page_17]

391   third section of the channel where a culvert was located. In our study, the expansion after the [SOURCE: Page_17]

392   hydraulic structures created large eddies, and their presence was positively linked with seed [SOURCE: Page_17]

393   deposition. In line with our results, Merritt and Wohl (2002) reported that the recirculation [SOURCE: Page_17]

394   index defined in their study was associated with a high rate of deposition, and Cunnings [SOURCE: Page_17]

395   (2013) linked the importance of storage areas with the magnitude of eddies. In our study, it [SOURCE: Page_17]

396   appears that the culvert had more influence on seed deposition than the sluice gates, but no [SOURCE: Page_17]

17 [SOURCE: Page_17]

397   study comparing the hydraulic effects of sluice gates with those of culverts could confirm this [SOURCE: Page_18]

398   result (the calculation of the length of eddies we made for the calculation of downstream [SOURCE: Page_18]

399   influence of hydraulic structures was based on a rectangular section for all the hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_18]

400   structures: sluice gates and culvert). However, the indirect effects caused by the presence of [SOURCE: Page_18]

401   hydraulic structures can both be linked with a change of the morphology of the channel over [SOURCE: Page_18]

402   time, and modify local hydraulic conditions, creating favourable conditions for the deposition [SOURCE: Page_18]

403   of seeds. This is a specificity of highly managed networks: the presence of hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_18]

404   structures (even in open position) creates discontinuities that partly shape the pattern of seed [SOURCE: Page_18]

405   deposition and the community composition in the long-term (Favre-Bac et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_18]

406   4.3. Effects of vegetation on the tail of the dispersal kernel [SOURCE: Page_18]

407   Emergent obstacles, such as vegetation, have been reported to enhance the deposition of seeds [SOURCE: Page_18]

408   (Soomers et al., 2010; Schneider and Sharitz, 1988). In this study, the role of emergent [SOURCE: Page_18]

409   vegetation was complex to interpret, because vegetation above water level could have a [SOURCE: Page_18]

410   positive or negative influence on deposition. Non-linear effects could explain this apparent [SOURCE: Page_18]

411   contradiction. Indeed, Gambi et al. (1990) found that the turbulent kinetic energy initially [SOURCE: Page_18]

412   increased with stem density, then decreased as stem density increased further. According to [SOURCE: Page_18]

413   Defina and Peruzzo (2010), another expected effect of emergent vegetation on floating seed [SOURCE: Page_18]

414   trapping in such hydraulic conditions would be the net-trapping effect, i.e. when plant leaves [SOURCE: Page_18]

415   form a netlike structure at the water surface that intercepts the floating seeds. In our study, [SOURCE: Page_18]

416   when the cover of emergent vegetation was sparse (between 0 and 20% of surface according [SOURCE: Page_18]

417   to the regression tree over the whole channel, see Appendix S$_3$), the increase of stem-scale [SOURCE: Page_18]

418   turbulences may explain why emergent vegetation has a negative effect on seed retention. [SOURCE: Page_18]

419   When emergent vegetation cover increases (more than 50% of the surface according to the [SOURCE: Page_18]

420   regression tree on the whole channel, see Appendix S$_3$), the cumulative effect of the decrease [SOURCE: Page_18]

18 [SOURCE: Page_18]

421   of turbulences and increase of net-trapping effect can explain a positive effect on seed [SOURCE: Page_19]

422   retention. Obviously, the thresholds associated with these different behaviours may vary both [SOURCE: Page_19]

423   with plant architectures and specific spatial organizations in the channel, and with the flow [SOURCE: Page_19]

424   regime. These preliminary results should be confirmed in controlled conditions using flume [SOURCE: Page_19]

425   experiments to disentangle the influence of plant architecture, spatial organisation, density, [SOURCE: Page_19]

426   and flow regime. [SOURCE: Page_19]

427   Moreover, these effects caused by emergent vegetation can interact with the effect of [SOURCE: Page_19]

428   submerged vegetation. In this study, the main effect of vegetation below water level is that it [SOURCE: Page_19]

429   decreases the deposition of seeds. Indeed, by increasing the roughness, vegetation below [SOURCE: Page_19]

430   water level decreases the depth-averaged velocity, but also can create local acceleration of [SOURCE: Page_19]

431   flow velocity or large-scale turbulence (Nepf, 2012a) in the upper layer of the water, [SOURCE: Page_19]

432   explaining this particular effect on the deposition of floating seeds. However, these effects of [SOURCE: Page_19]

433   vegetation are difficult to disentangle and assess simultaneously (Peruzzo et al., 2012), [SOURCE: Page_19]

434   especially at the reach scale. [SOURCE: Page_19]

435   4.4. Potential of the relocating method for intermittent channels [SOURCE: Page_19]

436   The method used in our experiment represents a significant improvement compared to other [SOURCE: Page_19]

437   approaches because it relies on a relocating method that makes a snapshot of the seed [SOURCE: Page_19]

438   deposition at several specific locations to characterize the whole dispersal kernel of the seeds. [SOURCE: Page_19]

439   Generally, methods attempting to characterize seed dispersal by hydrochory in natural [SOURCE: Page_19]

440   waterways use nets to quantify the diaspores at one specific location, the protocol being [SOURCE: Page_19]

441   difficult to reproduce multiple times across the length of the stream (Boedeltje et al. 2003, [SOURCE: Page_19]

442   2004, Greet et al., 2012). Combined with a kriging procedure to fill the non-observed [SOURCE: Page_19]

443   locations, our method provides a complete overview of one-species dispersal kernel [SOURCE: Page_19]

444   regardless of their buoyancy over hundreds of meters distances. [SOURCE: Page_19]

19 [SOURCE: Page_19]

445   The method is reproducible for characterizing the dispersal kernel of other small diaspores [SOURCE: Page_20]

446   and could be used for other linear water ecosystems. Note that the method requires [SOURCE: Page_20]

447   intermittent flows because the relocating method is hampered when water is still present in [SOURCE: Page_20]

448   the channel. The method also illustrated the possibility of describing microscale variability [SOURCE: Page_20]

449   linked with channel morphology and vegetation along an irrigation channel. We also [SOURCE: Page_20]

450   estimated that the method could be replicated within drainage channels that display similar [SOURCE: Page_20]

451   characteristics to irrigation channels, with similar shapes and operation rules, but different [SOURCE: Page_20]

452   hydraulic structures and greater slopes (Dollinger et al., 2015). This is a significant [SOURCE: Page_20]

453   improvement for the development of future mechanistic transport models, because as [SOURCE: Page_20]

454   underlined in Groves et al. (2009), these data are often lacking for field conditions and [SOURCE: Page_20]

455   therefore are rarely considered in modelling studies. [SOURCE: Page_20]

## 457   5. CONCLUSION

458   This study presents an experiment aimed at characterizing the dispersal kernel of [SOURCE: Page_20]

459   Johnsongrass seeds in a vegetated irrigation channel. Johnsongrass seeds can travel long- [SOURCE: Page_20]

460   distances (hundreds of meters) during a single irrigation event. Our results validate the [SOURCE: Page_20]

461   hypothesis that channel microscale features influence the tail of the dispersal kernel and [SOURCE: Page_20]

462   demonstrated the importance of the characteristics of open channels in comparison to natural [SOURCE: Page_20]

463   waterways in explaining seed deposition, such as channel width, emergent vegetation and [SOURCE: Page_20]

464   hydraulic structures. Compared to natural waterways, the vegetation found in open channels is [SOURCE: Page_20]

465   emergent and terrestrial and sometimes covers the whole section. The results suggest a [SOURCE: Page_20]

466   threshold of vegetation density has to be reached to enhance seed trapping. The hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_20]

467   structures, sluice gates and culvert, largely affect hydrochory by presenting physical barriers [SOURCE: Page_20]

20 [SOURCE: Page_20]

468   to dispersal. Based on these results, we recommend adjusting maintenance strategies of the [SOURCE: Page_21]

469   irrigation channels according to presence of Johnsongrass population gone to seed. Existing [SOURCE: Page_21]

470   hydraulic structures could have a beneficial role in limiting seed dispersal. High densities of [SOURCE: Page_21]

471   terrestrial vegetation should be locally maintained when no hydraulic structure is present on [SOURCE: Page_21]

472   the channel. [SOURCE: Page_21]
