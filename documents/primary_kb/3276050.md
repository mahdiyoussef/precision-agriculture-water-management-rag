17   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

18   Erosion and mass wasting processes on mountain slopes can benefit from or be adversely [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   affected by the presence of biopores formed by plant root systems or soil fauna. The relationship [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   between biopores and subsurface flow during rainstorms is poorly understood. Here, we [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   examined the link between subsurface flow and biopores formed through different processes, [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   including soil faunal activity and abundance of fine and coarse roots. As the distribution of [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   biopores is influenced by the type of vegetation present, we investigated the effect of plant [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   diversity (forest with or without understorey vegetation) on the pattern of water infiltration [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   throughout the soil. We hypothesized that increased species diversity would enhance the [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   extension of subsurface flow because biopores would be distributed throughout the soil profile [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   and that more coarse roots would create large biopores, increasing subsurface flow. In situ [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   experiments were conducted on hillslopes with plantations of rubber trees (Hevea brasiliensis) [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   growing on terraces, or with secondary mixed forests, in the tropical zone of Yunnan province, [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   China. Three sites with Ferralsol soils and different vegetation types were examined: (1) [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   plantation with no understorey; (2) clear-cut plantation with understorey; and (3) secondary [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   mixed forest with understorey. Irrigation experiments with Brilliant Blue FCF dyed water were [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   performed upslope of trees at each site and staining patterns resulting from infiltrated dyed [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   water were examined at two different scales. After dye irrigation, soil was removed in 1.0 × 0.8 [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   m slices starting 1 m downslope and soil profiles were photographed for subsequent mapping of [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   dyed areas in the profile (macroscale). Each profile was then divided into a 0.1 × 0.1 m grid [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   (microscale) and burrows formed by macrofauna and fine and coarse root densities were [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   measured. At the macroscale, the greatest lateral extension in subsurface flow occurred in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   natural forest and the least in the rubber tree plantation with no understorey vegetation. At the [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   microscale, and in all types of vegetation, fine roots significantly increased the incidence of [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   subsurface flow compared to coarse roots and macrofauna activity. We conclude that in tropical [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   Ferralsols, fine roots, and hence understorey vegetation, play a positive role in promoting [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   subsurface flow and therefore reducing water erosion and mass wasting processes. Thus, [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   Water erosion and mass wasting processes, such as shallow landslides, are recurring problems [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   around the world (Sidle and Ochiai, 2006; Stokes et al., 2013), notably in mountainous areas that [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   are particularly vulnerable to anthropogenic pressure and global change (Elkin et al., 2013). In [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   Southeast Asia, and China in particular, soil degradation on steep slopes has been accelerated [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   during the last 50 years, due to poor farming practices, deforestation, road and dam [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   construction (Sidle et al., 2006; Stokes et al., 2010; Sidle et al., 2014). The conversion of large [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   swaths of tropical rainforest to rubber tree (Hevea brasiliensis Mull. Arg.) plantations has also [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   led to major soil erosion and concerns about water cycling (Ziegler et al., 2009; Ahrends et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   2015; Häuser et al., 2015). In steep areas, the frequent practice of constructing bench terraces [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   before planting rubber trees changes soil physical and hydrological properties: (1) soil in the [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   bench terraces is destabilized by excavation and terrace construction (Sidle et al., 2006), and (2) [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   herbicides are used to remove understorey vegetation and the unvegetated riser faces are more [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   vulnerable to surface erosion than the original slope (van Dijk, 2002; Liu et al., 2015). Terraces [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   need constant maintenance and after forest operations such as clear-felling, they are susceptible [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   to failure because of changes in hydrological regime, such as an increase of the heterogeneity of [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   infiltration and wetting front (Fredlund et al., 2012), which correspond to vertical macropore [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   flow towards the potential slip plane (Cammeraat et al., 2005), or increases in the weight of soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   water (overburden effect). Therefore, to better manage soil conservation in rubber tree [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   plantations, it is necessary to understand how water moves through soil along natural and [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   terraced slopes. [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   Hydrological and mass wasting processes on vegetated slopes are influenced by soil structure [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   and plant root system morphology (Sidle and Ochiai, 2006). Water flows through two domains [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   within soils: (i) soil matrix flow, consisting of both uniform saturated and unsaturated flow [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   through fine pores and (ii) uniform and non-uniform preferential flow, occurring inside of single [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   or interconnected system of macropores with diameters >2 mm, although preferential flow can [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   also occur in more narrow pores (Tsuboyama et al., 1994; Sidle et al., 2001) and (iii) if the [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   macropores are not maintained by live roots, fauna or the frequent passage of water, they [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   gradually disappear and become “invisible pores”, or mesopores, but are still very permeable [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   and contribute significantly to preferential flow (Tsukamoto and Ohta, 1988). Biotic macropores [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   (or biopores, Figure S$_1$) include root channels (Mitchell et al., 1995), particularly large diameter [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   (coarse) roots (Bodner et al., 2014) and faunal burrows (Noguchi et al., 1997a; Farenhorst et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   2000; Shuster et al., 2002; Weiler and Naef, 2003), while non-biotic macropores are formed by [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   freeze-thaw, wetting-drying, dissolution of soil materials, physicochemical soil aggregation, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   subsurface erosion (Aubertin, 1971). Root systems can create stable channels when growing in [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   the soil, largely through: (i) compression of soil particles as the root penetrates the soil and (ii) [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   amalgamation of soil particles with sloughed root cells and mucilage (Bengough et al., 1997). [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   Both live and dead plant roots can promote slope drainage by functioning as hillslope-scale [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   subsurface flow paths that drain subsurface water away from potentially unstable sites (Noguchi [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   et al., 1999, 2001). Conversely, when root channels converge or when subsurface flow abruptly [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   terminates in the slope (e.g., dead-end channels), water pressure may concentrate in critical [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   zones of the slope, thus promoting instability. Therefore, flow paths can exert both positive and [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   negative consequences on slope stability (Ghestem et al., 2011; Sidle and Bogaard, 2016). The [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   effects of different land-use types on water flows in soil have been studied with a focus on soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   hydraulic properties related to matrix flow (Schwartz et al., 2003; Bodhinayake and Cheng Si, [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   2004; Bormann and Klaassen, 2008, Wu et al., 2017) more than preferential flow (Vogel et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   2006). It is not well known how different types of vegetation and land-use influence preferential [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   flow paths and especially how root morphology and pedofaunal activity affect the formation, [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   density and diameter of biopores. [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   Recently, Jiang et al., (2017, 2018) studied patterns of water infiltration through soil in rubber [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   tree plantations in Yunnan, China, using a single infiltration cylinder (with a diameter of 0.2 m). [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   Although infiltration was greater in rooted soil compared to bare soil, the effects of preferential [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   flow in biopores and matrix flow (in pores < 2mm in diameter) could not be distinguished. In [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   particular, the impact of coarse roots (> 2 mm in diameter) cannot be determined with small [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   diameter infiltration cylinders because of scaling and edge effects (Ghestem et al 2009; Zhang et [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   al 2017; Sidle et al., 2017). Coarse roots are thought to play a major, but poorly understood role, [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   in preferential flow processes (Lange et al 2009, Ghestem et al., 2011) but few field data are [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   available to quantify the effect (Wang and Zhang, 2017). [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   We explored the mechanisms through which root systems and soil macrofauna play a role on [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   subsurface flow processes along forested hillslopes in Yunnan, China. We hypothesized that: (i) [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   natural, mixed forests would have a greater extension of subsurface flow compared to [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   monospecific rubber tree plantations, because biopores should be distributed throughout the [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   entire soil profile, due to the increased diversity of insect and plant species and their different [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   root system morphologies, and (ii) a greater number of coarse roots would increase subsurface [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   flow. To understand the processes occurring, two different scales were considered: the pattern [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   of water infiltration throughout the soil profile, which reflected the impact of vegetation type on [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   subsurface flow (macroscale) and a finer ‘microscale,’ whereby the effects of the density and size [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   of individual roots and burrows on infiltration were elucidated. [SOURCE: Page_6]

279       Data analysis [SOURCE: Page_13]

280   We analysed data at two scales: i) at the macroscale (1.0 x 0.8 m) to describe how different [SOURCE: Page_13]

281   vegetation types influenced the lateral extension of subsurface flow and ii) at the microscale (in [SOURCE: Page_13]

282   squares of 0.1 x 0.1 m) to describe how the presence of root and insect burrows affect water [SOURCE: Page_13]

283   flow distribution. [SOURCE: Page_13]

284   At the macroscale, the number of roots per diameter class (< and > 2 mm diameter classes), the [SOURCE: Page_13]

285   presence of fauna, and resistance to penetration were aggregated at three soil depths for each [SOURCE: Page_13]

286   test. These depths corresponded to ‘surficial’ (0-0.3 m), which included O and A horizons, [SOURCE: Page_13]

287   ‘middle’ (0.3-0.6 m) comprising the B horizon, and ‘deep’ (0.6-0.8 m) corresponding to the C [SOURCE: Page_13]

288   horizon. Soil profile depths were different among sites, so the C horizon was not always reached. [SOURCE: Page_13]

289   For the forest and plantation sites, the C horizon was only present at one and two sites, [SOURCE: Page_13]

290   respectively (Figure 2). The inﬂuence of vegetation type and depth on roots and insect burrows, [SOURCE: Page_13]

291   and resistance to penetration were investigated using Kruskal Wallis and Wilcoxcon tests to [SOURCE: Page_13]

292   specify pairwise differences, as data did not follow a normal distribution. [SOURCE: Page_13]

293   At the microscale, each 0.1 x 0.1 m square was considered a sample. Data from above and below [SOURCE: Page_13]

294   the dam were analysed separately because they were linked to different hydrological processes. [SOURCE: Page_13]

295   Generally, flow was considered ‘matrix flow’ when a homogeneous wetting front was observed, [SOURCE: Page_13]

296   whilst ‘preferential flow’ was when isolated blue dye patches and wetting front instabilities [SOURCE: Page_13]

297   were observed (Cammeraat et al., 2005; Fredlund et al., 2012). We investigated if the subsurface [SOURCE: Page_13]

298   dye distribution was affected by the following factors: (i) depth (in 0.1 m classes from 0 to 0.8 [SOURCE: Page_13]

299   m), (ii) downslope distance from the dye source, (iii) fine and coarse root abundance, and (iv) [SOURCE: Page_13]

300   abundance of faunal burrows. Linear models with mixed effects (LME) were used to identify [SOURCE: Page_13]

301   factors influencing the abundance of the dyed area. As fixed effects, we tested different [SOURCE: Page_13]

302   combinations of factors (fine roots, coarse roots, fauna presence, soil penetration resistance and [SOURCE: Page_13]

303   depth). Plots and soil profiles were considered as random effects and the soil profiles were [SOURCE: Page_13]

304   nested inside the plots. We then selected models with different combinations of the fixed effect [SOURCE: Page_13]

305   predictors by comparing their Akaike Information Criterion (AIC, Burnham & Anderson 2002) [SOURCE: Page_13]

12 [SOURCE: Page_13]

306   with analysis of variance (ANOVA). At the microscale, we excluded unstained areas from the [SOURCE: Page_14]

307   statistical analysis, because the absence of dye in a cell does not necessarily indicate that the [SOURCE: Page_14]

308   conditions leading to preferential flow are absent, but could be due to different reasons, such as [SOURCE: Page_14]

309   lack of dye to the cell or other preferential flows occurring prior to arrival of dye in the cell. [SOURCE: Page_14]

310   Uniquely focusing on soil cells with the presence of dye (i.e., presence of flow) enables us to [SOURCE: Page_14]

311   ascertain the relationship between roots and insect burrows and dye distribution without noise [SOURCE: Page_14]

312   in the data. Dyed areas detected by the image analyses with a surface area smaller than 0.25 cm² [SOURCE: Page_14]

313   were considered as not dyed because they were potentially artefacts related to [SOURCE: Page_14]

314   microtopography, for example microshadows. Statistical analyses were performed using R 3.3.1 [SOURCE: Page_14]

315   (R Core Team, 2016) with the package ‘lme^4’ (Bates et al. 2015) for linear models with mixed [SOURCE: Page_14]

316   effects (LME). [SOURCE: Page_14]

385   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_17]

386   Subsurface flow and root distribution decreased with depth and distance from source under all [SOURCE: Page_17]

387   types of vegetation. Contrary to our hypothesis, and in all types of vegetation, these results were [SOURCE: Page_17]

388   consistent in terms of the dominant role of fine roots channelling water. We also observed [SOURCE: Page_17]

389   differences among sites for both the maximum downslope extension of dyed water flow and the [SOURCE: Page_17]

390   relationship between the dyed area and fine roots. These combined findings highlight the [SOURCE: Page_17]

391   complexity of hydrological processes in soils, at even a local scale. [SOURCE: Page_17]

392   Each site possessed its own particularities in terms of soil compaction and biotic activities, [SOURCE: Page_17]

393   resulting in disparities of subsurface flow. We found that natural forest had greater downslope [SOURCE: Page_17]

394   subsurface flow propagation than rubber tree plantations, similar to findings from Neris et al., [SOURCE: Page_17]

395   (2012) studying natural evergreen forest and pine (Pinus canariensis) plantations and Marín- [SOURCE: Page_17]

396   Castro et al (2017) investigating secondary tropical montane cloud forest and coffee (Coffea [SOURCE: Page_17]

397   arabica) plantations. Bodhinayake and Si (2004) also showed that greater preferential flow [SOURCE: Page_17]

398   occurred in flat grasslands compare to cultivated soils. Other authors demonstrated higher [SOURCE: Page_17]

399   preferential flows on sites with trees than in grasslands (Joffre and Rambal, 1988; Belsky et al., [SOURCE: Page_17]

400   1993; van Schaik, 2009; Perkins et al., 2012) or beneath trees in agroforests (Wang and Zhang [SOURCE: Page_17]

401   2017). In agreement with Zhu et al. (2018), we show that converting natural forest to [SOURCE: Page_17]

402   plantations may induce an increase of runoff due to a decrease of infiltration and subsurface [SOURCE: Page_17]

403   flow. This impact would be significantly reduced if monocropped plantations were converted to [SOURCE: Page_17]

404   e.g., alley cropping (Sun et al. 2018). [SOURCE: Page_17]

405 [SOURCE: Page_17]

406   We showed that coarse root abundance was significantly greater in the upper 0.3 m of soil in the [SOURCE: Page_17]

407   forest site, compared to soil (shallow or deep) of any site, probably due to the greater diversity [SOURCE: Page_17]

408   and density of trees along the slope. Increasing species diversity will lead to a greater number of [SOURCE: Page_17]

16 [SOURCE: Page_17]

409   root system morphologies and hence a more enhanced spatial occupation of the soil profile [SOURCE: Page_18]

410   (Stokes et al., 2009). Although we did not measure litter properties, enhanced litter thickness in [SOURCE: Page_18]

411   natural forests also increases hydraulic conductivity compared to plantation forests (Marín- [SOURCE: Page_18]

412   Castro et al., 2017). The greatest abundance of fine roots (<2 mm in diameter) was near the soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

413   surface in the clear-cut site, with many herbaceous species present, resulting in a higher [SOURCE: Page_18]

414   subsurface flow propagation in the top 0.3 m of soil, as also found by Bodhinayake and Si (2004). [SOURCE: Page_18]

415   With regard to biopores created by soil fauna, surprisingly, we did not see significant differences [SOURCE: Page_18]

416   among soil layers and sites. Léonard and Rajot, (2001) also discussed the difficulty in [SOURCE: Page_18]

417   quantifying the effect of termites on runoff and infiltration because data were highly variable. [SOURCE: Page_18]

418   These authors found a significant effect of fauna that increased infiltration and reduced runoff [SOURCE: Page_18]

419   (also shown by Beven and Germann, 1982; Yvan et al., 2012; Cheng et al., 2017; Schneider et al., [SOURCE: Page_18]

420   2018), and Jouquet et al., (2012) showed that macrofaunal activity reduced soil erosion on a [SOURCE: Page_18]

421   fallow slope in Vietnam. Soil resistance to penetration increased with depth in forest and [SOURCE: Page_18]

422   plantation sites, with the greatest resistance found in the deepest soil layers of the forest site. [SOURCE: Page_18]

423   Forest had different soil physical properties than those of the plantation sites (in which the soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

424   structure was possibly affected by terrace construction). The changes in slope geometry in [SOURCE: Page_18]

425   terraced terrain can affect subsurface flow and alter the position of the wetting front [SOURCE: Page_18]

426   (Cammeraat et al., 2005; Sidle et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_18]

427   We highlighted the positive effect of biological activity on subsurface flow. For each of the three [SOURCE: Page_18]

428   sites, we demonstrated that live, fine roots had a more dominant effect on subsurface flow [SOURCE: Page_18]

429   compared to coarse roots and soil fauna, contrary to our initial hypothesis. This finding shows [SOURCE: Page_18]

430   the importance of considering the multiple roles of fine roots when studying the soil-plant-water [SOURCE: Page_18]

431   continuum. Fine roots, which grow fast and are usually more numerous than thicker roots, may [SOURCE: Page_18]

432   make, in a short time, many non-capillary channels that can greatly improve the soil inﬁltration [SOURCE: Page_18]

433   capacity. Also, fine roots should be more susceptible to soil dryness and thus more prone to root [SOURCE: Page_18]

434   shrinkage that create gaps between the root and the soil (De Roo, 1968). Fine roots also have a [SOURCE: Page_18]

435   very fast turnover rate (30 to 1000 days) and thus form dead root channels more quickly than [SOURCE: Page_18]

17 [SOURCE: Page_18]

436   coarse roots which remain longer in the soil (McCormack and Guo, 2014; McCormack et al., [SOURCE: Page_19]

437   2015; Germon et al., 2016). These processes are also beneficial for enhancing soil inﬁltration [SOURCE: Page_19]

438   capacity. [SOURCE: Page_19]

439   Although we demonstrated that although the potential effect of land-use changes on preferential [SOURCE: Page_19]

440   flow was related to variations in the density and size of biopores, our field sites were not [SOURCE: Page_19]

441   replicated at the forest or watershed scale, therefore, it is difficult to extrapolate results beyond [SOURCE: Page_19]

442   the scale of individual trees. To understand infiltration and subsurface flow processes at a [SOURCE: Page_19]

443   greater scale, it would be necessary to perform more numerous and simpler infiltration tests, or [SOURCE: Page_19]

444   larger-scale assessments of infiltration. Nevertheless, our study contributes to the increasing [SOURCE: Page_19]

445   number of studies showing that subsurface flow and especially preferential flow, increase water [SOURCE: Page_19]

446   drainage, thereby reducing surficial runoff and erosion processes. Preferential flow can also [SOURCE: Page_19]

447   reduce the build-up of pore water pressure within soil, and so decrease the triggering of rainfall [SOURCE: Page_19]

448   induced landslides (but adverse effects are also possible, see Ghestem et al., 2011). Therefore, [SOURCE: Page_19]

449   our results suggest that mixtures of species should protect against soil erosion and possibly [SOURCE: Page_19]

450   slope instability. In cultivated soils, tree and shrubby/herbaceous mixtures, such as alley [SOURCE: Page_19]

451   cropping (Jiang et al., 2017), or no- or soft-intercropping weeding (Liu et al., 2019), would be [SOURCE: Page_19]

452   beneficial for soil conservation. [SOURCE: Page_19]

453   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_19]

454   We studied subsurface flow processes at the microscale (corresponding to individual biopores, [SOURCE: Page_19]

455   roots and fauna) and the macro-scale (corresponding to the soil profile around individual trees). [SOURCE: Page_19]

456   Our results show the complexity of the drivers of preferential flow in soils. The role of fine roots [SOURCE: Page_19]

457   promoting subsurface flow was dominant over macrofaunal burrows and coarse roots. The [SOURCE: Page_19]

458   consistent positive relationship between fine root density and subsurface flow can help to [SOURCE: Page_19]

459   support assumptions of the effects of roots on rapid drainage during an intense rain event. This [SOURCE: Page_19]

460   drainage should efficiently reduce surface runoff and surface erosion during heavy rainfall and [SOURCE: Page_19]

461   promote downslope drainage, which can have beneficial or adverse effects on slope stability. [SOURCE: Page_19]

18 [SOURCE: Page_19]

462   These results demonstrate the need to consider using species mixtures in local forest conversion [SOURCE: Page_20]

463   programs to avoid mass wasting processes and soil erosion, e.g. agroforestry, alley-cropping [SOURCE: Page_20]

464   systems or plantations with understorey species present. [SOURCE: Page_20]
