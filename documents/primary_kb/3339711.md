19   Abstract: [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   Soil available water content (SAWC) is becoming a crucial issue for forest management in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   context of climate changes. Nevertheless, SAWC indicator which was created for agricultural [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   soils needs specific adaptations when it is estimated for forest soils. This study aimed to find [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   the best way to apply the SAWC indicator with regard to its significance for explaining [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   variations of tree fertility across a given region. An extensive study over a spatial sampling of [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   100 Douglas-fir stand plots was conducted in the Haut Languedoc Regional Nature Parc [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   (Southern France). It involved stand fertility assessments, morphological observations of deep [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   soil pits including the saprolite, quantitative estimations of coarse fragments, texture and [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   organic matter laboratory analysis and water retention and density measurements on a [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   limited sample of horizons (35). Different SAWC were computed from the collected data. They [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   differed in the methods i) for estimating the fine earth water retention – national vs local [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   pedotransfer function -, ii) for dealing with the coarse fragments – assumed to contribute or [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   not to the water retention of soils- and iii) for estimating the soil volumes – variable [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   thicknesses and considerations of rooting. The results showed that the SAWC that explained [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   the best the variations of tree fertilities over the study area (21% of the variance) was obtained [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   by fine earth water retention estimated from a local pedotransfert function, by considering [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   the water retention of the coarse fraction and by exploring the largest volumes (thicknesses [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   of 200 cm, 300 cm and equal to the depth to bedrock) without explicit consideration of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   rooting observations. The ranking of SAWC component impact on SAWC values was i) soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   volume, ii) coarse fraction water retention and iii) fine earth retention. Those results revealed [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   that improvements in estimating coarse fraction water retention and soil volumes, and, to a [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   lesser extent, in estimating fine-earth water retention by forest soil adapted pedotransfer [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   function, would greatly benefit to the application of the SAWC indicator to forest soils. [SOURCE: Page_3]

45      1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   Soil available water capacity (SAWC) is a well-known concept that have been used for a long [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   time for expressing the capacity of soils to store water for plants (Veihmayer and Hendrickson, [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   1927). It has been demonstrated that SAWC is one of the most important soil factors for plant [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   growth, influencing photosynthesis rate, carbon allocation, and nutrient cycling (Lebourgeois [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   et al., 2005; Breda et al., 2006). It is therefore a first order parameter that is used in land [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   evaluations and recently in soil ecosystem service assessment (Dominati et al. 2014). For now, [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   in the literature, SAWC is expressed as follows (equation 1) (Cousin et al., 2003): [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   SAWC concept has been created for crop field soils, but is also largely applied to forest soils [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   (Curt et al., 2001; Chen et al., 2002; Seynave et al., 2005; Piedallu et al., 2011). SAWC is [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   commonly used in combination with climatic and topographic data in order to predict site [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   fertility of a forest stand (Chen et al., 2002; Seynave et al., 2005). It is also used for soil water [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   balance calculation (Granier et al., 1999) in order to study the forest-stand response to [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   drought that is becoming a crucial issue in context of climate changes (Breda et al., 2006; [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   Sergent et al., 2012; Littke et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   However, applying SAWC indicator to forest soils carries some difficulties related with the [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   specificities of forest soil and forest context. Firstly, trees are powerful plants whose roots [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   may colonize a greater soil thickness than cultivated plant. Many studies showed that [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   colonization by roots could go far beyond the soil, reaching the saprolite zone in which this [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   colonization can be very irregular (Curt et al., 1998; Graham et al., 2010; Brantley et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   This makes not straightforward to define in equation 1 an appropriate value of thickness that [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   account for this heterogeneity. Second, by reaching the saprolite zone, forest soils often show [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   high coarse fraction volumes. It has been demonstrated that coarse fragment can store water [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   which could be available for plant (Flint and Childs, 1984; Tetegan et al. 2011; Parajuli et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   2017). When the proportion of coarse fragment becomes important, this could play a major [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   role in SAWC. Hence, equation 1 should be modified for taking into account the AWC of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   coarse fraction (AWC(CF)). Lastly, the determination of the fine earth AWC (AWC(FE)) has been [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   by far much more investigated than the two previously evoked components. To cope with [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   metrologic difficulties in measuring gravimetric water content at field capacity (W(FC)) and at [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   wilting point (W(WP)) there is a wide use of pedotransfer functions (PTF) (Jamagne et al., 1977; [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   Wösten et al., 2001; Almajou et al., 2008). However, most of these PTF have been developed [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   from agricultural soil measurements and to our knowledge, no specific PTF for forest soils [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   exists (Vincke and Delvaux, 2005; Piedallu et al., 2011). Piedallu et al (2018) promoted the use [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   of Almajou 2008’ PTF which is derived from agricultural soils. The applicability of PTF to forest [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   soil remains unknown. [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   In this paper, we apply the concept of SAWC for characterizing the potential of growth of [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   Douglas-fir in the Haut Languedoc Regional Nature Parc (Southern France). Different [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   modalities for calculating the SAWC components are applied from a dataset of 100 deep soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

93    pits. The resulting SAWCs are evaluated with regard to their correlation with an indicator of [SOURCE: Page_6]

94    tree growth. [SOURCE: Page_6]

95       2. The study area and data [SOURCE: Page_6]

96    2.1. General description [SOURCE: Page_6]

97    The Haut Languedoc Regional Nature Park is a 314 000 hectares area located in the south of [SOURCE: Page_6]

98    France. This area is covered at 66% by forest, making wood production a crucial issue for the [SOURCE: Page_6]

99    region. The studied area lays through the confluence zone of three contrasted climates. At the [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   east, Mediterranean climate present an annual rainfall of 650mm, concentrated during [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   autumn and spring seasons, and is characterized by a high summer water deficit (-300mm). In [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   the west, oceanic climate is more humid (1650mm annual rainfall) with a more uniform [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   rainfall regime and a lower summer water deficit (-100mm). Finally, in the north, a semi- [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   mountain climate shows wetter and colder average conditions. Geology and topography are [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   also very heterogeneous. The south-west of the study area, called black mountain, is [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   composed on granitic and gneissic mountains reaching 1200 meters high. The center of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   area is composed on granitic and gneissic plateau ranging between 800 and 1100 meters high, [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   surrounded by schist and calcschist hills in the north west and schist and calcareous materials [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   in the south east. Such peripheric regions show lower altitude but very rugged terrain with [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   deep valleys and narrows crests. Such variability implies very heterogeneous forest soil type [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   distribution and thus, a large range of SAWC. [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   2.2. Plot sampling [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   A 100 plots sampling design was set up in order to exhaustively represent forest soils of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   studied area. The sampling design was performed on Douglas-Fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii) [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   plantations because this species is of high economic interest and is therefore widely [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   represented across the different climates and parent materials of the study area, which [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   ensures to get a large range of soil conditions. Sampling strategy was based on the [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   combination of climatic, geologic, and topographic classifications. Three sub-zones were [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   distinguished: the central mountain zone (mountain climate), the western zone (oceanic [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   climate) and the eastern zone (Mediterranean climate). Topography classification included [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   four topographic positions: plateau, top slope, mid slope and lower slope combined with three [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   exposition modalities (north, south and other). In the central zone, classification was based [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   on topographic conditions combined with an altitude threshold (800 meters). For the western [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   and eastern zones, topographic classification was combined with geologic classification: schist [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   and granites rocks in the western zone, schist and calcareous rocks in the eastern zone. [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   Sampling strategy also took into account practical conditions such as accessibility for a 25 tons [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   shovel, optimization of the shovel transport, and forest owner agreement. [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   2.3.   Soil observations [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   For each plot, a soil pit was dug using a 25 tons shovel. The soil pit was positioned at a two [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   meters distance from the tree localised at the centre of the trees spiral composing the plot. [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   Soil material was excavated until the inscrutable bedrock was reached. When bedrock was [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   too deep underground, the soil pit limit was 4.5 meters depth, corresponding on the maximum [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   accessible depth for the shovel mechanical arm. Soil profiles were visually described following [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   the French protocol of soil description (Infosol, 2018), including the measurement of the soil [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   horizons thickness. Sometimes the soil horizons presented a complex geometry i.e. non- [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   horizontal horizon limits, discontinuous, or enclosed horizons, into the saprolite zone. Non- [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   horizontal horizon limits were registered using the DONSOL protocol. Discontinuous and [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   enclosed horizons were considered as component of a complex horizons, which required to [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   modify the description protocol. Furthermore, graphical representations of each profiles were [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   performed to memorize the observed horizon complexity. [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   A particular attention was paid for characterizing coarse fraction, both quantitatively and [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   qualitatively. The classical estimation of the amount of coarse fragment from a visual [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   interpretation of the profile was replaced by a more precise technique that estimated [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   separately the gravel fraction (between 2mm and 2cm diameter) and the pebbles fraction [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   (more than 2 cm). The latter was determined in the field by a 2 cm sieving and weighing. The [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   determined fine earth and pebble densities were used to convert mass fraction into [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   volumetric fractions. The gravel fraction was determined in the laboratory from the 2cm- [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   sieved samples obtained from the previously evoked operation. The total coarse fraction was [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   obtained by adding the two previously determined fractions. [SOURCE: Page_8]

152   A total of 80 rock fragments were sampled for density and water retention measurement. The [SOURCE: Page_8]

153   sampling considered eight rock fragment types that corresponded to the mainly represented [SOURCE: Page_8]

154   lithologic classes of the studied area: mica-schist, black schist, shale, pelitic sandstone, banded [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   sandstone, dolomite, limestone, granite and gneiss. Ten samples of each lithologic class were [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   collected. The rock samples consisted of flat-shaped pebbles with a size varying between 3cm [SOURCE: Page_8]

157   and 6cm for the longer diameter. The samples were gently brushed prior measurement, in [SOURCE: Page_9]

158   order to remove their fine earth coatings. [SOURCE: Page_9]

159   Considering the fine earth, 200 grams samples were collected from each soil horizon in order [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   to measure five class particle size distribution and organic matter content. In addition, [SOURCE: Page_9]

161   undisturbed clods were sampled from 35 horizons (A and S horizons), for water retention [SOURCE: Page_9]

162   measurements. The sampled clods were chosen in order to exhaustively represent the soil [SOURCE: Page_9]

163   types of the studied area. The undisturbed clods were gently fragmented by hand in order to [SOURCE: Page_9]

164   form 5 cm diameter clods. The samples were kept in plastic boxes and stored at 4°C until [SOURCE: Page_9]

165   analyses. [SOURCE: Page_9]

343      5. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_21]

344      5.1 Fine earth water retention [SOURCE: Page_21]

345   The forest soils considered for the present study showed relative homogeneous sandy texture, [SOURCE: Page_21]

346   high organic matter content. Such soil properties induced specific water retention behavior [SOURCE: Page_21]

347   for the fine earth that were found to be very hardly predicted by using Almajou PTF. Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_21]

348   while the Almajou PTF predicted homogeneous AWCcoef(FE) , the coefficients based on the [SOURCE: Page_21]

349   measured values showed higher variability, especially for the surface horizons. [SOURCE: Page_21]

350   Texture, as an only factor, might not be able to predict water retention for the studied soils. [SOURCE: Page_21]

351   It seems that organic matter content may play an important role. Indeed, such relationship [SOURCE: Page_21]

352   was partly found by using the continue local PTF. However, even if the relationship was [SOURCE: Page_21]

353   statistically significant, it only allowed to predict 23 % of the measured water retention [SOURCE: Page_21]

354   variability. This lack of performance can be explained by the low number of samples used in [SOURCE: Page_21]

355   this study (35) combined with a low range of water retention properties (W(FC), W(WP)) in [SOURCE: Page_21]

356   comparison to other studies (ref). [SOURCE: Page_21]

357   Anyway, the fine earth retention seems not to impact less the variation of SAWC than other [SOURCE: Page_21]

358   factors. As an explanation, the volume of fine earth has a very homogeneous soil texture. [SOURCE: Page_21]

359   Furthermore, it is smaller than usually measured due to the richness in coarse fragments, and [SOURCE: Page_21]

360   the increase of coarse fragment with depth result in a decrease of fine earth AWC impact. As [SOURCE: Page_21]

361   a conclusion, the increase of variations of other AWC components than fine earth may explain [SOURCE: Page_21]

362   this weak importance. However, it should be tried in more texture contrasted pedological [SOURCE: Page_21]

363   context. It is also worth noting that recommended PTF failed to represent the fine earth [SOURCE: Page_21]

364   retention variability and that the local PTF stress the negative impact of organic matter on the [SOURCE: Page_22]

365   wilting point, which, to our knowledge have never been encountered in studies dealing with [SOURCE: Page_22]

366   agricultural soils. This re-enforce the need of specific PTF for forest soils. [SOURCE: Page_22]

367      5.2 Retention of coarse fragment [SOURCE: Page_22]

368   The water retention capacity of coarse fragment based on measurements was found [SOURCE: Page_22]

369   important to take into account for explaining tree growth. The correlation coefficients raised [SOURCE: Page_22]

370   significantly whatever the considered soil volume with however greater differences when [SOURCE: Page_22]

371   deeper layers were considered. It is explained by the importance of coarse fragment, [SOURCE: Page_22]

372   especially in the saprolite zone, but also to the substantial water retention of some of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

373   pebbles. [SOURCE: Page_22]

374   Indeed, we measured AWCcoef(CF) varying between 0.11 and 1.21 depending on the rock [SOURCE: Page_22]

375   fragment lithology. This is consistent with previous studies (Tetegan et al., 2011; Parajuli et [SOURCE: Page_22]

376   al., 2017). Parajuli et al., 2017 showed that the water retention capacity of sandstones [SOURCE: Page_22]

377   fragment was linked to the sample porosity. Tetegan et al. studied the water retention [SOURCE: Page_22]

378   capacities of different types of sedimentary rocks. They found that the AWC(CF) depends on [SOURCE: Page_22]

379   the rock fragment bulk density, with the higher AWCcoef(CF) for bulk density varying between [SOURCE: Page_22]

380   1.5 and 1.8. The results of the present study for AWCcoef(CF) values were very similar, even if [SOURCE: Page_22]

381   the lithologic nature of the studied samples was very different. Considering the studied soils, [SOURCE: Page_22]

382   the AWC(CF) represented 39 % of the AWC(Total) for the AWC(Bedrock) and 31 % for the AWC(200). [SOURCE: Page_22]

383   We recommend that water retention of coarse fragment could be also accounted for. The [SOURCE: Page_22]

384   variability shown in this study suggest that PTF should also be built for coarse fraction, and [SOURCE: Page_22]

385   especially for the weathered rock fragment showing a low bulk density and high porosity. [SOURCE: Page_22]

386   Finally, this result also underlined the crucial importance of a precise assessment of the coarse [SOURCE: Page_22]

387   fraction volume within the soil profile. A 2cm- sieving and weighing performed in the field [SOURCE: Page_23]

388   appeared to be a progress from classical visual estimations from the profile wall. [SOURCE: Page_23]

389      5.3 Soil Volume [SOURCE: Page_23]

390   Soil volume seems to be the main driver of SAWC in our study area. We showed that for [SOURCE: Page_23]

391   Douglas-fir fertility index, the best correlation coefficients were obtained with SAWC [SOURCE: Page_23]

392   calculated on the thickest and deepest materials: AWC(Bedrock), AWC(300) and AWC(200). Anyway, [SOURCE: Page_23]

393   with correlation coefficients quite similar than AWC(Bedrock), AWC(200) seemed to be deep and [SOURCE: Page_23]

394   thick enough for accounting for the soil impact on the tree growth. This might mean that the [SOURCE: Page_23]

395   critical AWC layer for tree fertility was not the global and potentially rooted saprolite zone, [SOURCE: Page_23]

396   but a thinner volume of material that concentrated the higher root density (Brantley et al., [SOURCE: Page_23]

397   2017). In this way, the SAWCs taking into account direct observations of rooting depths (AWC [SOURCE: Page_23]

398   (Max root depth) , AWC (Root density) ) would have been theoretically the best methods for assessing [SOURCE: Page_23]

399   SAWC. Indeed, root distribution is like a map of were water is most likely to be present during [SOURCE: Page_23]

400   the growing season (Brantley et al., 2017). Curt et al., 1998 proposed a typology for the root- [SOURCE: Page_23]

401   system architecture of Douglas-fir that was strongly correlated with Douglas-fir fertility index [SOURCE: Page_23]

402   (H25). However, it was not the case for the present study. The correlation coefficients with H50 [SOURCE: Page_23]

403   were lower for the SAWC weighted on the root density than for SAWC based on fixed floor [SOURCE: Page_23]

404   depth. This can be explained by the difficulty in observing functional roots that are very thin [SOURCE: Page_23]

405   (diameter <1mm) and by the omission of mycorrhizal effect. [SOURCE: Page_23]

406   Finally, the lower correlation coefficients were found for the surface material AWC(100) and [SOURCE: Page_23]

407   AWC(50) . Such material seemed to be not thick and deep enough to precisely characterize the [SOURCE: Page_23]

408   SAWC potential for a given site. This result questions the common field method for SAWC [SOURCE: Page_23]

409   assessment that used manual auger to probe the soil with a very limited depth. [SOURCE: Page_23]

410      5.4 AWC – tree growth relation [SOURCE: Page_23]

411   The SAWC-tree growth relation was found significative with SAWC explaining at best 23% of [SOURCE: Page_24]

412   the H50 variability. Many studies explored the relationships between tree growth and SAWC. [SOURCE: Page_24]

413   Piedallu et al. (2011) showed that SAWC calculated by using manual auger, Almajou PTF for [SOURCE: Page_24]

414   estimating AWC(FE), and without including the coarse fraction AWC could explained at most 12 [SOURCE: Page_24]

415   % of the fertility index of Fagus sylvatica, Picea Abies and Quercus Petraea. Curt et al., (2001) [SOURCE: Page_24]

416   showed that SAWC explained 29 % of Douglas-fir growth in the Limousin (French Massif [SOURCE: Page_24]

417   Central). For this study, SAWC was calculated on 1-meter depth pit using by the “texture [SOURCE: Page_24]

418   method” (Baize & Jabiol, 1995), and Douglas-fir growth was estimated at the reference age of [SOURCE: Page_24]

419   25 years. This stronger relationship for Curt et al. (2001) can be explained by a more [SOURCE: Page_24]

420   homogeneous climate and geology of the studied area than ours. Indeed, for the Haut- [SOURCE: Page_24]

421   Languedoc area, the climatic confluence situation and the geological heterogeneity induced [SOURCE: Page_24]

422   very complex interactions between the Douglas-fir growth factors. Climate variables such as [SOURCE: Page_24]

423   an early vegetative season water deficit (Sergent et al., 2012) and other factors such as [SOURCE: Page_24]

424   chemical fertility, former land use (Curt et al., 2001), or genetic origin of Douglas-fir trees [SOURCE: Page_24]

425   (Bansal et al., 2015) may also play an important role. SAWC appeared as a complementary [SOURCE: Page_24]

426   factor, but can be more useful when the first-order factors such as climate remain weakly [SOURCE: Page_24]

427   variable. [SOURCE: Page_24]

428      6. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_24]

429   SAWC concept which was created for agricultural soils requires specific adaptations when it is [SOURCE: Page_24]

430   estimated for forest soils. The present study pointed three possible improvements for forest [SOURCE: Page_24]

431   SAWC assessment: 1) the development of specific PTF for forest soils, integrating texture and [SOURCE: Page_24]

432   organic matter content as explanatory factors for fine earth water retention properties, 2) the [SOURCE: Page_24]

433   integration of the coarse fraction AWC for SAWC calculation and thus, the development of [SOURCE: Page_24]

434   rock fragment PTF, and 3) the consideration of a 200cm deep soil volume for SAWC [SOURCE: Page_24]

435   assessment. This last point appears to be the most difficult to practically take into account in [SOURCE: Page_25]

436   usual field sampling devices, because the digging of such a soil volume is expansive. Thus, the [SOURCE: Page_25]

437   modelling of the total soil thickness by using available surface and subsurface indicators could [SOURCE: Page_25]

438   be a crucial issue for forest soil AWC assessment. [SOURCE: Page_25]
