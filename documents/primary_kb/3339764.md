Abstract:                                 The soils of the Mediterranean basin are the products of soil processes that have been governed by a unique convergence of highly differentiated natural and anthropogenic drivers. These soils are expected to be dramatically affected by future climate and societal changes. These changes imply that suitable adaptive management strategies for these resources cannot simply be transposed from experiments that are performed in other regions of the world. Following a framework that considers the chain of "drivers - soil process - soil capital - Ecosystem Services/Disservices" the paper review the research undertaken in the Mediterranean area on three types of Mediterranean soil degradation than can be expected under Global Change i) soil losses due to the increase of drought and torrential rainfall, ii) soil salinization due the increase of droughts, irrigation and sea level and iii) soil carbon stocks depletion with the increase of temperature and droughts. The possible strategies for mitigating each of these degradations have been [SOURCE: Page_1]

Yemadje et al. (2016) recently employed a 433 23    Soudano-sahelian context that was in some ways comparable to Mediterranean climate conditions 434 24    (annual rainfall from 900-950 mm, but with a long dry period). Soil wetting and mulching were 435 25    shown to increase soil carbon mineralization, but frequent wetting-drying cycles did not increase 26 436 27    the total soil C mineralization (Yemadje et al., 2017). But, these last authors also reported that 437 28    conflicting results for the effect of wetting-drying cycles on C and N mineralization can be found in 438 29    the literature, with higher or lower, or similar values for C and N mineralization in soils subjected to 439 30    wetting-drying cycles. 31 440 32 441 33    In terms of adaptation strategies in combination with the need to fight agricultural soil degradation 442 34    and organic C depletion, several approaches were proposed and tested in a Mediterranean context, 443 35    and some of them were widely adopted by stakeholders at the plot or watershed level. 444 36    The change of land use in a semi-arid area is a strategy in which the major patterns are related to the 37 445 38    reduction in long-term fallowing (FAO, 2014). Annual soil cultivation that employs appropriate 446 39    nutrient management increases the soil C-return (root, stubble,…) because the above and 447 40    belowground biomass production was increased in comparison with that of the fallow system. The 448 41    choice of crop species and cultivars is an important key to maximizing the production of crop 42 449 43 residues, which are a major resource for organic inputs in Mediterranean croplands. In the cereal- 450 44    livestock system, straw is largely exported out of the plot instead of being incorporated back into 451 45    the soil. In addition, stubble is intensively grazed primarily to the south of the Mediterranean Sea 452 46    (Ryan et al, 2006). The implementation of crop rotations with plants that allocate more carbon to 453 47    top and subsoil through a deep root system is an adaptation measure for sinking C. The 48 454 49    improvement of rangeland productivity by legume sowing, shrub plantation and integrated livestock 455 50    are widely used strategies to boost the soil fertility in the arid part of the Mediterranean region. 456 51    Field studies that were undertaken in Morocco and Tunisia by Mrabet et al. (2001) and Jemai et al. 457 52    (2012) showed 14% and 28% increases in the SOC from the 0-20 cm soil layer after 11 and 7 years 458 53 of switching from conventional tillage to conservation agriculture practices, respectively. In Spain, 54 459 55    Alvaro-Fuentes et al. (2012) observed a 23% SOC increase in the top 30 cm layer of soil after it was 460 56    converted to conservation agriculture for 13 years. More generally, the concentrations of organic 461 57    matter in top-soils of Mediterranean regions were found to increase routinely under no-tillage 462 58    systems because of the favourable shift in the accumulation and decomposition balance (Kassam et 59 463 60 al, 2012; Friedrich et al, 2014). 61 62 63 64 65 9 [SOURCE: Page_11]

464   However, the wide adoption of conservation agriculture among farmers in the Mediterranean 465   regions is still limited. According to Kassam et al. (2012), the total Mediterranean cultivated land 466 1    under conservation agriculture is 1.53 million hectares. 467 2    Research questions are still focused on refining the expected level of C sequestration attained in the 3 468 4 different bioclimatic zones, and also by considering the deeper soil layers from which C losses have 469 5    been registered under other climatic conditions. However, one of the most important points 470 6    concerns the adoption and mainstreaming of conservation agriculture, which will have a strong 471 7    impact on the management of the residues at the landscape and even sub-national level. Residues 472 8    are also an important source of biomass resources that are made available for livestock. Synergies 9 473 10    (other services in the fight against erosion) and trade-offs (biomass competition should then be 474 11    addressed as well) to find the most appropriate technical solutions for farmers and smallholders 475 12    whose objectives are yield and income increases. 476 13    By contrast, the concentrations of organic matter in topsoils from Mediterranean regions routinely 14 477   increase under no-tillage systems because of the favourable shift in the balance of accumulation 15 478 16    and decomposition (Kassam et al, 2012; Friedrich et al, 2014). 479 17 480 18 481 19    4. Challenging transversal issues for the future of Mediterranean soil resources 20 482 21 483 22    Apart from the above-evoked ongoing research on each driver-process-soil capital-ecosystem 484 23    service chain upon separation, the management of soil resources under global change raises some 485 24    transversal issues that are detailed here. 486 25 26 487 27    Towards a reliable change in soil management practices within viable agrosystems 488 28    Many of the mitigation strategies evoked in the next sections are based on deep changes in 489 29    agricultural practices. Beyond the successful results obtained on experimental farms, the massive 490 30    introduction of such strategies within actual Mediterranean agrosystems is still a challenge. For 31 491 32 example, the broad adoption of conservation agriculture among farmers in the Mediterranean region 492 33    is still limited because of possible negative impacts such as the decrease in grazing resources for 493 34    livestock through the introduction of mulching, or water-stress on agricultural production from the 494 35    introduction of a permanent vegetation cover. Another aspect is the need for local and national 495 36    policies that should support actions for, e.g., building up and raising the status of SOC using the 37 496 38    adapted programmes and mechanisms with national-wide agricultural strategies such as those 497 39    within the framework of NAMA. It is now recognized that policies that support sustainable land 498 40    management practices are focused on both maintaining (preventing loss) and increasing (storing 499 41    even more) soil organic carbon production for far greater economic, social and environmental 42 500 43 impact than the absolute amount of sequestered carbon (Banwart et al., 2014). 501 44    The introduction of mitigation strategies should be applied in a wider context that considers the 502 45    whole agrosystem explicitly and its socio-economic environment. An integrated assessment of 503 46    agricultural systems is therefore required (Van Ittersum et al, 2008). 504 47 48 505 49    Towards site-specific adaptations for preserving the soil resource 506 50    Beyond the field and farm levels, IAASTD (2009) recommended that the landscape level be 507 51    considered and that “new cropping patterns adapted to site-specific conditions” be introduced. This 508 52    approach accounts for the diversity of local contexts (see the first issue in this section) and to admit 509 53 that "the search for universal truths about causes and remedies for desertification and the 54 510 55    appropriate actions to be taken are as diverse as the mosaic of landscape itself” (Thornes, 2002). 511 56    This interpretation is particularly relevant in Mediterranean areas where the complexity of soil 512 57    patterns (see above) involves a significant diversity of soil properties and soil processes, starting 513 58    from very small areas such as parcels, farm territories and small watersheds. 59 514 60 Significant advances are expected from employing spatial organizations of land uses and soil 515 61    management practices. These choices will ensure the optimal exploitation of local soil 62 63 64 65 10 [SOURCE: Page_12]

516   characteristics while providing the best pooled result in terms of soil cover protection at the scale of 517   resource watersheds or irrigation perimeters in which collective management is possible (10-100 518 1    km2). 519 2    An example of current research that is being performed in the Mediterranean area to address these 3 520 4 opportunities is the ALMIRA project (Adaptating Landscape Mosaïcs of rainfed Mediterranean 521 5    Agrosystems for a sustainable management of crop production, water and soil resources). ALMIRA 522 6    aims to explore the modulation of landscape mosaics to adapt to the Mediterranean Rainfed 523 7    Agrosystems to Climate Change. For that purpose, ALMIRA proposes to design, implement and 524 8    test a new Integrated Assessment Modelling approach that explicitly i) includes innovations and 9 525 10    actions in prospective scenarios for landscape evolutions, and ii) addresses landscape mosaics and 526 11    processes of interest from the agricultural field to the resource governance catchment. 527 12 528 13    Towards a high resolution for mapping Mediterranean soil properties 14 529   A systematic soil survey of the Mediterranean basin using the most updated technology and 15 530 16    scientific knowledge is still lacking for a site-specific adaptation of local mitigation strategies and 531 17    should be one of the priority actions in the near future. The specifications of this survey may follow 532 18    those of the new Digital Soil Map of the world as targeted by the GlobalSoilMap project (Sanchez 533 19    et al, 2009; Arrouays et al, 2014a). This targeted global soil map is expected to provide quantitative 20 534 21 estimates of major soil properties with associated uncertainties at the nodes of a 90 m x 90 m grid 535 22    covering the planet and for a set of fixed soil depth intervals ranging from 0 to 2 metres. This new 536 23    soil map is expected to be largely available through free-access web portals. Researchers (Arrouays 537 24    et al, 2014b) have suggested the rapid production of a first version of the global soil map by 538 25    applying largely tested Digital Soil Mapping (DSM) techniques (McBratney et al., 2003, 26 539 27    Lagacherie et al., 2007) to existing spatial datasets. These datasets include the input of globally 540 28    available landscape parameters (e.g., DTM, digital maps of land use, and multispectral remote 541 29    sensing) and legacy soil data (measured soil profiles, soil maps) that are available in existing soil 542 30    databases. Interest in the above-evoked Digital Soil Mapping approach for producing the new soil 31 543 32 map of the world was tested in various parts of the world including Mediterranean areas such as the 544 33    Cap Bon Region in northern Tunisia (Ciampalini et al, 2012) and the Languedoc-Roussillon Region 545 34    in southern France (Vaysse et Lagacherie, 2015). In this latter experiment, successful predictions 546 35    captured more than 50% of the variability in the mapped soil properties and provided realistic 547 36    estimates of the prediction uncertainty where possible (for the pH and OC in Languedoc- 37 548 38    Roussillon). However, the sparseness of the legacy soil data that were available in current soil 549 39    databases hampered the capture of the short-scale soil variations of soil properties within the 550 40    complex Mediterranean Soil patterns. A priority should be made for the densification of these soil 551 41    data by i) increasing the retrieval of legacy-measured soil profiles, ii) using soil sensing techniques 42 552 43 such as Vis-NIR hyperspectral imagery (Lagacherie & Gomez, 2013) or iii) reviving the field 553 44    collection of soil data following optimized sampling (Brus et al, 2011). These required efforts for 554 45    collecting soil data will likely not fully remove the uncertainty on the soil property predictions, 555 46    considering the short scale of variations of Mediterranean soils. However the expected progresses 556 47    brought by GlobalSoilMap – i.e. increasing the spatial resolution of soil predictions, providing 48 557 49    quantitative estimations and providing explicit values of expected uncertainty - may greatly help for 558 50    site-specific decision making. 559 51 560 52    Conclusions 561 53 The Mediterranean soil resource is a highly specific research object with regards to i) its remarkable 54 562 55    characteristics as caused by a unique combination of natural and anthropogenic soil-forming factors 563 56    and ii) the extent of the expected climate and societal changes that are expected to affect this 564 57    resource in this region of the world. 565 58    In this context, the preservation of Mediterranean soil resources encompasses three crucial 59 566 60 challenges: mitigating soil losses, avoiding soil salinization and maintaining carbon stocks. For 567 61    each of these challenges, there are specific studies to continue or initiate to try to gain a better 62 63 64 65 11 [SOURCE: Page_13]
