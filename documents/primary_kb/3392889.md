### 1. Introduction

After the seminal works of Costanza et al. (1997) and Daily (1997) and the publication of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

Millennium Ecosystem Assessment in 2005 (Reid et al., 2005), the ecosystem service concept has [SOURCE: Page_4]

been expanding worldwide. The relationships between ecosystem services have been the subject of [SOURCE: Page_4]

a growing number of studies (Cord et al., 2017) because their understanding is essential to the design [SOURCE: Page_4]

of public policies and management strategies able to foster the sustainability of ecosystem service [SOURCE: Page_4]

provision (Mouchet et al., 2014). There are both positive (synergies) and negative (tradeoffs) [SOURCE: Page_4]

relationships between multiple ecosystem services, which arise from underlying ecosystem functions [SOURCE: Page_4]

and/or management drivers (Bennett et al., 2009). As shown in the review of Lee and Lautenbach [SOURCE: Page_4]

(2016), synergies are dominant between regulating or cultural services, and the relationships [SOURCE: Page_4]

between regulating and provisioning services are dominated by tradeoffs. The minimization of [SOURCE: Page_4]

tradeoffs is a key challenge of management policies (Rodríguez et al., 2006). As reported by Mouchet [SOURCE: Page_4]

et al. (2014) and Cord et al. (2017), the analysis of ecosystem service relationships often includes the [SOURCE: Page_4]

identification of ecosystem service bundles that can help link ecosystem management to desirable or [SOURCE: Page_4]

undesirable sets of ecosystem services, following the pionneer work of Raudsepp-Hearne et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

(2010). [SOURCE: Page_4]

Landscape and regional scales are the most frequently considered scales in studies of patterns of [SOURCE: Page_4]

ecosystem service relationships (Lee and Lautenbach, 2016; Finney et al., 2017), at the expense of [SOURCE: Page_4]

smaller scales. In addition, causal relationships are often neglected (Cord et al., 2017) although a few [SOURCE: Page_4]

authors have explicitly considered socio-cultural or environmental factors (Martin-Lopez et al., 2012; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Renard et al., 2015; Meacham et al., 2016; Lamy et al., 2016; Feng et al., 2017). Ecosystem functions [SOURCE: Page_4]

that contribute to services are rarely addressed in those studies (with the exception of Lavorel et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

2011). Furthermore, ecosystem services are poorly quantified in many cases (Boerema et al., 2017) [SOURCE: Page_4]

and there is a lack of use of models to address the issues of quantification and of relationships [SOURCE: Page_4]

between services (Seppelt et al., 2011). Several studies of ecosystem service relationships have used [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

models, but only to quantify the services (Nelson et al., 2009; Bai et al., 2011; Leh et al., 2013; Kragt [SOURCE: Page_5]

and Robertson, 2014) rather than to highlight the ecosystem functions that underpin them. The [SOURCE: Page_5]

models also have the advantage of making it possible to test a wide range of management scenarios [SOURCE: Page_5]

in order to understand the effect of management on ecosystem service patterns, as shown in Kragt [SOURCE: Page_5]

and Robertson (2014) and Zanchi et al. (2014). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Agroecosystems, beyond the food production service, may provide multiple non-marketed services [SOURCE: Page_5]

such as soil structure and fertility, water quantity and quality, biological pest control, pollination and [SOURCE: Page_5]

climate regulation through carbon sequestration and greenhouse gas (GHG) mitigation (Heal and [SOURCE: Page_5]

Small, 2002; Swinton et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2007; Power, 2010). The relationships between [SOURCE: Page_5]

agroecosystem services are heavily impacted by agricultural practices as well as by pedoclimatic [SOURCE: Page_5]

conditions, as reported by Power (2010) and exemplified by Kragt and Robertson (2014), Schipanski [SOURCE: Page_5]

et al. (2014), Syswerda and Robertson (2014) and Finney et al. (2017). This is because, first, every [SOURCE: Page_5]

service depends on many ecosystem functions, possibly in opposite ways, and, second, practices and [SOURCE: Page_5]

pedoclimatic conditions have a strong influence on these functions, also possibly in opposite ways, as [SOURCE: Page_5]

reported in the case of orchards (Demestihas et al., 2017). We are convinced that explicit [SOURCE: Page_5]

consideration of the ‘cascade’ that links agricultural practices and pedoclimatic conditions to [SOURCE: Page_5]

ecosystem functions and then to ecosystem services (see Gaba et al., 2015 and Demestihas et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

2017, following Haines-Young and Potschin, 2009) can help us to understand the patterns of [SOURCE: Page_5]

ecosystem service relationships in agroecosystems and to address the challenge of supporting [SOURCE: Page_5]

regulating ecosystem services while maintaining or enhancing the provisioning services (Power, [SOURCE: Page_5]

2010). [SOURCE: Page_5]

To this day, there has been little focus on ecosystem services in fruit orchard research. However, [SOURCE: Page_5]

orchards appear to be an interesting field of study because of the impact of their perennial character [SOURCE: Page_5]

on biogeochemical cycles, their high potential in fruit production, and the importance of pest [SOURCE: Page_5]

management practices, among other reasons (Demestihas et al., 2017 ; Simon et al., 2017). In this [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

study, we analyzed, by means of model simulations, the patterns of relationships between multiple [SOURCE: Page_6]

ecosystem services in the case of apple crops and at the orchard scale by considering the cascade [SOURCE: Page_6]

that links agricultural practices to ecosystem functions and then to ecosystem services. We fixed the [SOURCE: Page_6]

pedoclimatic conditions in order to focus on the role of agricultural practices. We also made a [SOURCE: Page_6]

typology of simulated cropping systems according to their ecosystem service delivery, and identified [SOURCE: Page_6]

cropping systems that optimize the compromise between provisioning and regulating services. We [SOURCE: Page_6]

considered five important ecosystem services: fruit production, soil nitrogen availability, climate [SOURCE: Page_6]

regulation, water cycle maintenance and regulation, including water quality, and pest and disease [SOURCE: Page_6]

control. Together with these five services, we considered environmental disturbances due to [SOURCE: Page_6]

pesticides. According to our cascade approach, we considered, based on the review of Demestihas et [SOURCE: Page_6]

al. (2017), major ecosystem functions that underpin the selected ecosystem services, i.e., carbon, [SOURCE: Page_6]

nitrogen and water balance processes, as well as pest damage and important agricultural practices [SOURCE: Page_6]

that impact these functions, i.e., nitrogen fertilization, irrigation and the control of three main apple [SOURCE: Page_6]

pests. Our expectation is that this study contributes to a better understanding of the functional and [SOURCE: Page_6]

management bases of the relationships between ecosystem services in orchards, which is a [SOURCE: Page_6]

prerequisite for the design of cropping systems that achieve desired profiles of ecosystem services. [SOURCE: Page_6]

### 2. Materials and methods

2.1 General approach [SOURCE: Page_6]

Figure 1 describes the general approach used in this study. Indicators of ecosystem functions and [SOURCE: Page_6]

services (Section 2.2) were simulated on an annual time scale and at the spatial scale of the apple [SOURCE: Page_6]

orchard plot based on the association of a crop model, STICS, and a model of crop injuries caused by [SOURCE: Page_6]

pests, IPSIM (Section 2.3). Pedoclimatic data (temperature, humidity, radiation, potential [SOURCE: Page_6]

evapotranspiration, rainfall and soil characteristics) and agricultural practices were inputs of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

models. Model simulations used apple orchard cropping systems, i.e., combinations of agricultural [SOURCE: Page_6]

practices, created in silico and based on different modalities of five agricultural practices - nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

fertilization, irrigation, and codling moth (Cydia pomonella), rosy apple aphid (Disaphis plantaginae) [SOURCE: Page_7]

and apple scab (Venturia inaequalis) controls - in a given pedoclimatic context (Section 2.4). The [SOURCE: Page_7]

model outputs, i.e., the indicators of ecosystem functions and services, were then analyzed using two [SOURCE: Page_7]

principal component analyses with respect to instrumental variables (PCAIV) in order to formalize, on [SOURCE: Page_7]

the one hand, the links between agricultural practices and ecosystem functions and, on the other, [SOURCE: Page_7]

the links between ecosystem functions and ecosystem services. A typology of apple orchard cropping [SOURCE: Page_7]

systems was then defined using hierarchical clustering and characterized in terms of ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_7]

service profiles. This paved the way for an exploration of the optimal compromise between [SOURCE: Page_7]

provisioning and regulating services among the cropping systems analyzed (Section 2.5). [SOURCE: Page_7]

2.2 Ecosystem service and ecosystem function indicators [SOURCE: Page_7]

time scale of the study during which fruit growth contributes to lower air CO$_2$ concentration. It does [SOURCE: Page_7]

not claim to represent the ability of the orchard to store carbon over the years, contrary to several [SOURCE: Page_7]

studies of carbon budgets in orchards that consider fruit harvest as an export of carbon (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_7]

Montanaro et al., 2017; Scandellari et al., 2016). The mitigation of nitrous oxide is referred to as [SOURCE: Page_8]

2007): this way, a low level of the indicator indicates a low level of service. Water cycle maintenance [SOURCE: Page_8]

pesticides, and we used the reduction of the treatment frequency index, calculated in the same way [SOURCE: Page_8]

treatments concerned only the three main apple pests, codling moth, rosy apple aphid and apple [SOURCE: Page_8]

2.3 Models [SOURCE: Page_9]

Two models were used to quantify ecosystem functions and service indicators. [SOURCE: Page_9]

The first model was STICS (Simulateur mulTIdisciplinaire pour les Cultures Standards), a soil-plant [SOURCE: Page_9]

simulation model (Brisson et al., 2009, 1998b). STICS describes crop growth based on the [SOURCE: Page_9]

development of a foliar surface that intercepts light and converts it into biomass that is allocated to [SOURCE: Page_9]

roots and three aboveground annual organs: fruits, leaves and annual stems. STICS also describes [SOURCE: Page_9]

water and nitrogen balances within the plant and soil compartments. Stress indices are calculated in [SOURCE: Page_9]

water and nitrogen balance modules, reducing, if any, leaf growth and biomass accumulation. Inputs [SOURCE: Page_9]

concern climate, soil and crop management, as well as nitrogen and water quantities in the soil and [SOURCE: Page_9]

above and belowground biomass at the first date of the simulation. The model produces a [SOURCE: Page_9]

considerable set of outputs that can be used as indicators of ecosystem functions and services. STICS [SOURCE: Page_9]

is a widely used and robust generic model (Coucheney et al., 2015). It was successfully parameterized [SOURCE: Page_9]

and evaluated on apple orchards using nine apple orchard systems located in southeastern France [SOURCE: Page_9]

(northern and southern ends of this region: 44°58’N, 4°55’E and 43°43’N, 5°10’E, respectively), which [SOURCE: Page_9]

presented different climatic conditions, very contrasted soils and different types of agricultural [SOURCE: Page_9]

management in terms of nitrogen fertilization, irrigation and planting density (Demestihas et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

2018). We used the STICS model on an annual time scale. [SOURCE: Page_9]

IPSIM is an aggregative modeling framework that predicts injury profiles as a function of three [SOURCE: Page_9]

factors: agricultural practices, abiotic environment – climate, in particular - and biotic environment - [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

especially pest biological control potential (Aubertot and Robin, 2013). The model is based on a [SOURCE: Page_10]

qualitative prioritization of these three factors, structured in a decision tree that enables the [SOURCE: Page_10]

simulation of a damage profile on the considered crop for a given pest. IPSIM is implemented in DEXi [SOURCE: Page_10]

software, a computer program for multi-attribute decision-making (Bohanec et al., 2004). It was [SOURCE: Page_10]

parameterized on apple orchards for the three major apple pests: codling moth, rosy apple aphid and [SOURCE: Page_10]

apple scab (Demestihas, 2017). The factors impacting these pest injuries were determined using a [SOURCE: Page_10]

bibliographic study and experts’ opinions. The model was successfully tested using the nine apple [SOURCE: Page_10]

orchard systems used to evaluate the STICS model (see above; Demestihas, 2017). It is valid at least [SOURCE: Page_10]

in the geographical region covered by the experts, southeast of France. The outputs of the model [SOURCE: Page_10]

corresponded to a series (according to the IPSIM input factors) of mean quantitative values - [SOURCE: Page_10]

basically, ordinal values with expert-based quantitative correspondence - of pest injuries on either [SOURCE: Page_10]

fruits or shoots. This led to a link with the functioning of the STICS model (Appendix 1). Rosy apple [SOURCE: Page_10]

aphid injury was a percentage of daily leaf area loss, corresponding, in the STICS model, to leaf area [SOURCE: Page_10]

removal from the peak of rosy apple aphid flight, i.e., in mid-May, to the tree’s maximal leaf area [SOURCE: Page_10]

establishment (mid-July). Apple scab injury on leaves was not taken into account. Injury levels due to [SOURCE: Page_10]

codling moth and apple scab were percentages of marketable fruit loss. These two types of fruit loss, [SOURCE: Page_10]

considered as two compatible and independent events, were combined into a general fruit loss [SOURCE: Page_10]

percentage, which was simulated at harvest in the STICS model. [SOURCE: Page_10]

### 4.1. Main achievements and limits

Our simulation study demonstrated the strength of the links between agricultural practices, [SOURCE: Page_24]

ecosystem functions and the resulting patterns of relationships between ecosystem services in apple [SOURCE: Page_24]

orchards. It seemed that nitrogen fertilization, often interacting with irrigation, was the agricultural [SOURCE: Page_24]

practice with the greatest impact since most of the ecosystem services considered were linked to [SOURCE: Page_24]

nitrogen. Nitrogen fertilization has also been identified as one of the most important determinants of [SOURCE: Page_24]

the delivery of multiple agroecosystem services in a study conducted along a gradient of cropping [SOURCE: Page_24]

systems (Syswerda and Robertson, 2014). In the range of studied cropping systems, observable [SOURCE: Page_24]

synergies were found between yield, fruit mass and sequestrated carbon. The contribution of carbon [SOURCE: Page_24]

allocation to fruit in sequestrated carbon at the annual scale was considerable, and this is clearly a [SOURCE: Page_24]

specificity of orchards compared to other ecosystems. These services were in conflict with the [SOURCE: Page_24]

prevention of nitrogen denitrification and leaching, also reported by Syswerda and Robertson (2014). [SOURCE: Page_24]

Nitrogen absorption, which is impacted by fertilization and irrigation, was a major driver of these [SOURCE: Page_24]

relationships. Water supply appeared to impact ecosystem functions such as nitrogen mineralization [SOURCE: Page_24]

(De Neve and Hofman, 2002), and the resulting services such as organic nitrogen variation. The [SOURCE: Page_24]

functions related to pest and disease control only contributed to yield. Consequently, they were [SOURCE: Page_24]

poorly represented on the first plane of the second PCAIV (linking ecosystem functions to ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_24]

services). It can be observed that practices that impact many functions are more likely to reveal [SOURCE: Page_24]

tradeoffs or synergies between services. For example, irrigation influences a wide range of services [SOURCE: Page_24]

by its actions on ecosystem functions of the nitrogen cycle (nitrogen mineralization, denitrification, [SOURCE: Page_24]

nitrate leaching) and of the water cycle (runoff, infiltration, evapotranspiration, water allocation to [SOURCE: Page_24]

23 [SOURCE: Page_24]

tree organs), as shown here and reported by Demestihas et al. (2017). It also influences pest [SOURCE: Page_25]

regulation through the modification of the physical or chemical dimension of the ecological niche of [SOURCE: Page_25]

pests (e.g., Gibert et al., 2007; Gibert et al., 2009; Rousselin et al., 2018). Other practices investigated [SOURCE: Page_25]

in studies of bundles of agroecosystem services have similar multiple actions on ecosystem functions [SOURCE: Page_25]

and positive effects on a large range of services, such as the use of cover crops in annual crops [SOURCE: Page_25]

(Schipanski et al., 2014) or that of shade trees in coffee agroecosystems (Meylan et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_25]

With the exception of a synergy between sequestrated carbon and yield or fruit mass characteristic [SOURCE: Page_25]

of orchards (see above), our study showed tradeoffs between provisioning and regulating ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_25]

services. The five groups of apple orchard cropping systems described by ten ecosystem services [SOURCE: Page_25]

were distributed on a gradient expressing this tradeoff. This confirms a general trend, observed at [SOURCE: Page_25]

large scales (see Introduction) and at the scale of plots in agroecosystems (Syswerda and Robertson, [SOURCE: Page_25]

2014; Kragt and Robertson, 2014; Rapidel et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_25]

However, we found five non-dominated cropping systems that optimized both provisioning and [SOURCE: Page_25]

regulating services. Management options can be found that correspond to acceptable compromises [SOURCE: Page_25]

between agroecosystem services (Power, 2010; Kragt and Robertson, 2014; Finney et al., 2017). In [SOURCE: Page_25]

our study, the five non-dominated cropping systems were obtained using two very simple criteria. [SOURCE: Page_25]

However, these criteria were calculated considering the same weight for each service, whereas each [SOURCE: Page_25]

service weight should be discussed with experts in order to more clearly differentiate between [SOURCE: Page_25]

different types of criteria that enable multiple ecosystem service optimization. [SOURCE: Page_25]

The simulations were based on two models adapted to apple orchards, STICS and IPSIM. For STICS, [SOURCE: Page_25]

this adaptation relied on the estimation of some parameters using data from apple orchards located [SOURCE: Page_25]

in southeastern France. Even though more experimental data would be necessary to consolidate this [SOURCE: Page_25]

adaptation, we are confident in the fact that its domain of validity exceeds this area, as discussed in [SOURCE: Page_25]

### 4.2. Advantages of our approach

In our study, three innovative key features stand out, partially addressing the criticisms made by [SOURCE: Page_27]

Seppelt et al. (2011) of the current research in ecosystem service analysis. [SOURCE: Page_27]

The first feature was to analyze synergies and tradeoffs in multiple ecosystem services, which is [SOURCE: Page_27]

common in general studies at regional or landscape scales but less common in agroecosystem studies [SOURCE: Page_27]

on smaller scales. In particular, as shown in the review of Rapidel et al. (2015), most studies on [SOURCE: Page_27]

perennial crops have focused on two-way service relationships, considering a provisioning service [SOURCE: Page_27]

and a regulating service: for example, grapevine yield and fungal disease regulation (Guilpart et al., [SOURCE: Page_27]

2017), grapevine yield and runoff mitigation (Ripoche et al., 2011) or fruit production and [SOURCE: Page_27]

biodiversity conservation (Baumgärtner and Bieri, 2006). Small-scale modeling or experimental [SOURCE: Page_27]

studies of relationships among multiple agroecosystem services, including provisioning services and a [SOURCE: Page_27]

whole array of regulating services, are still scarce (Kragt and Robertson, 2014; Robertson et al., 2009; [SOURCE: Page_27]

Syswerda and Robertson, 2014, Finney et al., 2017). Unlike some of them that include different [SOURCE: Page_27]

agroecosystems, our study focused on a particular agroecosystem - the apple orchard – thus placing [SOURCE: Page_27]

greater emphasis on the comparison of specific types of apple orchard management. [SOURCE: Page_27]

The second feature was to link ecosystem functions to ecosystem service indicators using models. [SOURCE: Page_27]

Models make it possible to describe and quantify the relationships between functions and between [SOURCE: Page_27]

functions and services. In this study, they provided a clear picture of ecosystem service [SOURCE: Page_27]

26 [SOURCE: Page_27]

interdependency based on feedback loops and interactions between ecosystem functions, as already [SOURCE: Page_28]

demonstrated with the APSIM model by Kragt and Robertson (2014). [SOURCE: Page_28]

The third and last feature was the use of a wide range of cropping system situations. This enabled us [SOURCE: Page_28]

to connect types of cropping systems and profiles of ecosystem services, as well as to identify [SOURCE: Page_28]

cropping systems that provide a good compromise between provisioning and regulating ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_28]

services. Rapidel et al. (2015) stressed the importance of making farmers aware of the possibilities of [SOURCE: Page_28]

combining the best sets of practices to provide the desired combinations of ecosystem services. The [SOURCE: Page_28]

space of possibilities in terms of cropping systems could be expanded and its exploration could be [SOURCE: Page_28]
