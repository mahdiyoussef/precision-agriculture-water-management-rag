25   Abstract 26   The mechanisation of forestry operations is too recent in France to have enough perspective [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   and scientific knowledge of the effects on extensively managed soils. Two experimental sites [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   on soils sensitive to compaction (silty acidic soils laying on top of a clayey subsoil) were set [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   in Lorraine (France). A gentle and controlled compaction of these physically very analogous [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   soils was carried out during spring 2007 at the Azerailles site (AZ) and during spring 2008 at [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   the Clermont-en-Argonne site (CA). Immediate changes were observed for all ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   physical, chemical and biological functioning indicators. Soil restoration dynamics were [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   followed using continuous monitoring of numerous parameters, including soil moisture and [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   occurrence of a perched water table (PWT) (monitored at a daily time step) and PWT [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   chemistry (monthly time step). Mid-term monitoring (7 to 8 years) results showed that both [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   soils shifted towards a hydromorphic soil type attested by the increase in PWT frequency and [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   duration, which invaded the soil upper layers, leading to characteristic Fe, Mn and S mobility [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   and a change in organic compounds stability. Though soil types were very similar, the PWT [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   characteristics varied notably between both sites. The mean residence time of the PWT was [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   shown to be a major driver of its geochemistry, but with strong interaction with soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   characteristics. A previous study made on both sites suggested that soil clay content and clay [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   mineralogy controls the PWT dynamics through effects on aggregation. From a PWT [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   perspective, seven years of monitoring were insufficient to observe any soil compaction [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   recovery in the richer soil of AZ but a partial recovery was observed for the chemically poorer [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   soil (CA). Compaction durably impacted the two studied soils and the probability that a new [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   compaction event may occur before the complete recovery from the first disturbance must be [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   considered in forest management. [SOURCE: Page_3]

52      1- Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   Fine textured soils, more especially soils with a silty texture, are known to be very sensitive to [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   physical degradation (MEDDE, 2015). This texture associates both the low reactivity of sands [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   (small particles of quartz and feldspars with low specific area and no shrinking properties, and [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   low cation exchange capacity) and the potential low permeability of de-structured clayey [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   material. Sensitivity to degradation is increased with soil acidity because soil aggregation is [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   low and unstable. Antagonistic characteristics occur in acid soils: low levels of flocculent [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   alkaline earth cations (Ca and Mg) but high levels of flocculent Al (Haynes and Naidu, 1998), [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   high levels of protonated organic matter and low biological activity. Aggregation regulates [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   the organic matter turnover, the water and solute transfer, the gas exchange and consequently [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   the microbial community structure and function (Six et al., 2004). [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   The mechanization of silviculture can lead to soil deformation when the applied pressure [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   exceeds the soil bearing capacity; this phenomenon increases with soil moisture (Ampoorter [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   et al., 2012). Compaction, which is the decrease in porosity without any change in soil mass [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   (de Paul and Bailly, 2005), represents a major risk for soil sustainability. According to its [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   intensity, to the soil characteristics and moisture, it can drastically change soil properties e.g. [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   bulk density (Miller et al., 1996), porosity and mainly macro-porosity (Herbauts, 1996; [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   Bruand et al., 1996), soil hydraulic properties (Horton et al., 1994), water and gas transfer [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   (Stepniewski et al., 1994), and consequently soil biota (Doran and Power, 1983; Brussaard [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   and Faassen, 1994). Compaction changes root configuration and growth (Sands and Bowen, [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   1978; Wronski and Murphy, 1994), root penetration (Heilmann, 1981) and it alters water and [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   nutrient uptake of trees (Goranson et al., 2008; Alavi, 2002). It also decreases the earthworm [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   biomass and their burrowing activity (Jordan et al., 2003). [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   The mechanization of forest practices represents a major risk for forest soil sustainability for [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   fine textured acidic soils with low coarse material content if traffic of machinery is not strictly [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   regulated according to soil moisture. Inappropriate management practices could lead to more [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   or less irreversible changes (Pischedda et al., 2009) because, on the one hand, remediation of [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   physical degradation is costly and technically difficult, and on the other hand, natural [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   restoration of porosity is slow, especially in acidic soils where earthworms are not efficient [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   (Lee, 1985), dominated by epigeous worms whose habitat is limited to the soil surface layers [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   (Lee and Foster, 1991). Observations made in various situations, very often uncontrolled in [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   terms of soil moisture at the time of compaction, but also in terms of machinery (real [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   constraint per soil unit area, number of passes…), led to the conclusion that restoration of soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   porosity takes a long time. For example, observations made on skid trails showed that the soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   remained compacted for 30-40 years (literature quoted by Wronski and Murphy, 1994); [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   observations made on military fields still showed compaction traces after 50 to 100 years; in [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   Germany, the decreased gas diffusion still persisted after 10 years (Schack-Kirchner, 1994); [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   the restoration of porosity and soil organic matter turnover rate was not achieved 10 years [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   after compaction (Rab, 2004). In some specific conditions, restoration was more rapid e .g. [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   soil bulk density was restored 5 years after compaction in a sandy soil (Page-Dumroese et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   2006). [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   In fine textured soils with low permeability in the deep soil layer due to an abrupt change in [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   clay content, a perched water table (PWT) generally occurrs after heavy rain events when soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   is wet and evaporation rate low (Rockefeller et al., 2004) i.e. during the winter period in [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   temperate countries. This is an aggravating factor for soil degradation. [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   It could then be hypothesized first, that mechanization of practices in forests developed on [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   soils sensitive to physical degradation, would reduce the soil porosity and increase the [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   occurrence frequency of a PWT leading to hypo or anoxia processes in the soil, decreasing its [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   ability to sustain its major functions, and second that the time lapse before complete recovery [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   on this soil group would depend on soil mineral fertility i.e. quicker when higher because of [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   its role on soil biological activity. [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   Forest soils on the Lorraine plateau fall in the category of non-hydromorphic but very [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   sensitive soils to physical degradation (silt material laying on heavy clay, acid conditions) [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   (Staub, 2009). Because there is little information regarding the impact of land use on epi- [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   saturation and more or less no information on the duration of the phenomenon, we selected [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   physical and chemical characteristics of the perched water table as an indicator of soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   degradation and restoration in two experimental sites, where a controlled compaction was [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   carried out. [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   The objectives of the present study were then: [SOURCE: Page_6]

111       i- To identify the impacts of a controlled and limited soil compaction of sensitive soils [SOURCE: Page_6]

112       on the occurrence of a perched water table (frequency, level) and on geochemical [SOURCE: Page_6]

113       indicators of redox phenomena (ferrous iron, sulphur, manganese, total geochemistry), [SOURCE: Page_6]

114       ii- To clarify the effect of the mineral fertility level on these processes, comparing two [SOURCE: Page_6]

115       soil environments in the plateau lorrain, submitted to the same stress of compaction, [SOURCE: Page_6]

116       iii-To monitor the dynamics of the same parameters on a medium term, in order to [SOURCE: Page_6]

117       identify the time necessary for recovery of soil porosity, [SOURCE: Page_6]

536   4 Discussion [SOURCE: Page_23]

537   4.1 Soil compaction drastically changed the PWT behaviour [SOURCE: Page_23]

538   At both sites, compaction led to a decrease in soil porosity. Macro-porosity was the most [SOURCE: Page_23]

539   affected as it has been commonly observed (de Paul and Bailly, 2005). Consequently, for the [SOURCE: Page_23]

540   same rainfall input, the soil moisture tended to increase more in the T than in the C treatment, [SOURCE: Page_23]

541   because the size of the reservoir is smaller, and because during the wetter period of the year [SOURCE: Page_23]

542   the soil bottom is sealed. Vertical transfer of rainfall input was more limited in the T [SOURCE: Page_23]

543   treatment than in the control C thus increasing the number of occurrences (x 4 at AZ and by 2 [SOURCE: Page_23]

544   at CA), the height and mean residence time (x by 5 at AZ and by 2 at CA) of a perched water [SOURCE: Page_23]

22 [SOURCE: Page_23]

545   table in the T treatment. Though the soil physical characteristics (a silty soil laying on a [SOURCE: Page_24]

546   clayey subsoil) at the two experimental sites are similar, PWT dynamics, although showing [SOURCE: Page_24]

547   the same relative trends, were notably different in terms of absolute values. [SOURCE: Page_24]

548   What environmental characteristics are able to explain the specific behaviour of PWT in each [SOURCE: Page_24]

549   site? Mean total annual rainfall was exactly the same during the study period, so was the [SOURCE: Page_24]

550   decrease in total porosity due to compaction in both sites (from 45 to 50 dm^3 per m^3). Soil [SOURCE: Page_24]

551   characteristics like clay content (25 at AZ vs 15 % at CA) and total porosity (530 at AZ vs [SOURCE: Page_24]

552   500 dm^3 per m^3 at CA), and climatic parameters like rainfall in autumn (+6,5 mm) and in [SOURCE: Page_24]

553   winter (+65 mm) in the CA site, may explain the higher PWT occurrence in the control (C) at [SOURCE: Page_24]

554   CA than in AZ (41 days vs 26). But all these parameters failed to explain why the mean [SOURCE: Page_24]

555   number of days with PWT in the T treatment was lower at CA when compared to AZ (78 vs [SOURCE: Page_24]

556   136). The soil texture and mineralogy, and more particularly its clay fraction, on the one [SOURCE: Page_24]

557   hand, and the soil Ca-Mg saturation on the other hand, were found to play a major role in the [SOURCE: Page_24]

558   stability of the soil structure in the two sites (Bedel et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_24]

559   We hypothesised a complex and somewhat antagonistic role of the clay fraction, which would [SOURCE: Page_24]

560   interact with the soil richness, higher at AZ (mean soil “base” cation saturation value of 37) [SOURCE: Page_24]

561   than at CA (mean value of 19): [SOURCE: Page_24]

562      i-   The higher clay content at AZ favoured a relatively more stable structure than at CA. [SOURCE: Page_24]

563   After compaction, which destroyed the structure and increased the soil physical resistance [SOURCE: Page_24]

564   (Goutal et al, 2013), this higher clay content would play a major role in the loss of soil [SOURCE: Page_24]

565   permeability; the “base cation” rate being not enough high to compensate for the loss of [SOURCE: Page_24]

566   structure, [SOURCE: Page_24]

567      ii- The occurrence of a swelling beidellite-Fe clay type at CA (dominant in the subsoil, [SOURCE: Page_24]

568   and significant in the soil) (Bedel et al., 2018) was shown to be negative for the soil structural [SOURCE: Page_24]

23 [SOURCE: Page_24]

569   stability at CA, but would be efficient for a rapid cracking of the compacted soil when soil [SOURCE: Page_25]

570   moisture decreased. [SOURCE: Page_25]

571   Compaction therefore drastically changed the geochemical functioning of the soils, and two [SOURCE: Page_25]

572   parameters had strong consequences on it: i- the level reached by the PWT in the soil, which [SOURCE: Page_25]

573   mean value was multiplied by 5 in winter at AZ and by 2 at CA, because of the soil [SOURCE: Page_25]

574   horizonation, and, ii- its duration, as the mean annual number of days of occurrence was [SOURCE: Page_25]

575   multiplied by 4 at AZ and by 2 at CA, and as its mean residence time (MRT) was multiplied [SOURCE: Page_25]

576   by 4 at AZ and only by 1.1 at CA. [SOURCE: Page_25]

577   One major effect of compaction was the fact that PWT invaded upper soil layers, which [SOURCE: Page_25]

578   were never or very rarely affected by waterlogging processes. It is very well known that upper [SOURCE: Page_25]

579   soil layers contain most of the organic C, the organic forms of many other elements, and the [SOURCE: Page_25]

580   biological populations and activity. Compaction led to new soil forming-processes, which [SOURCE: Page_25]

581   strongly impacted both mineral (ion exchange, dissolution/precipitation, weathering rate) and [SOURCE: Page_25]

582   biological (organic C, N, P, S mineralization) processes. These two soils, classified as [SOURCE: Page_25]

583   sensitive to physical degradation, shifted towards typical hydromorphic soils, only a few [SOURCE: Page_25]

584   months after the compaction stress. The question of the reversibility of the processes is [SOURCE: Page_25]

585   strongly relevant, but difficult to investigate, just because no control situation is available for [SOURCE: Page_25]

586   the dynamics of changes in the upper soil layers of the compacted treatment. However, [SOURCE: Page_25]

587   whether restoration occurred or not, element redistribution clearly marked soil morphology [SOURCE: Page_25]

588   (unpublished information of the site database) with hydromorphic traits e.g. thin iron pans, [SOURCE: Page_25]

589   Fe/Mn stains or concretions, or drainage losses (Fe, Mn, C and nutrients), bleached areas, and [SOURCE: Page_25]

590   decreased the soil quality for all its functions. [SOURCE: Page_25]

591   Changing the MRT strongly impacted the processes leading to the geochemistry of the PWT. [SOURCE: Page_25]

592   When looking at common specific soil layers temporally waterlogged in both the C and T [SOURCE: Page_25]

593   treatments we found three cases: [SOURCE: Page_25]

24 [SOURCE: Page_25]

594    1-    Though trends were observed between C and T, these differences were not statistically [SOURCE: Page_26]

595   significant suggesting that MRT was not long enough to mobilize more than the immediately [SOURCE: Page_26]

596   available reserve of compounds, by ion exchange, by dissolution reactions, or by mixing with [SOURCE: Page_26]

597   tightly bound water, [SOURCE: Page_26]

598    2-    Significant negative relation between MRT and the concentration of a limited amount [SOURCE: Page_26]

599   of very soluble compounds suggesting a simple dilution effect, and, [SOURCE: Page_26]

600    3-    Significant positive relation between elemental concentrations (e.g. Fe, Mn and S) and [SOURCE: Page_26]

601   MRT indicating that the duration of waterlogging changed a physical parameter which led to [SOURCE: Page_26]

602   the development of specific processes affecting these sensitive elements to redox. This PWT [SOURCE: Page_26]

603   duration effect promoted the development of slower processes like dissolution of meta-stable [SOURCE: Page_26]

604   compounds, exchange with more tightly bound water, or even with the subsoil compartment. [SOURCE: Page_26]

605   Indirect effects of changes in the environment led for example to the mobilization of elements [SOURCE: Page_26]

606   that are not directly sensitive to redox processes like Al of mix-hydroxides. The mobilization [SOURCE: Page_26]

607   of Al and sulphur in turn may mobilize compounds such as available cations or anions after [SOURCE: Page_26]

608   ion-exchange or dissolution, and/or, change biological processes affecting directly C and N [SOURCE: Page_26]

609   compound stability. [SOURCE: Page_26]

610   At AZ, the rather long MRT (1 day in the C but 4 days in the T) led to the three situations [SOURCE: Page_26]

611   aforementioned while at CA, the shorter MRT (less than half a day in both C and T) led to [SOURCE: Page_26]

612   only types 1 and 3. Paradoxically, it was at CA that the highest Fe$_2$+ concentrations were [SOURCE: Page_26]

613   observed, suggesting that the redox conditions in solution for iron reduction were reached the [SOURCE: Page_26]

614   most rapidly after waterlogging (figure 11 figure 9). This was not explained by more labile [SOURCE: Page_26]

615   Fe-bearing compounds at CA compared to AZ: the FeDCB: FeTot ratios suggested a higher [SOURCE: Page_26]

616   lability at AZ. From a previous study, we hypothesized that the massive structure of the acidic [SOURCE: Page_26]

617   CA soil when wet (Bedel et al., 2018), would rapidly lead to anoxic conditions. [SOURCE: Page_26]

619   4.2 PWT time changes used as an indicator of the dynamics of soil restoration after [SOURCE: Page_27]

620   compaction [SOURCE: Page_27]

621   In the present experimental designs, changes occurring after compaction integrated several [SOURCE: Page_27]

622   effects which had consequences on both physical and chemical characteristics of PWT e.g. i- [SOURCE: Page_27]

623   the clear-cutting effect, ii- the changes in vegetation including both understory and oak [SOURCE: Page_27]

624   developing plantation, iii- the year-to-year variability of the climate, and finally iii- the [SOURCE: Page_27]

625   hypothetical restoration of soil porosity. [SOURCE: Page_27]

626   The change in soil porosity and its consequences on PWT were easy to characterize. The [SOURCE: Page_27]

627   deconvolution on a relatively short time series of the global signals to identify the effects of [SOURCE: Page_27]

628   individual parameters on porosity dynamics, was far more complex. It is known that clear- [SOURCE: Page_27]

629   cutting and vegetation development have opposing effects on the soil water regime and soil [SOURCE: Page_27]

630   water chemistry: the former usually increases soil moisture and elemental concentrations in [SOURCE: Page_27]

631   soil solutions, while the latter does the reverse (Dyck et al., 1994). Year-to-year changes in [SOURCE: Page_27]

632   the rainfall amount were able to disturb these general effects, especially when a systematic [SOURCE: Page_27]

633   gradient occurred. [SOURCE: Page_27]

634   At AZ no changes were recorded in the rain amount along the study period. The mid-term [SOURCE: Page_27]

635   trend was a reduction of the PWT in the control (attested by a significant decrease in mean [SOURCE: Page_27]

636   level, duration in days, duration of individual events) but to an increase of the PWT in the [SOURCE: Page_27]

637   compacted treatment (significant at 10% for the mean level and 5% for the duration in days). [SOURCE: Page_27]

638   At CA, where the winter-autumn rainfall significantly increased during the period of [SOURCE: Page_27]

639   monitoring, the trend was close to a decrease of the difference of PWT physical [SOURCE: Page_27]

640   characteristics between C (significant mean level increase in winter, and a individual event [SOURCE: Page_27]

641   duration decrease) and T (significant decrease of the maximum level and of the duration of [SOURCE: Page_27]

642   individual events). [SOURCE: Page_27]

26 [SOURCE: Page_27]

643   Physical traits of the PWT led to the conclusion that no restoration of the soil porosity [SOURCE: Page_28]

644   occurred 7 years after compaction at AZ, but that some restoration occurred at CA. [SOURCE: Page_28]

645   We hypothesize that the mean level of PWT, which integrates all the PWT episodes, is a more [SOURCE: Page_28]

646   robust indicator of soil restoration than the maximum level, which could be the result of few [SOURCE: Page_28]

647   events. [SOURCE: Page_28]

648   The aforementioned confusing effects and the fact that at AZ no significant sampling was [SOURCE: Page_28]

649   possible in the upper soil layers, made it more difficult to use the PWT geochemistry traits as [SOURCE: Page_28]

650   indicators for soil restoration after compaction. Similarly to clear-cutting effects for example, [SOURCE: Page_28]

651   some compaction effects could be mitigated and/or delayed in the deep soil layers. The [SOURCE: Page_28]

652   vertical distribution in the soil profile of numerous elements in the T treatment, positively [SOURCE: Page_28]

653   (DOC, NH$_4$+, NO$_3$-, Fe$_2$+, Mn$_2$+, K+, Al$_3$+) or negatively (Mg$_2$+, Si, SO42-) correlated with soil [SOURCE: Page_28]

654   depth, consolidated this point. [SOURCE: Page_28]

655   Taking all these parameters into account led to the conclusion that indexes of soil restoration [SOURCE: Page_28]

656   using PWT geochemistry showed very limited changes in AZ where only mineral-N indicated [SOURCE: Page_28]

657   some restoration. At 45 cm depth, sensitive element like Fe$_2$+ or Mn$_2$+ did not show any [SOURCE: Page_28]

658   changes with time, excepted the decrease in the strong initial effect of compaction. The [SOURCE: Page_28]

659   behaviour Na+ was peculiar and seemed to indicate a degradation of the situation! There was [SOURCE: Page_28]

660   probably a more pronounced restoration rate in the upper soil layers, but we already presented [SOURCE: Page_28]

661   the difficulty to estimate it. [SOURCE: Page_28]

662   On the contrary, at CA, all elements but Si and SO42-, for both 20 and 45 cm levels and NO$_3$- [SOURCE: Page_28]

663   at 45 cm, showed a significant dampening of the initial effect of compaction. As previously [SOURCE: Page_28]

664   explained, we hypothesized that clay content and nature, through the soil structure stability [SOURCE: Page_28]

665   was a major parameter controlling the physical characteristics of the PWT, and indirectly its [SOURCE: Page_28]

666   geochemistry. [SOURCE: Page_28]

27 [SOURCE: Page_28]

667   The PWT physical and geochemical indicators were in rather good agreement, and both [SOURCE: Page_29]

668   suggest the restoration of soil porosity in the specific situation of each experimental site. This [SOURCE: Page_29]

669   was clearer at CA in a situation where the PWT flooded the medium and lower soil layers of [SOURCE: Page_29]

670   both the C and T treatments, than at AZ, where only the lower layer could be investigated [SOURCE: Page_29]

671   with both physical and chemical indicators, limiting the relevance of the information collected [SOURCE: Page_29]

672   from the geochemical investigation. [SOURCE: Page_29]

673   Our experience leads to the conclusion that physical traits of the PWT, far easier to monitor [SOURCE: Page_29]

674   than chemical ones, could be investigated alone for a satisfactory diagnosis of the restoration [SOURCE: Page_29]

675   of soil porosity. Nevertheless, to characterize the short and long terms effects on all the [SOURCE: Page_29]

676   components of soil fertility, the geochemical traits add strongly relevant information. [SOURCE: Page_29]

678   4.3 Consequences for forest management? [SOURCE: Page_29]

679   Using the PWT as an indicator of forest soil recovery after application of a limited [SOURCE: Page_29]

680   compaction stress showed its relevance and its ability to sort between two relatively similar [SOURCE: Page_29]

681   situations inside an identical soil type. [SOURCE: Page_29]

682   We hypothesized that the richer soil would recover more rapidly than the poorer one, because [SOURCE: Page_29]

683   recovery should be related to soil quality, including physical (better structure stability), [SOURCE: Page_29]

684   chemical (richness in ‘base cations’) and biological (flora indication, earthworms activity). [SOURCE: Page_29]

685   This hypothesis was not verified. [SOURCE: Page_29]

686   At AZ, because limited or no soil recovery occurred during the 7 years after compaction, and [SOURCE: Page_29]

687   because the complete recovery would not happen suddenly, the probability that an additional [SOURCE: Page_29]

688   mechanized silviculture operation would take place before the complete restoration of the first [SOURCE: Page_29]

689   disturbance is very high (in France rotation lengths are about 8 to 10 years for coniferous and [SOURCE: Page_29]

690   12 to 15 years for broadleaved species). The effects of a second compaction event would be [SOURCE: Page_29]

691   more dramatic and virtually impossible to predict. In such conditions, mechanized forest [SOURCE: Page_29]

28 [SOURCE: Page_29]

692   operations must be strictly regulated to ensure the sustainable management of the forest: [SOURCE: Page_30]

693   extraction tracks are recommended. This practice is already applied by forest managers in [SOURCE: Page_30]

694   State-owned forests in France (Pischedda et al., 2009) but its evaluation is still on-going [SOURCE: Page_30]

695   because the trafficability of the tracks must be maintained. Concerning the consequences of [SOURCE: Page_30]

696   the disturbance, an unknown parameter is the mechanical behaviour of forest soils that have [SOURCE: Page_30]

697   never been impacted by strong and repeated mechanical constraints: their pre-consolidation [SOURCE: Page_30]

698   point (threshold pressure separating an elastic and recoverable soil deformation from a plastic [SOURCE: Page_30]

699   and non recoverable soil deformation) should change, modifying the reversibility of the [SOURCE: Page_30]

700   compaction (Dias Junior and Pierce, 1995). [SOURCE: Page_30]

701   The consequences on vegetation appeared to be site-dependent. On the short-term time scale, [SOURCE: Page_30]

702   tree mortality increased (strongly at AZ and less at CA) and tree growth was reduced in T [SOURCE: Page_30]

703   treatments compared to C (at both sites) (Ranger et al., 2015). In both cases, cleaning [SOURCE: Page_30]

704   operations for eliminating the strong competition of understory vegetation in the compacted [SOURCE: Page_30]

705   plots increased the costs for ensuring the development of young oak stands. Compaction [SOURCE: Page_30]

706   directly affects the tree root system by increasing the soil bulk density and the soil physical [SOURCE: Page_30]

707   resistance (Goutal et al., 2017).      The resulting hydromorphy, drastically changing the [SOURCE: Page_30]

708   functioning of the soil, represented a strong constraint for vegetation in terms of root [SOURCE: Page_30]

709   respiration, water and nutrient uptake. The limited but real soil restoration at the low fertility [SOURCE: Page_30]

710   site of CA was fortunate: Bedel et al. (2016) demonstrated that trees mobilised the sub-soil [SOURCE: Page_30]

711   water and nutrient reserves during the dry periods. Nevertheless, it is necessary to [SOURCE: Page_30]

712   demonstrate that the restoration of the deepest layers of the soil is real by investigating the [SOURCE: Page_30]

713   soil solid phase (volume mass, physical resistance, morphology…). Any degradation of soil [SOURCE: Page_30]

714   quality represents a real constraint for the durability of such ecosystems in the long term. [SOURCE: Page_30]

717   5 Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_31]

718      1- The results demonstrated that the limited controlled compaction of two sensitive silty [SOURCE: Page_31]

719   soils belonging to the same ruptic neo-luvisols type, immediately and drastically changed the [SOURCE: Page_31]

720   soil water regime, as shown by the physical and geochemical characteristics of the perched [SOURCE: Page_31]

721   water table. Both soils shifted towards a hydromorphic type attested by an increase in the [SOURCE: Page_31]

722   number of occurrences of a PWT and its duration, which flooded the soil upper layers and [SOURCE: Page_31]

723   lead to characteristic mobility of Fe, Mn and S. Stability of organic C and N compounds was [SOURCE: Page_31]

724   also changed: our first hypothesis is validated. [SOURCE: Page_31]

725      2- Even within the same soil type, a polycyclic soil composed of a 50 cm silty stratum [SOURCE: Page_31]

726   laying on a clayey subsoil, the PWT characteristics notably varied. The mean residence time [SOURCE: Page_31]

727   of the PWT was shown to be a major driver of its geochemistry, but with strong interaction [SOURCE: Page_31]

728   with soil characteristics. A previous study made on both sites (Bedel et al.,2018) suggested [SOURCE: Page_31]

729   that soil clay content and clay mineralogy controlled the PWT behaviour through aggregation. [SOURCE: Page_31]

730      3- Seven years after the application of the constraint were not enough to observed [SOURCE: Page_31]

731   significant recovery, judged from the PWT, in the richer soil of AZ, but led to partial recovery [SOURCE: Page_31]

732   of the poorer one at CA: our second hypothesis is not validated. [SOURCE: Page_31]

733      4- Short-term negative consequences on soil and vegetation were easily observable. [SOURCE: Page_31]

734   Long-term consequences were more complex to simulate, most especially in the richer soil of [SOURCE: Page_31]

735   AZ where little recovery occurred after seven years. This means that compaction durably [SOURCE: Page_31]

736   impacted the soils and that the probability of a new compaction before the complete recovery [SOURCE: Page_31]

737   of the first disturbance, has to be considered. Contrarily to agriculture soils that have been [SOURCE: Page_31]

738   subject to compaction for years, forest soils have not, and their reaction to successive [SOURCE: Page_31]

739   disturbances needs to be reliably investigated, particularly for changes in their intrinsic [SOURCE: Page_31]

740   mechanical characteristics. [SOURCE: Page_31]
