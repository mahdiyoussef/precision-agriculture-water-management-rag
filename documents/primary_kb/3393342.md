24   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   Recent initiatives, such as the United Nations declaring 2015 as the International Year of [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   Soils and the French « 4 per 1000 » initiative call attention on soils and on the importance of [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   maintaining and increasing soil organic matter stocks for soil fertility and food security, and [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   for climate change adaptation and mitigation. We stress that soil organic carbon storage (i.e. [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   an increase of soil organic carbon stocks) should be clearly differentiated from soil organic [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   carbon sequestration, as the latter assumes a net removal of atmospheric CO$_2$. Implementing [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   management options that allow increasing soil organic carbon stocks at the local scale raises [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   several questions, which are discussed in this article: how can we increase SOC stocks, at [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   which rate and for how long; where do we prioritize SOC storage; how do we estimate the [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   potential gain in C and which agricultural practices should we implement? We show that [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   knowledge and tools are available to answer many of these questions, while further research [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   remains necessary for others. A range of agricultural practices would require a re-assessment [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   of their potential to store C and a better understanding of the underlying processes, such as no [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   tillage and conservation agriculture, irrigation, practices increasing below ground inputs, [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   organic amendments, and N fertilization. The vision emerging from the literature, showing [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   the prominent role of soil microorganisms in the stabilization of soil organic matter, draw the [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   attention to more exploratory potential levers, through changes in microbial physiology or soil [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   biodiversity induced by agricultural practices, that require in-depth research. [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   1- Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

44   The 4 per 1000 initiative aims at increasing the organic matter content of soils, with a special [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   focus on agricultural land, in order to contribute to food security, adaptation to climate [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   change, and mitigation of climate change (http://4p^1000.org). The first two aims of the [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

47   initiative are based on common knowledge regarding the central role of soil organic matter [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   (SOM) in soil fertility and soil water retention (Lal, 2008). Thus SOM indirectly contributes [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   to agro-ecosystem productivity, and hence to food security. Soil organic matter also provides [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   agro-ecosystems with the ability to adapt to a changing climate with less frequent and regular [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   precipitation and more extreme rainfall, with related erosion problems (Chenu et al., 2000; [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   Pan et al., 2009). The third aim of the initiative, regarding mitigation, is based on quantitative [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   data on soil organic carbon (SOC) pools (Jobbagy and Jackson, 2000) and fluxes (Le Quéré et [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   al., 2015) showing that small changes in world SOC stocks could aggravate or mitigate the [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   global greenhouse gas (GHG) effect, as early suggested by Balesdent and Arrouays (1999) [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   and developed for example in recent articles (Chambers et al., 2016; Lal, 2016; Minasny et [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   al., 2018; Minasny et al., 2017; Paustian et al., 2016; Soussana et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   Accounting for soil carbon as part of the GHG assessment may be subject of [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   misunderstanding (Minasny et al., 2018). Two terms are often used interchangeably: carbon [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   sequestration and carbon storage, which can sometimes lead to confusion. Here, according to [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   Olson et al. (2014), we define Carbon sequestration as “the process of transferring CO$_2$ from [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   the atmosphere into the soil of a land unit, through plants, plant residues and other organic [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   solids which are stored or retained in the unit as part of the soil organic matter (humus). [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   Retention time of sequestered carbon in the soil (terrestrial pool) can range from short-term [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   (not immediately released back to atmosphere) to long-term (millennia) storage.”. [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   Sequestration can therefore be quantified for a given duration. Twenty years is often chosen [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   (IPCC, 2006). Carbon storage is broader as it is defined as the increase in SOC stocks over [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   time in the soils of a given land unit, not necessarily associated with a net removal of CO$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   from the atmosphere. For example adding the available manure resources on a given [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   agricultural field rather than spreading it homogenously over the landscape may locally [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   increase SOC stocks (where manure has been added), but not increase the associated CO$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   removal from the atmosphere at the landscape scale. While storing organic carbon for long [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   times is preferable in terms of GHG mitigation, labile fractions of SOC (e.g. with residence [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   times of months to years) are essential in terms of soil fertility (their mineralization provides [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   nutrients to plants), of soil physical condition (aggregate stability largely depends on labile C, [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   (e.g., Angers and Mehuys, 1989; Cosentino et al., 2006), and of soil biodiversity (labile [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   organic matter being the trophic resource of organisms). Hence it is desirable to increase [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   stocks of both labile and stable forms of organic matter. In any case, the time horizon at [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   which SOC storage or SOC sequestration are being considered should be specified. Here, we [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   consider both organic carbon storage and sequestration and will use the term soil carbon [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   storage when no assumption is made on a net atmospheric CO$_2$ removal. [SOURCE: Page_4]

82 [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   Increasing organic matter in soils via management practices not only encompasses soil carbon [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   related questions, but also agronomic and environmental dimensions, as well as sociological, [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   economical and ethical ones. These will not be considered here, while we do not disregard [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   their importance, e.g., Aubert et al. (2017). Moreover, mitigating GHG emissions by [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   increasing SOC stocks should not be regarded as a way to compensate for CO$_2$ emissions due [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   to the combustion of fossil fuel nor for N2O or CH$_4$ emissions related to agricultural activities [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   and hence allow to continue business as usual, but rather as an additional lever in the portfolio [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   of options that countries can consider to reduce their agricultural GHG emissions (Wollenberg [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_4]

99   2. SOC stocks result from a balance between inputs and outputs [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   If the OC inputs to a soil become larger than the OC outputs by mineralization or erosion, [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   then its SOC stock will increase. SOC stock evolution can be described as follows: [SOURCE: Page_5]

102          dC/dt = I(t) - k(t).C                 (1) [SOURCE: Page_5]

103          where I is OC inputs and k is the rate of C loss (i.e., the probability to be lost in the [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   interval dt, by mineralization, erosion or leaching). Assuming that OC inputs and C loss rates [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   are constant over time, SOC stock displays a steady-state value, [SOURCE: Page_5]

106          C* = I/k                              (2) [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   Let’s assume that either input or loss rate is altered due to a change in land use or land [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   management. The SOC stock will evolve out of steady state for a certain period, to eventually [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   reach the new equilibrium value. This is illustrated in Figure 1. When considering a soil, [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   except well-documented plots for which records of SOC stocks over time are available, it is [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   not easy to determine a priori if the OC stock of this soil is at equilibrium with the OC inputs, [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   or somewhere between two equilibria. It explains why, even if annual OC inputs are constant [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   in a given period, SOC stocks can increase or decrease during that period. This phenomenon [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   may explain for example the changes of SOC stocks observed in French forest soils. SOC [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   stocks in mineral soils (0-40 cm) were monitored between 1993 and 2012 in 102 plots across [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   France and increased on average by 0.035 kgC m-2 y-1 (Jonard et al., 2017): SOC stocks were [SOURCE: Page_5]

117   probably not yet at equilibrium at the start of the monitoring. Furthermore the C accumulation [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   rate was found to decline with stand age (Jonard et al., 2017), what could be interpreted as [SOURCE: Page_5]

119   getting closer to an equilibrium between the soil C stock and C input. This result among [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

120   others stresses the need to have good constraints on the soil past land-uses when studying its [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   OC stock evolution. However, in the context of a continuously changing climate SOC values [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   at equilibrium may also continuously change, although these aspects will not be considered [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   here. [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   If we consider that the loss rate is constant over time, an increase in OC inputs will result in [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   SOC stock accrual. Such an increase can be obtained either by increasing C inputs from the [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   vegetation (increase in net primary production or increase in residue return) or by increasing [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   exogenous organic matter addition (manure, organic wastes, organic amendments…). [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   The reduction of C outputs (i.e., a decrease in the decay rate k) can be achieved by reducing [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   SOC mineralization by soil micro-organisms or SOC erosion. The role of microorganisms as [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   drivers of soil C cycling, storage and sequestration in soil is not conceptually new, but is [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   increasingly acknowledged as crucial (Dungait et al., 2012; Lehmann and Kleber, 2015; [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   Schimel and Schaeffer, 2012). Microorganisms contribute both to the biodegradation and [SOURCE: Page_6]

139   mineralisation of soil organic substrates, and to the genesis of new organic metabolites. Their [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   action could be viewed as decreasing C storage through the mineralisation of SOM, but [SOURCE: Page_6]

141   increasing the residence time of C as their metabolites exhibit a high affinity for the [SOURCE: Page_6]

142   protecting mineral phases (Cotrufo et al., 2013; Kallenbach et al., 2015; Miltner et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_6]

143   The breakdown and biosynthesis rates of the various biochemical components of OM are [SOURCE: Page_6]

144   determined by the microbial community dynamics and by their functional traits. These rates [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

145   are limited by physical accessibility of substrate to microbes or to their enzymes and by the [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   micro-habitat conditions (oxygen, pH, water content, nutrient resources) (e.g. Juarez et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   2013b; Pinheiro et al., 2015; Ruamps et al., 2011). As such, any agricultural practice altering [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   the soil physical structure or affecting the microbe resources and the environmental conditions [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   can modify the mineralisation rate of SOC. Regarding erosion, it is established that the [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   downslope transport of SOC in the landscape might redistribute it to sites where it persists [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   longer that it would have in its original location (Berhe et al., 2012; Chaopricha and Marin- [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   Spiotta, 2014; Quinton et al., 2010). The relative effects of the loss of SOC from eroded areas, [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   the loss of C during transportation and its accumulation in depositional areas on the global [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   carbon budget are still under debate (Sanderman and Berhe, 2017; Wang et al., 2017; Yue et [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   al., 2016). While erosion may create a C sink (Berhe et al., 2007), it remains a major soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   degradation process, not compatible with sustainable food security and environmental quality [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   (Sanderman and Berhe, 2017). [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   3. By how much, at which rate and for how long can SOC stocks [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   be increased? [SOURCE: Page_7]

161   The carbon storage potential of a given soil unit may be defined as the maximum gain in [SOURCE: Page_7]

162   soil OC stock attainable under a given climate and a given timeline (e.g. time required to [SOURCE: Page_7]

163   attain a new equilibrium or a given time period, such as 20 y for IPCC) (Figure 1). Similarly, [SOURCE: Page_7]

164   the carbon sequestration potential of a given soil would be the maximum gain in SOC [SOURCE: Page_7]

165   allowing a net removal of CO$_2$ from the atmosphere under a given climate and for a specified [SOURCE: Page_7]

166   timeline. From current knowledge of SOM dynamics, we can infer the magnitude and kinetics [SOURCE: Page_7]

167   of SOC change as related to yearly carbon inputs to the soil and pedoclimatic conditions. This [SOURCE: Page_7]

168   knowledge is synthesized in several models of SOC dynamics (see the review of models by [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

169   (Campbell and Paustian, 2015). In Table 1 we calculated for a few representative situations [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   the increase in plant material input or the decrease in decay rates that are required to increase [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   SOC by 4 per 1000 per year during 20 years, i.e., increase soil C by +8% after 20 years, using [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   the model RothC 26.3 (Coleman and Jenkinson, 2014). Typically, the magnitude of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

173   relative changes in plant C inputs or decay rates to reach the target stands in the range of 15 to [SOURCE: Page_8]

174   25% (Table 1). It is noticeable that the maximum C gain is much higher than the gain in 20 [SOURCE: Page_8]

175   years: from 1.6 times higher in light-textured tropical soil where OC turnover is rapid, to 3.0 [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   times higher in cool, clayey soils where OM turnover is slowest. Similarly, differences [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   between environments regarding the required OM input increment typically depends on (i) the [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   differences in mineralization kinetics, and (ii) the initial C content, since the calculation is [SOURCE: Page_8]

179   based on a relative increase in SOC. Around these general features, other inputs than plant [SOURCE: Page_8]

180   material, such as composted amendments, are expected to have a higher yield in SOC (see [SOURCE: Page_8]

181   section 5); in case of progressive increase in inputs, e.g., through the positive feedback of [SOURCE: Page_8]

182   SOC on net primary production, SOC increase will be slower. [SOURCE: Page_8]

184   Soil organic matter dynamics models have been developed in the 1970’s and 1980’s based on [SOURCE: Page_8]

185   long-term experiments in either temperate or tropical agricultural environments. Their [SOURCE: Page_8]

186   parameters have been calibrated so as to fit observed C changes over decades under controlled [SOURCE: Page_8]

187   C inputs or management practices. The major difficulties encountered in the use of these [SOURCE: Page_8]

188   model predictions at the local scale concern: (i) the initial state of soil carbon, which can be [SOURCE: Page_8]

189   under steady or non-steady state depending on past conditions (see above discussion in [SOURCE: Page_8]

190   section 2); (ii) the difficulty in initializing accurately the size of the SOC pools (Luo et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

191   2016), in particular those with long turnover times such as for example the “passive” pool in [SOURCE: Page_8]

192   the Century model having turnover times of several centuries (Parton et al., 1988) and the [SOURCE: Page_8]

193   “inert” pool, of the RothC model with residence times of several thousands of years [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

194   (Jenkinson and Rayner, 1977); (iii) the estimate of the actual C inputs to the soil; (iv) a [SOURCE: Page_9]

195   generally poor accounting of soil types besides their clay content; (v) the impact of practices [SOURCE: Page_9]

196   on decay rates of SOC (the management practices currently simulated in SOC models being [SOURCE: Page_9]

197   limited and SOC models do not simulate the effects of innovative cropping systems such as [SOURCE: Page_9]

198   associated crops or agroforestry); and (v) the lack of consideration of deep soil layers. As a [SOURCE: Page_9]

199   result, models parameterization may be still rough concerning important factors driving SOC [SOURCE: Page_9]

200   dynamics. Furthermore, model’s linearity (i.e., amounts of SOC formed on the long-term [SOURCE: Page_9]

201   assumed to be proportional to inputs) has been questioned. [SOURCE: Page_9]

202 [SOURCE: Page_9]

203   Hassink (1997) suggested that soils have a finite capacity to protect organic matter from [SOURCE: Page_9]

204   mineralization, i.e., a finite capacity to store carbon for decades or more, which was termed [SOURCE: Page_9]

205   the carbon saturation capacity. In this concept, which was expanded by Six et al. (2002) and [SOURCE: Page_9]

206   then by Beare et al. (2014), long residence times of organic matter are essentially due to fine [SOURCE: Page_9]

207   sized minerals (<20 µm), clay minerals and sesquioxides, that provide protection by organo- [SOURCE: Page_9]

208   mineral interactions (physico-chemical protection) or reduced accessibility of organic [SOURCE: Page_9]

209   compounds to decomposers or oxygen to decomposers (physical protection). The abundance [SOURCE: Page_9]

210   of the < 20 µm size fraction would determine the amount of C that can be stored for decades [SOURCE: Page_9]

211   to centuries, in association with this fraction. [SOURCE: Page_9]

212 [SOURCE: Page_9]

213   If we assume a linear relationship between C inputs and SOC stocks at steady state (as [SOURCE: Page_9]

214   assumed by almost all soil C dynamic models, Figure 2a), the only limitation for SOC stock [SOURCE: Page_9]

215   increase is the amount of OC inputs that can be provided. Several authors challenged the [SOURCE: Page_9]

216   concept that an extra C input would always produce a similar SOC stock increase (Stewart et [SOURCE: Page_9]

217   al., 2007). Their alternative referred to two concepts. The first concept assumes a saturation [SOURCE: Page_9]

218   behaviour towards C inputs, C inputs becoming less and less efficient as the soil is [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

219   approaching its saturation capacity (Figure 2b). Here the saturation concept was expanded to [SOURCE: Page_10]

220   the total soil organic carbon, contrary to the original proposal of Hassink (1997). This [SOURCE: Page_10]

221   alternative view allows explaining the results of the few studies showing little or no increase [SOURCE: Page_10]

222   of SOC stocks following increased OC inputs. However, there is little or no difference [SOURCE: Page_10]

223   between the linear and saturation relationships for most of field data (Stewart et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_10]

224   The second concept is the so-called “priming effect”, which has been evidenced in deep soil [SOURCE: Page_10]

225   layers: when SOC mineralization is limited by the energy supply to microorganisms, [SOURCE: Page_10]

226   increased C inputs of fresh material may accelerate old SOC mineralization (Fontaine et al., [SOURCE: Page_10]

227   2007). Whatever the considered formalism, establishing which factors are playing on [SOURCE: Page_10]

228   dSOC/dI remains largely an open question. Establishing quantitatively the SOC saturation [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   limit for fine particles associated organic matter and for bulk soil organic matter, if it exists, is [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   also still a debated scientific issue. [SOURCE: Page_10]

237   4. Where to store carbon? Are some soils or soil horizons better [SOURCE: Page_10]

238   targets than others? [SOURCE: Page_10]

239 [SOURCE: Page_10]

240   Increasing SOC stocks is not necessarily simple or even possible at all locations. Obviously, it [SOURCE: Page_10]

241   can be more difficult to increase C inputs at some places due to limited access to necessary [SOURCE: Page_10]

242   resources such as fertilizer or water. Using a proportion of actual SOC (4 per 1000) to name [SOURCE: Page_10]

10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

243   the so-called initiative and not an actual SOC amount, implicitly expresses the need to adapt [SOURCE: Page_11]

244   the effort to the climate-dependency of C sequestration costs, which are, for instance, much [SOURCE: Page_11]

245   higher in water-limited and hot areas than in cool moist climates. Soils that have the higher [SOURCE: Page_11]

246   carbon storage potential (i.e., that have SOC stocks well below an estimated SOC stock under [SOURCE: Page_11]

247   favourable practices) could be prioritized. They also exhibit the faster storage rate (Fig. 1). As [SOURCE: Page_11]

248   illustrated in Table 1, for a given increase in C inputs different soils can present different SOC [SOURCE: Page_11]

249   stock increases in relation with soil properties, i.e. the efficiency of the storage may vary with [SOURCE: Page_11]

250   the decay rate of organic matter (k in eqn. 1). In the examples of Table 1, a given additional [SOURCE: Page_11]

251   yearly carbon input, leads to higher additional C stocks in temperate situations compared to [SOURCE: Page_11]

252   tropical ones and in clayey soils compared to sandy ones. [SOURCE: Page_11]

253 [SOURCE: Page_11]

254   For a given soil, different horizons can be targeted. Subsoil horizons have lower SOC [SOURCE: Page_11]

255   concentration (Jobbagy and Jackson, 2000), and thus their SOC storage potential, i.e. the [SOURCE: Page_11]

256   difference between present and potential stocks, would be higher than topsoil. Furthermore, [SOURCE: Page_11]

257   the contribution of belowground inputs (roots) to SOC are much higher than aboveground [SOURCE: Page_11]

258   residues (e.g. Balesdent et al., 2017; Rasse et al., 2005; Rumpel and Kogel-Knabner, 2011). [SOURCE: Page_11]

259   An option to increase the efficiency of C inputs to the soil can therefore be to place C inputs [SOURCE: Page_11]

260   preferentially towards deep soil layers where the storage potential is large and their [SOURCE: Page_11]

261   mineralization rates may be lower. Indeed, certain soil types exhibit deep organic rich layers, [SOURCE: Page_11]

262   i.e. chernozems, vertisols and more generally black soils (Jobbagy and Jackson, 2000). These [SOURCE: Page_11]

263   are characterized by medium to fine textures and developed under grassland vegetation, i.e. [SOURCE: Page_11]

264   characterized by perennial deep rooting systems. The high potential of deep horizons for SOC [SOURCE: Page_11]

265   storage was quantified by a meta-analysis of studies tracking the incorporation of plant [SOURCE: Page_11]

266   residues into the soil profile on the basis of the natural 13C labelling technique (Balesdent et [SOURCE: Page_11]

267   al., 2017). This work showed that the global mean residence time of SOC is approximately [SOURCE: Page_11]

11 [SOURCE: Page_11]

268   four times higher in the subsoil (30-100 cm depth) than in the topsoil (0-30 cm) (Figure 3). [SOURCE: Page_12]

269   The analysis of world soil radiocarbon profiles also provides evidence for both the long-term [SOURCE: Page_12]

270   stabilization of C in deep horizons of such soils, as well as the deep incorporation of active C [SOURCE: Page_12]

271   by bioturbation and rooting (Mathieu et al., 2015). However, the balance between increased [SOURCE: Page_12]

272   OC inputs to subsoil and destabilization of pre-existing SOC at depth due to priming effects [SOURCE: Page_12]

273   remains uncertain. While the occurrence of a priming effect induced by roots and their [SOURCE: Page_12]

274   rhizodeposits has been widely demonstrated (e.g. reviews by Cheng et al., 2014; Kuzyakov, [SOURCE: Page_12]

275   2002), very few estimates of the priming are available in situ, in conditions corresponding to [SOURCE: Page_12]

276   the subsoil (e.g., Shahzad et al., 2015). Another process that warrants thorough estimation is [SOURCE: Page_12]

277   the burial of topsoil organic matter by anecic earthworms and its consequences in terms of [SOURCE: Page_12]

278   SOC storage and persistence (e.g., Arrouays and Pelissier, 1994; Don et al., 2008). [SOURCE: Page_12]

284   was found to affect SOC storage in two ways : the buried topsoil material decomposed more [SOURCE: Page_12]

305   5. Practically, how to estimate the SOC storage potential? [SOURCE: Page_13]

306 [SOURCE: Page_13]

307   The carbon storage potential of a soil may be defined as the maximum gain in soil OC stock [SOURCE: Page_13]

308   attainable under a given climate,soil type and timeline (e.g. time required to attain a new [SOURCE: Page_13]

309   stock after IPCC time period: 20 y, or a new equilibrium) (Figure 1). In the previous sections, [SOURCE: Page_13]

310   we have given some qualitative insights on how SOC stocks could be increased but no [SOURCE: Page_13]

311   quantitative indications on storage potential. As presented in Figure 1, there is a transient [SOURCE: Page_13]

312   period between the two steady-states. To quantify SOC accrual rates, one should be able to [SOURCE: Page_13]

313   determine the difference between steady-state 1 and steady-state 2, and the trajectory between [SOURCE: Page_13]

314   the two. This remains a major research question. [SOURCE: Page_13]

13 [SOURCE: Page_13]

315 [SOURCE: Page_14]

316   To estimate SOC changes, the IPCC developed a tiered approach depending on the spatial [SOURCE: Page_14]

317   scale of the project or study and the data available. Tier 1 corresponds to very large scale [SOURCE: Page_14]

318   approaches, with default SOC stocks values for eco-regions and average SOC stock changes [SOURCE: Page_14]

319   values (soil carbon change factors) for changes in land-use, management or organic input [SOURCE: Page_14]

320   applied to a given surface area over a time period of 20 years for the 0-30 cm depth (IPCC, [SOURCE: Page_14]

321   2006). The Tier 2 approach is similar but uses data specific to a state or region. For example, [SOURCE: Page_14]

322   a Tier 2 approach has been used to estimate the potential of agricultural practices to mitigate [SOURCE: Page_14]

323   GHG emissions and to store C in soils of Canada (VandenBygaart et al., 2008) and of [SOURCE: Page_14]

324   mainland France (Pellerin et al., 2013). Tier 2 SOC stock change factors are still missing for [SOURCE: Page_14]

325   many regions, soil type, and management practices, calling for meta-analyses (Söderström et [SOURCE: Page_14]

326   al., 2014). However, in some agricultural regions, agronomists have for long established [SOURCE: Page_14]

327   reference SOC stocks values (Hénin and Dupuis, 1945) that allow estimating the reachable [SOURCE: Page_14]

328   SOC content of a given soil according to the most common practices (Bayer et al., 2006; [SOURCE: Page_14]

329   Saffih-Hdadi and Mary, 2008). The Tier 3 approach, which is either based on a [SOURCE: Page_14]

330   comprehensive acquisition of field data or on the use of models, has seldom been applied at [SOURCE: Page_14]

331   large scales, although is the basis of the United States estimate of its soil related agricultural [SOURCE: Page_14]

332   GHG emissions (Milne et al., 2007; Ogle et al., 2010). Indeed, SOC dynamics models are a [SOURCE: Page_14]

333   priori good candidates to estimate the potential gain of organic carbon following land-use or [SOURCE: Page_14]

334   agricultural practices changes (to determine the new steady-state value as well as the temporal [SOURCE: Page_14]

335   trajectory to reach it). However, they require to be fully validated and properly represent land [SOURCE: Page_14]

336   use management effects relevant to the study area. [SOURCE: Page_14]

337   Another possible methodology is based on the use of available SOC stocks data at the [SOURCE: Page_14]

338   territory to the regional scale, i.e. what may be called a data-driven approach. Stolbovoy and [SOURCE: Page_14]

339   Montanarella (2008) calculated the carbon storage potential for cultivated soils in EU by [SOURCE: Page_14]

14 [SOURCE: Page_14]

340   subtracting the average present SOC stock, for each soil typological unit, from the maximum [SOURCE: Page_15]

341   SOC content observed for that soil typological unit, both numbers being obtained from the [SOURCE: Page_15]

342   1:1,000,000 European soil spatial data set (JRC, 2003). Lilly and Baggaley (2013) applied [SOURCE: Page_15]

343   this method to Scotland, i.e. at a finer resolution. The same approach could be developed at [SOURCE: Page_15]

344   the scale of small regions, provided a soil map is available as well as sufficient data on SOC [SOURCE: Page_15]

345   stocks. [SOURCE: Page_15]

346   Estimates of the storage potential of SOC with very long residence time is more difficult to [SOURCE: Page_15]

347   provide as there exists to date no method to estimate the size of the pluri-decadal/ pluri- [SOURCE: Page_15]

348   centennial persistent SOC. As a result, the only way to quantitatively estimate the potential of [SOURCE: Page_15]

349   soils to store additional SOC with residence times of decades to centuries with changes in [SOURCE: Page_15]

350   management is to use models to estimate the change in the size of pools with high residence [SOURCE: Page_15]

351   times (e.g. the Passive pool in Century model with a turnover time of 400-2000 y, (Parton et [SOURCE: Page_15]

352   al., 1987). Designing a method that allows determining the concentration in pluri-decadally [SOURCE: Page_15]

353   persistent SOC would be very helpful to progress in the estimate of SOC storage potential, [SOURCE: Page_15]

354   but the various attempts towards this goal using hydrolysis or oxidation methods have been [SOURCE: Page_15]

355   questioned (Chenu et al., 2015; Greenfield et al., 2013; Lutfalla et al., 2014). Indeed, it would [SOURCE: Page_15]

356   allow providing direct measures to assess the storage potential of various management [SOURCE: Page_15]

357   techniques. In addition, several authors have proposed estimates of SOC storage potentials [SOURCE: Page_15]

358   based on the carbon saturation deficit approach (Angers et al., 2011; Wiesmeier et al., 2014, [SOURCE: Page_15]

359   McNally et al., 2017). However, these estimates have to date neither been compared to [SOURCE: Page_15]

360   modelling ones nor to actual data. In practice, the methods available to estimate the SOC [SOURCE: Page_15]

361   storage potential of a soil are the tiered ones proposed in the IPCC methodology, and future [SOURCE: Page_15]

362   research should aim at comparing them with the carbon saturation approach. [SOURCE: Page_15]

390   No-tillage and conservation agriculture [SOURCE: Page_17]

391   Tillage is generally considered to increase SOC mineralization due to the mechanical and [SOURCE: Page_17]

392   rain-induced disruption of soil aggregates and the consequent release of CO$_2$. Therefore no- [SOURCE: Page_17]

399   Luo et al., 2010; Meurer et al., 2018; Powlson et al., 2014; Powlson et al., 2016; Virto et al., [SOURCE: Page_17]

402   al., 2018; West and Post, 2002). Hence when reducing soil tillage, there may be an additional [SOURCE: Page_17]

426   Conservation agriculture encompasses a range of cropping systems that propose alternative [SOURCE: Page_18]

532   Managing soil nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_22]

533   The role of nitrogen input on SOC storage and sequestration is still debated. Nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_22]

534   availability has two opposite effects on SOC stocks. On the one hand, N can increase primary [SOURCE: Page_22]

535   production and thus increase the amount of above and below ground litter added to soil, [SOURCE: Page_22]

536   which can result in increased SOC (e.g. Campbell et al., 2000). In this case, the availability of [SOURCE: Page_22]

537   nitrogen can be a limiting factor to additional SOC storage, as discussed by van Groenigen et [SOURCE: Page_22]

22 [SOURCE: Page_22]

538   al. (2017). On the other hand, increased N availability may also accelerate the rate of [SOURCE: Page_23]

539   biodegradation of both litter and indigenous soil organic matter (Melillo et al., 1982 ; Recous [SOURCE: Page_23]

540   et al., 1995) and thereby lead to enhanced loss of SOC. The balance between these processes [SOURCE: Page_23]

541   seems to depend on the ecosystem considered. In a meta-analysis, nitrogen fertilization was [SOURCE: Page_23]

542   found to reduce the rate at which SOC is declining in agricultural soils worldwide (Ladha et [SOURCE: Page_23]

543   al., 2011), i.e. it promoted SOC storage. Lu et al., (2011) observed a 3.48 % relative increase [SOURCE: Page_23]

544   of SOC stocks with N fertilization in agricultural soils in a meta-analysis with 340 paired [SOURCE: Page_23]

545   observations. However, grasslands receiving high nitrogen fertilization may have lower OC [SOURCE: Page_23]

546   content than less fertilized ones (Soussana et al., 2004). Lu et al. ( 2011) concluded from their [SOURCE: Page_23]

547   meta-analysis that N stimulation of SOC storage primarily occurred in plant pools and less in [SOURCE: Page_23]

548   soil pools. The small magnitude of the effect of N addition on SOC stocks was explained by [SOURCE: Page_23]

549   the higher stimulation of above ground biomass production than that of belowground biomass [SOURCE: Page_23]

550   while, as described previously above ground biomass contributes less to soil carbon compared [SOURCE: Page_23]

551   to below ground biomass. Furthermore, the dataset gathered by Lu et al. (2011) showed that [SOURCE: Page_23]

552   N addition stimulated soil organic matter mineralization. They suggested that earth system [SOURCE: Page_23]

553   models need to treat soil OC inputs from aboveground and belowground sources differentially [SOURCE: Page_23]

554   for soil C storage in response to N deposition and fertilization. [SOURCE: Page_23]

555 [SOURCE: Page_23]

556   Regarding stabilization of organic matter, obviously, old SOM is N-rich, and soil [SOURCE: Page_23]

557   proteinaceous material belong to pools of SOM with decadal or centennial turnover times [SOURCE: Page_23]

558   (Bol et al., 2009). The role of proteinaceous compounds on SOM sorption and stabilization on [SOURCE: Page_23]

559   the long term has been hypothesised (Kleber et al., 2007), but the quantitative impact of N [SOURCE: Page_23]

560   input on the long term C stabilization still requires quantitative assessments. Kirkby et al. [SOURCE: Page_23]

561   (2014) demonstrated that the “stable” organic C pool, defined in their study as the SOC fine [SOURCE: Page_23]

562   fraction (< 0.4 mm) was increased with N additions, due to two processes: more fine-sized [SOURCE: Page_23]

23 [SOURCE: Page_23]

563   SOC was formed from the added fresh organic matter, i.e. there was more litter [SOURCE: Page_24]

564   decomposition, but also less old organic matter was lost, i.e. less priming effect occurred (less [SOURCE: Page_24]

565   microbial nutrient mining) (Kirkby et al., 2014). Indeed, microorganisms were shown in other [SOURCE: Page_24]

566   studies to meet their nutrient requirements by mining N from stable SOM by inducing a [SOURCE: Page_24]

567   priming effect (Chen et al., 2014; Derrien et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_24]

568   Assessing the role of nitrogen on SOC storage, and the same comment applies to phosphorus, [SOURCE: Page_24]

569   requires research differentiating its effect on primary production and its allocation between [SOURCE: Page_24]

570   above and below ground biomass, litter decomposition, priming effect and long term SOC [SOURCE: Page_24]

571   stabilization. [SOURCE: Page_24]

572 [SOURCE: Page_24]

575   Plant secondary metabolites [SOURCE: Page_24]

576   Northup et al. (1998) illustrated the production of polyphenol-rich litters as an example of [SOURCE: Page_24]

577   fitness, enhancing ecosystem productivity through several mechanisms, including the [SOURCE: Page_24]

579   and soil organic matter. The effect of plant secondary metabolites on biotic regulation of [SOURCE: Page_24]

581 [SOURCE: Page_24]

582   Mineral amendments [SOURCE: Page_24]

583   Fine cristallized-minerals are undoubtedly considered as the first factor of C stabilization in [SOURCE: Page_24]

584   soils (Mathieu et al., 2015). Extremely high SOC stabilization is found in andosols (Torn et [SOURCE: Page_24]

585   al., 1997) and is explained by the presence of very-fine aluminosilicates called allophanes, [SOURCE: Page_24]

586   which are derived from volcanic glasses. Adding fine minerals to soils is not a common [SOURCE: Page_24]

587   management practice. Although some soils have been ameliorated for a long time by addition [SOURCE: Page_24]

24 [SOURCE: Page_24]

588   of clay-rich minerals (e.g., marls) the effects of this management practice on CO$_2$ balance [SOURCE: Page_25]

589   remains questionable due to the large amounts needed and the related energy consumption to [SOURCE: Page_25]

590   transport them. Basile-Doelsch et al. (2015) developed a new paradigm of co-stabilization of [SOURCE: Page_25]

591   OM with the poorly-crystallized minerals that result from the alteration of primary minerals. [SOURCE: Page_25]

592   In the light of this work, alumino-silicates such as those present in basalt rocks, which are [SOURCE: Page_25]

593   sometimes used as amendments of base-poor soils (Gilmann et al., 2002), or clay refuses from [SOURCE: Page_25]

594   bauxite processing (Churchman et al., 2014) could be tested as OM stabilizers. [SOURCE: Page_25]

595 [SOURCE: Page_25]

596   Manipulating microbial physiology. [SOURCE: Page_25]

597   Several organic cropping systems, characterized by a diversified rotation including legume [SOURCE: Page_25]

598   cover crops, exhibited similar or higher SOC stocks than their conventional counterparts, [SOURCE: Page_25]

599   while fresh OC inputs to soil were not higher and tillage was more frequent (Autret et al., [SOURCE: Page_25]

600   2016; Gregorich et al., 2001; Syswerda et al., 2011) (a process not represented in Figure 4). [SOURCE: Page_25]

601   Kallenbach et al. (2015) recently showed that in an organic cropping system, soil [SOURCE: Page_25]

602   microorganisms had a higher carbon use efficiency and higher growth rates than under the [SOURCE: Page_25]

603   reference conventional system. As suggested by the authors, this should result in more [SOURCE: Page_25]

604   microbial necromass being formed per unit of C input. Microbial necromass represents a [SOURCE: Page_25]

605   significant fraction of soil organic matter and a major constituent of SOM stabilized in the [SOURCE: Page_25]

606   long term (Cotrufo et al., 2013; Grandy and Neff, 2008), which would explain the increased [SOURCE: Page_25]

607   or preserved SOC stocks cited above. The carbon use efficiency depends on the type of [SOURCE: Page_25]

608   microorganisms (i.e. fungi and actinomycetes vs bacteria, oligotrophs vs copiotrophs), and on [SOURCE: Page_25]

609   the quality of organic inputs (compound providing more available energy and with lower C:N [SOURCE: Page_25]

610   ratio are assimilated with a higher carbon use efficiency). The effects of cropping practices [SOURCE: Page_25]

611   such as cover crops, reduced fertilization, or organic fertilization on the carbon use efficiency [SOURCE: Page_25]

25 [SOURCE: Page_25]

612   of microorganisms remain to be studied as well as the consequences of such changes in terms [SOURCE: Page_26]

613   of SOC stocks. [SOURCE: Page_26]

614 [SOURCE: Page_26]

615   Managing soil biodiversity [SOURCE: Page_26]

616   A range of studies have shown that increasing the diversity of soil organisms and plants can [SOURCE: Page_26]

617   result in greater SOC stocks and more stabilized organic carbon. Plant diversity increases [SOURCE: Page_26]

618   SOC stocks and the magnitude of SOC storage is related to the abundance of fine roots [SOURCE: Page_26]

619   (Lange et al., 2015; Steinbeiss et al., 2008). Regarding the effects of earthworms on SOC [SOURCE: Page_26]

620   stocks, very contrasted results have been observed (Blouin et al., 2013) and a meta-analysis [SOURCE: Page_26]

621   showed that, in the short term, they increase SOC mineralization (Lubbers et al., 2013). In [SOURCE: Page_26]

622   fact, earthworms would have two opposite effects on SOC stocks, at different time scales. [SOURCE: Page_26]

623   They simultaneously increase the mineralization of both fresh and old soil organic matter (by [SOURCE: Page_26]

624   fragmentation, by their own respiration and their stimulation of soil microorganisms), and [SOURCE: Page_26]

625   incorporate fresh residues into aggregates, i.e., their casts (e.g. (Bossuyt et al., 2005)) and [SOURCE: Page_26]

626   bury carbon at depth (e.g., (Schon et al., 2015) which would increase their stabilization. The [SOURCE: Page_26]

627   simultaneous occurrence of these processes that impact SOC at different time scales has been [SOURCE: Page_26]

628   demonstrated in incubation experiments (Lubbers et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_26]

629   A similar debate has taken place concerning mycorrhizae (Verbruggen et al., 2013), which [SOURCE: Page_26]

630   were found to increase the mineralization of litter (Cheng et al., 2012) in the short term, while [SOURCE: Page_26]

631   increasing soil aggregation and thus presumably the stabilization of organic matter in the long [SOURCE: Page_26]

632   term (Verbruggen et al., 2016), with opposite effects on SOC stocks depending on the time [SOURCE: Page_26]

633   scale of observation and the relative importance of the two processes (Verbruggen et al., [SOURCE: Page_26]

634   2013). These results, along with the demonstration that soil organic matter with decadal [SOURCE: Page_26]

635   residence time is mostly microbial (microbial necromass and metabolites, (Liang and Balser, [SOURCE: Page_26]

636   2008; Miltner et al., 2012), suggest that while increasing the abundance and diversity of [SOURCE: Page_26]

26 [SOURCE: Page_26]

637   decomposers will increase decomposition rates of organic matter, it may result in increased [SOURCE: Page_27]

638   SOC stocks, and/or in a higher proportion of the SOC having long residence times. A recent [SOURCE: Page_27]

639   study by Sanderman et al. (2017) shows that increased productivity due to different rotations [SOURCE: Page_27]

640   in a long term experiment was associated with increased SOC stocks after 40 years, but [SOURCE: Page_27]

641   decreased turnover time of SOC, which supports the idea that increased microbial activity [SOURCE: Page_27]

642   promotes carbon storage. [SOURCE: Page_27]

643 [SOURCE: Page_27]

644   These findings call for further research on the effects of agricultural practices that have [SOURCE: Page_27]

645   positive effects on the abundance and diversity of soil organisms in order to analyse the [SOURCE: Page_27]

646   consequences on SOC stabilization. SOC dynamics in intensive cropping systems could be [SOURCE: Page_27]

647   revisited: such systems may have higher C inputs to the soil than natural ecosystems, thanks [SOURCE: Page_27]

648   to their very high productivity and the return of crop remains to the soil, but lower soil C [SOURCE: Page_27]

649   content. The much higher decay rates of SOM in cropped systems (e.g. (Arrouays et al., [SOURCE: Page_27]

650   1995) are not fully explained by tillage nor N inputs. These intensive cropping systems are [SOURCE: Page_27]

651   also characterized by decreased soil biodiversity, especially mycorrhizal and invertebrates, [SOURCE: Page_27]

652   due to the effects of the concurrent plant diversity reduction (monocultures or simple [SOURCE: Page_27]

653   rotations) and pesticides use. However, when comparing different cropping systems or land [SOURCE: Page_27]

654   uses it is difficult to disentangle the effects of fresh OC inputs to soil from that of modified [SOURCE: Page_27]

655   trophic network and decomposers communities or modified environmental conditions. [SOURCE: Page_27]

## 657   7. PERSPECTIVE

658 [SOURCE: Page_27]

659   The vision emerging from recent studies on soil organic matter dynamics and on the [SOURCE: Page_27]

660   processes explaining its persistence show that there is still a need to appraise or reappraise [SOURCE: Page_27]

27 [SOURCE: Page_27]

661   and understand the effects of cropping systems and practices on SOC storage and [SOURCE: Page_28]

662   sequestration, and that there is potential for innovation. Long term experiments, which are [SOURCE: Page_28]

663   indispensable, cannot however cover the huge diversity of management options available to [SOURCE: Page_28]

664   farmers. A participatory approach is then needed, which faces the difficulty to measure [SOURCE: Page_28]

665   reliably SOC stock changes at the field scale over only a few years, a time scale relevant to [SOURCE: Page_28]

666   the farmer (Perez et al., 2007). To this regard, since the residence time of SOM under the [SOURCE: Page_28]

667   tropics is ca. four times shorter than under temperate climates, and the effects of management [SOURCE: Page_28]

668   thus measurable much faster, medium-term experiments in tropical countries could provide [SOURCE: Page_28]

669   results of general interest even concerning colder climates, and should be clearly encouraged [SOURCE: Page_28]

670   (Milne et al., 2016). Besides, as mentioned in the introduction, the selection of management [SOURCE: Page_28]

671   practices to increase SOC stocks cannot be made solely on criteria based on the potential SOC [SOURCE: Page_28]

672   storage or sequestration. An increase in NPP can be obtained through irrigation or higher [SOURCE: Page_28]

673   addition of mineral fertilizers, which may compromise the sustainability of resources (water [SOURCE: Page_28]

674   use) and have adverse environmental impacts (altered water quality due to nitrate leaching, a [SOURCE: Page_28]

675   negative GHG balance, the increase in soil OC being offset by increased N2O and CH$_4$ [SOURCE: Page_28]

676   emissions (e.g. Schultz et al., 2009). SOC sequestration in agricultural soils must thus be [SOURCE: Page_28]

677   envisioned in a much broader perspective than considering only the global carbon cycle. [SOURCE: Page_28]

678   Furthermore, the feasibility of SOC storing management practices may be limited, such as for [SOURCE: Page_28]

679   instance, increasing crop residues return to the soil can be socially and economically very [SOURCE: Page_28]

680   difficult (e.g., (Naudin et al., 2011). While a process-oriented approach shows the need to [SOURCE: Page_28]

681   reappraise or test a wide range of practices for their ability to sequester carbon in soils and for [SOURCE: Page_28]

682   the permanence and vulnerability of the storage, a much broader approach of these practices, [SOURCE: Page_28]

683   including environmental, agricultural, social and economic evaluations, is also needed to [SOURCE: Page_28]

684   ensure a sustainable management of agricultural soils. [SOURCE: Page_28]

28 [SOURCE: Page_28]

685   The 4 per 1000 initiative promotes carbon storage in soils. Its Scientific and Technical [SOURCE: Page_29]

686   Committee made it clear that the aim is to increase SOC stocks compared to business as [SOURCE: Page_29]

687   usual, with no numerical target value (www.4p^1000.org). The initiative remains very [SOURCE: Page_29]

688   ambitious, with its threefold objective of contributing to food security, to climate change [SOURCE: Page_29]

689   adaptation and to mitigation of climate change and with its commitment to the UN [SOURCE: Page_29]

690   Sustainable Development Goals. It has the merit of challenging the scientific community, and [SOURCE: Page_29]

691   in particular the soil organic matter scientific community, asking to clarify concepts and [SOURCE: Page_29]

692   vocabulary and make them known broadly, asking for quantitative estimates of the current [SOURCE: Page_29]

693   state of soil organic resources and of SOC storage potential in a wide variety of pedoclimatic [SOURCE: Page_29]

694   contexts, requiring to position SOC storage questions in a much broader perspective, and [SOURCE: Page_29]

695   revealing gaps in knowledge that need to be explored. The 4 per 1000 initiative is thereby [SOURCE: Page_29]

696   very stimulating for research. [SOURCE: Page_29]
