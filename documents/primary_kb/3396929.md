1      The dynamic of crop-livestock systems in the Mediterranean and future prospective at local level: [SOURCE: Page_1]

2                     A comparative analysis for South and North Mediterranean systems [SOURCE: Page_1]

3    Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

4    Mediterranean livestock farming systems have evolved to adapt to current and future pressures, including [SOURCE: Page_1]

5    strong demographic growth and urbanization in the coastal zone, greater competition for land and water, [SOURCE: Page_1]

6    and a big shift in the hinterland where farming activities are hardly maintained. We aim to explore future [SOURCE: Page_1]

7    pathways for integrated crop-livestock systems in South and North Mediterranean countries to identify [SOURCE: Page_1]

8    potential sustainable increases in efficiency and adaptability of resource utilization. The research was [SOURCE: Page_1]

9    conducted in three countries, Egypt, France and Morocco, through case studies in a gradient of socio- [SOURCE: Page_1]

10    ecological contexts, from favourable (plains and irrigated lands) to harsher ones (mountains, rain-fed [SOURCE: Page_1]

11    areas). We mobilized farm surveys and monitoring, open-ended interviews, databases and previous studies. [SOURCE: Page_1]

12    Based on a transversal analysis at the local level, we identified two main trends and five archetypical [SOURCE: Page_1]

13    systems: (1) a centrifugal trend of specialization, towards cash crops or dairy herds in favourable areas, [SOURCE: Page_1]

14    and pastoral system for meat production in harsher environments, and (2) a centripetal trend of [SOURCE: Page_1]

15    diversification based on mixed crop-livestock systems in irrigation areas and agro-pastoral livestock-crop [SOURCE: Page_1]

16    systems in intermediate rain-fed areas. The analysis showed an overwhelming antagonism between social [SOURCE: Page_1]

17    vulnerability and ecological efficiency. Crop and livestock integration reduced the risk of biodiversity loss [SOURCE: Page_1]

18    and low environmental efficiency observed in specialized systems, but mixed systems were more socially [SOURCE: Page_1]

19    vulnerable. Those results call for dedicated rural development policies that favor the diversification as a [SOURCE: Page_1]

20    lever of sustainable development but taking into account the land fragmentation and developing higher [SOURCE: Page_1]

21    value added products chains. Taking advantage of spatial mobility abilities of livestock farming at the [SOURCE: Page_1]

22    regional level, promoting collective actions must be encouraged to allow a wider range of livestock [SOURCE: Page_1]

23    farmers in the hinterlands to live from their activities. [SOURCE: Page_1]

28 [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   Over the last several decades, the Mediterranean agriculture systems have faced increasing pressures that [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   include strong demographic growth, urbanization, increasing demand for animal products (especially in [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   southern regions), a demand for safer animal products, and high competition for land and water. In this [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   context, pressure on biomass to feed animals raises many challenges and sometimes competition in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   trade-offs of the use of resources (land, water, and nutrients) that can affect the sustainable development of [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   these systems (Dixon et al., 2010). Meanwhile, the synergies between cropping and livestock husbandry [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   offer many opportunities for a sustainable increase in production, notably by raising productivity and [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   improving resource use efficiency for both households and territories (Herrero, 2010). For example, Sraïri [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   et al. (2011) showed that feeding system and agricultural practices improve water use efficiency in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   crop-livestock systems in Morocco. Livestock activities are also recognized for their multiple roles in [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   reducing vulnerability in fragile environments (Ashley, 1999; Alary et al., 2015; 2018) and their roles in [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   diversification and intensification (Duteurtre and Faye, 2009). However, the main recent scientific [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   assessments (IAASTD, 2009; IPCC, 2007) provide evidence of the difficulties of capturing the complex [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   biological, social, and economic dynamics of the challenges likely to confront future crop and livestock [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   production and their integration. [SOURCE: Page_2]

44           All over the world and especially in the Mediterranean basin, livestock farming systems have [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   experienced important changes due to the extension of irrigation schemes and the social and political [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   changes that have affected livestock management (settlement, mobility, and transhumance), land tenure [SOURCE: Page_2]

47   and land use, and the sustainability of whole production systems. Although the South Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_2]

48   countries show an increasing demand for animal products that is linked to the demographic growth and [SOURCE: Page_2]

49   emergence of a medium social class, North Mediterranean countries are experiencing a stagnating [SOURCE: Page_2]

50   consumption of animal products of local origin (especially sheep meat). Over the last two decades, the [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

51   North Mediterranean trends have been more driven by the public agricultural policies at the production [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   level and, more recently, by the new environmental preoccupations embedded in the new supports (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   European agro-environmental measurements - AEM -).These trends endanger ecological equilibrium and [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   socio-economic viability in these zones: the intensification in the South raises the question of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   sustainable use of natural resources (soil and water), and the agricultural decline trend in the North [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   threatens landscapes, biodiversity, and the social life in its hinterlands. [SOURCE: Page_3]

57           Finally, important cultural food changes linked to urban expansion and new life conditions are [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   observed, as is increasing demand for “safe” and “ecological” or even organic products. These dynamics [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   constitute new opportunities for agro-ecological systems in the Mediterranean zone, with potential [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   pathways for livestock development. More generally, livestock has always played an important role in [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   adding value to the resources in these environments, but high competition in animal products at the [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   international level reduces profitability, while land access and comparative valorisation prospects at the [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   regional level threaten the social and environmental sustainability of these systems and their future. This [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   highlights future challenges concerning the efficiency of these systems and their contribution to food [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   security. [SOURCE: Page_3]

66           In this direction, one of the key elements for developing a sustainable crop-livestock system is to [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   promote intensification methods to produce more food using less land, water, and other resources [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   (Matson et al., 1997). This concept that integrates ecological and economic efficiency was highlighted at [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   Earth Summit 1992 by the World Business Council for Sustainable Development (WBCSD), as reviewed [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   by Camarero et al. (2013). OECD (2000) has called eco-efficiency the efficiency with which ecological [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   resources are used to meet human needs. It is approached by a ratio of an output (i.e., the value of products [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   and services produced) divided by the input used (that measure the environmental pressures). This ratio [SOURCE: Page_3]

73   can be assessed at different scales, from the firm to a sector, or even a local economy. From the end of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   2000s, the concept of ecological intensification focused more on sustainable processes in agriculture. [SOURCE: Page_3]

75   Ecological intensification is achieved by considering the functioning and dynamic of ecosystems, the [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

76   profitability and feasibility of technical options, and the social and technical viability of the new practices [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   in liaison with the social and environmental system (CIRAD, 2010). This integrates the interactions [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   between the actors, their activities, the environment and society. However, farm systems in the [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   Mediterranean are diverse in their links with the socio-ecological environments. The Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   livestock farming systems need therefore to adapt multiple and complex changes related to the recent [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   history of the area and the predicted events in the near future (climate change, urbanization). This led us to [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   pay special attention to the third pillar of sustainability, i.e., social sustainability, and address it, crossing [SOURCE: Page_4]

316   4. Attempt of prospective for future livestock systems in Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_13]

317 [SOURCE: Page_13]

318           From the three country case studies, we can identify five common farming systems (FS) pathways [SOURCE: Page_13]

319   in these Mediterranean contexts. First, we observe three trends of specialization of farming systems due [SOURCE: Page_13]

320   mainly to policy incentives and infrastructure development. The first trend concerns the development of [SOURCE: Page_13]

321   high-value cash crop-specialized systems in the favourable zones, mainly irrigation zones. This [SOURCE: Page_13]

322   specialization is largely driven by labour productivity and social valorisation linked with the educational [SOURCE: Page_13]

323   level or aspirations in terms of living conditions. This trend extends in all the plains of the coastal line in [SOURCE: Page_13]

13 [SOURCE: Page_13]

324   the south of France and Morocco and in the recent NRL in Egypt. It has also been encouraged by regional [SOURCE: Page_14]

327   specialized system which is developing in the favourable zones of Morocco and Egypt. This specialization [SOURCE: Page_14]

328   concerns mainly large dairy farms and some smallholder farms that have been boosted, and sometimes [SOURCE: Page_14]

329   structured, by the milk agro-industrial sector through a secured farm gate milk price and regular sales of [SOURCE: Page_14]

330   calves. The third and last trend is related to meat specialized systems in vulnerable zones like the [SOURCE: Page_14]

331   mountainous areas in France or the arid and semi-arid zones in Morocco or Egypt. This specialization [SOURCE: Page_14]

388       5. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_16]

389           Our main hypothesis of this transversal research work was that the integration of crop-livestock [SOURCE: Page_16]

390   activities, at the farm or regional level, is a pillar of sustainability of the farming system mainly in terms of [SOURCE: Page_16]

391   environmental efficiency. However, in the South Mediterranean, the integrated crop-livestock systems [SOURCE: Page_16]

392   remain the only option for small-scale farms in rain-fed and irrigated zones due to limited assets, either [SOURCE: Page_16]

393   land or market access, and given their overwhelming number of workers. The weak access to market [SOURCE: Page_16]

394   constitutes a major handicap for new settlers in the recently reclaimed lands of Egypt located more than [SOURCE: Page_16]

395   100 km from large cities such as Cairo and Alexandria. The integrated crop-livestock systems, however, [SOURCE: Page_16]

396   still ensure the survival of many families and workers, particularly in contexts of significant risks, like [SOURCE: Page_16]

397   prices volatility and climate hazards. In fact, in the South Mediterranean context, where irrigation water [SOURCE: Page_16]

16 [SOURCE: Page_16]

398   may not be sufficient to cover all crop needs, in case of acute drought, livestock remains the only source of [SOURCE: Page_17]

399   income to cover farmers’ expenses and the main security in case of major social events (like marriages, [SOURCE: Page_17]

400   funerals, hospitalizations). In addition, livestock in many situations adds value to rainfall, with almost no [SOURCE: Page_17]

401   effect on groundwater depletion (Sraïri et al., 2016), in contrast many summer crops that have a huge [SOURCE: Page_17]

402   effect on the sustainability of ground water resources (Ameur et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_17]

403           In the three countries, the compared labour productivity of crop and livestock activities explained [SOURCE: Page_17]

404   the specialization trends towards cash crops such as vineyards, tree plantations, and market gardening in [SOURCE: Page_17]

405   favourable areas like irrigation plains. Over the last two decades, this farm specialization has led to a [SOURCE: Page_17]

406   territorial specialization in France: livestock disappeared in coastal plains and valleys, and cash crops were [SOURCE: Page_17]

407   no longer present in the hinterlands. In Egypt and Morocco, mixed systems still exist in irrigation areas, in [SOURCE: Page_17]

408   combination with specialized crop farms. In those mixed systems, labour is a key factor in terms of [SOURCE: Page_17]

409   quantity, routine tasks and remuneration. At the family level, the diversion of youth from agricultural [SOURCE: Page_17]

410   activities, given its limited attractiveness due its limited revenues, can be a threat to viability; however, it is [SOURCE: Page_17]

411   not a recent phenomenon in Morocco (Bencherifa, 1993; Lubbock and Borquia, 1998). Furthermore, [SOURCE: Page_17]

412   recently, we may find new arrangements, notably the remuneration of family work or new social [SOURCE: Page_17]

413   arrangements such as farm associations and work sharing between farms to keep interest in agriculture [SOURCE: Page_17]

414   (Bencherifa, 1993). However, this may weaken the whole system in the long term, whenever the [SOURCE: Page_17]

415   equilibrium between social demands and the existing resources might collapse due to the end of state [SOURCE: Page_17]

416   technical support for farmers and the emergence of new needs such as better education services for the [SOURCE: Page_17]

417   children (Sraïri, 2005). In Egypt, we observed the highest profits of livestock activity for medium-size and [SOURCE: Page_17]

418   large farms while small mixed crop-livestock systems registered the higher return by capital invested in [SOURCE: Page_17]

419   livestock. This reflects the multiple roles of livestock in farm assets. [SOURCE: Page_17]

420           In the mixed farm systems, as in Egypt and Morocco, or in specialized livestock farms relying [SOURCE: Page_17]

421   partially on fodder and grains provided by cropland in France, the crops provided most of the feed [SOURCE: Page_17]

422   resources to herds or supply resources to get through harsh seasons. The results obtained in the three [SOURCE: Page_17]

17 [SOURCE: Page_17]

423   countries stressed also the crucial function of livestock biomass preservation in the Mediterranean context, [SOURCE: Page_18]

424   through the role of manure to improve the stock of organic matter of the soil. Using manure was a way to [SOURCE: Page_18]

425   maintain soil fertility and reduce the use of mineral fertilizers. Thus, the crop-livestock integration, [SOURCE: Page_18]

426   through feeding and manure management, decreases external inputs. Nevertheless, the practices of crop- [SOURCE: Page_18]

427   livestock integration, especially manure management, created a heavy workload. Even in farms managing [SOURCE: Page_18]

428   both crops and livestock, those activities could be not integrated, or in a one-way path only, with any [SOURCE: Page_18]

429   recycling. [SOURCE: Page_18]

430           Previous research showed that increased demographic pressure and cropping intensity favoured the [SOURCE: Page_18]

431   degree of integration of crop-livestock activities (Boserup, 1965; Ruthenberg, 1980; Pingali et al., 1987). [SOURCE: Page_18]

432   This process was fostered with market access, diffusion of technology packages, or the presence of cash [SOURCE: Page_18]

433   crops in the cropping pattern (McIntire et al., 1992). This process is still on-going in the south [SOURCE: Page_18]

434   Mediterranean countries. However, our results show that these systems,while ecologically most efficient, [SOURCE: Page_18]

435   are also socially deeply vulnerable. They are maintained in Morocco and Egypt “by default” and as [SOURCE: Page_18]

436   protection from uncertainties and risks, supported by social solidarities that substitute for lack of dedicated [SOURCE: Page_18]

437   policies. This antagonism between social vulnerability and ecological efficiency should be a major [SOURCE: Page_18]

438   consideration for rural development policies in the south to avoid the disappearance of such systems that [SOURCE: Page_18]

439   we may notice in many areas in northern Mediterranean countries. [SOURCE: Page_18]

440           Consequently, for the future, limiting micro-regional specialization processes through by [SOURCE: Page_18]

441   maintaining a diversity of systems, both crop-oriented and livestock-oriented, is at stake. This relies on [SOURCE: Page_18]

442   special supports to reduce competition between these systems, reinforcing the ability of livestock systems [SOURCE: Page_18]

443   to be perennial and reducing the common trends observed of substitution of livestock systems (mainly [SOURCE: Page_18]

444   oriented for meat production) by enlargement of crop oriented systems. This requires dedicated actions [SOURCE: Page_18]

445   confronting local feeding systems. This could rely on special incentives and development of territorial [SOURCE: Page_18]

446   food projects. Special environmental rules would reintroduce diversity in cropland occupation such as [SOURCE: Page_18]

447   reintroducing legumes in cultural successions is another way to foster a mix within farming systems. While [SOURCE: Page_18]

18 [SOURCE: Page_18]

448   we may notice that specialization at a micro-regional level results in loss of environmental efficiency for [SOURCE: Page_19]

449   areas devoted to crop systems, taking advantage of the spatial mobility abilities of livestock farming in the [SOURCE: Page_19]

450   Mediterranean is an another option to reinforce crop-livestock integration at the regional level. This [SOURCE: Page_19]

451   mobility has been traditionally operated by medium to large-size farmers (Lasseur et al, 2016). However, [SOURCE: Page_19]

452   promoting collective actions allowing a wider range of livestock farmers in the hinterlands to participate [SOURCE: Page_19]

453   should reinforce sustainability for vulnerable livestock farmers and those with limited animal wealthwhile [SOURCE: Page_19]

454   extending cropping areas. These collective actions should involve a wide array of stakeholders. [SOURCE: Page_19]

455           Nowadays, the adaptive capacity in the Mediterranean area is strongly based on regional [SOURCE: Page_19]

456   integration and diversification. These processes involve different configurations at the time and spatial [SOURCE: Page_19]

457   levels according to the agro-ecological gradient (from rain-fed to irrigated zone) but also the regional [SOURCE: Page_19]

458   policy of territory development. [SOURCE: Page_19]

459           This research has also developed a series of indicators to approach the efficiency of crop/livestock [SOURCE: Page_19]

460   systems according to a gradient of intensification and using tools ranging from livelihood and micro- [SOURCE: Page_19]

461   economic tools (for economic and social assessment) to environmental tools like network analysis. Beyond [SOURCE: Page_19]

462   these data sources and tools, the transversal analysis highlights that livestock at the interface between land [SOURCE: Page_19]

463   management (local) and livelihood diversification (family) plays a vital role in the current rural [SOURCE: Page_19]

464   sustainability, although its future will depend on the societal challenges and policy orientation between [SOURCE: Page_19]

465   employment, food security and resources management. Moreover, the function of livestock in biomass [SOURCE: Page_19]

466   management appears crucial for the medium- and long- term resilience of Mediterranean agrarian systems. [SOURCE: Page_19]

468       6. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_19]

469 [SOURCE: Page_19]

470           For small ruminant systems (sheep and goat) that are dominant in the rain-fed and mountainous [SOURCE: Page_19]

471   areas of the Mediterranean, we observed an increasing flexibility of medium and large flock systems based [SOURCE: Page_19]

472   on spatial and temporal mobility. This geographical and social expansion went hand in hand with a certain [SOURCE: Page_19]

19 [SOURCE: Page_19]

473   level of specialization. The permanence of small flock systems in the South Mediterranean countries was [SOURCE: Page_20]

474   observed, where the pillar of viability depended on the diversification of farm outputs. In the irrigation [SOURCE: Page_20]

475   areas, we found more contrasted situations between the locations according to the social and political [SOURCE: Page_20]

476   environment. The irrigated systems in the Mediterranean zones tended to specialize in high remunerative [SOURCE: Page_20]

477   crops like orchards or market gardening in the south of France or plain of Gharb in Morocco or specialized [SOURCE: Page_20]

478   fruit production in the West Delta of Egypt. Only farms with small-irrigation land area (which constituted [SOURCE: Page_20]

479   the majority in the South countries) maintained a crop-livestock diversification to secure not only their [SOURCE: Page_20]

480   source of income, but also their family's food supply and savings. [SOURCE: Page_20]

481           Facing this trend of specialization with detected environmental (and economic) risks and the [SOURCE: Page_20]

482   maintenance of a mass of small mixed crop-livestock systems (not necessarily viable, notably in terms of [SOURCE: Page_20]

483   transmissibility and reproducibility), collective regulations appear as a key-driven solution, and incentives [SOURCE: Page_20]

484   to increase resilience of the whole system should rely on maintaining contrasted pathways for farms and [SOURCE: Page_20]

485   diversity management. [SOURCE: Page_20]

486           This study shows that different pathways of development can be proposed according to policy [SOURCE: Page_20]

487   priorities or farmers' expectations. In the rain-fed areas, the results highlight that the local (territorial) [SOURCE: Page_20]

488   perception of development is often far removed from the spatial dynamics of livestock that generally [SOURCE: Page_20]

489   favour a diversity of livelihoods at the landscape level. In this context, mobility driven by agro-ecological [SOURCE: Page_20]

490   conditions and stakeholders’ networks constitutes a powerful adaptive strategy in the face of climate [SOURCE: Page_20]

491   change. Nevertheless, these networks have also favoured agricultural development (mainly through [SOURCE: Page_20]

492   irrigation in PACA or Wadi development with orchards in rain-fed Egypt). Consequently, a new integrated [SOURCE: Page_20]

493   livestock-crop model based on local fodder and concentrates is needed. This shows the capacity of [SOURCE: Page_20]

494   livestock activities to reach new agricultural areas adapting to land pressure, but also to respond to [SOURCE: Page_20]

495   emerging food demand with new lifestyles. The permanence, yet adaptability, of livestock confirm its role [SOURCE: Page_20]

496   as a security net in this type of harsh environment. In the irrigated areas, particularly in large scale [SOURCE: Page_20]

497   irrigation schemes, we observe the development of dairy or crop-specialized systems, encouraged by [SOURCE: Page_20]

20 [SOURCE: Page_20]

498   regional and national policies of rural development, but with a loss of environmental efficiency. This [SOURCE: Page_21]

499   model could be improved by taking advantage of the spatial mobility abilities of livestock farming in the [SOURCE: Page_21]

500   Mediterranean to reinforce crop/livestock integration at the regional level. This will certainly also improve [SOURCE: Page_21]

501   the value addition to several resources like rainfall and surface irrigation water (small dams, springs) at a [SOURCE: Page_21]

502   time when severe regulation should be devoted to groundwater resources to avoid amplifying rural exodus. [SOURCE: Page_21]
