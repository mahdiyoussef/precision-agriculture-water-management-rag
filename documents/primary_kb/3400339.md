1   1.     Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

2   1.1.   Background [SOURCE: Page_2]

3   Cities facing population expansion and densification amid shifting climates require [SOURCE: Page_2]

4   practicable solutions to meet biophilic, health, and safety needs of city dwellers. This can [SOURCE: Page_2]

5   begin to be accomplished by bringing nature into dense urban environments on all feasible [SOURCE: Page_2]

6   surfaces of a city’s buildings and infrastructure [1] [2] [3] [4]. After greening all available [SOURCE: Page_2]

7   horizontal surfaces, only vertical surfaces remain for leveraging, yet existing living wall [SOURCE: Page_2]

8   systems lack affordability and/or longevity [5] [6]. Their high economic cost and [SOURCE: Page_2]

9   environmental burden means living wall use is largely restricted to luxury applications and [SOURCE: Page_2]

10   greenwashing [7] [8]. [SOURCE: Page_2]

11 [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   The goal of this work is to determine the possibility of having a living wall system which is [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   durable, has the same longevity as its building, is unlimited by location and typology [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   (especially high-rises), and is more affordable than currently available systems. The [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   hypothesis asks if combining living-wall functions with the structural functions of a [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   building’s exterior will lower cost and increase longevity. The outcome sought is to push [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   living walls beyond the economic threshold limiting their proliferation to extend nature’s [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   reach into the urban milieu. [SOURCE: Page_2]

19 [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   This interdisciplinary study, of architecture, botany, and material science, shows how shifting [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   the existing living wall paradigm from an additive to a systemic model creates the potential [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   for affordable living walls. The material tested is concrete, because, after water [9], it is the [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   most widely used building material in the world, and due to its widespread use [10], [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   durability, cost, and flexibility, is currently a pragmatic choice to democratize living walls. [SOURCE: Page_2]

1 [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   However, although recyclable [11] [12], concrete is not made from renewable materials. And [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   because of its ubiquity (2014 cement production was 4,180,000,000 tons [13]), its [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   environmental impact is enormous [14] [15], which this study aims to ameliorate by [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   permitting concrete to spread nature. [SOURCE: Page_3]

29 [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   1.2.   About living walls [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   Green wall terminology is evolving. They have two categories: green façades and living walls [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   [16] [17], although extensive and intensive have been borrowed from the green roof industry [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   to describe them [18] [19] [20], these terms are best avoided to elude the confusion bred in [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   that domain. Green walls are also called vertical greening systems [18], an allusion to their [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   effect beyond surface treatment, i.e., they are complex systems with proportionate [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   consequences. For example, a living wall may require a water storage system connected to a [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   network of horizontal surfaces used to capture rainwater and a mechanical room to filter, [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   irrigate, and fertilize. In other words, much more than just a wall. [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   Living walls and green façades have the same fundamental requirements, i.e., plants’ [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   indispensable needs: daylight, water, and nutrients [21]. Living walls share the same potential [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   advantages of other green infrastructure: they can have biophilia-related health benefits [4] [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   [22] [23], capture air pollutants [24] [25] [26], diminish noise pollution [27] [28], reduce the [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   heat-island effect [29] [30], provide natural cooling [31] [32], add privacy, and promote [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   biodiversity [33] [34]. [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   Whereas green façades use climbing plants, living wall plants are distributed over the entire [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   wall [35]. The two main types of living walls are hydroponic and soil-cell systems. [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   Hydroponic systems often use a dense mat or felt-like material as a growing medium. [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   Discovered by the French botanist Patrick Blanc –the father of modern green walls – these [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   lightweight systems are wetted with nutrient-enriched water, and roots grow on and in- [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   between layers of matted or felted substrate [36]. Soil-cell systems compartmentalize the [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   growth of plants in individual cells of soil. Cells are grouped together in panels attached to a [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   frame. However, some hydroponic systems use a modular, cell-based typology, replacing soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   with horticultural growing media, e.g., rock wool or polyurethane foam. Cell-systems are [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   essentially a collection of inter-connected potted plants subject to the same challenges facing [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   potted house plants: soil compaction, drainage issues, climatic stress, and nutrient [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   replenishment [37]. However, exterior soil-cell walls also face the problem of soil loss due to [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   wind and water-driven erosion [38]. [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   Green walls can humidify and oxygenate air, and, depending on the plants, improve air [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   quality by trapping dust and absorbing pollutants like formaldehyde indoors and nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   dioxide outdoors [39] [40]. When mechanically forced, living wall systems can be designed to [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   actively filter indoor air [41], though usually its the soil filtering. [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   Both systems can face plant stress, foremost because the growth-plane is vertical (although [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   some canted cell-based systems have a more orthodox growing surface). Both hydroponic and [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   modular-cell living walls require expert design and on-going maintenance, and both are prone [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   to failure if their design and operation are not successfully synthesized [42]. Most hydroponic [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   systems and cellular living wall systems have a life expectancy [43] [44], i.e., they require [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   replacement, typically every 15–25 years [45], which adds to lifecycle costs. [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   1.3.    Living wall costs [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   Costs include initial installation’s labor and materials, ongoing maintenance, environmental [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   burden, and total lifecycle cost. Collecting this data is challenging because of input [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   variability. For example, initial costs of systems identically sourced and installed will differ [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   because price is tailored by project; no two projects are identical. Geometrical or [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   environmental differences between projects – orientation, microclimate, size, neighboring [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   buildings – will affect solar access, wind patterns, and humidity, which affects plant species [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   selection and growth, which in turn affects system efficacy and usefulness, but also initial and [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   continued maintenance costs. How costs are defined also affect the data, e.g., some living [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   walls necessitate replacing 30% of their plants in the first year [8], and depending on how the [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   project is budgeted, this expense can be considered either installation or maintenance. Despite [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   these challenges, predictable costs emerge. [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   Concerning initial and ongoing costs, living walls are the more expensive category of green [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   walls [18]. Green façade installed system costs begin around €100/m^2, but go as high as [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   €800/m^2; whereas living wall system initial costs begin about €400/m^2 and go as high as [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   €1200/m^2 [5] [6] [46]. Ongoing maintenance costs can be as low as 2-5€/m^2 for yearly [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   pruning of green screen climbing plants, and between 40-100€/m^2/year for living walls [46]. [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   The marked contrast in cost between the two green wall categories are a result of differing [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   complexities; living walls require a larger support network of: water supply, filtering, [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   collection, storage, mechanical distribution (pumps), irrigation components, fertilizer, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   maintenance for this equipment. Until cost and environmental burden are reduced, the [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   potential biophilic richness of living walls will most likely remain stunted. [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   1.4.   Objectives of the study [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   Principally, this study investigates a strategy to proliferate urban living walls. As cost is the [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   major limiting factor, this study explores combining a living wall with the building structure [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   as an approach to reduce costs and have a lifespan commensurate with its building. This study [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   determines the validity of concrete growing plants for walls and green infrastructure. The [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   initial objectives are to: create a concrete to host plants, determine its mechanical properties, [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   verify its constructability, identify candidate native plant species, and study growing plants [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   from seed. The intention of sowing seeds directly on the new concrete is to encourage the [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   plants to create their own environment, eliminating the costs of raising and transplanting [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   nursery plants and annual replacement. The final objectives are to: incorporate the new [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   concrete into a conventional wall system, develop and test the new system’s construction [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   methodology, analyze how the new concrete’s chemical composition will affect plants and [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   irrigation, test the new system outdoors for germination and perenniality, and determine the [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   new system’s cost. [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   2.     Methodology [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   The research methodology is in seven steps. Step 1 creates a new concrete to support plant life [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   (see 2.1). Step 2 verifies constructability (see 2.2). Step 3 and its sub-steps validates plant [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   growth on the new concrete (see 2.3). Step 4 verifies a new construction methodology (see [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   2.4). Step 5 analyzes the new concrete’s chemical effect on plants and irrigation (see 2.5). [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   Step 6 tests mechanical properties (see 2.6). Step 7 validates outdoor germination and [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   perenniality (see 2.7). Step 8 analyzes costs (see 2.8). [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   2.1.   Testing new concrete formulas [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   The first step required finding a mix design for living concrete. Pervious concrete was chosen [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   for its interconnected pores accommodating water percolation and plant roots. Three pervious [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   concrete mix designs were tested: pure cement, pure cement with metakaolin and limestone [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   filler, and the same but with white cement. The designs were measured for slump, density, [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   and porosity [47] [48] [49]. [SOURCE: Page_6]

118 [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   The merits of using pure cement are economy and efficiency, while metakaolin was proposed [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   for its ability to lower pH and suppress lime content in hardened concrete [50]. With [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   metakaolin natural carbonation reaction is accelerated, eventually lowering pH to ~9, whereas [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   with pure cement the initial pH is ~12-13. The choice of limestone filler is to complete the [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   original volume of pure cement [51]. The white cement option was for aesthetic reasons [52]. [SOURCE: Page_7]

124 [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   The rheology of the mix designs were adjusted to obtain sufficient coating around each [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   aggregate (stone aggregates are not used in the laboratory when studying cement rheology) [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   and a lubricating effect. Once found, this rheological state facilitates an optimized [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   configuration of stone aggregates and contact between aggregates, which in turn facilitates [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   obtaining the desired mechanical resistance and porosity. Cement rheology targets were met [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   when measured values of viscosity and spread fell within acceptable ranges of workability. [SOURCE: Page_7]

131 [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   2.2. Verify constructability [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   Once met, an A$_1$-sized wall was vertically cast using standard formwork. The merit of [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   vertically cast-in-place pervious concrete – as opposed to pre-fabricated – is it forgoes [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   specialized labor or equipment, expanding market potential beyond prefabrication. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   formula was considered validated if the cast pervious concrete “wall” was homogenous in [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   appearance, permeable (laid flat, tested with running water), without cavities, and without [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   compacting flaws. Once the construction methodology had been validated the mix designs’ [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   mechanical characteristics were tested to determine the concrete’s strength by measuring [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   compression, density, permeability and porosity. [SOURCE: Page_7]

279   The mix designs, now patented [67] [68] [69] [70] [71], were tested for slump, density and [SOURCE: Page_14]

280   porosity. Measuring pervious concrete slump was found to be inutile: the mix is either too [SOURCE: Page_14]

281   stiff (zero slump) or too liquid (maximum slump), relative to aggregate size. [SOURCE: Page_14]

282 [SOURCE: Page_14]

283   Vibrating pervious concrete, or otherwise manipulating its compaction, was found to be [SOURCE: Page_14]

284   unrealistic; picking is workable only for small castings. Concrete immersion (needle) [SOURCE: Page_14]

288   risk: a damming effect via slurry pooling, heterogeneous vibration – consequently non- [SOURCE: Page_14]

289   uniform porosity, and the premium cost of uncommon construction practices [72]. [SOURCE: Page_14]

579   expensive contemporary systems, validating the approach of rethinking the additive living [SOURCE: Page_32]

580   wall paradigm to encourage their proliferation. [SOURCE: Page_32]

581   5.     Discussion [SOURCE: Page_32]

582   The study resulted in the development of a new living material for interior and exterior [SOURCE: Page_32]

583   architectural and green infrastructural applications. And the first cast-in-place living wall: the [SOURCE: Page_32]

584   innovation of vertically-cast pervious concrete. The trials also validate growing living wall [SOURCE: Page_32]

585   plants from seed in-situ. The interior and exterior trials validated the repeatability of [SOURCE: Page_32]

586   germination, formulas, and construction methodologies of a concrete living wall. Still [SOURCE: Page_32]

31 [SOURCE: Page_32]

587   flourishing after three years, the interior trials show the perenniality of living concrete given [SOURCE: Page_33]

588   the right conditions. [SOURCE: Page_33]

589   The interior trials demonstrate living wall plants survive without fertilizer – and soil, since [SOURCE: Page_33]

590   plants grow in Rockwool, which lacks the minerals in the soil-substrate. Likewise, no [SOURCE: Page_33]

591   fertilizer was used on the exterior trials; further tests are needed to confirm sustainability. [SOURCE: Page_33]

592   The exterior trials establish the likelihood of large-scale applications, but require further study [SOURCE: Page_33]

593   to produce the lush aesthetic of interior trials. Perenniality was ascertained, but prior to [SOURCE: Page_33]

594   commercialization long-term testing is necessary to master plant development and [SOURCE: Page_33]

595   maintenance protocols. For example, increasing the pervious layer’s width - partially filled [SOURCE: Page_33]

596   by C25 concrete – or aggregate diameter (to resemble the porosity available to roots in the [SOURCE: Page_33]

597   unencumbered interior test-blocks) could improve plant development. [SOURCE: Page_33]

598   Cardinal orientation notwithstanding, all four exterior walls hosted plant growth, implying [SOURCE: Page_33]

599   any orientation can support a concrete living wall, given the right environmental conditions. [SOURCE: Page_33]

600   Only grass was unaffected by location. Biodiversity may hold the key to ensure plant [SOURCE: Page_33]

601   perenniality irrespective of orientation. A minimum number should be set for species in a [SOURCE: Page_33]

602   substrate without grasses; 30 is recommended. This number will ensure plants will install [SOURCE: Page_33]

603   themselves in all wall zones regardless of orientation or micro-environment, e.g., wind [SOURCE: Page_33]

604   turbulence-driven moisture variances. [SOURCE: Page_33]

605   Several watering lessons were learned. A concrete living wall consumption can average 1 [SOURCE: Page_33]

606   liter/m^2/day. More water is needed for initial germination and spring regeneration. [SOURCE: Page_33]

607   Furthermore, homogenous plant coverage requires running irrigation until moistening the [SOURCE: Page_33]

608   entire substrate, thus defining the event’s duration. If internal irrigation moistens completely, [SOURCE: Page_33]

609   supplemental watering is unnecessary for germination. Evidently, the height between the [SOURCE: Page_33]

32 [SOURCE: Page_33]

610   irrigation pipe and collection point will dictate the event’s duration. Future tests are [SOURCE: Page_34]

611   recommended to validate usage data in closed-circuit irrigation (recycled water). [SOURCE: Page_34]

612   Concrete living wall durability and structural potential foretell its use for green infrastructure, [SOURCE: Page_34]

613   but also portent its weight disadvantage compared to contemporary additive living walls, such [SOURCE: Page_34]

614   as Patrick Blanc’s lightweight felt system or heavier-weight modular-cell systems. For [SOURCE: Page_34]

615   example with retrofitting. The proposed system is for new construction only, limiting its use. [SOURCE: Page_34]

616   However, alternative solutions for retrofitting are detailed in the dissertation on this subject [SOURCE: Page_34]

617   [79]. In these cases, weight may become an adverse factor: a 5 cm layer of living concrete [SOURCE: Page_34]

618   weighs nearly 88 kg/m^2. [SOURCE: Page_34]

619   Conversely, concrete living walls would share benefits of green walls and have advantages [SOURCE: Page_34]

620   over existing living wall systems. The primary advantage is the likelihood to be half the cost. [SOURCE: Page_34]

621   Reasons for lower cost include: concrete’s durability that gives it a life cycle equal to its [SOURCE: Page_34]

622   building; growing plants from seeds in-situ bears considerable savings over growing plants in [SOURCE: Page_34]

623   a nursery; and its integration into the building’s exterior load-bearing wall. This means [SOURCE: Page_34]

624   subtracting the some of the building’s exterior structure from the concrete living wall’s initial [SOURCE: Page_34]

625   costs. No fertigation also predicts a cost – and environmental – advantage, foreshadowing a [SOURCE: Page_34]

626   living wall that allows local plants to create their own environment. This prospective to adapt [SOURCE: Page_34]

627   to its surroundings could prove valuable to cities confronting shifting climates. [SOURCE: Page_34]

628   Consideration was given to the system’s potential to host pests such as mosquitos, which in [SOURCE: Page_34]

629   turn could harbor Vector borne diseases, e.g., the Zika virus in Europe [80]. The [SOURCE: Page_34]

630   interconnected pores of the living concrete are unconnected to outside air by the seeded- [SOURCE: Page_34]

631   substrate. Thus, exposure is limited to water distribution and collection points. Here, covers, [SOURCE: Page_34]

33 [SOURCE: Page_34]

632   insect screens, and adequately pitched collection trays are necessary to allay access to [SOURCE: Page_35]

633   irrigation or standing water. [SOURCE: Page_35]

634   The ongoing financial cost of yearly maintenance was not calculated. No regular maintenance [SOURCE: Page_35]

635   was executed during the study, only daily observations, except for the incidents discussed in [SOURCE: Page_35]

636   section 3.7.3. Growing plants from seed in situ is meant to eliminate the need to replace plants [SOURCE: Page_35]

637   and allow the wall to evolve naturally with relatively little maintenance, the benefit of having [SOURCE: Page_35]

638   plants create their own environment. However, the presence of self-installing tree seedlings [SOURCE: Page_35]

639   show regular inspection and weeding is necessary to prevent damage to the pervious concrete [SOURCE: Page_35]

640   structure. Furthermore, the irrigation system will require the regular maintenance obligatory [SOURCE: Page_35]

641   to all living walls – minus fertigation expenses. Yearly ongoing operation and maintenance [SOURCE: Page_35]

642   costs are anticipated to be between the ranges of green screens and living walls noted in [SOURCE: Page_35]

643   section 1.3. [SOURCE: Page_35]

644   If green walls are to play a positive role in confronting the three most pressing challenges [SOURCE: Page_35]

645   facing contemporary cities – population densification, shifting climates, and access to nature – [SOURCE: Page_35]

646   they are obliged to satisfy the requirements of a sustainable city. They must contribute to [SOURCE: Page_35]

647   satisfying the social, environmental, and economic needs of their urban environments, [SOURCE: Page_35]

648   including durability and affordability. The impact of green walls on a city can be multi- [SOURCE: Page_35]

649   faceted and multi-scalar [81], but only if system innovation leans toward their [SOURCE: Page_35]

650   democratization. Further tests on the concept’s perenniality are required before [SOURCE: Page_35]

651   commercialization, but the initial results show rethinking living wall design holds potential to [SOURCE: Page_35]

652   have broad contextual impact on the urban hardscape. [SOURCE: Page_35]
