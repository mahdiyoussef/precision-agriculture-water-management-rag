Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

Global discourses have advocated women’s empowerment as a means to enhance food [SOURCE: Page_3]

security. Our objective was to critically review the causal linkages between women’s [SOURCE: Page_3]

empowerment and food availability and access. We relied on mixed methods and a cross- [SOURCE: Page_3]

country analysis, using household survey data from Bangladesh, Nepal and Tajikistan and [SOURCE: Page_3]

qualitative data from Nepal. The quantitative analysis highlights the diversity of patterns [SOURCE: Page_3]

linking empowerment and food security indicators and the roles socio-economic determinants [SOURCE: Page_3]

play in shaping these patterns across countries. The qualitative analysis further stresses the [SOURCE: Page_3]

need for a truly intersectional approach in food security programmes that supports challenging [SOURCE: Page_3]

the structural barriers that keep marginalised men and women food insecure. Lastly, our [SOURCE: Page_3]

findings call for informing standardised measures of empowerment with an assessment of [SOURCE: Page_3]

local meanings and values. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Keywords: food security; women’s empowerment; gender; intersectionality; multi-country [SOURCE: Page_3]

analysis; mixed-methods study [SOURCE: Page_3]

1            1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

2   Global food security debates have framed gender equality as an effective tool to support [SOURCE: Page_4]

3   agricultural and economic growth and enhance food security (ADB, 2013; Duflo, 2012) – a [SOURCE: Page_4]

4   narrative that particularly resonates with increasing patterns of male out-migration and the so- [SOURCE: Page_4]

5   called ‘feminisation of agriculture’. Many studies have evidenced strong linkages between [SOURCE: Page_4]

6   gender inequality and food insecurity, notably in the case of poor and marginalised women [SOURCE: Page_4]

7   who have less access than men to critical resources for agricultural livelihoods (Quisumbing [SOURCE: Page_4]

8   and Pandolfelli, 2010) or in respect to how women’s disempowerment affects maternal and [SOURCE: Page_4]

9   child nutrition (Malapit et al., 2015a; Malapit and Quisumbing, 2015; Nisbett et al., in press; [SOURCE: Page_4]

10   Sraboni et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_4]

11   Food security discourses envision that if women have similar access to productive resources [SOURCE: Page_4]

12   as men, agricultural productivity will increase, thereby contributing to enhanced food [SOURCE: Page_4]

13   security, e.g. “Increasing women’s access to land, livestock, education, financial services, [SOURCE: Page_4]

14   extension, technology and rural employment would boost their productivity and generate [SOURCE: Page_4]

15   gains in terms of agricultural production, food security, economic growth and social welfare” [SOURCE: Page_4]

25   Yet the operationalization of this narrative linking women’s empowerment through access to [SOURCE: Page_5]

26   productive resources to increased food security has so far largely failed to deliver on its [SOURCE: Page_5]

27   promises, both in terms of actual investments dedicated to women’s empowerment, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

28   resulting outcomes on gender equality and enhanced food security (Galie and Kantor, 2016). [SOURCE: Page_5]

29   Some scholars remark that such policy discourse promotes a narrow and apolitical vision of [SOURCE: Page_5]

30   women’s empowerment (Galie and Kantor, 2016), which is symptomatic of development [SOURCE: Page_5]

31   programmes in general. The latter have often focused on economic empowerment without [SOURCE: Page_5]

32   necessarily addressing the norms, institutions and broader political economic structures that [SOURCE: Page_5]

33   (re)produce gender inequalities in the first place (Batliwala and Dhanraj, 2004; Cornwall and [SOURCE: Page_5]

34   Edwards, 2010; Kabeer, 2005; O'Laughlin, 2007). Stevano (2017) also points to the structural [SOURCE: Page_5]

35   gendered forms of deprivation that generate food insecurity such as lack of secure [SOURCE: Page_5]

36   employment and conflicting labour demands. [SOURCE: Page_5]

38   empowerment and food security through an intersectional perspective and a mixed methods [SOURCE: Page_5]

40   social difference (race, class, gender, disability, among identities) ‘add up’ to circumscribe [SOURCE: Page_5]

41   how people see themselves and how they are seen in the social milieu, leading to various [SOURCE: Page_5]

43   women’s empowerment and food security relationships relying on quantitative data from [SOURCE: Page_5]

46   communities in Nepal. The communities were selected from project interventions under the [SOURCE: Page_5]

47   same development project and region as where the household survey was conducted, in order [SOURCE: Page_5]

48   to link the qualitative data analysis to the quantitative data analysis. [SOURCE: Page_5]

49      2. Conceptual framework [SOURCE: Page_6]

50   We largely draw on Kabeer’s empowerment framework (1999) for our analysis. Kabeer [SOURCE: Page_6]

51   (1999) defines power as one’s ability to make choices and distinguishes three inter-related [SOURCE: Page_6]

52   dimensions to the process of empowerment: resources (material and non-material, i.e. human [SOURCE: Page_6]

53   and social), agency and achievements. Agency does not only encompass observable actions [SOURCE: Page_6]

54   but also the motivation for these actions and the meanings given to them. We also include [SOURCE: Page_6]

55   under agency one’s ability to influence through positions and identities. For instance, in some [SOURCE: Page_6]

56   societies, the elder man can influence the actions or behaviours of his family members solely [SOURCE: Page_6]

57   by his mere position and identity, without any verbal actions or decisions. [SOURCE: Page_6]

58   We also reckon that power does not necessarily lie in individuals but is also pervasive in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

59   form of what Bourdieu coined ‘doxa’ (1977 in Kabeer, 1999) or the latent form of power [SOURCE: Page_6]

60   (Lukes, 2005) that ensures acceptance of domination: what appears normal and natural is not [SOURCE: Page_6]

61   contested or even perceived unfair. This pervasive form of power was particularly evidenced [SOURCE: Page_6]

62   in Foucault’s writings on how power is embedded in daily practices, institutions and [SOURCE: Page_6]

63   discourses (e.g. Foucault, 1975). In feminist studies, this is what Rowlands (1998) refers to as [SOURCE: Page_6]

64   ‘internalised oppression’ whereby power is exercised through the mere acceptance of [SOURCE: Page_6]

65   dominant norms, rules, relationships and the development of a belief that these are normal, [SOURCE: Page_6]

66   just and legitimate. This leads us to add the concept of ‘critical consciousness’, developed by [SOURCE: Page_6]

67   Freire (1970) to the three other dimensions of empowerment, as proposed earlier by other [SOURCE: Page_6]

68   gender and development scholars (Cornwall, 2016; Cornwall and Gaventa, 2001; Goldman [SOURCE: Page_6]

69   and Little, 2015; Leder, 2016). Critical consciousness denotes one’s ability to realize and [SOURCE: Page_6]

70   react on the structures and other forms of power that produce inequalities and exclusion. In [SOURCE: Page_6]

71   this perspective, reflective argumentation skills are a pre-condition for transformative change [SOURCE: Page_6]

72   and sustainable development (Leder, 2018). [SOURCE: Page_6]

4 [SOURCE: Page_6]

73   To define food security, we rely on the FAO definition: « Food security, at the individual, [SOURCE: Page_7]

74   household, national, regional and global levels [is achieved] when all people, at all times, [SOURCE: Page_7]

75   have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food to meet their dietary [SOURCE: Page_7]

76   needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life » (FAO, 1996). We distinguish [SOURCE: Page_7]

77   between food insecurity as a state and as a process (as Kabeer proposes for poverty, see [SOURCE: Page_7]

78   Kabeer, 2015). Food insecurity as a state is what is observed at a given point in time, whereas [SOURCE: Page_7]

79   food insecurity as a process reflects the mechanisms and structural causes that create food [SOURCE: Page_7]

80   insecurity. We expect that food insecurity as a state affects and is perceived by men and [SOURCE: Page_7]

81   women differently (Coates et al., 2010) while food insecurity as a process is created through [SOURCE: Page_7]

82   distributive and procedural mechanisms that exclude men and women differently. [SOURCE: Page_7]

83   In our quantitative analysis, we particularly examine the linkages between agency and [SOURCE: Page_7]

84   achievements, as it has been, until recently, relatively less studied than the relationship [SOURCE: Page_7]

85   between resources and achievements in the gender and food security literature. Food security [SOURCE: Page_7]

86   is the broad achievement considered here, but we mostly considered food availability and, to [SOURCE: Page_7]

87   some extent, food access, among the four following elements of food security: availability; [SOURCE: Page_7]

88   access; safety, nutrition and sanitation; stability and environment. Following the entitlement [SOURCE: Page_7]

89   approach, we recognise the various mechanisms which people draw upon to acquire food, as [SOURCE: Page_7]

90   well as their ability to draw on multiple mechanisms and diversify risk, understood as [SOURCE: Page_7]

91   ‘capabilities’ (Sen, 1981). A central assumption in our approach is to posit that these [SOURCE: Page_7]

92   capabilities are shaped not only by gender but also by other social markers and identities, such [SOURCE: Page_7]

93   as age, caste, class, ethnicity, religion etc. These do not simply ‘add up’ to each other but [SOURCE: Page_7]

94   intersect to form new forms of vulnerability to food insecurity. Empowerment is a key [SOURCE: Page_7]

95   process that allows entitlements to be realised, secured or claimed (Drè ze and Sen, 1989). In a [SOURCE: Page_7]

96   food sovereignty perspective, empowerment is also critical for smallholder farmers to gain the [SOURCE: Page_7]

5 [SOURCE: Page_7]

97   right to control over food production and define their own food and agriculture policy (Patel, [SOURCE: Page_8]

98   2012). [SOURCE: Page_8]

99      3. Data and methodology [SOURCE: Page_8]

100   3.1 Quantitative data collection and analysis [SOURCE: Page_8]

101   We used the quantitative data collected through a household survey across three different [SOURCE: Page_8]

102   projects conducted in Bangladesh, Nepal and Tajikistan. In the case of Nepal, the survey was [SOURCE: Page_8]

103   designed by the research team for the purpose of the impact evaluation of a development [SOURCE: Page_8]

104   project implemented by an international non-governmental organisation (INGO). The surveys [SOURCE: Page_8]

105   for these three projects did not specifically aim at exploring relationships between food [SOURCE: Page_8]

106   security and women’s empowerment but collected data on agricultural practices, water [SOURCE: Page_8]

107   management and on different components of food security and women’s [SOURCE: Page_8]

108   empowerment/agency (Table 1). We therefore used a heterogeneous set of variables as [SOURCE: Page_8]

109   indicators of women’s empowerment and food security. Our intention was not to compare [SOURCE: Page_8]

110   results across countries but rather to explore different types of relationships between [SOURCE: Page_8]

111   empowerment and food security across a wide range of contexts. [SOURCE: Page_8]

112   In the case of Bangladesh and Tajikistan, the variables considered for women’s empowerment [SOURCE: Page_8]

113   are largely related to agency in the form of decision-making. For Nepal, we used the [SOURCE: Page_8]

114   indicators of a more elaborate index, drawing on the WEAI developed by IFPRI and OPHI [SOURCE: Page_8]

115   (Alkire et al., 2013). The WEAI is an aggregate index based on individual level data on men [SOURCE: Page_8]

116   and women within the same households. The WEAI comprises of two sub-indexes: 1) the five [SOURCE: Page_8]

117   domains of empowerment (5DE); and 2) the gender parity index (GPI). The 5DE sub-index [SOURCE: Page_8]

118   attempts to assess the roles of women in agriculture as well as their level of engagement in [SOURCE: Page_8]

119   this sector to reflect their status of empowerment in five domains of empowerment: (1) [SOURCE: Page_8]

6 [SOURCE: Page_8]

120   decisions over agricultural production, (2) access to and decision-making power over [SOURCE: Page_9]

121   productive resources, (3) control over use of income, (4) leadership in the community, and (5) [SOURCE: Page_9]

122   time use (Alkire et al., 2013). We used the Abbreviated WEAI (A-WEAI) for the purpose of [SOURCE: Page_9]

123   the project impact evaluation. The A-WEAI was developed as a shorter and streamlined [SOURCE: Page_9]

124   version of the WEAI and includes six indicators under the 5DE: inputs in productive [SOURCE: Page_9]

125   decisions, ownership of assets, access to and decisions on credit, control over use of income, [SOURCE: Page_9]

126   group membership and workload. We used for our analysis, the indicator variables for [SOURCE: Page_9]

127   whether the primary female in the household is empowered (binary) (Malapit et al., 2015b), [SOURCE: Page_9]

128   as we felt that considering different domains of empowerment would allow a more nuanced [SOURCE: Page_9]

129   analysis than if we used the aggregate A-WEAI scores. [SOURCE: Page_9]

130   For food security, we relied on a mix of indicators related to self-consumption strategies, [SOURCE: Page_9]

131   likelihood of facing a food shortage, coping strategies in case of food shortage and [SOURCE: Page_9]

132   malnutrition anthropometric indicators (wasting, stunting and under-weight for children under [SOURCE: Page_9]

133   two years old) (Table 1). These are broadly related to food availability, food access and actual [SOURCE: Page_9]

134   undernutrition. [SOURCE: Page_9]

135   To quantify the correlation between the women’s empowerment variables and the food [SOURCE: Page_9]

136   security indicators, we used multivariate regressions to control for household-level attributes [SOURCE: Page_9]

137   that may conjointly affect the food consumption behaviour, food shortages as well as the [SOURCE: Page_9]

138   choice of a coping strategy. Ordinary Least Square regressions are used when the dependant [SOURCE: Page_9]

139   variable is continuous. In the case of binary dependant variables, Logit regressions were [SOURCE: Page_9]

140   preferred and marginal effects reported. Finally, for censored dependant variables, as the [SOURCE: Page_9]

141   number of months, results from Tobit regression are presented. All tables and figures [SOURCE: Page_9]

142   showing the results from the quantitative analysis are provided in the Appendix. [SOURCE: Page_9]

143   In the same time, we explored to which extent our indicators of women’s empowerment and [SOURCE: Page_9]

144   food security were associated with household-level socio-economic indicators. We used three [SOURCE: Page_9]

7 [SOURCE: Page_9]

145   types of socio-economic indicators: wealth, class (land ownership), female-headed household [SOURCE: Page_10]

146   and we added one indicator related to caste and ethnicity for Nepal. Poverty has been found to [SOURCE: Page_10]

147   aggravate gender inequalities, notably in terms of nutrition in Bangladesh (Kabeer, 2015; [SOURCE: Page_10]

148   Sraboni et al., 2014). Land ownership is a strong determinant of both wealth and food security [SOURCE: Page_10]

149   in the South Asian context particularly – it was not included in the analysis for Tajikistan, [SOURCE: Page_10]

150   where land is less a limiting factor for food security. Female headship is also a recurrent [SOURCE: Page_10]

151   indicator of how gender inequalities intersect with poverty across countries (Kabeer, 2015) [SOURCE: Page_10]

152   and with food security (ADB, 2013). In the case of Nepal, Dalit women (Dalits are former [SOURCE: Page_10]

153   untouchables and lowest in the social hierarchy) are particularly vulnerable to poverty and [SOURCE: Page_10]

154   food insecurity (UNDP, 2014), as they face double discrimination and marginalisation [SOURCE: Page_10]

155   (Bennett, 2008). Similarly Janajati, who include diverse indigenous groups in the hills and [SOURCE: Page_10]

182   3.2 Qualitative data collection and analysis [SOURCE: Page_16]

183   The qualitative component of our study considers food insecurity as a process, exploring [SOURCE: Page_16]

184   some of the structural causes that create food insecurity. [SOURCE: Page_16]

185   In Nepal, the qualitative study was conducted between November 2015 and May 2016 as part [SOURCE: Page_16]

186   of the BRACED project under which also the quantitative data were collected. We selected [SOURCE: Page_16]

187   two communities in Dadeldhura and Doti districts, based on the diversity of caste and ethnic [SOURCE: Page_16]

188   groups among the beneficiaries and different types of interventions conducted. [SOURCE: Page_16]

189   The INGO envisioned in this project multiple pathways linking women’s empowerment and [SOURCE: Page_16]

190   increased food security: women’s enhanced access to water for both domestic and productive [SOURCE: Page_16]

191   uses and women’s membership in vegetable farming groups would allow them engaging in [SOURCE: Page_16]

192   irrigated homestead vegetable gardening. Vegetable production would in turn contribute to a [SOURCE: Page_16]

193   more diverse diet and enhanced maternal and child nutrition, while the sale of vegetable [SOURCE: Page_16]

194   products would increase household income and thus its resilience to external climatic shocks. [SOURCE: Page_16]

195   The objective of our study was to capture local meanings of empowerment and understand the [SOURCE: Page_16]

196   causal processes and mechanisms that link women’s empowerment in agriculture to [SOURCE: Page_16]

197   household food security and resilience to shocks. [SOURCE: Page_16]

198   The research team started with a transect walk, village resource mapping and a participatory [SOURCE: Page_16]

199   power ranking to understand social hierarchies and differentiated access to natural resources [SOURCE: Page_16]

200   in the community. Then, 5-6 key informant interviews were conducted in each community [SOURCE: Page_16]

201   and several focus group discussions (FGDs) disaggregated by sex and household power [SOURCE: Page_16]

202   category on empowerment. Eight women were selected based on their household power [SOURCE: Page_16]

203   category covering high, middle and low power categories, to conduct and discuss power-self [SOURCE: Page_16]

14 [SOURCE: Page_16]

204   rankings and jointly reflect on their own A-WEAI score. Lastly in-depth interviews were [SOURCE: Page_17]

205   conducted with 12-15 women in each community to explore life histories, household [SOURCE: Page_17]

206   livelihood strategies, project implementation outcomes, access to drinking water and [SOURCE: Page_17]

207   irrigation facilities, group membership and access to credit, and involvement in household and [SOURCE: Page_17]

208   community-level decision-making. [SOURCE: Page_17]

210   4.1 Relationships between indicators of women’s empowerment and food security [SOURCE: Page_17]

211   4.1.1. Self-consumption [SOURCE: Page_17]

212   In Bangladesh, results indicate that women’s decision on how the agricultural production [SOURCE: Page_17]

213   should be used (sold, self-consumed or stored) significantly determine the share of the [SOURCE: Page_17]

214   vegetable harvest retained for self-consumption. However, their decision is not a determinant [SOURCE: Page_17]

215   of the share of aman rice and fish kept for self-consumption (Table B$_1$). This is in line with [SOURCE: Page_17]

216   earlier research findings on women’s role to ensure household food security in Bangladesh [SOURCE: Page_17]

217   (Sraboni et al. 2014) and traditional gendered division of family labour, with women having [SOURCE: Page_17]

218   more labour input and control over homestead vegetable gardening and men more control [SOURCE: Page_17]

219   over rice production, fishing and marketing of these products (Rahman, 2000; Sultana and [SOURCE: Page_17]

220   Thompson, 2008). [SOURCE: Page_17]

221   In Nepal, results are mixed: when women are empowered in terms of access to and decisions [SOURCE: Page_17]

222   about credit, a significantly larger share of both vegetable and cereal production is kept for [SOURCE: Page_17]

223   home consumption (with cereal production and marketing usually under the male domain). [SOURCE: Page_17]

224   The relationship is the opposite for vegetable production when women were empowered in [SOURCE: Page_17]

225   terms of control over the income (Table N$_1$). This could be explained by the fact that [SOURCE: Page_17]

226   homestead vegetable production and sales are often a significant – or the only – component of [SOURCE: Page_17]

15 [SOURCE: Page_17]

227   rural women’s income in the sampled region – so the direction of the relationship might [SOURCE: Page_18]

228   therefore be opposite than the one expected: when women sell a larger share of vegetables, [SOURCE: Page_18]

229   they have more control over income. [SOURCE: Page_18]

230   In Tajikistan, women’s decision-making only had a significant effect on the amount retained [SOURCE: Page_18]

231   in the case of maize, which is used both for animal and human consumption: when women [SOURCE: Page_18]

232   decide on the use of maize product, slightly less of the harvest is retained for home [SOURCE: Page_18]

233   consumption (Table T$_1$). [SOURCE: Page_18]

234   4.1.2 Access to food [SOURCE: Page_18]

235   In Bangladesh, decisions by women on the use of the agricultural produce and the use of the [SOURCE: Page_18]

237   faced with food shortage, with acute food shortage and even the likelihood to face a food [SOURCE: Page_18]

238   shortage (Table B$_2$). Interestingly, the coefficients are higher for the decision on the incomes [SOURCE: Page_18]

241   relationship between women’s decisions over income and wheat productivity is positive [SOURCE: Page_18]

242   (Table N$_2$). However the access to and decision about credit is negatively associated with [SOURCE: Page_18]

243   wheat productivity. The relationship between women’s empowerment and food availability [SOURCE: Page_18]

244   and its direction is therefore unclear – but in line with earlier results on self-consumption. [SOURCE: Page_18]

245   Women’s empowerment over credit is both significantly correlated with lower wheat [SOURCE: Page_18]

246   productivity and a greater share of cereals kept for self-consumption. [SOURCE: Page_18]

249   likelihood to rely on cheap food is reduced by 8%, all things being equal and compared with [SOURCE: Page_18]

263   In the case of Nepal, anthropometric measures have been collected for children under two [SOURCE: Page_19]

311      4.3 Understanding the complexity of empowerment/food security relationships [SOURCE: Page_21]

312   We now turn to our qualitative data from two communities in Western Nepal to delve into the [SOURCE: Page_21]

313   complexity that characterises the relationships between women’s agency and food availability [SOURCE: Page_21]

314   and access. Our findings evidence new variables and processes that were not captured by the [SOURCE: Page_21]

315   quantitative analysis, notably related to empowerment. We first examine agency at the intra- [SOURCE: Page_21]

316   household level, in the particular situation of women with migrated husbands. Then, we [SOURCE: Page_21]

317   broaden our analysis to consider how social relationships beyond the household shape access [SOURCE: Page_21]

318   to productive resources and agency. [SOURCE: Page_21]

319   4.3.1. Intra-household agency [SOURCE: Page_21]

320   Nepal has seen an increase in long-term male out-migration to India, Malaysia and Gulf [SOURCE: Page_21]

321   countries, which has affected the distribution of gender roles, responsibilities and resources [SOURCE: Page_21]

322   (Leder et al., 2017; Sugden et al., 2014). In this context, we found that high levels of women’s [SOURCE: Page_21]

323   agency, as assessed with the A-WEAI, do not necessarily translate into a feeling of [SOURCE: Page_21]

19 [SOURCE: Page_21]

324   empowerment (‘shashaktikaran’ in Nepali) and in increased food security. In contrast, several [SOURCE: Page_22]

325   women with permanently out-migrated husbands, who were rated “empowered” according to [SOURCE: Page_22]

326   their A-WEAI scores, were rated by other women and self-rated themselves as the least [SOURCE: Page_22]

327   empowered and marginalised in their community. [SOURCE: Page_22]

328   For example, for one of our respondents, Kamala, the A-WEAI indicators indicated that she is [SOURCE: Page_22]

329   empowered in decision-making, based on her position as household head without the presence [SOURCE: Page_22]

330   of in-laws or her husband. However, she stated to feel lonely and worried about her ability to [SOURCE: Page_22]

331   provide quality education and adequate medical care for her son and pay back multiple loans. [SOURCE: Page_22]

332   As phone calls to her husband abroad are expensive, communication and mental support from [SOURCE: Page_22]

333   him are very limited, and her maternal family lives far away. In fact, in FGDs, women [SOURCE: Page_22]

334   identified kins’ mental, financial and labor support as a contributing factor to their feeling of [SOURCE: Page_22]

335   empowerment. Conversely, they perceived support to others as a key achievement of [SOURCE: Page_22]

336   empowered women including the ability to help other women in cultivating crops and [SOURCE: Page_22]

337   vegetables: “They [empowered women] can tell us what to grow and how much to plant”. [SOURCE: Page_22]

338   Another woman, Sunita, resides with her three young children while her husband works in [SOURCE: Page_22]

339   Malaysia. She makes minor household decisions, decides on agricultural production and is an [SOURCE: Page_22]

340   active member of savings groups. However, when asked to describe her life, Sunita described [SOURCE: Page_22]

341   her life full of ‘dukkha’ (‘sadness’ in Nepali). Her husband’s passport was taken away, [SOURCE: Page_22]

342   making him unsure of how to return home and he has only managed to send her NPR 20,000 [SOURCE: Page_22]

343   (USD 200) in the last three years, a small amount considering he took a loan of NPR 160,000 [SOURCE: Page_22]

344   (USD 1,600) to get to Malaysia. When harvest fails due to drought, she works as daily wage [SOURCE: Page_22]

345   labourer, carrying construction material for 200 NPR (1.7 USD) a day, as a coping strategy to [SOURCE: Page_22]

346   be able to purchase food. [SOURCE: Page_22]

348   the household does not necessarily match with women’s own perceptions of their [SOURCE: Page_23]

349   empowerment. We encountered many women who did not feel empowered by taking [SOURCE: Page_23]

362   4.3.2. Looking beyond the household: caste and class [SOURCE: Page_23]

363   In Nepal, Dalits usually reside at the fringe or outside the main settlement, with limited access [SOURCE: Page_23]

364   to land, social networks and information. They lack access to water resources due to religious [SOURCE: Page_23]

365   norms and structural constraints (Nightingale, 2011). In mixed caste communities, existing [SOURCE: Page_23]

366   inequalities in water access have often been reproduced through external water and food [SOURCE: Page_23]

368   (Clement et al., 2014; Leder et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_23]

369   In one of the researched communities in Nepal, the project staff invited all women and men [SOURCE: Page_23]

370   from the communities to participate in meetings to decide on the location of taps before a [SOURCE: Page_23]

371   multiple-use water system (MUS) was built. However, the Dalits, who represent 15% of the [SOURCE: Page_23]

21 [SOURCE: Page_23]

372   population, perceive that the distribution has not been fair as only one of the 22 water taps [SOURCE: Page_24]

373   was installed in their settlement. One contributing factor was the limited ability of Dalit [SOURCE: Page_24]

374   women to influence decisions due to the dominance of higher caste men during the meetings. [SOURCE: Page_24]

375   In addition, their landlessness or almost landlessness was stated as an important hindering [SOURCE: Page_24]

376   factor to receive a tap, as the BRACED project targeted households who could use the water [SOURCE: Page_24]

377   to grow vegetables in their home garden. Dalits were not only excluded but also negatively [SOURCE: Page_24]

378   affected by the project intervention. While higher caste households benefited from greater [SOURCE: Page_24]

379   access to water taps, women of these households would less frequently go to the old [SOURCE: Page_24]

380   communal water taps and natural water sources. Because Dalit women have to rely on other [SOURCE: Page_24]

381   women to fill their vessel, as touching water is culturally not allowed for them, they now have [SOURCE: Page_24]

382   to wait longer times to get water. [SOURCE: Page_24]

385   her two daughters, and her out-migrated husband in Malaysia has neither sent remittances nor [SOURCE: Page_24]

387   laborer, she is dependent on landowners for her household food security, and in particular on [SOURCE: Page_24]

389   next to her house in exchange of free agricultural and domestic labor. Thanks to her close [SOURCE: Page_24]

390   relation to this Chhettri woman, she developed both as an outspoken leader among the Dalits, [SOURCE: Page_24]

391   but also as a critically scrutinized woman in the village. Her privileged geographical location [SOURCE: Page_24]

393   stream and are more exposed to monsoonal floods and landslides. . Her multiple gendered and [SOURCE: Page_24]

394   caste subjectivities demonstrates how leadership is complicated and shifting over social [SOURCE: Page_24]

400      5. Discussion and conclusion [SOURCE: Page_25]

401   Reflecting on our findings in light of our framework constituted by resources, agency, [SOURCE: Page_25]

402   achievements and critical consciousness, a few interesting lessons for food security debates [SOURCE: Page_25]

403   and programmes emerge. [SOURCE: Page_25]

404   First, when examining the linkages between women’s agency and food security achievements [SOURCE: Page_25]

405   with a quantitative analysis, we evidenced that different indicators of agency and even [SOURCE: Page_25]

406   different types of decisions within one indicator of agency are associated with different [SOURCE: Page_25]

407   components of food security. These results are in line with earlier studies (e.g. Malapit et al., [SOURCE: Page_25]

408   2015a; Malapit and Quisumbing, 2015) but our cross-country analysis further highlights the [SOURCE: Page_25]

409   diversity of patterns and the diversity of roles that socio-economic structures play in shaping [SOURCE: Page_25]

410   these patterns across countries. [SOURCE: Page_25]

411   This points to the challenges to select the right indicators and to conduct cross-country [SOURCE: Page_25]

412   comparative analyses with similar sets of indicators. Our quantitative analysis shows [SOURCE: Page_25]

413   interesting patterns between women’s empowerment and food security but their diversity [SOURCE: Page_25]

414   across countries and across indicators of empowerment and food security raises more [SOURCE: Page_25]

415   questions than provides answers. Whereas the quantitative findings point to some of the intra- [SOURCE: Page_25]

416   community and intra-household factors that influence the pathways linking women’s [SOURCE: Page_25]

417   empowerment and food security, the qualitative analysis from Nepal allows digging into some [SOURCE: Page_25]

418   of this complexity and evidences how some of these factors, such as caste, class and [SOURCE: Page_25]

419   migration status, among others, intersect to produce different empowerment and food security [SOURCE: Page_25]

23 [SOURCE: Page_25]

420   trajectories. This leads us to question the relevance of implementing a one-size-fits-all [SOURCE: Page_26]

421   intervention for empowering rural women farmers or increasing household food security – [SOURCE: Page_26]

422   even within one community. We rather defend the importance of adopting a contextualised [SOURCE: Page_26]

423   and intersectional approach in food security programmes that addresses the structural barriers [SOURCE: Page_26]

424   that keep the marginalised, excluded and powerless food insecure. This means disaggregating [SOURCE: Page_26]

425   the category of ‘women’ for project design, monitoring and evaluation, and moving beyond [SOURCE: Page_26]

426   single distinct categories (e.g. separately considering gender, class, ethnicity), by intersecting [SOURCE: Page_26]

427   categories (e.g. landless Dalit woman, Chhetri woman with migrated husband in small [SOURCE: Page_26]

430   this paper, but better developed elsewhere, drawing on data from the same sites (Anonymous, [SOURCE: Page_26]

431   forthcoming). [SOURCE: Page_26]

432   Such an intersectional approach then would allow a better understanding of food insecurity as [SOURCE: Page_26]

433   a process: namely, how one’s ability to exercise agency to become food secure is embedded [SOURCE: Page_26]

434   in institutions (including social norms) and political-economic structures. In our findings for [SOURCE: Page_26]

435   Bangladesh, increased women’s agency reduces the likelihood of their household to face food [SOURCE: Page_26]

437   controlling for women’s agency. Similarly, our interviews in Nepal show that the entrenched [SOURCE: Page_26]

438   social barriers for Dalit women to access land and water resources affect their ability to [SOURCE: Page_26]

439   benefit from water and food security projects. Global food security discourses therefore need [SOURCE: Page_26]

440   to bring the role of institutions and political-economic structures to the fore: how they shape [SOURCE: Page_26]

441   the diverse women’s and men’s differentiated capabilities to benefit from food security [SOURCE: Page_26]

442   programmes, engage in markets, influence food production and distribution systems and food [SOURCE: Page_26]

443   policies. Increased individual agency in agricultural production does not necessarily allow [SOURCE: Page_26]

444   marginalised women overcoming the structural barriers that prevent them achieving what they [SOURCE: Page_26]

445   value, including a decent and non-exploitative way to be food secure. Furthermore, in Nepal, [SOURCE: Page_27]

446   social relationships play a central role in perceptions of empowerment through emotional, [SOURCE: Page_27]

448   ethnicity differences – in this perspective, empowerment largely goes beyond increasing the [SOURCE: Page_27]

449   agency of isolated individuals. [SOURCE: Page_27]

450   Lastly, our qualitative findings underline the importance of jointly examining one’s increased [SOURCE: Page_27]

451   agency with the local meanings and values associated to this agency. Increased agency feels [SOURCE: Page_27]

452   like empowerment only when it helps achieving what one values. This has major implications [SOURCE: Page_27]
