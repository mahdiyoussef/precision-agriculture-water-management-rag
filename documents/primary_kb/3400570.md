## 17   ABSTRACT

18   Climate changes will increase the probability of drought, which is likely to dramatically [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   increase tree mortality. The capacity of trees to withdraw water in deep soil layers is [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   an important trait likely to account for tree survival over prolonged droughts. Our [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   study aimed to gain insight into the maximum distance from the trunk where [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   Eucalyptus fine roots take up water and mobile nutrients in deep sandy soils during [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   dry periods. NO$_3$--15N was injected in the soil at the end of the rainy season in [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   commercial Eucalyptus stands planted with the same E. urophylla x E. grandis clone. [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   The 15N tracer was applied in the middle of the inter-row (replicated in 3 plots): at 5 [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   depths (from 0.1 to 6 m) at age 0.6 year, at 4 depths (from 0.1 to 9 m) at age 1.2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   years, at 5 depths (from 0.1 to 12 m) at age 2.2 years, and at 6 depths (from 0.1 to [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   15 m) at age 6.4 years. δ15N was determined in leaves sampled in dominant and [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   suppressed trees at different distances from each injection area, 4-5 months after [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   NO$_3$--15N injection (after the dry season). While dominant trees took up NO$_3$--15N [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   down to a depth of 6 m between 7 and 12 months after planting, the maximum depth [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   of NO$_3$--15N uptake for suppressed trees was between 3 and 4.5 m. From 1.5 to 6 [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   years after planting, a foliar enrichment in 15N was mainly detected when the NO$_3$-- [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   15N tracer was injected in the upper soil layers and only for a few trees at a depth of 6 [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   m. Most of the uptake of 15N occurred within 2 m of horizontal distance from the [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   injection site, whatever tree age and tree social status. Low amounts of NO$_3$--15N [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   were taken up for injection sites located between 2 m and 5 m from the trunk, and [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   15N uptake was never detected at horizontal distances greater than 6 m from the [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   trunk. Eucalyptus fine roots can take up nitrates at depths between 6 and 8 m the first [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   year after planting. However, the NO$_3$--15N tracer injected at a depth of 6 m was only [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   taken up by dominant trees and a 15N foliar enrichment of suppressed trees was only [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   detected when the tracer was injected in the upper 3 m. Fertilizers must be applied [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   within 2 m of the trunks in Eucalyptus plantations to be taken up by all trees, [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   regardless of their social status. When fertilizations are concentrated the first months [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   after planting in sandy soils, nutrient leaching in deep layers might increase the [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   heterogeneity of the stands since mobile nutrients could only be taken up by [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   dominant trees. [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_5]

53   Climate changes will increase the probability of exceptional droughts, which may [SOURCE: Page_5]

54   dramatically increase tree mortality worldwide (Wu et al., 2011). Improving our [SOURCE: Page_5]

55   understanding of the structural and physiological mechanisms by which trees [SOURCE: Page_5]

56   maintain tissue hydration and photosynthesis in response to water deficit is therefore [SOURCE: Page_5]

57   essential to predict the effects of climate change on tree survival, carbon [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   sequestration, and use of water in forest ecosystems (McDowell et al., 2013; Klein et [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   al., 2014; Venegas-González et al., 2018). A better understanding of the effects of [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   tree nutrition on the mechanisms involved in tree response to drought is essential to [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   adapt the silviculture to a probable future drier climate (Battie-Laclau et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   Both an increase of atmospheric carbon dioxide (Iversen et al., 2010; Nie et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   2013) and prolonged droughts (Germon et al., 2019) should increase the exploration [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   of deep soil layers by plant roots in the future. Deep rooting can enhance the [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   tolerance of some plant species to drought making it possible to reach water stored in [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   the subsoil (Chaves et al., 2003; Hoekstra et al., 2015). In a context of climate [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   change, the maximum depth reached by plant roots is therefore an important trait to [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   select genotypes tolerant to prolonged droughts (Comas et al., 2013; Pinheiro et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   2016). [SOURCE: Page_5]

70 [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   Roots have been little studied at depths of more than 5 m (Jackson et al., 1997; [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   Pierret et al., 2016), and the maximum rooting depth has probably been [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   underestimated in many tropical forests (Freycon et al., 2015). Most of the studies [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   dealt with the description of fine root distributions and fine root dynamics throughout [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   very deep soil profiles (Maeght et al., 2013; Pinheiro et al., 2016; Lambais et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   2017). Even though labelling techniques have been used to locate the areas of [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   nutrient uptake in the soil (Lehmann and Muraoka, 2001; Lehmann, 2003; Göttlicher [SOURCE: Page_6]

78   et al., 2008), the contribution of deep roots to supply tree nutrient requirements is still [SOURCE: Page_6]

79   poorly documented in highly weathered tropical soils (Poszwa et al., 2002; Pradier et [SOURCE: Page_6]

80   al., 2017). A functional specialization of fine roots to take up cations depending on [SOURCE: Page_6]

81   the depth has been shown for tree species (Göransson et al., 2008; da Silva et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

82   2011) but the uptake of nitrate was little influenced by soil depth for Eucalyptus [SOURCE: Page_6]

83   grandis trees (Bordron et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_6]

84 [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   The depth of water uptake by tree roots is commonly estimated monitoring soil water [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   contents throughout soil profiles (Guderle and Hildebrandt, 2015), through modelling [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   approaches (e.g. Kumar et al., 2014; Christina et al., 2017), or using stable isotopes [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   of hydrogen (deuterium, 2H) and oxygen (18O) (Beyer et al., 2016, 2018; Koeniger et [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   al., 2016; Trogisch et al., 2016). Deuterium and 18O techniques can be used to [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   estimate the depth of water uptake when their natural abundances in soil solutions [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   follow a clear gradient from the soil surface to deep soil layers (Roupsard et al., 1999; [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   Brum et al., 2018). Injecting 2H and 18O at different depths makes it possible to detect [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   the areas of water uptake in the soil at the date of sampling. For example, a dual- [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   isotope labelling technique in a tropical rainforest (2H applied at soil surface and 18O [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   injected at 120-cm depth) showed that some tree species had a high plasticity in their [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   depth of water uptake, exhibiting an efficient strategy for water resource acquisition [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   below 100-cm depth during dry periods (Stahl et al., 2013). However, the depth of [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   water uptake in tropical forests can be highly variable from one week to another [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   depending on the occurrence of rainfall events (Christina et al., 2017). It would be [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   therefore necessary to repeat deuterium and 18O sampling along the year to estimate [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   the maximum distance of water uptake from trees. Moreover, isotopic techniques [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   based on deuterium and 18O measurements are expensive and time-consuming. [SOURCE: Page_7]

103   Repeated measurements of 2H and 18O uptake at different depths in the soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

104   throughout the year are therefore rare in tropical forests. [SOURCE: Page_7]

105 [SOURCE: Page_7]

106   Da Silva et al. (2011) showed that 15N from labelled nitrates injected at a depth of 3 [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   m were only detected in young leaves of 6-year-old Eucalyptus trees when the [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   gravitational soil solutions reached 3 m depth, which suggests that NO$_3$--15N uptake [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   could be a tracer of water uptake in these plantations. Nitrate is highly mobile in soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   solutions and the uptake of large amounts of nitrates is dependent on mass flow [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   transport up to fine roots (Hinsinger et al., 2011; White et al., 2013; McMurtrie and [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   Näsholm, 2018). Nitrate labelling could therefore be a proxy of the cumulative uptake [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   of water between the date of NO$_3$--15N injection in the soil and leaf sampling a few [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   months later, since a clear enrichment in foliar 15N content can only occur if large [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   amounts of NO$_3$--15N are transported through mass flow up to fine roots at the vicinity [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   of the injection area. [SOURCE: Page_7]

117 [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   Eucalyptus planted forests cover more than 20 million hectares worldwide and are [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   expanding rapidly in tropical and subtropical regions (Booth, 2013; Gonçalves et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   2013). In Brazil, these fast-growing plantations are intensively managed over 7.4 [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   million hectares and account for 75 % of the total area of planted forests (IBGE, [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   2017). However, highly productive Eucalyptus plantations are sensitive to prolonged [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   drought periods. The cumulative area affected by tree mortality as a result of drought [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   over the last decade in the state of Minas Gerais (the largest producer of Eucalyptus [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   in Brazil) was about 200,000 hectares (Gonçalves et al., 2017). The variability among [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   trees within the same stand to reach water and nutrients far from the trunk [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   (horizontally and in depth) might be an important factor likely to help explain why only [SOURCE: Page_8]

128   some trees survive during drought periods. While the influence of deep roots on [SOURCE: Page_8]

129   drought-adaptative mechanisms of tree species is well documented (McDowell et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   2008; Fang and Xiong, 2015), the relationship between the social status of trees and [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   their ability to withdraw water in very deep soil layers has been little investigated in [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   monospecific forests. [SOURCE: Page_8]

133 [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   Our study aimed to assess the maximum distance from the trunk where Eucalyptus [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   fine roots take up water and mobile nutrients in soil solutions in very deep sandy [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   soils. We hypothesized that mobile nutrients are taken up farther from the trunk and [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   more deeply for dominant trees than for suppressed trees. [SOURCE: Page_8]

202   2.3. Tracer applications [SOURCE: Page_11]

203   Our study (aiming to assess the maximum depth where labelled nitrates can be taken [SOURCE: Page_11]

204   up) was carried out during the dry season because a recent study showed that [SOURCE: Page_11]

205   Eucalyptus trees withdraw preferentially water in the topsoil after rainfall events [SOURCE: Page_11]

206   (Christina et al., 2017). One day before application in the field, a labelled solution [SOURCE: Page_11]

207   was prepared in the laboratory and maintained at 4 ºC. 16.6 g of NH415NO$_3$ [SOURCE: Page_11]

208   commercial compound (10 atom% or 29,221.98 ‰ NO$_3$--15N) dissolved in 20 ml of [SOURCE: Page_11]

209   distilled water were injected at a single depth at the centre of each plot. The holes [SOURCE: Page_11]

210   were drilled down to the target application depth using a cylindrical auger with an [SOURCE: Page_11]

211   inner diameter of 9 cm and the soil water content was measured every meter in all [SOURCE: Page_11]

212   samples collected during drilling (Appendix 2). Soil blocks from the inner part of the [SOURCE: Page_11]

213   auger (to avoid any contamination with fine roots from upper soil layers) were [SOURCE: Page_11]

214   collected for the deepest meter in each hole to assess fine root densities close to the [SOURCE: Page_11]

215   area of NO$_3$--15N injection. [SOURCE: Page_11]

216 [SOURCE: Page_11]

217   A PVC tube (2.5 cm in diameter) was inserted into each hole to avoid any [SOURCE: Page_11]

218   contamination of the soil with NO$_3$--15N during tracer application. A 0.4-cm [SOURCE: Page_11]

219   polyethylene tube, attached to an iron rod, was inserted into the PVC tube, with a [SOURCE: Page_11]

220   length depending on the application depth. A plastic sheet was placed around each [SOURCE: Page_11]

221   hole to avoid any contamination with NO$_3$--15N at the soil surface. 20 ml of the [SOURCE: Page_11]

222   labelled solution was applied at the selected depth using a syringe. Thereafter, 280 [SOURCE: Page_11]

223   ml of distilled water were injected to rinse the polyethylene tube and to promote [SOURCE: Page_11]

224   nitrate uptake in the soil area where the tracer was applied. Lastly, the polyethylene [SOURCE: Page_11]

225   and PVC tubes were carefully withdrawn from the holes, which were filled with the [SOURCE: Page_11]

226   soil removed during drilling, respecting the original order of the soil layers. Similar [SOURCE: Page_11]

227   methodologies were used by da Silva et al. (2011) and Bordron et al. (2019) to study [SOURCE: Page_12]

354   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_17]

355   4.1 Reliability of our method to detect the maximum distance of water uptake from [SOURCE: Page_17]

356   trees [SOURCE: Page_17]

357   The depths of NO$_3$--15N uptake throughout the rotation in our study are consistent [SOURCE: Page_17]

358   with other studies estimating the depth of water withdrawal in tropical Eucalyptus [SOURCE: Page_17]

359   plantations, which suggests that the uptake of labelled nitrate could be a simple [SOURCE: Page_17]

360   proxy of cumulative water uptake in specific soil areas. In particular, a modelling [SOURCE: Page_17]

361   approach based on soil moisture and eddy-covariance measurements over 4.5 years [SOURCE: Page_17]

362   after planting in a commercial Eucalyptus stand at about 20 km from our study sites [SOURCE: Page_17]

363   (in a similar soil) showed that water was withdrawn from the soil surface to a depth of [SOURCE: Page_17]

364   6-7 m the first year after planting, from the soil surface to a depth of 10 m the second [SOURCE: Page_17]

365   dry season, then only in the upper soil layers and close to the water table at a depth [SOURCE: Page_17]

366   of more than 12 m until harvesting (Christina et al., 2017). The depth of uptake of [SOURCE: Page_17]

367   labelled nitrate by Eucalyptus trees in our study is consistent with the dynamics of [SOURCE: Page_17]

368   water uptake throughout deep soil profiles over long periods in other Eucalyptus [SOURCE: Page_17]

369   plantations in the same region (Laclau et al., 2013; Marsden et al., 2013; Christina et [SOURCE: Page_17]

370   al., 2018). However, a strong demonstration of the validity of our method (enrichment [SOURCE: Page_17]

371   in foliar 15N after injection of NO$_3$--15N in the soil) as a proxy of water uptake by tree [SOURCE: Page_17]

372   roots would require to monitor foliar 15N as well as 2H and 18O in the trees after the [SOURCE: Page_17]

373   injection in the same soil areas of NO$_3$--15N, 2H and 18O tracers. [SOURCE: Page_17]

374 [SOURCE: Page_17]

16 [SOURCE: Page_17]

375   Although we injected NO$_3$--15N down to a depth of 15 m, we did not detect any [SOURCE: Page_18]

376   significant uptake of labelled nitrate at depths of more than 9 m. This pattern does [SOURCE: Page_18]

377   not demonstrate that water was not taken up at these depths but suggests that large [SOURCE: Page_18]

378   amounts of labelled nitrate were not taken up by neighboring trees. Fine root [SOURCE: Page_18]

379   densities are very low at depths of more than 6 m in Eucalyptus plantations (Christina [SOURCE: Page_18]

380   et al., 2011; Laclau et al., 2013), and it is possible that the trees sampled to [SOURCE: Page_18]

381   determine foliar 15N contents in our study did not explore the small soil areas where [SOURCE: Page_18]

382   the tracer was injected. The simple method used here to detect water and nitrate [SOURCE: Page_18]

383   uptake is also probably less sensitive than isotopic methods based on 2H and 18O [SOURCE: Page_18]

384   measurements to detect small amounts of water taken up throughout the study [SOURCE: Page_18]

385   period. [SOURCE: Page_18]

387   4.2 Influence of the social status of the tree on the distance of uptake of the labelled [SOURCE: Page_18]

388   nitrate [SOURCE: Page_18]

389   Our study clearly shows in monoclonal stands that dominant trees have a higher [SOURCE: Page_18]

390   capacity to use soil resources in very deep soil layers than suppressed trees. From [SOURCE: Page_18]

391   0.6 to 1 year after planting, most of the trees close to the injection sites took up NO$_3$-- [SOURCE: Page_18]

392   15N in the upper 3 m of the soil profile and only dominant trees took up the tracer at [SOURCE: Page_18]

393   depths of 4.5 m and 6 m. While some dominant trees took up large amounts of 15N at [SOURCE: Page_18]

394   a depth of 6 m the second and third years after planting, the lack of clear enrichment [SOURCE: Page_18]

395   in foliar 15N for all the suppressed trees suggests that their ability to access to soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

396   resources in very deep soil layers was low relative to dominant trees. [SOURCE: Page_18]

397 [SOURCE: Page_18]

398   This pattern is consistent with a recent study in a seasonal Amazon forest using the [SOURCE: Page_18]

399   stable isotope ratios (δ18O and δ2H) of water collected from tree xylem and soils [SOURCE: Page_18]

17 [SOURCE: Page_18]

400   down to a depth of 10 m during an extreme dry event. Rooting depth differences [SOURCE: Page_19]

401   were strongly related to tree size and water uptake by understory species was limited [SOURCE: Page_19]

402   to shallow soil layers. Coexisting trees were mainly segregated along a single [SOURCE: Page_19]

403   hydrological niche axis defined by root depth, access to light and tolerance of low [SOURCE: Page_19]

404   water potential (Brum et al., 2018). Our results in monoclonal Eucalyptus stands [SOURCE: Page_19]

405   suggest that the strong relationship between tree size and rooting depth showed for [SOURCE: Page_19]

406   co-existing species in this tropical forest could be valid between big and small trees [SOURCE: Page_19]

407   of the same species. However, another isotopic study in a tropical rainforest showed [SOURCE: Page_19]

408   that tall trees preferentially extracted water from layers below a depth of 1 m, while [SOURCE: Page_19]

409   smaller trees exhibited large variations in the mean depth of water uptake, which [SOURCE: Page_19]

410   prevented the use of tree dimensions to parameterize functional models in this multi- [SOURCE: Page_19]

411   specific forest (Stahl et al., 2013). Further studies on the relationship between tree [SOURCE: Page_19]

412   size and water uptake down to a depth of 10 m (or more) are needed to get an [SOURCE: Page_19]

413   insight into the sharing of limited water resources between plants during drought [SOURCE: Page_19]

414   events in tropical forests. [SOURCE: Page_19]

415 [SOURCE: Page_19]

416   Root exploration limited to shallow soil layers for small trees seems contradictory with [SOURCE: Page_19]

417   the observation that suppressed trees can survive better to drought events than [SOURCE: Page_19]

418   dominant trees (Bennett et al., 2015; McDowell and Allen, 2015). However, many [SOURCE: Page_19]

419   factors other than the maximum depth reached by fine roots can account for tree [SOURCE: Page_19]

420   survival during drought periods. Low water demand of suppressed trees relative to [SOURCE: Page_19]

421   dominant trees might help avoid hydraulic failure during drought (Grote et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_19]

422   While drought periods impacted less the growth of suppressed trees relative to [SOURCE: Page_19]

423   dominant trees in some forests (Martín-Benito et al., 2008; Martínes-Vilalta et al., [SOURCE: Page_19]

424   2012; Zang et al., 2012), the pattern was less clear in other forests and a more [SOURCE: Page_19]

18 [SOURCE: Page_19]

425   pronounced impact of drought in the understory than for dominant trees has also [SOURCE: Page_20]

426   been reported (Castagneri et al., 2012; Trouvé et al., 2014; Merlin et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_20]

427 [SOURCE: Page_20]

428   The horizontal distance of NO$_3$--15N uptake from the trunk was not clearly influenced [SOURCE: Page_20]

429   by the social status of the trees in our study. Only a small proportion of the sampled [SOURCE: Page_20]

430   trees took up the NO$_3$--15N tracer, which is consistent with an uneven distribution of [SOURCE: Page_20]

431   tree roots shown in other studies. In monospecific boreal forests, the average lateral [SOURCE: Page_20]

432   root spread of trees was 4-5 m but not all trees took up the 15N tracer close to the [SOURCE: Page_20]

433   injection area, which suggested that the root system was highly asymmetric [SOURCE: Page_20]

434   (Göttlicher et al., 2008). Throughout deuterium pulses, small trees in an Amazonian [SOURCE: Page_20]

435   tropical forest showed different rooting patterns, while most species took up the [SOURCE: Page_20]

436   deuterium tracer only close to the trunk, a few species had high concentrations of [SOURCE: Page_20]

437   deuterium up to 10 m of horizontal distance from the injection area (Sternberg et al., [SOURCE: Page_20]

438   2002). [SOURCE: Page_20]

440   4.3 Consequences for the management of Eucalyptus stands in deep sandy soils [SOURCE: Page_20]

441   Our study was also designed to answer a question from forest managers in Brazil: [SOURCE: Page_20]

442   which proportion of the inter-rows in Eucalyptus plantations must be fertilized (after [SOURCE: Page_20]

443   weeding) to make it possible for all the trees to take up the applied nutrients? [SOURCE: Page_20]

444   Fertilization and weeding are seasonal activities with a peak at the mid-rainy season, [SOURCE: Page_20]

445   and an extension of these activities throughout the year could be useful to reduce the [SOURCE: Page_20]

446   cost of the silviculture. The staff and the equipment needed to cope with the peak of [SOURCE: Page_20]

447   activity over the rainy season could indeed be reduced if the intervention could be [SOURCE: Page_20]

448   postponed of a few months in a significant proportion of the inter-rows within each [SOURCE: Page_20]

449   stand. [SOURCE: Page_20]

19 [SOURCE: Page_20]

450 [SOURCE: Page_21]

451   Whatever stand age and tree social status, most of the labelled nitrate injected at [SOURCE: Page_21]

452   different soil depths was taken up by trees located within 2-3 m from the injection [SOURCE: Page_21]

453   site. Our results show that N fertilizers must be applied close to the trunks to be taken [SOURCE: Page_21]

454   up by all the trees, whatever stand age. Spacing in commercial Eucalyptus [SOURCE: Page_21]

455   plantations managed in short rotations is commonly 2 m x 3 m or 3 m x 3 m. Our [SOURCE: Page_21]

456   results suggest that fertilization (and weeding) in half of the inter-rows during the [SOURCE: Page_21]

457   rainy season could be sufficient to make it possible for all the trees to take up the [SOURCE: Page_21]

458   applied nutrients. However, the proportion of fertilized inter-rows cannot be lower [SOURCE: Page_21]

459   than 50%. Applying fertilizers in 1/3 of the inter-rows would prevent most trees that [SOURCE: Page_21]

460   are not adjacent to the fertilized inter-row from taking up the nutrients and would [SOURCE: Page_21]

461   probably lead to a high heterogeneity of growth within the stand. [SOURCE: Page_21]

462 [SOURCE: Page_21]

463   The risk of nutrient losses by deep drainage will be higher if only 50% of the inter- [SOURCE: Page_21]

464   rows are fertilized because only about half of the root system would have access to [SOURCE: Page_21]

465   the applied nutrients. Laclau et al. (2003; 2010) showed that nutrient requirements [SOURCE: Page_21]

466   are high in the first two years after planting Eucalyptus trees to produce leaves and [SOURCE: Page_21]

467   fine roots. A combination of high nutrient requirements and high transpiration rates of [SOURCE: Page_21]

468   Eucalyptus trees in highly weathered tropical soils account for very low nutrient [SOURCE: Page_21]

469   losses by deep drainage (Mareschal et al., 2013; Versini et al., 2014; Binkley et al., [SOURCE: Page_21]

470   2018). We show that nitrates leached in deep sandy soils can be taken up by tree [SOURCE: Page_21]

471   roots at a depth of 6 m between 0.6 and 2.5 years after planting in commercial [SOURCE: Page_21]

472   Eucalyptus plantations. However, the velocity of exploration of deep soil layers [SOURCE: Page_21]

473   depends on the social status of the trees. When N fertilization is concentrated in the [SOURCE: Page_21]

474   first months after planting (and even more if fertilizers are only applied in 50% of the [SOURCE: Page_21]

20 [SOURCE: Page_21]

475   inter-rows), nitrate leaching in very deep soil layers might increase the heterogeneity [SOURCE: Page_22]

476   of stands since deep nitrates could only be available for dominant trees. Field trials [SOURCE: Page_22]

477   are needed to assess if weeding and fertilizing only 50% of the inter-rows during the [SOURCE: Page_22]

478   rainy season (and the other 50% a few months later) would affect stand productivity. [SOURCE: Page_22]

479   In drought-prone areas, an increase in stand evapotranspiration due to weeds during [SOURCE: Page_22]

480   the rainy season might reduce the availability of water for trees during the next dry [SOURCE: Page_22]

481   season and therefore might increase the risk of tree mortality during prolonged [SOURCE: Page_22]

482   drought periods. The suitability of an extension of weeding and fertilization [SOURCE: Page_22]

483   throughout the year is therefore probably dependent on climate and soil properties. [SOURCE: Page_22]

484   Further studies dealing with the relationship between the social status of the trees [SOURCE: Page_22]

485   and the distance of water and nutrient uptake are also needed to improve tree-based [SOURCE: Page_22]

486   modeling approaches that intend to predict the response of tropical forests to climate [SOURCE: Page_22]

487   change. [SOURCE: Page_22]
