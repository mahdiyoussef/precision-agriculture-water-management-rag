19   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   Carbon and water budgets of forest plantations are spatially and temporally variable and [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   hardly empirically predictable. We applied G’DAY, a process-based ecophysiological model, [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   to simulate carbon and water budgets and stem biomass production of Eucalyptus plantations [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   in São Paulo State, Brazil. Our main objective was to assess the drivers of spatial variability [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   in plantation production at regional scale. We followed a multi-site calibration approach: the [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   model was first parameterized using a detailed experimental dataset. Then a subset of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   parameters were re-calibrated on two independent experimental datasets. An additional [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   genotype-specific calibration of a subset of parameters was performed. Model predictions of [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   key carbon-related variables (e.g., gross primary production, leaf area index and stem [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   biomass) and key water-related variables (e.g., plant available water and evapotranspiration) [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   agreed closely with measurements. Application of the model across ca. 27,500 ha of forests [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   planted with different genotypes of Eucalyptus indicated that the model was able to capture [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   89% of stem biomass variability measured at different ages. Several factors controlling [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   Eucalyptus production variability in time and space were grouped in three categories: soil, [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   climate, and the planted genotype. Modelling analysis showed that calibrating the model for [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   genotypic differences was critical for stem biomass prediction at regional scale, but that [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   taking into account climate and soil variability significantly improved the results. We [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   conclude that application of process-based models at regional scale can be used for accurate [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   predictions of Eucalyptus production, provided that an accurate calibration of the model for [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   key genotype-specific parameters is conducted. [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

44      Forest vegetation plays a major role in determining the state of the global climate system [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   and carbon cycle, both of which are undergoing significant anthropogenic perturbations. [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   Among forest vegetation types, Eucalyptus is the most widely planted tropical hardwood [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   genus, covering more than 20 million ha worldwide (Albaugh et al., 2013). In Brazil, forest [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   plantations cover 7.6 million ha, of which 72% are planted with high-productivity Eucalyptus [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   clones (average annual increment of 40 m^3 ha-1 yr-1 of roundwood, ranging from 25 to 60 m^3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   ha-1 yr-1 (Gonçalves et al., 2013)). Eucalyptus plantations in Brazil are generally managed in [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   6-7 years rotation, with canopy closure occurring within 2-3 years after planting. The [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   relatively low susceptibility to pests and diseases, rapid growth and high productivity, [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   adaptability to varying soil and climate, and adequate fiber quality for the industry explain [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   the expansion of commercial Eucalyptus tropical plantations worldwide. [SOURCE: Page_4]

55      Water deficit, nutrient deficiency, soil type and compaction are the main drivers of [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   Eucalyptus plantation functioning in Southern Brazil (Ryan et al., 2010; Stape et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   However, predicting how forests grow in response to soil and climate constraints, and [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   determining their carbon storage capacities remains a key challenge for modelers. Forest [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   productivity is driven by complex interactions and feedbacks among biological mechanisms. [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   Furthermore, spatial variability in resources supplies, management, and characteristics of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   genetic tree material critically influence forest productivity. [SOURCE: Page_4]

62      Ecophysiological process-based models that simulate water and carbon fluxes in forests [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   proved to be useful tools to formalize biophysical hypotheses on forest functioning and to test [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   for the importance of environmental drivers on productivity. Over the last two decades, a [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   range of process-based models was developed, varying in resolution, complexity, generality, [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   and applicability (Mäkelä et al., 2000; Battaglia et al., 2004; Corbeels et al., 2005a; Dufrêne [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   et al., 2005; Marsden et al., 2013). Some of these models were developed for research [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   purposes to understand and quantify carbon and water cycling at fine time-scale (Dufrêne et [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   al., 2005), others were constructed as management tools, in close collaboration with intended [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   end-users (Landsberg and Waring, 1997; Sands et al., 2000; Almeida et al., 2004b). [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   Importantly, wood productivity relates on both the net amount of carbon (C) sequestered by [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   trees (net C balance) and the way this C is allocated among tree organs. [SOURCE: Page_5]

73      The modelling of Eucalyptus plantations at regional scale is of critical economic [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   importance and has been the focus of a growing body of studies (e.g., Almeida et al. (2004a). [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   Marsden et al. (2013) modified the Generic Decomposition And Yield model (G’DAY) to [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   simulate the productivity of 16 Eucalyptus plantation stands of the same genotype in São [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   Paulo region, Brazil. It was shown that soil water holding capacity explains a large part of [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   spatial variability in tree growth rate and biomass productivity in this area. This study also [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   highlighted two important limitations that currently hinder regional modelling using G’DAY. [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   First, the impact of water availability on the C allocation toward root growth, leaf growth and [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   litterfall production is not correctly represented in the model. Because leaf and root areas are [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   critical C and water exchange surfaces, further improvements of the G’DAY C allocation [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   scheme is required for accurate simulations of carbon and water fluxes and productivity along [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   soil water gradients. Second, the diversity of species and hybrid Eucalyptus genotypes used [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   in Brazil’s plantations was shown to be challenging for large-scale modelling (Almeida et al. [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   (2010). Eucalyptus materials may indeed strongly differ in the control of several key [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   processes, such as photosynthesis and light use efficiency (Warrier and Venkataramanan, [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   2010; le Maire et al., accepted) and carbon allocation (Ngugi et al., 2003). However, a [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   detailed parameterization of process-based models for each genotype from field [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   measurements is today out of reach, because physiological and C partitioning measurements [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   along plantation rotation are missing for most of the numerous cultivated genotypes. Previous [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   studies therefore generally used a unique model parameterization over a whole region, either [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   by using generic parameterization at the level of plant functional type (e.g., Almeida et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   2004a), or by calibrating      the model based on some genotype-specific measurements [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   (Almeida et al., 2004a; Gonzalez-Benecke et al., 2016). Calibration can be conducted by [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   using independent measurements to parameterize the equations of the model, or by inverting [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   the model to constrain the values of these parameters (Gonzalez-Benecke et al., 2016). This [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   last option is considered more reliable for application purposes, since the model simulations [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   are constrained within the range of a plausible domain, even though equifinality of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   simulations (i.e., the possibility of ‘getting the right answers for the wrong reason’) may lead [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   to a wrong interpretation of the results. Although using a unique parameterization for regional [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   modelling proved useful to capture the response of productivity to large environmental [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   gradient, it creates an additional uncertainty that need to be quantified if the simulations are [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   to be used for management purposes. [SOURCE: Page_6]

105      The overall objectives of the present study was 1) to predict temporal and spatial [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   variations of stem biomass production in Eucalyptus plantations in the São Paulo state of [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   Brazil using the G’DAY model 2) to use the validated model to test for the effect of [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   genotype, soil and climate on plantation wood productivity at regional scale. Efforts were [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   done to improve the ability of the model in capturing the responses of forest functioning and [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   C allocation to water stress. We used a multi-site calibration approach to provide an end-to- [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   end calibration and application scheme from local to regional scale. G’DAY was first [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   optimized using detailed measurements from one experimental site. A subset of “genotype- [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   specific” parameters was then calibrated at two other sites where physiological data were also [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   available, and presenting differences in genotype and location. Finally a calibration and [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   application of the model at large spatio-temporal scale was performed using a network of [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   1472 stands. Constrained simulations were then conducted using the final model to highlight [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   the drivers of productivity at regional scale. [SOURCE: Page_6]

205   2.3. G’DAY model [SOURCE: Page_10]

206       In the present study, the G’DAY model (Comins and McMurtrie, 1993; Corbeels et al., [SOURCE: Page_10]

207   2005a; Corbeels et al., 2005b; Marsden et al., 2013) was used to simulate water and carbon [SOURCE: Page_10]

208   budgets of Eucalyptus plantations at a number of experimental sites and commercial stands in [SOURCE: Page_10]

209   the state of São Paulo, Brazil. The G’DAY model is an ecophysiological process-based [SOURCE: Page_10]

210   model, functioning at a daily time-step, which uses minimum and maximum air temperatures, [SOURCE: Page_10]

211   precipitation, vapor pressure deficit and solar radiation as daily weather inputs. G’DAY [SOURCE: Page_10]

212   simulates the water and carbon fluxes between the environment and a number of soil and tree [SOURCE: Page_10]

213   biomass pools. The ecophysiological processes in the model are represented by several sub- [SOURCE: Page_10]

214   models of plant production, soil water balance, and litter decomposition. Marsden et al. [SOURCE: Page_10]

215   (2013) modified the soil water balance in order to reflect the dynamics of moisture content in [SOURCE: Page_10]

216   three layers: litter, top soil, and rooting zone. The maximum PAW in the litter layer was [SOURCE: Page_10]

9 [SOURCE: Page_10]

217   modified to increase linearly as the C mass of the forest floor increased, whereas in the [SOURCE: Page_11]

218   rooting zone the total maximum PAW increased with rooting depth during the rotation. Here, [SOURCE: Page_11]

219   we used the version presented in details in Marsden et al. (2013), but added a new [SOURCE: Page_11]

220   modification of the C allocation scheme to better consider the impact of environmental [SOURCE: Page_11]

221   constraints such as water availability on the partitioning of C between shoots and roots and to [SOURCE: Page_11]

222   enhance the model capability of capturing seasonal leaf area index (LAI) variations. [SOURCE: Page_11]

223   Allocation fraction of the net primary production (NPP) to the different organs, is obtained [SOURCE: Page_11]

224   following a “goal-seeking” scheme, where a constant allocation value was modulated in [SOURCE: Page_11]

225   function of a target compartment biomass (see Corbeels et al. (2005a) and Marsden et al. [SOURCE: Page_11]

226   (2013)), and mortality is computed as turnover rates. We slightly modified this C allocation [SOURCE: Page_11]

227   scheme as following: [SOURCE: Page_11]

228           i)       The proportion of carbon allocated to fine roots compared to foliage [SOURCE: Page_11]

229                    production was based on an assumption of a higher allocation to root when [SOURCE: Page_11]

230                    the soil is dry (Landsberg and Sands, 2010); [SOURCE: Page_11]

231           ii)      The leaf turnover was set to increase when the soil was dry (severe and [SOURCE: Page_11]

232                    prolonged stress episodes) and was positively correlated to the production [SOURCE: Page_11]

233                    of new leaves, as observed in litterfall measurements (Pook et al., 1997); [SOURCE: Page_11]

234           iii)     The “target” value of leaf area, which came from a height-dependent target [SOURCE: Page_11]

235                    ratio between leaf area and sapwood cross-sectional area (Corbeels et al., [SOURCE: Page_11]

236                    2005a; Corbeels et al., 2005b; Marsden et al., 2013), was previously [SOURCE: Page_11]

237                    reached by modulating the carbon allocation to leaves. Within the new [SOURCE: Page_11]

238                    allocation scheme, we kept the same principle but modulated the leaf [SOURCE: Page_11]

239                    turnover ratio in order to match this algometrical constraint, while C [SOURCE: Page_11]

240                    allocation to new leaves was modulated by the equation described in i) [SOURCE: Page_11]

10 [SOURCE: Page_11]

241   Use of water from very deep layers was shown to sustain transpiration of Eucalyptus [SOURCE: Page_12]

242   plantation during extreme drought events (Christina et al., 2017), permitted by the fast soil [SOURCE: Page_12]

243   exploration of Eucalyptus trees along the rotation and the ability for the root to access soil [SOURCE: Page_12]

244   water table. A minimum access to water (i.e, a minimum value for soil PAW), was therefore [SOURCE: Page_12]

245   included in the model in order to avoid a total transpiration break and reproduce the effect of [SOURCE: Page_12]

246   deep soil water use on tree functioning observed in the field. Such minimum soil PAW was [SOURCE: Page_12]

247   however very low, and correspond to high water stress effects on trees (reduced transpiration [SOURCE: Page_12]

248   and photosynthesis). [SOURCE: Page_12]

460   4.1. Parameterization and evaluation of the model [SOURCE: Page_20]

461   We used a slightly modified version of the G’DAY allocation model based on the “balanced [SOURCE: Page_20]

462   growth hypothesis” that postulates that plants invest more carbon to roots when the limiting [SOURCE: Page_20]

19 [SOURCE: Page_20]

463   factor for growth is water or nutrients, and reduce the foliage size as drought avoiding [SOURCE: Page_21]

464   mechanism (Shipley and Meziane, 2002). In addition, the influence of water stress on [SOURCE: Page_21]

465   litterfall production and LAI dynamics was also taken into consideration, as observed on long [SOURCE: Page_21]

466   term measurements (Pook et al., 1997). Evaluation of the model showed that the [SOURCE: Page_21]

467   incorporation of the modified carbon scheme enhanced the model’s ability to capture the [SOURCE: Page_21]

468   decline of LAI as a result of soil drought, and consequently improved the simulation of leaf [SOURCE: Page_21]

469   biomass. [SOURCE: Page_21]

470          In the DATASET 1 calibration, the final values of some parameters were restrained [SOURCE: Page_21]

471   by the lower or upper bounds of prior distributions (Supplementary Table 1). In the present [SOURCE: Page_21]

472   work the objective was mainly to get plausible prior probability distribution of parameters to [SOURCE: Page_21]

473   be further included on larger scale applications. These parameter values, even when [SOURCE: Page_21]

474   constrained on bound limits, are still plausible and gave good results at this site with respect [SOURCE: Page_21]

475   to carbon and water measurements. On other datasets, parameters also were constrained by [SOURCE: Page_21]

476   bounds, but this was necessary because of the fewer measurements available for calibration. [SOURCE: Page_21]

477   The overall picture and the good results obtained on independent data on DATASET 4 [SOURCE: Page_21]

478   showed that these limits were sufficient for a first trial. More spatial information on [SOURCE: Page_21]

479   vegetation characteristics, such as variables estimated from remote sensing (le Maire et al., [SOURCE: Page_21]

480   2011b; Baghdadi et al., 2014; Baghdadi et al., 2015), and to more datasets could allow to [SOURCE: Page_21]

481   improve data assimilation, with a refinement of the distribution of calibrated parameters [SOURCE: Page_21]

482   probability distribution. [SOURCE: Page_21]

483   Widely distributed Eucalyptus species show high levels of genetic diversity in light [SOURCE: Page_21]

484   absorption, gross primary production and differences in C allocation which determine [SOURCE: Page_21]

485   together genotype productivity (Aspinwall et al., 2018; le Maire et al., accepted). In the [SOURCE: Page_21]

486   current study, we calibrated 22 parameters to address differences among genotype. [SOURCE: Page_21]

487   Parameters related to leaf and branch mortality and turnover rate, C allocation, and stomatal [SOURCE: Page_21]

20 [SOURCE: Page_21]

488   conductance in response to climatic factors showed the greatest variations among genotypes [SOURCE: Page_22]

489   (Supplementary Table 1). Previous research on similar Brazilian Eucalyptus plantations has [SOURCE: Page_22]

490   reported variations in photosynthetic capacity between E. grandis × urophylla hybrids in [SOURCE: Page_22]

491   comparison with E. grandis impacting productivity (Almeida et al., 2004a). Leaf [SOURCE: Page_22]

492   photosynthetic parameters of Eucalyptus plantations can also vary considerably within clones [SOURCE: Page_22]

493   of the same species (Shem et al., 2009; Warrier and Venkataramanan, 2010; le Maire et al., [SOURCE: Page_22]

494   accepted). Other reports suggested that C allocation to roots and other C sinks may be [SOURCE: Page_22]

495   dominant drivers of genotypic variation in productivity in response to environmental factors [SOURCE: Page_22]

496   (Resco de Dios et al., 2016). This results in large differences in light absorption and light use [SOURCE: Page_22]

497   efficiencies and growth along rotations (le Maire et al., accepted). [SOURCE: Page_22]

498   4.2. Drivers of modelled spatial variability of stand biomass [SOURCE: Page_22]

499          Several factors influence biomass production in Eucalyptus plantations among which [SOURCE: Page_22]

500   the climate, soil type, genetic material, and their interactions. One of the main advantages of [SOURCE: Page_22]

501   process-based models, following proper parameterization, is the possibility to identify, [SOURCE: Page_22]

502   quantify and disentangle the influence of spatial variation in soil, climate, and genotypes on [SOURCE: Page_22]

503   productivity. Among these factors, genotype specific parameters were the most important for [SOURCE: Page_22]

504   the accuracy of stem biomass predictions (Fig. 7 and Table 3). Genotype-specific calibration [SOURCE: Page_22]

505   of ecophysiological models is critical for accurate characterization of stem biomass [SOURCE: Page_22]

506   production among stands planted with different hybrids of Eucalyptus. In the present study, [SOURCE: Page_22]

507   genotype-specific parameters were obtained by constrained optimization on only two [SOURCE: Page_22]

508   variables, trunk biomass and height. In the future, other measurements could help improving [SOURCE: Page_22]

509   the estimation of these parameters, such as data issued from remote sensing (le Maire, 2018). [SOURCE: Page_22]

510          The spatial variability of climate was the second factor in importance for prediction [SOURCE: Page_22]

511   accuracy of stem biomass (Fig. 7 and Table 3). G’DAY was sensitive to varying climate [SOURCE: Page_22]

512   scenarios: a reduced error in stem biomass and plant height predictions of about 20% was [SOURCE: Page_22]

21 [SOURCE: Page_22]

513   obtained when taking into account the local climate compared with holding the climate [SOURCE: Page_23]

514   scenario constant for all stands (from a gridded dataset). While this result is expected on large [SOURCE: Page_23]

515   climate gradient (“regional scale”), we showed here that even on reduced area (“regional [SOURCE: Page_23]

516   scale”) of 183 × 151 km the climate has a major importance. Rainfall and drought occurrence [SOURCE: Page_23]

517   was the most important climatic factor constraining the growth of Eucalyptus in this area, as [SOURCE: Page_23]

518   observed in other studies (Mummery and Battaglia, 2004; Stape et al., 2004; Whitehead and [SOURCE: Page_23]

519   Beadle, 2004). In addition, research conducted in tropical Brazilian forest, near the Atlantic [SOURCE: Page_23]

520   Coast, reported 70 to 110% annual variations in production in response to soil and air [SOURCE: Page_23]

521   humidity (Almeida et al., 2010). Accuracy of the input rainfall dataset could be ameliorated [SOURCE: Page_23]

522   using denser networks of pluviometers. [SOURCE: Page_23]

523   Among the three drivers of modelled spatial variability of stand biomass, varying the soil [SOURCE: Page_23]

524   type parameter was the least important driver, reducing the error in prediction by only 15% [SOURCE: Page_23]

525   (Fig. 7 and Table 3). Nevertheless, the soil x climate or soil x genotype interaction effects [SOURCE: Page_23]

526   reduced the error in stem biomass prediction in a larger way than climate alone or genotype [SOURCE: Page_23]

527   alone, indicating an improvement of the simulations when considering variations in soil water [SOURCE: Page_23]

528   holding capacity, in the studied range. A previous study aiming at simulating the spatial [SOURCE: Page_23]

529   variability of Eucalyptus plantation in São Paulo state using the G’DAY model reported [SOURCE: Page_23]

530   significant improvement in stem biomass prediction when using stand-specific PAW values [SOURCE: Page_23]

531   compared with holding it constant for all stands (Marsden et al., 2013). The authors attributed [SOURCE: Page_23]

532   the higher performances of simulations using stand-specific PAW values to the linkage of [SOURCE: Page_23]

533   maximum PAW with tree height. The current version of the model has shown improvements [SOURCE: Page_23]

534   in simulation of stem biomass using stand-specific PAW values as well; however, it did not [SOURCE: Page_23]

535   show much improvements when comparing all drivers variable (scenario 8) with varying [SOURCE: Page_23]

536   climate and genotype (scenario 5) (Figure 7). This indicates that further improvements in the [SOURCE: Page_23]

537   model responses to the climate x genotype x soil interactions is needed. Improvement should [SOURCE: Page_23]

22 [SOURCE: Page_23]

538   also be done on spatial parameterization of the maximum plant available water (PAWmax). [SOURCE: Page_24]

539   In the current study, this gridded map has several limitations: 1) the texture class associated [SOURCE: Page_24]

540   to each soil type of the soil map was not always precise, in particular for some complex soils; [SOURCE: Page_24]

541   2) associating a unique soil textural class in function of soil types do not allow to represent [SOURCE: Page_24]

542   eventual variability within a soil type; 3) the association of a texture class to a single [SOURCE: Page_24]

543   PAWmax value does not reflect the variability visible on Supplementary Figure 5; 4) [SOURCE: Page_24]

544   differences in soil depth is not taken into account: here all stands are supposed to have access [SOURCE: Page_24]

545   to the first ~3 meters of soil, and keep having minimal water access along their rotation. [SOURCE: Page_24]

546   However, even with these limitations, the use of these gridded estimates of soil PAWmax did [SOURCE: Page_24]

547   improved the results compared to using a single value at all location. [SOURCE: Page_24]

548 [SOURCE: Page_24]

549   5. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_24]

550          A modified version of the G’DAY model was able to simulate seasonal variations in [SOURCE: Page_24]

551   growth and the exchange of key C and H2O variables between the ecosystem and the [SOURCE: Page_24]

552   atmosphere along complete commercial rotations of Eucalyptus plantations. Application of [SOURCE: Page_24]

553   the model at the regional scale showed reasonable level of accuracy in the simulation of stem [SOURCE: Page_24]

554   biomass and plant height. The main drivers of spatial variability in simulated stem [SOURCE: Page_24]

555   productivity was the genetic differences among genotypes followed by climatic and soil [SOURCE: Page_24]

556   variables. This work will benefit in the future from other data sources, such as remote- [SOURCE: Page_24]

557   sensing, allowing to further constrain the mechanisms embodied in ecophysiological models [SOURCE: Page_24]

558   at various temporal and spatial scales. [SOURCE: Page_24]
