44   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

45     Inland waters are a habitat for rich species diversity. Approximately 126 000 inland aquatic [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   species have been described according to IUCN (2009), representing the 9.5% of all currently [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   identified species and circumscribed in a living environment which is equal to just 0.01% of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   total terrestrial surface (Balian et al., 2008). Nevertheless, 65% of continental waters are [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   moderately or highly threatened by anthropogenic disturbance and climate change. With the [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   prospect of an intensification of anthropic pressure on ecosystems and an increase in freshwater [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   needs driven by population growth, a lot of efforts have been dedicated to the development of [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   more sustainable water management strategies (Davis et al., 2015; Lapointe et al., 2014). While [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   these efforts have been capable of ensuring substantial improvement of water security for [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   humans, there is still a mismatch with what has been achieved in terms of biodiversity [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   conservation, partly because of the likelihood that the ways to meet water needs of humans and [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   ecosystems can be substantially antagonistic (Vörösmarty et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_4]

57     In this context, several life cycle impact assessment (LCIA) models have been proposed to link [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   freshwater consumptive use to biodiversity loss (Núñez et al., 2016) and addressing specifically [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   wetlands and surface water-dependent ecosystems (Amores et al., 2013; Hanafiah et al., 2011; [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   Tendall et al., 2014; Verones et al., 2013b). These approaches provide endpoint indicators built [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   on cause-effect pathways in which the impact characterization factor (CF, eq. 1) results from the [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   combination of three sub-factors (Núñez et al., 2018). The fate factor (FF) represents the [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   environmental change (e.g. change in m^3/y river discharge or m^2 wetland area) due to water [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   consumption, defined as the withdrawn water that is not returned to the original drainage basin [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   (International Organization for Standardization ISO/TC 207/SC 5, 2014). The exposure factor [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   (XF) indicates how far this alteration can be offset, e.g. in a river it can be approximated to 1 [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   since most freshwater species have less mobility than terrestrial species to compensate the lack [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   of water. Finally, the effect factor (EF) describes the consequence on the ecosystem (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   potentially disappeared fraction of species, PDF). [SOURCE: Page_5]

127   As for the abiotic components of habitat (e.g. morphodynamic and physicochemical aspects of [SOURCE: Page_8]

128   groundwater and surface water bodies), the whole ecosystem may undergo cascading effects [SOURCE: Page_8]

129   triggered by water balance changes. In fact, while an ecological system remains relatively stable [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   over time, a human induced alteration may promote a directional shift to a new dynamic [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   equilibrium resulting from the ecological response of the first affected ecosystem compartments [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   and of all those subsequently connected. These mechanisms involve all inter- and intra-specific [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   relationships between different biota and are only generically represented in Fig. 1. As an [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   example, species loss and reduced riparian vegetation cover limits shading on rivers which in [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   turn influences food and shelter availability for reproduction and juvenile growth in fish and [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   macroinvertebrate species (Li and Dudgeon, 2008; Mokany et al., 2008; Riley et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   Moreover, under environmental stress, trophic webs play an important role in determining the [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   stability and evolution of an ecological community (Downing and Leibold, 2010; Thompson et [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   It is also necessary to bear in mind that habitat characteristics can be influenced by multiple [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   drivers, for instance nutrient availability in water can be determined by flow regime, thermal [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   regime and the presence or absence of forest buffers (Kennen et al., 2008; Kløve et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   This draws attention to the fact that habitats not only drive the establishment of a specific type of [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   ecosystem, but can also be shaped themselves by the biota they harbor. These ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   mechanisms involve all abiotic and biotic changes induced or limited by the presence of certain [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   ecological communities (Berke, 2010; Jones et al., 2010). In addition, habitat modifications can [SOURCE: Page_8]

405   5. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_21]

406     River reach characterization factors may represent a useful instrument for site-specific LCA, [SOURCE: Page_21]

407   complementary to Environmental Risk Assessment (ERA) and environmental impact assessment [SOURCE: Page_21]

408   (EIA) (Larrey-Lassalle et al., 2017). Multi-species aggregation at the reach scale and spatial [SOURCE: Page_21]

409   aggregation at the watershed scale represent a parsimonious approach to modeling habitat change [SOURCE: Page_21]

410   potential, which is necessary to respond to the need for large-scale spatialized water management [SOURCE: Page_21]

411   and LCIA approaches advocated by ISO 14044 (International Organization for Standardization [SOURCE: Page_21]

412   ISO/TC 207/SC 5, 2006) and increasingly applied in LCA (Loiseau et al., 2014; Nitschelm et al., [SOURCE: Page_21]

413   2016; Patouillard et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_21]

414      5.1. Model uncertainty [SOURCE: Page_21]

20 [SOURCE: Page_21]

415     Optimizing the spatial resolution is crucial to reduce the uncertainty of the impact assessment [SOURCE: Page_22]

416   coming from neglecting spatial variability. In principle, the higher the spatial resolution of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

417   CF, the smaller the uncertainty contribution due to spatial variability. However, the choice of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

418   most appropriate scale depends largely on the availability of regionalized inventory data [SOURCE: Page_22]

419   (Henderson et al., 2017; Mutel et al., 2012). In addition, the characterization factor presented in [SOURCE: Page_22]

420   this study is built on the HCP model which is essentially an effect factor. The development of a [SOURCE: Page_22]

421   regionalized fate factor at comparable spatial resolutions is therefore necessary for the [SOURCE: Page_22]

422   optimization of the resolution of the characterization model. [SOURCE: Page_22]

423     Section 8 of Appendix A discusses another potential source of spatial uncertainty deriving [SOURCE: Page_22]

424   from the aggregation formula of watershed CF. The weighted average approach implies [SOURCE: Page_22]

425   overestimating impact assessment results for certain river segments compared to the weighted [SOURCE: Page_22]

426   median method which, on the contrary, shows higher risk of underestimation. The choice of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

427   best aggregation method can thus be subject to the need of more or less conservative approaches [SOURCE: Page_22]

428   depending on the specific application scenario (e.g. LCA in critical regions where small stream [SOURCE: Page_22]

429   habitats are endangered or exposed to multiple stressors). [SOURCE: Page_22]

430     The uncertainty deriving from model parameters should also be considered in the [SOURCE: Page_22]

431   interpretation of characterization results. For instance, since discharge data for ungauged river [SOURCE: Page_22]

432   reaches are hardly available, in the RHT database flow duration curves are modeled for the [SOURCE: Page_22]

433   whole river network, and therefore Q50 and Q90 values. The same goes for hydraulic geometry [SOURCE: Page_22]

434   variables modeled through Estimkart. Uncertainty plays a relevant role especially for small [SOURCE: Page_22]

435   catchments, low discharges and mountainous areas. On the contrary, parameters estimates are [SOURCE: Page_22]

436   more accurate in downstream river segments and bigger catchments, where the boundaries are [SOURCE: Page_22]

437   defined more precisely (Lamouroux et al., 2014). Contrary to spatial uncertainty, model [SOURCE: Page_22]

21 [SOURCE: Page_22]

438   parameters uncertainty is therefore potentially higher at the river reach scale than it is for habitat [SOURCE: Page_23]

439   alteration quantification over large areas and thus spatially aggregated CFs taking into account [SOURCE: Page_23]

440   average watershed conditions. In support of these considerations, the uncertainty analysis by [SOURCE: Page_23]

441   Miguel et al. (2016) demonstrated that habitat changes at the regional scale are generally robust [SOURCE: Page_23]

442   despite high uncertainties at the reach scale. For this reason, a reliable CF for LCIA should aim [SOURCE: Page_23]

443   at minimizing spatial variability of inventory data, FF and EF, although ensuring reasonable [SOURCE: Page_23]

444   modeling of average watershed characteristics. [SOURCE: Page_23]

445      5.2. Operationalization and model extension [SOURCE: Page_23]

446     For a complete operationalization of the CF, associating a multimedia fate factor to the HCP [SOURCE: Page_23]

447   model would be needed (Núñez et al., 2018). This implies including the consideration of [SOURCE: Page_23]

448   different water sources (groundwater and surface water), the water flow transport between [SOURCE: Page_23]

449   different compartments in the hydrological cycle (e.g. lateral flow from irrigated land, returning [SOURCE: Page_23]

450   to the original water basin), along with water withdrawal and discharge areas and therefore the [SOURCE: Page_23]

451   spatialization of hydrologic alteration induced by water consumption (e.g. direct and indirect [SOURCE: Page_23]

452   alteration, potential longitudinal cascade effects on surface waters as in Loubet et al., 2013). In [SOURCE: Page_23]

453   the same way, the characterization of different water uses in the life cycle inventory should [SOURCE: Page_23]

454   justify the adoption of an effect factor for marginal water consumption (i.e. life cycle impact of [SOURCE: Page_23]

455   river damming would imply non-marginal hydrologic alteration in river basins). [SOURCE: Page_23]

456     Data availability could be the major constraint for refining and extending the model outside of [SOURCE: Page_23]

457   France. At present, comprehensive databases including all necessary input data (i.e. discharge, [SOURCE: Page_23]

458   substrate composition, hydraulic geometry) are not available globally, although useful data [SOURCE: Page_23]

459   sources are currently being developed (e.g. Lehner, 2018). The model can be therefore applied [SOURCE: Page_23]

22 [SOURCE: Page_23]

460   using local data. For an application on the larger scale the input variables should be modeled if [SOURCE: Page_24]

461   not available, such as in the French RHT hydrographic network, or the model should be [SOURCE: Page_24]

462   simplified retaining the factors that most determine characterization results (see section 4.1). For [SOURCE: Page_24]

463   improving taxa coverage, more habitat equations should be included when possible (other [SOURCE: Page_24]

464   freshwater fish species could be assigned to the four guilds based on habitat preference). In [SOURCE: Page_24]

465   addition, using Q50 and Q90 at monthly resolution would improve the precision and temporal [SOURCE: Page_24]

466   relevance of the CF, since river flow regimes can be different in relation to topographical and [SOURCE: Page_24]

467   climatic conditions. [SOURCE: Page_24]

468   6. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_24]

469     In this study a new, mechanistic characterization factor based on freshwater physical habitat [SOURCE: Page_24]

470   modeling has been presented. The effect factor model has been applied at the river and watershed [SOURCE: Page_24]

471   scale to assess marginal impact of water consumption on instream ecosystems. HCP has been [SOURCE: Page_24]

472   calculated at Q50 and Q90 as representative of the median discharge condition and dry season [SOURCE: Page_24]

473   respectively. [SOURCE: Page_24]

474     A simplified fate factor has been associated to the HCP model, limiting the CF applicability to [SOURCE: Page_24]

475   surface water consumption LCIA. In addition, the appropriate impact assessment spatial scale [SOURCE: Page_24]

476   has to be evaluated considering spatialized life cycle inventory data availability and the [SOURCE: Page_24]

477   resolution of the fate factor, to limit the risk of hyper-regionalization (Heijungs, 2012) and the [SOURCE: Page_24]

478   uncertainty of the model’s parameters. However, modeling the potential midpoint impact on [SOURCE: Page_24]

479   freshwater physical habitat availability can be considered as a first breakthrough from empirical [SOURCE: Page_24]

480   towards mechanistic quantification of instream ecosystems damage due to marginal water [SOURCE: Page_24]

481   consumption. [SOURCE: Page_24]

23 [SOURCE: Page_24]

482     Notwithstanding the environmental relevance of HCP as a proxy for ecosystem damage, the [SOURCE: Page_25]

483   development of large scale endpoint indicators for LCIA against this background should consider [SOURCE: Page_25]
