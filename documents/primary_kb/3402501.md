## 8    ABSTRACT

9    Agricultural practices are expected to have significant effects on soil physical properties, such [SOURCE: Page_2]

10    as soil structure and soil water retention (SWR) properties are among the relevant properties [SOURCE: Page_2]

11    that can be used to assess these effects. Ex-situ measurements are costly and time-consuming. [SOURCE: Page_2]

12    Visible (vis) and near infrared (NIR) spectroscopy has been widely used as a rapid, cost- [SOURCE: Page_2]

13    effective and nondestructive technique to predict many soil properties, including soil water [SOURCE: Page_2]

14    content (SWC). In this study, we explored the ability of vis-NIR to assess the effects of soil [SOURCE: Page_2]

15    tillage and fertilization on SWR. The study was performed on silty soil samples with 10.4 g [SOURCE: Page_2]

16    kg-1 of organic carbon content and a pH of 5-6.5. Undisturbed soil blocks were collected from [SOURCE: Page_2]

17    an experimental station located in Brittany, France. In 2012, the field was designed as two [SOURCE: Page_2]

18    separate experiments, each with a different tillage system (conventional tillage (CT) and [SOURCE: Page_2]

19    shallow tillage (ST)). For each experiment, two fertilizer types were considered (mineral and [SOURCE: Page_2]

20    organic). Six undisturbed soil blocks were sampled at each of two depths (0-15 cm and 15-25 [SOURCE: Page_2]

21    cm) and from each agricultural practice. Soil was sampled on two dates: before starting the [SOURCE: Page_2]

1 © 2019 published by Elsevier. This manuscript is made available under the CC BY NC user license https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/ [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   treatment (2012) and after 5 years of agricultural practices (2017). From each soil block, four [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   aggregates 3-4 cm wide and 5-6 cm long were collected. The whole aggregates were set at a [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   number of matric potentials, from saturation to the permanent wilting point (pF=4.2). At a [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   given pressure head, soil samples were scanned in triplicate to acquire reflectance spectra [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   from 400-2500 nm using a handheld spectrometer (ASD Fieldspec®). We focused on the [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   absorption band near 1920 nm and used a new NIR spectral index based on the band’s full [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   width at half maximum, called SWSI. Our results showed a linear relation between SWSI and [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   SWC (R² > 0.99). Moreover, the slope and intercept of this linear relation were significantly [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   correlated with water holding capacity (WHC) and soil quality. Using these parameters, we [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   found that SWR and soil physical quality under CT were significantly greater under cattle [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   manure (CTC) than under mineral fertilizer (CTM) or at T$_0$. This significant effect was more [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   pronounced at 15-25 cm. In ST treatments, SWR properties and soil quality were significantly [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   lower than at T$_0$. More importantly, our results show that the slope and intercept of the linear [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   relation offer a way to summarize and assess the effects of agricultural practices on SWR [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   properties and soil structural quality. [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   Keywords: vis-NIR, NIR index, SWSI, soil water retention, soil physical quality, tillage, [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

40   Improved soil management techniques require identifying agricultural practices that are [SOURCE: Page_4]

41   environmentally friendly and sustain crop yields. Therefore, there is growing interest in [SOURCE: Page_4]

42   developing systems of shallow tillage (ST) as an alternative to conventional tillage (CT) [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   (Roger-Estrade et al., 2010). CT systems have adverse effects on soil physical, chemical and [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   biological properties and degrade soil ecosystems (IPBES, 2018). They may lead to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   breakdown of organic matter, loss of soil moisture and change of water balance, the spread of [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   salinity, increased susceptibility to wind and water erosion, and loss of soil biodiversity [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   (Strudley et al., 2008). Conservation tillage systems, such as no-tillage (NT) and ST, can [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   therefore help protect soil against compaction, erosion and degradation. ST and NT affect [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   soil physical properties, such as bulk density (BD), soil water content (SWC), soil stability [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   (Bottinelli et al., 2017; Czyż and Dexter, 2008; Lipiec et al., 2006; Strudley et al., 2008) and [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   soil physical quality (Dexter, 2004b; Kabiri et al., 2015). However, concern has been [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   expressed that NT crop production could lead to excess soil compaction. Excess compaction [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   may decrease soil aeration and crop root development, thus restricting water uptake, nutrient [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   availability and overall crop growth (Dalal et al., 1991; Lipiec and Nosalewicz, 2004; [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   Mahmoodlu et al., 2016). Compaction can be assessed by BD measurements and soil water [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   retention (SWR) properties. [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   For agricultural soils, fertilization is also a source of variability for SWR properties both in [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   space and time (Bottinelli et al., 2017; Jiao et al., 2006; Mikha and Rice, 2004). Conventional [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   soil management (i.e. intensive cultivation and the use of synthetic agrochemicals) has often [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   been associated with degraded soil quality, whereas organic farming has been suggested as an [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   approach to conserve and protect the soil environment (Abiven et al., 2009; Maillard and [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   Angers, 2014). Several studies have investigated the differences between organically and [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   conventionally managed soils (Bai et al., 2018; Maillard and Angers, 2014), but few have [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   sought to deal with potential differences in SWR properties and soil quality quantitatively in [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   combination with tillage systems. [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   Accurate assessment of soil properties, traditionally using elemental analysis, is time- [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   consuming and costly. Hence, sensor techniques are increasingly used to rapidly determine [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   soil properties in the field (Minasny et al., 2014; Tauro et al., 2018). Among them, visible [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   (vis) and near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy have proven their potential to quickly estimate [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   several soil properties, including particle-size distribution (Gomez et al., 2013; Janik et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   2009), soil aggregate-size distribution (Cañasveras et al., 2010; Sarathjith et al., 2014), pH [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   (Viscarra Rossel and Behrens, 2010), soil organic carbon (SOC) content (Nocita et al., 2013) [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   and cation-exchange capacity (Soriano-Disla et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   Rather than only estimating specific points of the SWR curve, recent studies developed [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   transfer functions for soil hydraulic properties using spectral reflectance data. Using multiple [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   linear regression between soil hydraulic properties and multiple attributes of measured [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   spectral reflectance, Santra et al. (2009) defined transfer functions, called spectrotransfer [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   functions (STFs). They used stepwise regression analysis and principal-component- [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   transformed spectral data (350-2500 nm) as well as mimicked Landstat-ETM+ spectral bands [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   to estimate van Genuchten–Mualem (vGM) soil hydraulic parameters (α, n, and Ks) of the wet [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   part of the SWR curve (pressure head > −80 kPa). Their results were promising for parameter [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   n, but they failed to estimate parameters α and Ks. A major weakness of this approach is that [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   the soil hydraulic parameters were estimated only from a small range of measured SWC. [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   Other limitations include the use of disturbed soil samples, which usually lose some structural [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   attributes, and the fact that they were collected from topsoil. Thus, the STFs may only be [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   applicable to topsoils. Using spectral data as pedotransfer function (PTF) input provides an [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   effective way of including temporal processes in hydrological models. Babaeian et al. (2015a, [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   2015b) derived and validated the accuracy of STFs and spectral pedotransfer functions [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   (SPTFs) to predict the hydraulic parameters of the vGM SWR model. STFs relate hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   vGM parameters directly to spectral reflectance values, while SPTFs use additional basic soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   data such as particle-size distribution and BD. The parametric STFs and SPTFs for the vGM [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   model parameters were more accurate at low and intermediate water contents than PTFs, [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   which were more accurate close to saturation. Both STFs and SPTFs estimated the SWR [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   curve with accuracy similar to PTFs. [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   Recently,Soltani et al. (2019) evaluated the potential of NIR spectroscopy to estimate SWC. [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   They defined a spectral index called the “soil water spectral index” (SWSI), based on the full [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   width at half maximum of the absorption band near 1920 nm. They showed that the SWSI [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   decreases linearly with an increase in SWC. Moreover, they found that the parameters of this [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   linear relation were correlated with soil texture and SOC content. Furthermore, their results [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   indicate that SWSI can be useful for studying how texture and SOC content affect SWR [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   properties, water holding capacity (WHC) and therefore soil structure. [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   Based on Soltani et al.’s (2019) results, we examined the effectiveness and robustness of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   SWSI in estimating medium-term (five year) impacts of CT and ST, combined with two [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   fertilizer inputs, on SWR properties. We present a comparative analysis of spectral [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   information and soil property data to assess tillage and fertilization impacts on SWR [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   properties and soil physical quality. [SOURCE: Page_6]

371   4. 1. Impacts on soil organic carbon content [SOURCE: Page_18]

372   The effects of soil tillage and fertilization on soil properties vary, and these variations depend [SOURCE: Page_18]

373   on the system chosen. Many studies have compared the influence of CT and ST on soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

374   physical, chemical and biological properties, often reporting contradictory results (Alvarez [SOURCE: Page_18]

375   and Steinbach, 2009; Balesdent et al., 2000; Kabiri et al., 2015). The effects of tillage and [SOURCE: Page_18]

376   fertilization on SOC content dynamics have long been studied because of their impacts on soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

377   quality and functioning. As expected, organic fertilization immediately increases SOC [SOURCE: Page_18]

378   content, generally in proportion to the amount of carbon applied (Larney and Angers, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_18]

379   Our results showed significantly higher SOC content after five years of cattle manure [SOURCE: Page_18]

380   fertilization than with mineral fertilization. Besides fertilization, SOC content was higher [SOURCE: Page_18]

381   under ST than under CT. In this case, the positive effect of ST on SOC content was observed [SOURCE: Page_18]

382   only at 0-15 cm, and CT had higher SOC contents at 15-25 cm than ST. In ST soils, crop [SOURCE: Page_18]

383   residues and cattle manure enter and decompose in the topsoil. Thus, SOC content at 0-15 cm [SOURCE: Page_18]

384   is generally higher under ST than under CT. The fresh organic matter that is lost during tillage [SOURCE: Page_18]

385   and decomposes at depth can also explain the high SOC content under CT at 15-25 cm. Many [SOURCE: Page_18]

386   studies tend to show considerably higher rates of carbon mineralization in the topsoil under [SOURCE: Page_18]

387   ST or NT than under CT (Bescansa et al., 2006; Green et al., 2007). Our results suggest that [SOURCE: Page_18]

388   five years of CT and cattle manure input increase SOC contents at 15-25 cm. [SOURCE: Page_18]

389   4. 2. Impacts on bulk density [SOURCE: Page_18]

390   Reviews of how tillage systems influence BD have yielded contradictory results. Some [SOURCE: Page_18]

391   studies found that BD increased under NT compared to CT or ST (Alvarez and Steinbach, [SOURCE: Page_18]

392   2009; Bescansa et al., 2006; Osunbitan et al., 2005; Salem et al., 2015). Conversely, other [SOURCE: Page_18]

393   reviews found that tillage systems had inconsistent effects on BD and total porosity (Kabiri et [SOURCE: Page_18]

17 [SOURCE: Page_18]

394   al., 2015; Strudley et al., 2008). In the present study, BD at 0-15 cm was higher under both ST [SOURCE: Page_19]

395   and CT than at T$_0$, which agrees with Strudley et al.’s (2008) results. BD was also slightly [SOURCE: Page_19]

396   higher under CT than under ST, perhaps due to the longer persistence of loosened soil after [SOURCE: Page_19]

397   chiseling than after moldboard plowing (Richard et al., 2004). The decrease in BD after ST [SOURCE: Page_19]

398   agrees with the results obtained by Green et al. (2003), Moret and Arrúe (2007) and Strudley [SOURCE: Page_19]

399   et al. (2008), among others. However, BD decreased only at 0-15 cm, with no significant [SOURCE: Page_19]

400   difference between ST and CT at 15-25 cm, which is similar to Ferreras et al.’s (2000) [SOURCE: Page_19]

401   findings. These results suggest that soils under CT were denser, most likely due to subsequent [SOURCE: Page_19]

402   compaction in the topsoil. Plant root development may have increased soil porosity and [SOURCE: Page_19]

403   consequently decreased BD under ST, since ST could favor more root development in the [SOURCE: Page_19]

404   topsoil. Indeed, increased soil porosity due to increased SOC content at 15-25 cm in tilled [SOURCE: Page_19]

405   soils may explain the lower BD in the study plots. [SOURCE: Page_19]

439   4. 4. Impacts on soil water retention properties [SOURCE: Page_20]

440   The relation between pore-size distribution and SWR properties among soil tillage systems [SOURCE: Page_20]

441   and fertilization is complex. Water is held in the spaces (pores) between soil particles and [SOURCE: Page_20]

442   within films surrounding these particles. SOC content and particle-size distribution affect a [SOURCE: Page_20]

443   soil’s ability to retain moisture and its WHC. The sum of the adsorbed and retained SWC is [SOURCE: Page_20]

19 [SOURCE: Page_20]

444   equal to the saturated SWC (Liu et al., 2013). Many factors can influence WHC, and the [SOURCE: Page_21]

445   response to tillage may be uncertain (Strudley et al., 2008). Azooz and Arshad (2001) [SOURCE: Page_21]

446   measured SWR at six matric suctions from 5-160 kPa and found that the rate of soil drying at [SOURCE: Page_21]

447   0-30 cm was significantly higher under CT than NT, while the rate of wetting was [SOURCE: Page_21]

448   significantly higher under NT. In the present study, SWR characteristics, particularly WHC, [SOURCE: Page_21]

449   were greater under CT than at T$_0$. The differences in SWR observed between treatments [SOURCE: Page_21]

450   resulted from a change in the pore-size distribution for compaction in a range of sizes [SOURCE: Page_21]

451   corresponding to pores that contribute to SWR. Our results showed that tillage and [SOURCE: Page_21]

452   fertilization affect SWR properties for pressure heads ranging from –1 to –1585 kPa. The [SOURCE: Page_21]

453   increase in retained SWC from –1 to –31.6 kPa probably corresponds to an increase in macro- [SOURCE: Page_21]

454   and mesopore volume. In the same way, the increase in retained SWC from –31.6 to –1582 [SOURCE: Page_21]

455   kPa would correspond to the increase in storage micropore volume. The presence of small [SOURCE: Page_21]

456   pores could have favored higher SWC, and under CT, cattle manure fertilization helped [SOURCE: Page_21]

457   maintain higher SWC at 15-25 cm. [SOURCE: Page_21]

458   4. 5. Impacts on soil structure quality [SOURCE: Page_21]

459   Soil physical quality, as indicated by SDexter, was adequate for the soil and layers studied. [SOURCE: Page_21]

460   Regardless of the treatment, mean SDexter was above the critical value (0.035) proposed by [SOURCE: Page_21]

461   Dexter (2004a), indicating good soil structural and physical quality. Even after the repeated [SOURCE: Page_21]

462   traffic over a five year period in both the ST and CT experimental trials, the soil maintained [SOURCE: Page_21]

463   good structure. In addition, SDexter tended to increase in organic fertilizer treatments, but not [SOURCE: Page_21]

464   with mineral fertilization. Overall, compared to T$_0$, physical soil quality in both CT and ST [SOURCE: Page_21]

465   trials decreased, except for an improving trend at 15-25 cm with cattle manure input under [SOURCE: Page_21]

466   CT. It is likely that spreading cattle manure increased SOC content and improved soil [SOURCE: Page_21]

467   structure. SOC content is an important indicator of soil quality because of its significant [SOURCE: Page_21]

468   effects on soil physical, chemical and biological properties. SOC is closely related to soil [SOURCE: Page_21]

20 [SOURCE: Page_21]

469   aggregate formation and stabilization (Balesdent et al., 2000). It is also strongly affected by [SOURCE: Page_22]

470   agricultural management practices such as tillage (Six et al., 2002). In general, increasing [SOURCE: Page_22]

471   SOC content can generally improve aggregate stability, suggesting that organic fertilization [SOURCE: Page_22]

472   promotes greater structural stability and subsequently an improvement in soil structural [SOURCE: Page_22]

473   quality. [SOURCE: Page_22]

474   According to Dexter (2004a), SDexter generally decreases with increasing BD. This agrees with [SOURCE: Page_22]

475   the data shown here, in which the lowest SDexter values coincided with the highest BDs. The [SOURCE: Page_22]

476   relation between SDexter and BD, however, depends on soil texture (Dexter, 2004a). Recently, [SOURCE: Page_22]

477   Naderi-Boldaji and Keller (2016) found a positive correlation between soil compaction and [SOURCE: Page_22]

478   Ln (1/SDexter) that is less sensitive to soil texture. SDexter was generally lower under CT and ST [SOURCE: Page_22]

479   than at the initial state. The compaction of soil due to tractor use in CT and ST could explain [SOURCE: Page_22]

480   the decrease in SDexter and thus soil physical quality. SDexter is derived from the SWR curve. [SOURCE: Page_22]

481   Therefore, it is closely related to pore-size distribution, which agrees with the variations in [SOURCE: Page_22]

482   pore-size distribution observed among the treatments. [SOURCE: Page_22]

486   the SWSI NIR spectral index developed by Soltani et al. (2019) and the parameters of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

487   SWC-SWSI relation. SWSI showed a significant linear relation with measured SWC, with an [SOURCE: Page_22]

488   R$_2$ > 0.99 for all soil samples, which agrees with Soltani et al.’s (2019) results. They obtained [SOURCE: Page_22]

489   a similar linear relation between SWC and SWSI from several soil samples taken from the [SOURCE: Page_22]

490   organo-mineral A horizon (0-30 cm) in Brittany. SWC decreases linearly as SWSI increases. [SOURCE: Page_22]

491   As suggested by Soltani et al. (2019), the AvS indicates how much SWC changes during [SOURCE: Page_22]

492   drying per unit of change in SWSI. The smaller the AvS, the smaller the change in SWC and [SOURCE: Page_22]

21 [SOURCE: Page_22]

493   in the associated amount of water released. The AvS is thus related to SWR. The intercept [SOURCE: Page_23]

494   was obtained when SWSI equaled zero, which was at saturation for FWHM and corresponded [SOURCE: Page_23]

495   to the theoretical value of SWC at saturation. According to Soltani et al. (2019), the intercept [SOURCE: Page_23]

496   and the AvS appeared relevant for studying the effects of texture and SOC content on SWR [SOURCE: Page_23]

497   properties. Therefore, the distribution and relative positions of soil samples on the plane of the [SOURCE: Page_23]

498   intercept vs. AvS graph result from the combination of their respective porosity and SWR [SOURCE: Page_23]

499   capacity. In the present study, only soil depth and fertilizer under CT had a significant effect [SOURCE: Page_23]

500   on the intercept and therefore on SWR properties, and on WHC in particular. Overall, [SOURCE: Page_23]

501   compared to T$_0$, CT treatments had the largest mean intercept and mean AvS, which indicated [SOURCE: Page_23]

502   an overall positive effect of tillage on SWR properties and soil porosity. Moreover, the [SOURCE: Page_23]

503   correlation matrix showed significant positive correlations between WHC, SDexter and the two [SOURCE: Page_23]

504   parameters (intercept, AvS). This thus explains the overall consistency observed between soil [SOURCE: Page_23]

505   sample distribution on the plane and the effects of agricultural practices on physical and [SOURCE: Page_23]

506   chemical parameters (e.g. porosity, WHC, SDexter, BD, SOC content). For example, cattle [SOURCE: Page_23]

507   manure fertilization and the increase in SOC content had a pronounced effect on the intercept [SOURCE: Page_23]

508   and the AvS under CT, which also had the highest WHC. These significant correlations [SOURCE: Page_23]

509   strengthen the hypothesis that the AvS and the intercept are relevant for assessing the effects [SOURCE: Page_23]

510   of agricultural practices on soil physical properties. [SOURCE: Page_23]

511   The soil under CTC at 15-25 cm had a larger intercept and AvS than at T$_0$ or under STM and [SOURCE: Page_23]

512   STC, at both depths. Thus, this soil was assumed to have better structure and physical quality [SOURCE: Page_23]

513   than the other soil samples. This was reflected in its poral distribution that allowed i) water to [SOURCE: Page_23]

514   be easily available, since it had higher SWC variation for a SWSI unit variation and, ii) higher [SOURCE: Page_23]

515   SDexter and WHC, likely in combination with the increase in SOC content due to cattle manure [SOURCE: Page_23]

516   input. At 0-15 cm, according to Dexter’s criterion, both T$_0$ and CTM had good physical [SOURCE: Page_23]

517   quality. Soil quality was even better for T$_0$, which had a higher SDexter. However, the relative [SOURCE: Page_23]

22 [SOURCE: Page_23]

518   position of CTM on the plane with respect to T$_0$ pointed to the opposite conclusion. Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_24]

519   CTM had a higher AvS and intercept. The same observation can be made with T$_0$ and STM at [SOURCE: Page_24]

520   15-25 cm when comparing their SDexter and their relative position on the plane. Soil physical [SOURCE: Page_24]

521   quality is multifactorial and therefore not straightforward to evaluate. Thus, a single index, [SOURCE: Page_24]

522   such as SDexter, may be insufficient to characterize this quality. The plane has an advantage [SOURCE: Page_24]

523   since it offers a bidimensional approach combining SWR properties and poral distribution, [SOURCE: Page_24]

524   through the use of intercept and Avs coordinates, to evaluate the physical quality. However, [SOURCE: Page_24]

525   for this plane to be even more relevant, thresholds must be defined for both the AvS and the [SOURCE: Page_24]

526   intercept, thus making it possible to characterize soil physical quality. [SOURCE: Page_24]

527   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_24]

528   In the present study, we used two approaches to study the effects of five years of tillage and [SOURCE: Page_24]

529   fertilization on SWR properties and soil structural quality. The first approach consisted of [SOURCE: Page_24]

530   using soil physical, chemical and SWR properties to assess the effects of agricultural practices [SOURCE: Page_24]

531   on SWR properties and then on soil structural quality. The second approach used spectral soil [SOURCE: Page_24]

532   information to study the effects of tillage and fertilization on SWR properties and structural [SOURCE: Page_24]

533   quality. This second approach is derived from results obtained by Soltani et al. (2019), based [SOURCE: Page_24]

534   on the water characteristic absorption band near 1920 nm. We further compared the results of [SOURCE: Page_24]

535   the two approaches. [SOURCE: Page_24]

536   Five years of tillage combined with mineral and organic fertilization significantly impacted [SOURCE: Page_24]

537   SWR properties. CT generally had a more positive effect on SWR properties than T$_0$ and this [SOURCE: Page_24]

538   effect was more pronounced with organic than with mineral fertilization and at 15-25cm. [SOURCE: Page_24]

539   SWC, WHC and porosity were greater under CTC than under either CTM or T$_0$. SDexter under [SOURCE: Page_24]

540   CTC was also greater than under CTM and at T$_0$, indicating a generally positive effect on [SOURCE: Page_24]

541   physical soil quality, but the difference was not significant. However, ST appeared to have a [SOURCE: Page_24]

23 [SOURCE: Page_24]

542   more negative effect on SWR properties than at T$_0$, and cattle manure fertilization did not [SOURCE: Page_25]

543   lead to a significant increase in SWR properties compared to T$_0$ or STM. Overall, fertilization [SOURCE: Page_25]

544   had no significant effect on soil properties, which directly influence SWR. Nevertheless, [SOURCE: Page_25]

545   cattle manure fertilization played an important role in improving the physical quality of tilled [SOURCE: Page_25]

546   soils. To achieve these results, several soil properties were analyzed, but similar results have [SOURCE: Page_25]

547   been achieved when analyzing only NIR spectral properties of soil. Our results suggest that [SOURCE: Page_25]

548   the AvS and the intercept of the linear relation between SWSI and SWC were relevant for [SOURCE: Page_25]

549   studying the effects of tillage type and fertilization type on SWR properties and soil structural [SOURCE: Page_25]

550   quality. What is more, plotting the intercept vs. AvS offers a way to summarize and assess the [SOURCE: Page_25]

551   impacts of agricultural practices on these properties. An increase in the intercept and AvS was [SOURCE: Page_25]

552   generally associated with an improvement in soil quality. [SOURCE: Page_25]

553   Our findings suggest that spectral information, as a promising alternative approach, can be [SOURCE: Page_25]

554   used to asses SWR properties and soil structural quality. Reflectance is a dynamic soil [SOURCE: Page_25]

555   property that can change rapidly due to changes in soil composition, compaction, structural [SOURCE: Page_25]

556   degradation and biological processes. Using spectral data, one can infer a variety of soil [SOURCE: Page_25]

557   properties that may influence SWR properties and soil quality. Since five years is a medium- [SOURCE: Page_25]

558   term time horizon, some effects were not significantly discriminated. It thus appears that our [SOURCE: Page_25]

559   alternative method would likely be even more relevant in long-term trials. Along with [SOURCE: Page_25]

560   reflectance, the SWR curve (SWC vs. pressure head) is one of the key soil characteristics that [SOURCE: Page_25]

561   convey a great deal of soil information. For example, SDexter is theoretically derived from the [SOURCE: Page_25]

562   inflection point of the SWR curve. Estimating hydraulic properties at multiple locations, even [SOURCE: Page_25]

563   within an agricultural field, is time-consuming and costly. Nevertheless, in the present study, [SOURCE: Page_25]

564   NIR spectroscopy showed promising results for studying SWR properties. Therefore, further [SOURCE: Page_25]

565   research could focus on estimating SWSI to predict the SWR curve. [SOURCE: Page_25]
