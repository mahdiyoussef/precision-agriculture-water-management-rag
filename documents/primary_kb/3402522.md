8   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_5]

9 [SOURCE: Page_5]

10   Tidal habitats sustain fragile ecosystems, undergoing pressures from coastal artificialization [SOURCE: Page_5]

11   and rising sea levels. Saltpans are a substitution habitat for birds that breed, winter or stop- [SOURCE: Page_5]

12   over along coastlands where most pristine tidal habitats have been removed. Balancing the [SOURCE: Page_5]

13   economical, patrimonial and biodiversity values of former saltpans is thus needed to [SOURCE: Page_5]

14   mitigate the threats posed by global changes on waterbirds. In this study, we scrutinized the [SOURCE: Page_5]

15   influence of management practices on waterbirds on two isolated saltpans located on the [SOURCE: Page_5]

16   French Mediterranean shore, several tens of kilometres apart from other suitable habitats. [SOURCE: Page_5]

17   We analysed three years of bird counts for nine protected species that breed, forage and [SOURCE: Page_5]

18   roost on these saltpans. We used a multispecies hierarchical model to relate variations in [SOURCE: Page_5]

19   bird counts to water levels, oxygenation and salinity, the three parameters targeted by the [SOURCE: Page_5]

20   saltpans management plan to promote bird settlement. We showed that the hypersaline [SOURCE: Page_5]

21   conditions that dominate in these saltpans are suboptimum to most species, suggesting that [SOURCE: Page_5]

22   waterbird concentrations are dictated by the lack of alternatives in the surrounding [SOURCE: Page_5]

23   landscape rather than by habitat suitability. Intraspecific variations in species’ responses to [SOURCE: Page_5]

24   these variables should orient towards the creation of a habitat mosaic within the saltpans. [SOURCE: Page_5]

25   Eventually, between-site differences in bird responses to water conditions pointed the [SOURCE: Page_5]

26   effects of disturbance, predation and other landscape-level features. Our results reveal that [SOURCE: Page_5]

27   high waterbird numbers on isolated saltpans may be a misleading measure of their [SOURCE: Page_5]

28   ecological suitability, and that management on these sites needs to incorporate conflicts and [SOURCE: Page_5]

29   complementarity in species’ habitat use. [SOURCE: Page_5]

31   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_6]

32 [SOURCE: Page_6]

33   Wetlands, among the most biodiverse ecosystems on earth, sustain a wide range of essential [SOURCE: Page_6]

34   ecosystem services such as harvesting and fishing, water purification, floods buffering and [SOURCE: Page_6]

35   mitigation of catastrophic climatic events (Zedler & Kercher, 2005; Gardner et al., 2015; [SOURCE: Page_6]

36   Montanarella et al., 2018; Ramsar Convention on Wetlands, 2018). They also provide habitat [SOURCE: Page_6]

37   and resources for a high diversity of organisms organized into trophically complex [SOURCE: Page_6]

38   ecosystems. Although considered as foreground habitats for conservation and human [SOURCE: Page_6]

39   welfare, the global surface of wetlands has been reduced by 35% between 1970 and 2015 [SOURCE: Page_6]

40   under various pressures, among which climate change and human land use are especially [SOURCE: Page_6]

41   prominent (Barbier et al., 2011; Gardner et al., 2015; Ramsar Convention on Wetlands, [SOURCE: Page_6]

42   2018). Wetlands are restricted in surface, highly unstable due to variations in water levels [SOURCE: Page_6]

43   and quality, and thus vulnerable to even small levels of disturbance (Adam, 2002; Kirwan & [SOURCE: Page_6]

44   Megonigal, 2013). Appropriate conservation effort is therefore needed to warrant the [SOURCE: Page_6]

45   maintenance of wetland-related ecosystems, and requires in-depth studies to evaluate the [SOURCE: Page_6]

46   effects of water management practices on targeted species (Clausen et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_6]

47 [SOURCE: Page_6]

48   Coastal wetlands are highly productive, yet unstable ecosystems with site-specific dynamics [SOURCE: Page_6]

49   tied to variations in sea levels, balance of freshwater and saltwater, and human usage [SOURCE: Page_6]

50   (Adam, 2002). The vulnerability of coastal wetlands facing rising sea levels as a result of [SOURCE: Page_6]

51   ongoing climate change is listed as one of the most severe threats to coastal biodiversity in [SOURCE: Page_6]

52   the coming decades (Boesch, 2000; Craft et al., 2009; Hoegh-Guldberg & Bruno, 2010). [SOURCE: Page_6]

53   However, coastal wetlands are more immediately threatened by human land use, which 3 [SOURCE: Page_6]

54   accounts for 25-50% of the decrease in their global surface cover during the past centuries [SOURCE: Page_7]

55   through direct destruction, draining for agriculture or urbanization, eutrophication, invasive [SOURCE: Page_7]

56   species and water pollution (Gedan et al., 2009; Deegan et al., 2012; Kirwan & Megonigal, [SOURCE: Page_7]

57   2013). As a result, many coastal wetlands are now restricted to small isolated patches of [SOURCE: Page_7]

58   marshy habitats within deeply man-modified seashores, some of which are maintained [SOURCE: Page_7]

59   artificially by conservation-oriented management, especially in northern America, western [SOURCE: Page_7]

60   Europe, and locally elsewhere (Hughes & Paramor, 2004). [SOURCE: Page_7]

62   Most waterbirds (mainly Charadriidae, Scolopacidae, Laridae and a few other related [SOURCE: Page_7]

63   families ; Burger & Olla, 1984) are strict invertebrate eaters or piscivores, and thus occupy a [SOURCE: Page_7]

64   top-position in trophic networks that make them suitable indicators to monitor the [SOURCE: Page_7]

65   functioning of coastal wetland ecosystems (Piersma et al., 2004). Because most waterbirds [SOURCE: Page_7]

66   are migratory, the preservation of their breeding, stop-over and wintering habitats all along [SOURCE: Page_7]

67   migration routes is critical to their regional or global population persistence (Piersma et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

68   2006), for instance along the Pacific north-american coast (Velasquez, 1992), in southeastern [SOURCE: Page_7]

69   Asia (Sripanomyom et al., 2011; Xia et al., 2017) or on Mediterranean shores (AEWA, 2018; [SOURCE: Page_7]

70   Giosa et al., 2018). Along these coasts, suitable wetlands are small and rare due to [SOURCE: Page_7]

71   topographic constraints and intense human land use (Myers, 1983; Piersma et al., 2006). As [SOURCE: Page_7]

72   a consequence of climate change-induced erosion of coastal marshes and habitat loss [SOURCE: Page_7]

73   (Ramírez et al., 2018), waterbird conservation thus largely benefits from the maintenance of [SOURCE: Page_7]

74   appropriate resources in artificial wetlands such as fishponds and saltpans (Velasquez, 1992; [SOURCE: Page_7]

75   Takekawa et al., 2006; Giosa et al., 2018). It is therefore of paramount importance to assess [SOURCE: Page_7]

79   Mudflat-feeding waders (Charadriidae and Scolopacidae) account for a large proportion of [SOURCE: Page_8]

80   waterbirds biomass and thus bear a strong role on tidal trophic networks, in addition to their [SOURCE: Page_8]

81   high patrimonial value associated with hunting, conservation status and legal use as [SOURCE: Page_8]

82   indicator species (Colwell, 2010). These small-bodied species cannot cope with large [SOURCE: Page_8]

83   variations in water depth, which therefore determine habitat availability. Furthermore, the [SOURCE: Page_8]

84   abundance and quality of their invertebrate preys peaks in mesohaline waters with high [SOURCE: Page_8]

85   oxygenation levels (Velasquez, 1992; Takekawa et al., 2006; Dias, 2009; Athearn et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_8]

86   These conditions are specifically met in saltpans, in which water levels, variations in [SOURCE: Page_8]

87   physiochemical conditions and other sources of disturbance are buffered artificially by [SOURCE: Page_8]

88   hydraulic control, rendering these artificial saltmarshes suitable for high waterbird [SOURCE: Page_8]

89   concentrations (Velasquez, 1992; Sánchez et al., 2006; Takekawa et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_8]

91   Because waterbird numbers and diversity decrease in hypersaline conditions, the suitability [SOURCE: Page_8]

92   of saltpans to birds is conditioned to the maintenance of optimum physicochemical [SOURCE: Page_8]

93   conditions (Warnock et al., 2002). Gulls (Laridae) and terns (Sternidae) breeding on saltpans [SOURCE: Page_8]

94   nest on small islets or dykes, as a substitution to sandpits or other coastal habitats subjected [SOURCE: Page_8]

95   to erosion and human disturbance (Catry et al., 2004). By contrast with waders, these birds [SOURCE: Page_8]

96   benefit from higher water levels that isolate their breeding sites from predators (Warnock et [SOURCE: Page_8]

97   al., 2002; Bluso-Demers et al., 2016), although gulls also require mudflats for feeding. [SOURCE: Page_8]

115   of management practices still needs to determine whether local water management [SOURCE: Page_9]

360   Discussion [SOURCE: Page_21]

361 [SOURCE: Page_21]

362   Most species exhibited strong non-linear responses to at least one of the three variables [SOURCE: Page_21]

363   investigated, supporting the hypothesis that water level, salinity and dissolved oxygen [SOURCE: Page_21]

364   concentration are relevant parameters to control saltpans suitability to waterbirds, in line [SOURCE: Page_21]

365   with previous studies in other biogeographic areas (Velasquez, 1992; Warnock et al., 2002; [SOURCE: Page_21]

366   Giosa et al., 2018). As a general pattern, macroinvertebrate species diversity peak at mid- [SOURCE: Page_21]

367   salinity (Britton & Johnson, 1987), which in turn forces a decrease in bird occurrence [SOURCE: Page_21]

368   towards hypersaline waters due to a lack of prey and osmoregulation stress (Gutiérrez et al., [SOURCE: Page_21]

369   2011). Salinity is thus crucial for waterbird assemblages through its direct influence on [SOURCE: Page_21]

370   trophic resources fluctuations (Warnock et al., 2002; Takekawa et al., 2006; Ramírez et al., [SOURCE: Page_21]

371   2018). In our study, all species tied to saltpans for foraging, except flamingo (i.e., all but [SOURCE: Page_21]

372   terns, which also fish at sea), exhibited negative response curves to salinity at least on [SOURCE: Page_21]

373   Pesquiers, which points that water on this saltpan is overly saline to most breeding [SOURCE: Page_21]

374   waterbirds. Accordingly, the levels of salinity recorded in our study site were well above the 18 [SOURCE: Page_21]

Waterbird responses to wetland management [SOURCE: Page_22]

375   range found in comparable protected saltpans elsewhere in the world (e.g. in San Francisco [SOURCE: Page_22]

376   bay, Warnock et al., 2002). In line with studies on other artificial marshes (Takekawa et al., [SOURCE: Page_22]

377   2006; Bellio et al., 2009; Giosa et al., 2018), these patterns imply that our study site is a [SOURCE: Page_22]

378   suboptimum and stressful habitat which waterbirds are forced to use by the lack of more [SOURCE: Page_22]

379   suitable marshes in the vicinity, the closest coastal wetland to our study site being the [SOURCE: Page_22]

380   Camargue, 120 km to the west. [SOURCE: Page_22]

381 [SOURCE: Page_22]

382   While increasing salinity had clearly detrimental effects on most species, the effects of water [SOURCE: Page_22]

383   levels and dissolved oxygen were more species-specific, reflecting both species’ habitat [SOURCE: Page_22]

384   preferences and site use. Waders and gulls peaked at low to intermediate water levels, [SOURCE: Page_22]

385   consistent with their need of mudflats for foraging (Piersma et al., 2004), while terns, which [SOURCE: Page_22]

386   fish on open waters, exhibited flatter responses explained by the fact that they breed on [SOURCE: Page_22]

387   ponds where water levels are maintained permanently high. At the other extreme, the [SOURCE: Page_22]

388   particular ecology of flamingo, a large-bodied specialist of highly saline waters relying on [SOURCE: Page_22]

389   saltpans as up to 60% of its habitat (Béchet et al., 2009), explains its maximum counts at [SOURCE: Page_22]

390   lower oxygenation, higher salinity and higher water levels than most other species. Its [SOURCE: Page_22]

391   unexpected apparent reluctance to high salinity in Vieux Salins can be explained by the [SOURCE: Page_22]

392   confusing fact that the few sufficiently large and deep ponds on this saltpan also happen to [SOURCE: Page_22]

393   be the least saline. In sum, waterbirds’ responses to changes in water physicochemical [SOURCE: Page_22]

394   parameters were highly asynchronic across species and consistent with their differential use [SOURCE: Page_22]

395   of the saltpans, either for breeding, foraging or both. [SOURCE: Page_22]

397   Although species’ responses to the three studied variables were generally well explained by [SOURCE: Page_23]

398   breeding and feeding behaviours, we observed marked differences between the two studied [SOURCE: Page_23]

399   saltpans suggesting the interplay of additional factors not accounted for in our study. Most [SOURCE: Page_23]

400   notably, responses to all tested variables were more marked, and abundances were higher, [SOURCE: Page_23]

401   on the barren Pesquiers saltpans than on the more vegetalized Vieux Salins, reflecting the [SOURCE: Page_23]

402   tendency of shorebirds to forage and breed preferentially in open habitats (Tavares et al., [SOURCE: Page_23]

403   2015; Żmihorski et al., 2016). This discrepancy between the two sites may also imply that [SOURCE: Page_23]

404   breeding waterbirds limit distances between breeding and foraging areas to reduce energy [SOURCE: Page_23]

405   expenditure and predation risk. Among undesired effects which may also contribute to inter- [SOURCE: Page_23]

406   site differences, herbaceous cover and wooded areas can increase predation pressure by [SOURCE: Page_23]

407   wild boar, red fox, yellow-legged gull, falcons and sparrowhawk (Żmihorski et al., 2016), and [SOURCE: Page_23]

408   the small size of Vieux Salins exposes birds more to external disturbance (in particular the [SOURCE: Page_23]

409   proximity of an airport), although both sites are permanently closed to the public. These [SOURCE: Page_23]

410   speculations based on field experience demand formal testing with dedicated data, which [SOURCE: Page_23]

411   were not available during our study. Our results suggest, however, that waterbirds’ [SOURCE: Page_23]

412   responses to water parameters interact with landscape and habitat-level determinants, [SOURCE: Page_23]

413   calling for an ecosystem-based management approach (Ma et al., 2010; Clausen et al., [SOURCE: Page_23]

414   2013), which would account for habitat complementation behaviours in the species under [SOURCE: Page_23]

415   focus. [SOURCE: Page_23]
