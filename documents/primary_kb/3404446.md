20          Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   Improving our understanding of the spatiotemporal dynamics of fine roots in deep soil layers [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   is of utter importance to manage tropical planted forests in a context of climate change. Our [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   study aimed to assess the effect of clear-cutting and drought on fine-root dynamics down to [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   the water table in Brazilian ferralsol under eucalypt plantations conducted in coppice. Fine [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   roots (i.e. diameter < 2 mm) were sampled down to 17 m deep in a throughfall exclusion [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   experiment comparing stands with 37% of throughfall excluded by plastic sheets (-W) and [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   stands without rain exclusion (+W). Root dynamics were studied using minirhizotron in two [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   permanent pits down to 17 m deep, over 1 year before clear-cutting, then over 2 years in [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   coppice, as well as down to 4 m deep in a non-harvested plot (NH) serving as a control. After [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   harvesting, a spectacular fine root growth of trees conducted in coppice occurred in very deep [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   soil layers (> 13 m) and, surprisingly, root mortality remained extremely low whatever the [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   depth and the treatment. Total fine-root biomass in coppice down to 17 m depth was 1,266 and [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   1,017 g m-2 in +W and -W, respectively, at 1.5 year after the clear-cut and was 1,078 g m-2 in [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   NH 7.5 years after planting. Specific root length and specific root area were about 15% higher [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   in -W than in +W. Proliferation of fine roots at great depths could be an adaptive mechanism [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   for tree survival, enhancing the access to water stored in the subsoil. The root system [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   established before clear-cutting provides access to water stored in very deep layers that [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   probably contribute to mitigate the risk of tree mortality during prolonged drought periods [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   when the eucalypt plantations is conducted in coppice after the clear-cut. [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   Key words: Brazil, coppice, deep ferralsol profile, deep root growth, Eucalyptus grandis, [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   minirhizotron, throughfall exclusion [SOURCE: Page_2]

43              1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   Future climate projections predict longer and more severe dry periods in tropical and [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   subtropical regions (Solomon et al., 2009; Dai, 2011; He and Soden, 2017). Extensive tree [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   mortality triggered by drought and changes in rainfall patterns has been reported worldwide [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   (Allen, 2009; Williams et al., 2013; McDowell et al., 2018). Tree survival greatly depends on [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   rooting system behavior and functioning (Nepstad et al., 1994; Markewitz et al., 2010; Pierik [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   and Testerink, 2014; Christina et al., 2017), as plant growth is highly dependent on the [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   absorptive function of fine roots for water and nutrients (Hinsinger, 2001). Fine roots also play [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   a major role in the global carbon (C) cycle, representing significant C input into the soil by the [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   incorporation of exudates and root necromass, and also generating a return of C to the [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   atmosphere through respiration and decomposition processes (Balesdent and Balabane, 1996; [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   Strand et al., 2008). Improving our understanding of how root systems respond to drought is [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   therefore crucial for terrestrial biosphere models to predict the effect of climate change on tree [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   survival and carbon sequestration in forest and tree-based ecosystems. [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   Plant species use a large range of rooting patterns to cope with periodic drought, from “drought [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   tolerant strategies” with fine roots surviving in periodically dry soil, to “drought avoiding [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   strategies” shedding fine roots from dry soil horizons while rapidly developing roots in moister [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   areas (Brunner et al., 2015; Vries et al., 2016; Bristiel et al., 2018). Drought can increase the [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   root-to-shoot ratio, the root area or root length-to-leaf area ratio, as well as the proportion of [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   fine roots in deep soil layers and/or the specific root area (Markesteijn and Poorter, 2009; Ma [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2018). Root growth peaks have been shown in deep soil layers during [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   dry periods for eucalypt and rubber trees in tropical soils (Maeght et al., 2015; Lambais et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   2017). Drought tolerance strategies are common for herbaceous plants, while drought avoiding [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   strategies are often adopted by trees in evergreen tropical forests (Brunner et al., 2015). Despite [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   the crucial role of fine roots in coping with drought, root phenology remains poorly understood [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   in comparison to leaf ecophysiology (Radville et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   Deep roots commonly reported as roots growing beyond 1 m in depth can play an important [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   role in supplying water to trees (Kell, 2012; Pierret et al., 2016). Trees can be deeply rooted [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   (Canadell et al., 1996; Schenk and Jackson, 2002), and some studies suggest that very deep [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   roots (at depths > 10 m) are common in highly weathered tropical soils (Nepstad et al., 1994; [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   Saleska et al., 2007; Broedel et al., 2017). Even though low fine root densities are generally [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   found at great depth (Pierret et al., 2016), they can take up substantial amounts of water needed [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   for tree survival during drought periods (McDowell et al., 2008; Nardini et al., 2016). Deep [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   roots can also contribute to closing the biogeochemical cycles in tropical forests through [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   nutrient uptake in deep soil layers (Lehmann, 2003; Jobbágy and Jackson, 2004; Da Silva et [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   al., 2011; Bordron et al., 2018), which reduces nutrient losses by deep leaching (Lehmann and [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   Schroth, 2003; Laclau et al., 2010). While many studies show that the role of very deep roots [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   in tropical forest functioning and productivity has been greatly underestimated (Jackson et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   2000; Markewitz et al., 2010; Freycon et al., 2015), as far as we are aware, fine root dynamics [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   and mortality have never been studied at depths > 10 m. [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   Eucalyptus plantations cover more than 20 million hectares and account for around 8% of forest [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   plantations in the world (Booth, 2013). The diversity of Eucalyptus species planted in tropical [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   regions has led to a wide range of products and management practices (Gonçalves et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   Although many Eucalyptus plantations are intensively managed to produce raw materials for [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   industry (mainly pulp and paper, but also solid-wood products, fiberboards and charcoal for [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   steel production), used as a domestic source of energy and also contributes to alleviating [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   poverty in developing countries (Cossalter and Pye-Smith, 2003). Most eucalypt plantations are [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   located in areas with low soil fertility and prolonged drought periods (Keenan et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   Coppice management could be an adaptive solution to cope with water deficit in these [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   plantations, because the sprouts growing on stumps are likely to benefit from the pre-existing [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   root system that explores deep soil layers where water availability is generally higher than in [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   the topsoil. The effects of clear-cutting on fine root dynamics in coppiced-managed forest [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   plantations are poorly documented and tendencies are not clear. Sequential coring in Brazilian [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   coppiced-managed eucalypt plantations showed that fine root density decreased in the 0-1 m [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   soil layer in the first 60 days after harvesting, while fine root decomposition was accelerated [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   (Mello et al., 2007). Fine root mortality exceeded fine root production after clear-cutting in [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   Populus stands (Berhongaray et al., 2015). In contrast, Dickmann et al. (1996) observed little [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   change in fine root production and mortality in the 0-1 m soil layer after clear-cutting in other [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   Populus stands. While 50% of fine root biomass can be found below a depth of 1 m in tropical [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   eucalypt plantations (Christina et al., 2011; Laclau et al., 2013), fine root dynamics in coppice- [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   managed forests have only been studied in the upper meter of the soil profile. [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   Our study was carried out in Eucalyptus grandis (Hill ex. Maid) stands planted in a throughfall [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   exclusion experiment in Brazil (Battie‐Laclau et al., 2014). We aimed to gain insight into the [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   effects of contrasting rainfall regimes on fine root dynamics in coppice-managed eucalypt [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   plantations after tree clear-cutting in very deep tropical soils. We put forward the hypothesis [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   that trees invest in belowground biomass in response to throughfall exclusion, in order to [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   increase the exploration of fine roots in deep soil layers (H$_1$), and that they adjust fine root traits [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   to increase the specific root area, in order to capture more resources per gram of C invested in [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   fine roots (H$_2$). In addition, we put forward the hypothesis that Eucalyptus grandis stands with [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   clear-cutting and tree regrowth in coppices would increase fine root turnover throughout the [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   soil profile (H$_3$). [SOURCE: Page_5]

114               2. Materials and methods [SOURCE: Page_5]

115       a. Study site [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   The experiments were conducted at the research station owned by the Luiz de Queiroz College [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   of Agriculture (ESALQ) near Itatinga (São Paulo, Brazil, 23°02′S 48°38′W). The study area, [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   located 300 km from the sea, has a relief typical of the São Paulo Western Plateau, with a [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   topography varying from flat to hilly (slopes < 3%). The altitude is 850 m, and the climate is [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   humid subtropical with a dry winter (Cwa) according to the Köppen classification. Over the 15 [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   years prior to this study, the mean annual rainfall was 1390 mm (with 74% between October [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   and May), and the mean air temperature and relative humidity were 20°C and 77%, [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   respectively. A dry and cool (15°C) season occurs between June and September. The total [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   rainfall over the study period (from May 2015 to July 2018) was 5,629 mm. The annual rainfall [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   was 1,557 mm and 2,303 mm in 2016 and 2017, respectively. The soils are very deep Ferralsols [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   (IUSS Working Group WRB, 2015) developed on Cretaceous sandstone. Clay content ranges [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   from 160 mg g-1 soil in the topsoil to about 250 mg g-1 soil at a depth of 15 m, and clay minerals [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   are mainly kaolinite (Christina et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_6]

129      b. Experimental layout [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   A split-plot experimental design was set up in June 2010 with a Eucalyptus grandis clone used [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   in commercial plantations by the Suzano Company (São Paulo, Brazil). The experimental [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   layout with 6 treatments and 3 blocks was described in detail by Battie‐Laclau et al. (2014). We [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   studied two treatments out of all those available in the experimental design: one treatment with [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   undisturbed rainfall (+W) and one treatment with throughfall exclusion (-W), which was [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   equipped with plastic sheets installed since September 2010, allowing the exclusion of 37% of [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   throughfall (Battie‐Laclau et al., 2014). Treatments -W and +W were used to determine the [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   water deficit effect. The trees were planted in June 2010 at a spacing of 3 m x 2 m with a [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   stocking density of 1666 trees per hectare. The plots received a standard commercial fertilizer [SOURCE: Page_6]

139   (at planting: 3.3 g P m-2, 200 g m-2 of dolomitic lime and trace elements; at 3 months of age: 12 [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   g N m-2, 0.45 mol K m-2 applied as KCl) and herbicides were applied to avoid the presence of [SOURCE: Page_6]

141   other understory species. In +W and -W, the eucalypt stands were harvested six years after [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   planting, in June 2016, and the plantation was coppice-managed thereafter. Several new shoots [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   were regenerated from the stumps after the clear-cut and 1 or 2 shoots per stump were selected [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   to maintain the same stocking density and prevent the growth of additional new shoots. A third [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   treatment served as a control, with undisturbed rainfall and no harvest (NH), to assess the clear- [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   cutting effect. Tree height and circumference at breast height were recorded each year after the [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   clear-cut on 36 central trees per plot during the first rotation (May 2010 - June 2016) and during [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   the second rotation in coppice (starting in June 2016). [SOURCE: Page_7]

149       c. Soil water monitoring [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   The volumetric soil water content (SWC) was monitored in the +W and -W treatments [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   throughout the study period at half-hourly intervals using CS616 probes (Campbell Scientific [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   Inc., Logan, UT, USA) installed at the following depths: 0.15, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14 [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   and 16 m, with 3 probes at each depth in block 1. Extractable water (mm) was calculated as the [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   difference between the current soil water stock (mm) and the minimum soil water stock (i.e. [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   lower limit of soil water content in mm) (Granier et al., 1999). [SOURCE: Page_7]

156       d. Deep permanent pits for root phenology observations [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   Between February and March 2014, two deep permanent pits were excavated in +W and -W in [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   block 1 to gain access to the complete soil profile from the top soil down to the water table. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   pits were 1.5 m in diameter and reached a depth of 17 m and were located between four [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   Eucalyptus trees (Figure 1) at a distance of 90 cm, 90 cm, 130 cm and 130 cm from each four [SOURCE: Page_7]

161   trees respectively. The pit walls were made of concrete rings in direct contact with the soil. [SOURCE: Page_7]

162   Clear-colored roofs of the same diameter as the pits were used to prevent light and rain entering [SOURCE: Page_7]

163   the pits. Platforms were set up at two-meter intervals in the pits, equipped with artificial lighting [SOURCE: Page_7]

164   and fans used during working sessions, allowing access and safe work down to the water table [SOURCE: Page_7]

165   (Figure 1). The high cost of opening up and securing these pits prevented further replications [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   in the other two blocks. However, other measurements, including deep fine root sampling and [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   tree growth were carried out in all three blocks, making it possible to extrapolate some of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   observations made in the two deep pits. In October 2015, a third pit was excavated in the non- [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   harvested treatment (NH) down to 3.5 m to determine the clear-cut effect. [SOURCE: Page_8]

170       e. Minirhizotrons [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   Fine root dynamics were studied through transparent polyvinyl chloride tubes (length:180 cm; [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   inner diameter: 6.5 cm), commonly called minirhizotrons (Maeght et al., 2013). In October [SOURCE: Page_8]

173   2014, twenty-four transparent minirhizotrons were installed using a powerhead drill in the +W [SOURCE: Page_8]

174   and -W treatments of block 1. Outside the pits, three minirhizotrons were inserted into the soil [SOURCE: Page_8]

175   surface in the same plots a minimum of 10 m apart and at a distance of 90 cm from the trunk [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   (Figure 1). Nine tubes were inserted into the pit walls down to a depth of 17 m: two tubes at a [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   depth of 1 m and one tube at depths of 3.5, 5.5, 7.5, 9.5, 11.5, 13.5, 15.5 m (Figure 1). The tubes [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   were inserted at a 45-degree angle and allowed an observation depth of 1.3 m below the depth [SOURCE: Page_8]

179   at which they were inserted into the soil profile. In October 2015, 3 additional tubes were [SOURCE: Page_8]

180   installed at the soil surface in the +W and -W treatments of blocks 2 and 3 (12 tubes in total) to [SOURCE: Page_8]

181   increase the number of replications in the top meter. In October 2015, seven tubes were installed [SOURCE: Page_8]

182   in the NH treatments (3 into the soil surface, 2 tubes inserted at depths of 1 m and 3.5 m into [SOURCE: Page_8]

183   the pit walls). [SOURCE: Page_8]

184   Root dynamics were recorded using a circular scanner system (CI-600 Root Growth Monitoring [SOURCE: Page_8]

185   System, CID, USA). This scanner was selected for the quality of the images it produces, an [SOURCE: Page_8]

186   essential element for the analysis (Graefe et al., 2008). In order to have a significant [SOURCE: Page_8]

187   stabilization period after soil disturbance from the installation, fine root monitoring began eight [SOURCE: Page_8]

188   months after minirhizotron installation (Graefe et al., 2008; Germon et al., 2016). Eight images [SOURCE: Page_8]

189   (21.59 x 19.56 cm, 100 dpi) per tube (43 tubes in total) were taken every two weeks for more [SOURCE: Page_9]

190   than three consecutive years from May 2015 to July 2018: over one year before the clear-cut [SOURCE: Page_9]

191   and two years after harvesting, in a coppice. Images were taken at a resolution of 100 dpi, as [SOURCE: Page_9]

192   we obtained the same root lengths and diameter compared to images of 300 dpi and 600 dpi, [SOURCE: Page_9]

193   and it was less time-consuming in the field than with a higher resolution. [SOURCE: Page_9]

194      f. Root image processing [SOURCE: Page_9]

195   WinRhizoTron™ software (Régent Instrument Inc., Quebec, Canada) was used to analyze more [SOURCE: Page_9]

196   than 24,000 root images taken in the minirhizotrons. This software was chosen as it allowed the [SOURCE: Page_9]

197   analysis of large number of images and overlayed the images to visualize the time evolution of [SOURCE: Page_9]

198   the roots throughout the tubes. This manual root measurement program estimated the length [SOURCE: Page_9]

199   and diameter of each root observed and stored each data item in a text file. By comparing two [SOURCE: Page_9]

200   consecutive images chronologically, it was possible to estimate changes in root length and [SOURCE: Page_9]

201   phenology features. Root mortality was evaluated based on the absence of growth over the [SOURCE: Page_9]

202   entire study period up to the last images, when roots turned black and presented clear signs of [SOURCE: Page_9]

203   decomposition (Germon et al., 2016; Lambais et al., 2017). For each fine root (diameter < 2 [SOURCE: Page_9]

204   mm) we recorded the time of the first appearance, the diameter, the length over time, and the [SOURCE: Page_9]

205   time of disappearance. In this study, we considered only root appearing during the study period. [SOURCE: Page_9]

206      g. Root length calculations [SOURCE: Page_9]

207   As described in Germon et al. (2016) and Lambais et al. (2017) the following metrics were used [SOURCE: Page_9]

208   to estimate root production and the root elongation rate: living and dead root length production [SOURCE: Page_9]

209   (LP, cm m-2) was calculated for each root as the individual root length (living or dead) at time [SOURCE: Page_9]

210   t divided by the observed soil area of each image. The cumulative living or dead root length [SOURCE: Page_9]

211   production (CLP, cm m-2) was calculated summing, at each time t, the individual length of all [SOURCE: Page_9]

212   the roots observed, divided by the observed soil area of each image. The individual root [SOURCE: Page_9]

213   elongation rate (RER, cm day-1) was calculated as the difference in individual root length [SOURCE: Page_10]

214   observed between two consecutive sessions (t and t-1) divided by the time in days between t and [SOURCE: Page_10]

215   t-1. The daily root elongation rate (DRER, cm m-2 day-1) was calculated by summing, at each [SOURCE: Page_10]

216   time t, each individual root elongation rate of all the roots observed, divided by the observed [SOURCE: Page_10]

217   soil area of each image. The mean root elongation rate (MeanRER, cm day-1) and the maximum [SOURCE: Page_10]

218   root elongation rate (MaxRER, cm day-1) were calculated as the mean and the maximum of [SOURCE: Page_10]

219   individual root elongation rates considering all the roots growing during the study period. LLP, [SOURCE: Page_10]

220   CLP, RER, DRER, MeanRER and MaxRER were estimated for each treatment (+W, -W and [SOURCE: Page_10]

221   NH), each soil layer and between consecutive image sessions over the entire study period. [SOURCE: Page_10]

222      h. Root sampling [SOURCE: Page_10]

223   In order to check the consistency of fine root dynamics in deep soil layers observed by the [SOURCE: Page_10]

224   minirhizotron method, total fine root biomass, fine root length and fine root area down to the [SOURCE: Page_10]

225   water table were measured in October 2017, i.e. 1.5 years after the clear-cut in the coppices [SOURCE: Page_10]

226   (+W and -W) and 7.5 years after planting in NH. Fine roots (diameter < 2 mm) were sampled [SOURCE: Page_10]

227   down to 17 m in the two treatments (+W and -W) inside the three blocks and in the non- [SOURCE: Page_10]

228   harvested (NH) treatment inside one block. Four trees were randomly selected in each plot and [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   soil samples were collected around each tree between the topsoil and a depth of 2 m and around [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   2 other trees between depths of 2 and 17 m (i.e. 12 sampling points down to a depth of 2 m and [SOURCE: Page_10]

231   6 further down to a depth of 17 m in the +W and -W treatments and 4 sampling points down to [SOURCE: Page_10]

232   a depth of 2 m and 2 down to a depth of 17 m in the NH treatment). At each sampling position, [SOURCE: Page_10]

233   soil layers at 0-0.2, 0.2-0.5, 0.5-1, 1-1.5, 1.5-2 m were collected using a cylindrical auger with [SOURCE: Page_10]

234   an inner diameter of 4.5 cm. The Brazilian “cavadeira” tool, a cylindrical auger with an inner [SOURCE: Page_10]

235   diameter of 9 cm and a length of 30 cm, was used to collect soil cores every 50 cm from 2 m [SOURCE: Page_10]

236   down to a depth of 17 m. From each soil core, about 1.5 and 2 kg of soil was collected using [SOURCE: Page_10]

237   the same methodology as described in Germon et al. (2018) and Christina et al. (2011). To [SOURCE: Page_10]

238   avoid contamination of the collected soil samples, only soil blocks from the inner part of the [SOURCE: Page_11]

239   auger were considered. Total fresh soil mass was measured, and a sub-sample of 5 g was [SOURCE: Page_11]

240   weighed in the laboratory to estimate the soil water content by drying the sample at 105°C for [SOURCE: Page_11]

241   72 h. Each soil sample was identified and stored at 4°C before being processed a maximum of [SOURCE: Page_11]

242   1 week after sampling. Living roots (i.e. living stele, bright color and elasticity) and dead roots [SOURCE: Page_11]

243   (i.e. by sight, touch and flotation) > 1 cm long were carefully separated by hand after gentle [SOURCE: Page_11]

244   washing away of the soil with tap water on a sieve with a mesh size of 0.5 mm. For the 0-0.2, [SOURCE: Page_11]

245   0.2-0.5 and 0.5-1 m soil layers, 10 % of the weight of each soil sample was used to estimate the [SOURCE: Page_11]

246   mass of extremely fine roots (i.e. length < 1 cm). For the other soil layers 100% of the weight [SOURCE: Page_11]

247   was considered. Living and dead roots more than 1 cm in length were scanned using a double- [SOURCE: Page_11]

248   sided scanning procedure at a resolution of 800 dpi. Then, living roots and dead roots over > 1 [SOURCE: Page_11]

249   cm in length and fragments of living and dead roots less than 1 cm in length were dried for 72h [SOURCE: Page_11]

250   at 65° C and weighed (±0.1 mg). For the upper layers where only 10% of the weight of the soil [SOURCE: Page_11]

251   was screened for living and dead roots < 1 cm in length, the mass of fragments was multiplied [SOURCE: Page_11]

252   by 10 to estimate the mass of fragments of the whole soil sample. Root weight was then obtained [SOURCE: Page_11]

253   for each soil layer, sampling position, treatment and block. [SOURCE: Page_11]

254      i. Root trait calculations [SOURCE: Page_11]

255   The fine root scans obtained where analyzed using WinRhizo Version Pro V. 2009c software [SOURCE: Page_11]

256   (Régent Instruments Inc., Quebec, Canada) to estimate root lengths and areas per soil layer, [SOURCE: Page_11]

257   sampling position, treatments (+W, -W and NH) and blocks. As described in Germon et al. [SOURCE: Page_11]

258   (2018) the following metrics were used to estimate fine root traits. The specific root length [SOURCE: Page_11]

259   (SRL, m g-1, i.e. total length of scanned roots divided by their dry mass) and specific root area [SOURCE: Page_11]

260   (SRA, cm^2 g-1, i.e. total area of scanned roots divided by their dry mass) were calculated for [SOURCE: Page_11]

261   each soil sample. Fine root mass density (g kg-1 soil) was calculated as the total root dry mass [SOURCE: Page_11]

262   divided by the dry weight of the soil used for root separation. Fine root density (FRD, g dm-3 [SOURCE: Page_11]

263   soil) was obtained by multiplying fine root mass density by the soil bulk densities (measured [SOURCE: Page_12]

264   by the standard core method down to a depth of 17 m in each treatment). Fine root biomasses [SOURCE: Page_12]

265   (FRB, g m-2) were calculated in each soil layer multiplying the mean fine root density by the [SOURCE: Page_12]

266   soil layer volume (dm^3). The root area index (RAI, m^2 m-2) was calculated as the surface area [SOURCE: Page_12]

267   and length of fine roots divided by the sampled soil area respectively. FRD, FRB, SRL, SRA, [SOURCE: Page_12]

268   and RAI were obtained for each soil layer from the soil surface down to a depth of 17 m, for [SOURCE: Page_12]

269   the sampling position, +W, -W and NH treatments, and blocks. [SOURCE: Page_12]

270      j. Statistical analyses [SOURCE: Page_12]

271   Linear mixed-effect models were used to test the effect of soil depth, treatment and the [SOURCE: Page_12]

272   interaction between depth and treatment on cumulative length production (CLP), root [SOURCE: Page_12]

273   elongation rate (RER), daily root elongation rate (DRER), mean root elongation rate [SOURCE: Page_12]

274   (MeanRER), maximum root elongation rate (MaxRER), fine root density (FRD), fine root [SOURCE: Page_12]

275   biomass (FRB), specific root length (SRL), specific root area (SRA), root area index (RAI) and [SOURCE: Page_12]

276   root diameter for the whole soil profile. Blocks were considered as fixed effects and residues [SOURCE: Page_12]

277   were modeled by a first-order autoregressive correlation model to account for the correlations [SOURCE: Page_12]

278   between soil depths. Two-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were used to assess the effect [SOURCE: Page_12]

279   of treatments and blocks for individual soil layers on CLP, RER, DRER, MeanRER, MaxRER, [SOURCE: Page_12]

280   FRD, FRB, SRL, SRA, RAI and root diameter. Measurements within a given soil layer were [SOURCE: Page_12]

281   considered independent, since the sampling positions were located near different trees in each [SOURCE: Page_12]

282   treatment and plot. The homogeneity of variances was verified, and log-transformations were [SOURCE: Page_12]

283   used when the residuals did not follow a normal distribution. Tukey’s post-hoc Honest [SOURCE: Page_12]

284   Significant Difference (HSD) was used to determine the significant differences between [SOURCE: Page_12]

285   treatments. R software version 3.4.4 (Team R 2013) was used for all the calculations and [SOURCE: Page_12]

286   statistical analyses with a 5% significance level. The “lmerTest” package was used to perform [SOURCE: Page_12]

287   the linear mixed-effect models (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_12]

288              3. Results [SOURCE: Page_13]

289      a. Tree growth [SOURCE: Page_13]

290   Initial vertical growth was faster in the coppices than in the replanting: nine months after [SOURCE: Page_13]

291   harvesting (in February 2011 for the replanting, and February 2017 for the coppices), mean tree [SOURCE: Page_13]

292   height was 1.3 m and 1.5 m in the +W and -W replanted plots and was 2.7 m and 3.1 m in the [SOURCE: Page_13]

293   +W and -W coppice plots, respectively (Figure 2). There were no significant differences in [SOURCE: Page_13]

294   vertical growth between the +W and -W treatments for either replanting or coppice in the first [SOURCE: Page_13]

295   3 years after harvesting. [SOURCE: Page_13]

296      b. Volumetric soil water contents (SWCs) [SOURCE: Page_13]

297   In both the +W and -W treatments, rainfall events led to large variations in SWC (Figure 3). At [SOURCE: Page_13]

298   a depth of 0.5 m, SWC ranged from 5.9% to 15.8% in -W and from 7% to 19.4% in +W. At a [SOURCE: Page_13]

299   depth of 14 m, SWC ranged from 9.9% to 10.9% in -W and from 11.3% to 18.1% in +W. Over [SOURCE: Page_13]

300   the study period, throughfall reduction led to a sharp decrease in SWC, on average, by 12.9 ± [SOURCE: Page_13]

301   4.9% from the topsoil to a depth of 16 m. At the end of the rainy season, the mean SWC values [SOURCE: Page_13]

302   of the soil profile in April 2016, i.e. 2 months before the clear-cut, were 12.5% and 14.0% in - [SOURCE: Page_13]

303   W and +W, respectively and in April 2017, i.e. 10 months after the clear-cut, there were 14.4% [SOURCE: Page_13]

304   and 16.2% in -W and +W, respectively. After clear-cutting, the soil profiles were recharged [SOURCE: Page_13]

305   with water due to the interruption of tree transpiration. In +W, the gravitational soil solutions [SOURCE: Page_13]

306   reached a depth of 16 m only 10 months after clear-cutting, while in -W they reached a [SOURCE: Page_13]

307   maximum depth of only 12 m 13 months after clear-cutting. Gravitational soil solutions did not [SOURCE: Page_13]

308   reach the water table in -W over the study period (one year before the clear-cut and the first 2 [SOURCE: Page_13]

309   years of coppice management). [SOURCE: Page_13]

310      c. Fine root length production [SOURCE: Page_13]

311   The highest cumulative root length production (CLP) over the study period was at depths > 4 [SOURCE: Page_14]

312   m, in both +W and -W (Figure 4). In –W, CLP reached about 19 m m-2 at a depth of 13.5-14.8 [SOURCE: Page_14]

313   m, and only 3.9 m m-2 in the upper 2.3 m of the soil profile. In +W, the highest CLP was found [SOURCE: Page_14]

314   in an intermediate soil layer (5.5-6.8 m deep) with a CLP of 18.9 m m-2. CLP reached about 12 [SOURCE: Page_14]

315   m m-2 in very deep soil layers (13.5-14.8 m deep) and only about 2 m m-2 in the upper 2.3 m of [SOURCE: Page_14]

316   the soil profile in +W. [SOURCE: Page_14]

317      d. Fine root elongation rates [SOURCE: Page_14]

318   Daily root elongation rates (DRERs) were highly variable between the soil layer and the season, [SOURCE: Page_14]

319   in both +W and -W (Figure 5). Fine root growth started in the top soil after the clear-cut and [SOURCE: Page_14]

320   occurred more and more deeply over time in +W and -W. The intensity (measured as the [SOURCE: Page_14]

321   maximum DRER) and the period of fine root growth differed depending on soil depth. DRER [SOURCE: Page_14]

322   measured during flushes of root growth was much lower in the topsoil than at great depth in [SOURCE: Page_14]

323   +W, -W and NH (no observation below 4.8 m in NH). At a depth of 3.5-4.8 m, flushes of DRER [SOURCE: Page_14]

324   in NH came earlier than in clear-cut plots. In the coppices, the first flush of DRER occurred [SOURCE: Page_14]

325   about 6 months after clear-cutting in the topsoil, 12 months after clear-cutting in the 3.5-4.8 m [SOURCE: Page_14]

326   soil layer and 16 months after clear-cutting in the 13.5-14.8 m soil layer, in both +W and -W. [SOURCE: Page_14]

327   Moreover, DRER sharply increased in the 15.5-16.8 m layer of +W 18 months after clear- [SOURCE: Page_14]

328   cutting. [SOURCE: Page_14]

329   DRER peaks ranged from 1.5 cm m-2 day-1 in the topsoil to 94.7 cm m-2 day-1 at a depth of [SOURCE: Page_14]

330   about 12 m in +W, and from 3.5 cm m-2 day-1 in the topsoil to 83.7 cm m-2 day-1 at a depth of [SOURCE: Page_14]

331   about 14 m in -W. The maximum elongation rate of individual roots (MaxRER) reached 4.3 cm [SOURCE: Page_14]

332   day-1 in -W and 3.0 cm day-1 in +W (Table 1) and was much higher at great depths than in the [SOURCE: Page_14]

333   topsoil (data not shown). DRER values peaked at the end of the dry season in all the soil layers [SOURCE: Page_14]

334   at depths > 6 m when SWC decreased in the upper soil layers (Figure 3, Figure 5). Thus, DRER [SOURCE: Page_14]

335   values were not necessarily correlated with soil water contents for a given soil layer. A [SOURCE: Page_15]

336   synchrony between the decrease in cumulated extractable water in the topsoil and DRER peaks [SOURCE: Page_15]

337   was observed in -W (Figure 6) after the clear-cut. Successive DRER peaks appeared more and [SOURCE: Page_15]

338   more deeply at the end of the dry season when the extractable water stocks in the 0-2 m layer [SOURCE: Page_15]

339   fell below about 80 mm. [SOURCE: Page_15]

340      e. Fine root distributions [SOURCE: Page_15]

341   Auger sampling carried out on the same date in the NH plot and in the +W and -W coppices [SOURCE: Page_15]

342   1.5 years after clear-cutting showed a similar pattern of deep rooting, whatever the treatment [SOURCE: Page_15]

343   (Figure 7). Fine root densities dropped below a depth of 20 cm, then decreased gradually down [SOURCE: Page_15]

344   to the water table in NH, +W and -W. Fine roots where found down to 17 m in all treatments. [SOURCE: Page_15]

345   Total fine root biomass down to a depth of 17 m was 1,078 g m-2 in NH, 1,017 ± 301 g m-2 in [SOURCE: Page_15]

346   +W and 1,266 ± 363 g m-2 in -W (Table 2). Fine root densities were about 25% higher in -W [SOURCE: Page_15]

347   than in +W and NH in most of the soil layers. At depths > 7 m, fine root densities were at least [SOURCE: Page_15]

348   twice as high in -W as in +W and NH. [SOURCE: Page_15]

349      f. Fine root traits [SOURCE: Page_15]

350   The total root area index (RAI) was significantly (F = 32.78 , P < 0.001) higher in –W (32.8 m^2 [SOURCE: Page_15]

351   m-2) than in +W (26.5 m^2 m-2) (Figure 8). Similar RAI values in the NH stand (26.8 m^2 m-2) [SOURCE: Page_15]

352   and in the +W coppice suggested that the effect of clear-cutting on fine root dynamics was low, [SOURCE: Page_15]

353   in agreement with elongation and mortality rates observed in the upper 4 m on minirhizotron [SOURCE: Page_15]

354   tubes. RAI in the 0-1 m surface soil layer accounted for only one third of the total RAI down [SOURCE: Page_15]

355   to the water table in NH, +W and -W (about 11 m^2 m-2 on average). Significant differences [SOURCE: Page_15]

356   between +W and -W were found at depths > 5 m (P < 0.001, Figure 8). In the 5-10 m soil layers, [SOURCE: Page_15]

357   RAI was 5.0 m^2 m-2 in -W and 2.8 m^2 m-2 in +W. At depths > 10 m, RAI was 3.2 m^2 m-2 in -W [SOURCE: Page_15]

358   and only 0.4 m^2 m-2 in +W. [SOURCE: Page_15]

359   In the NH, +W and -W treatments, depth had little effect on specific root length (SRL), specific [SOURCE: Page_16]

360   root area (SRA) and fine root diameter (data not shown). SRL and SRA were significantly [SOURCE: Page_16]

361   higher in -W than in +W (P < 0.001), and similar values were observed in +W and NH, although [SOURCE: Page_16]

362   the sampling in NH (pseudo-replication on one block) did not allow a confirmation with [SOURCE: Page_16]

363   statistics (Table 2). Down to a depth of 17 m, mean SRL values were 28.8, 30.6 and 34.4 m g- 1 364       , mean SRA values were 258.8, 267.5 and 305.3 cm^2 g-1, and mean fine root diameters were [SOURCE: Page_16]

365   0.31, 0.32 and 0.36 mm in NH, +W and -W, respectively (Table 2). [SOURCE: Page_16]

366         g. Fine root mortality [SOURCE: Page_16]

367   Cumulative dead root length in the minirhizotron images over the study period of 3 years across [SOURCE: Page_16]

368   all soil layers accounted for only 6-7% of cumulative root length production, in both +W and - [SOURCE: Page_16]

369   W (Table 1). Surprisingly, dead fine root length did not increase after clear-cutting and did not [SOURCE: Page_16]

370   differ between depths and between the +W and -W treatments. Dead fine root mass estimated [SOURCE: Page_16]

371   1.5 years after clear-cutting amounted to 163.2 g m-2 in +W and 167.9 g m-2 in -W (Table 2). [SOURCE: Page_16]

372                4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_16]

373   The main novelty of our results lies in the first direct observation of fine root phenology at [SOURCE: Page_16]

374   depths of more than 10 m and derived knowledge raised from a comparison between the [SOURCE: Page_16]

375   treatments. The consequences of tree harvesting on fine root dynamics in coppice-managed [SOURCE: Page_16]

376   plantations raise specific questions never studied before along a deep tropical soil profile. Since [SOURCE: Page_16]

377   the 1980s, the minirhizotron technique has been widely used to study fine root dynamics and [SOURCE: Page_16]

378   turnover (Graefe et al., 2008; Maeght et al., 2013). Many studies have shown that the soil [SOURCE: Page_16]

379   environment close to minirhizotrons can be modified relative to undisturbed soil (Majdi and [SOURCE: Page_16]

380   Nylund, 1996), which is likely to influence fine root dynamics. However, in our study the very [SOURCE: Page_16]

381   low root mortality rates after clear-cutting revealed by minirhizotron monitoring was consistent [SOURCE: Page_16]

382   with the small amounts of dead fine roots measured by soil coring. Despite some unavoidable [SOURCE: Page_16]

383   limitations, direct observations from minirhizotron tubes or field rhizotrons is for now the most [SOURCE: Page_17]

384   accurate way of studying fine root phenology in situ (Dipesh and Schuler, 2013; Radville et [SOURCE: Page_17]

385   al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_17]

386   Root behavior in response to drought [SOURCE: Page_17]

387   The main purpose of our study was to investigate whether fine roots explore very deep soil [SOURCE: Page_17]

388   layers and do so more intensively when trees are subjected to prolonged drought periods. This [SOURCE: Page_17]

389   study, carried out in a throughfall exclusion experiment and including a non-harvested plot, [SOURCE: Page_17]

390   made it possible to assess the effect of clear-cutting under two contrasting rainfall regimes. In [SOURCE: Page_17]

391   agreement with our first hypothesis, Eucalyptus trees responded to drought by increasing fine [SOURCE: Page_17]

392   root densities at great depth. Previous studies in Brazil showed that Eucalyptus trees have the [SOURCE: Page_17]

393   ability to explore very deep layers in soils without hindrance to root growth (Christina et al., [SOURCE: Page_17]

394   2011; Pinheiro et al., 2016). In a survey of 62 tropical tree species, Markesteijn and Poorter [SOURCE: Page_17]

395   (2009) showed that trees increase belowground biomass and the proportion of deep roots in [SOURCE: Page_17]

396   response to dry conditions. Root growth in deep soil layers can increase the amount of water [SOURCE: Page_17]

397   available to sustain tree growth, which could be a key advantage for eucalypt trees in coping [SOURCE: Page_17]

398   with severe drought events (Christina et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_17]

399   After clear-cutting, fine root growth at more than 4 m deep was spectacular at the end of the [SOURCE: Page_17]

400   dry season, whatever the water supply regime. Lambais et al. (2017) also showed flushes of [SOURCE: Page_17]

401   fine root growth down to a depth of 6 m at the end of dry periods in a Brazilian eucalypt [SOURCE: Page_17]

402   plantation. Fine root growth in deep soil layers was initiated when the extractable water content [SOURCE: Page_17]

403   in the 0-2 m soil layer fell below a threshold of about 80 mm, which suggests that soil water [SOURCE: Page_17]

404   content in a particular soil layer was not the main driver of fine root growth. Endogenous and [SOURCE: Page_17]

405   exogenous factors are major drivers of fine root phenology (Moroni et al., 2003; Tierney et al., [SOURCE: Page_17]

406   2003; Abramoff and Finzi, 2015), but are difficult to disentangle in deep soil layers. The [SOURCE: Page_17]

407   successive flushes of fine root growth from the topsoil to the deepest soil layers at the end of [SOURCE: Page_17]

408   the dry season and the first months after the onset of the rainy season in +W and -W might have [SOURCE: Page_18]

409   resulted from a rapid exhaustion of water resources in the topsoil, inducing tree roots to grow [SOURCE: Page_18]

410   deeper in the soil (Schenk and Jackson, 2002; Billings, 2015). Furthermore, a strong increase [SOURCE: Page_18]

411   in sugar sap concentration and sugar allocation belowground has been shown in forests during [SOURCE: Page_18]

412   dry periods (Pate and Arthur, 1998; Scartazza et al., 2015), which could be a physiological [SOURCE: Page_18]

413   response to a rapid exhaustion of water in the top soil and could help to explain the root growth [SOURCE: Page_18]

414   in deep soil layers at the end of dry periods in our study. [SOURCE: Page_18]

415   In agreement with our second hypothesis, Eucalyptus trees facing drought adapted their root [SOURCE: Page_18]

416   morphology to maximize the root surface area to take up limited resources. SRL and SRA were [SOURCE: Page_18]

417   higher in –W than in +W, which in combination with higher fine root densities led to a much [SOURCE: Page_18]

418   higher root area index. Despite many uncertainties due to soil disturbance by the auger, the [SOURCE: Page_18]

419   results obtained are consistent with those of other studies in plantations of the same type [SOURCE: Page_18]

420   (Germon et al., 2018) as well as with other methods based on excavations (Maurice et al., 2010; [SOURCE: Page_18]

421   Laclau et al., 2013). In a meta-analysis, Ostonen et al. (2007) showed that SRL response to [SOURCE: Page_18]

422   drought varies greatly between species. While Arend et al. (2011) did not observe any [SOURCE: Page_18]

423   significant change in SRL, root area index and root tissue density for oak trees (Quercus sp.) [SOURCE: Page_18]

424   exposed to drought, Olmo et al. (2014) showed an increase in SRL and root tissue density under [SOURCE: Page_18]

425   drought conditions for 10 tree species. Eucalyptus grandis trees coped with drought in our [SOURCE: Page_18]

426   experiment by increasing their capacity to take up soil resources for a relatively low investment [SOURCE: Page_18]

427   in belowground biomass. A large increase in SRL and SRA was recently shown throughout [SOURCE: Page_18]

428   deep soil profiles down to 17 m deep for Acacia mangium Wild and E. grandis roots in response [SOURCE: Page_18]

429   to the mixture relative to monospecific stands (Germon et al., 2018). Our study showed that [SOURCE: Page_18]

430   Eucalyptus trees can modify fine root traits to enhance resource capture and the exploration of [SOURCE: Page_18]

431   very deep soil layers to survive in a context of climate change, which contributes to maintaining [SOURCE: Page_18]

432   water uptake during dry periods (Brunner et al., 2015; Christina et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_18]

433   Key role of deep roots in coppice management [SOURCE: Page_19]

434   Contrary to our third hypothesis, relatively few roots were lost by mortality (< 10% out of all [SOURCE: Page_19]

435   the roots observed over 3 years), which suggested that most of the root system remained [SOURCE: Page_19]

436   functional after clear-cutting. The effect of clear-cutting on fine root mortality remains unclear [SOURCE: Page_19]

437   in coppice-managed forest plantations. Unlike our observations, sequential coring in the 0-1 m [SOURCE: Page_19]

438   soil layer showed a decrease in fine root density after harvesting coppice-managed E. grandis [SOURCE: Page_19]

439   stands in Brazil (Mello et al., 2007). Wildy and Pate (2002) also showed high fine root mortality [SOURCE: Page_19]

440   rates after cutting the trees in plantations of Eucalyptus kochii Maiden & Blakely subsp. [SOURCE: Page_19]

441   plenissima Gardner (Brooker), but the root system superstructure was sustained down to a depth [SOURCE: Page_19]

442   of at least 4 m. Teixeira et al. (2002) showed from sequential coring in the 0-0.6 m soil layer [SOURCE: Page_19]

443   that fine-, medium- and coarse-root biomasses increased over time after harvesting coppice- [SOURCE: Page_19]

444   managed Eucalyptus urophylla S. T. Blake trees. Using the minirhizotron technique, Dickmann [SOURCE: Page_19]

445   et al. (1996) showed an absence of root mortality for coppice-managed poplar clones. Our study [SOURCE: Page_19]

446   suggested that 6-year-old Eucalyptus grandis trees have enough reserves within the root system [SOURCE: Page_19]

447   and the appropriate edaphic surrounding conditions to maintain the fine root biomass [SOURCE: Page_19]

448   established down to the water table after clear-cutting, which probably contributed to promoting [SOURCE: Page_19]

449   early shoot growth (Drake et al., 2013; Brunner et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_19]

450   Fine root growth after clear-cutting started in the topsoil, then continued successively in deeper [SOURCE: Page_19]

451   and deeper soil layers, which confirmed our third hypothesis, even though the lapse of several [SOURCE: Page_19]

452   months after clear-cutting was not expected. This pattern might have resulted from high nutrient [SOURCE: Page_19]

453   and water availabilities in the upper layers after harvesting Eucalyptus stands due to an [SOURCE: Page_19]

454   interruption of tree water uptake and fertilizer application (Laclau et al., 2010). The asynchrony [SOURCE: Page_19]

455   within the root system, delaying root growth in deep soil layers, might be a strategy for [SOURCE: Page_19]

456   maximizing the water and nutrient uptake needed to meet the high demand of these fast-growing [SOURCE: Page_19]

457   trees. The behavior observed in this study is original compared to what has been observed in [SOURCE: Page_19]

458   other types of coppice stands where repeated clear-cutting increased root growth in the top soil [SOURCE: Page_20]

459   and increased tree sensitivity to drought (Corcuera et al., 2006; Zadworny et al., 2014). In this [SOURCE: Page_20]

460   study, root growth occurred at a depth of 14 m less than one year after clear-cutting of coppice- [SOURCE: Page_20]

461   managed E. grandis trees, while the maximum depth reached by E. grandis roots one year after [SOURCE: Page_20]

462   planting is about 7 m in very deep soils (Christina et al., 2011; Laclau et al., 2013). Deep roots [SOURCE: Page_20]

463   can provide access to large amounts of water stored in the subsoil after clear-cutting and small [SOURCE: Page_20]

464   fine root densities in deep soil layers can be sufficient to take up substantial amounts of water [SOURCE: Page_20]

465   (Christina et al., 2017). Low fine root mortality rates after clear-cutting suggest that coppice- [SOURCE: Page_20]

466   management of Eucalyptus plantations might be a promising option for coping with water [SOURCE: Page_20]

467   scarcity, since the pre-existing root system can provide access to water stored throughout deep [SOURCE: Page_20]

468   soil profiles. [SOURCE: Page_20]

469   Carbon sequestration and implication for the management of eucalypt plantations [SOURCE: Page_20]

470   Surprisingly, cutting the trees did not lead to an increase in root mortality throughout the soil [SOURCE: Page_20]

471   profile, whatever the water supply regime. Fine roots play an active role in carbon (C) cycling [SOURCE: Page_20]

472   in forest ecosystems, through respiratory processes, exudation and root mortality (Balesdent [SOURCE: Page_20]

473   and Balabane, 1996; Marsden et al., 2008; Strand et al., 2008). The consequences of deep [SOURCE: Page_20]

474   rooting on subsoil C stocks in tropical planted forests and other forest ecosystems remain poorly [SOURCE: Page_20]

475   known (Nepstad et al., 1994; Harper and Tibbett, 2013; Gao et al., 2018; Meyer et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_20]

476   On the one hand, the supply of fresh carbon might promote the activity of microbes and affect [SOURCE: Page_20]

477   the stability of pre-existing organic matter through a “priming effect” (Kuzyakov et al., 2000; [SOURCE: Page_20]

478   Fontaine et al., 2007; Derrien et al., 2014). Promoting the mineralization of ancient C would [SOURCE: Page_20]

479   result in high emissions of carbon dioxide (CO$_2$) under aerobic conditions and of methane (CH$_4$) [SOURCE: Page_20]

480   under anaerobic conditions. However, a complementary study in our plots showed that CO$_2$ and [SOURCE: Page_20]

481   CH$_4$ emissions did not increase significantly after clear-cutting in the coppice-managed stands [SOURCE: Page_20]

482   compared to the non-harvested stand (Germon et al. in prep.). Gas emission rates after clear- [SOURCE: Page_20]

483   cutting in our experiment were therefore consistent with the low rates of root mortality [SOURCE: Page_21]

484   observed. On the other hand, deep roots might also contribute to sequestrating large amounts [SOURCE: Page_21]

485   of C in soil. Total below-ground carbon allocations account for about 20-30% of gross primary [SOURCE: Page_21]

486   production in Eucalyptus plantations (Ryan et al., 2010; Epron et al., 2012; Nouvellon et al., [SOURCE: Page_21]

487   2012). Fine root elongation rates were higher at great depth compared to the topsoil, with an [SOURCE: Page_21]

488   increase in fine root density after the clear-cut in very deep horizons. Moreover, microbial [SOURCE: Page_21]

489   biomass is lower in deep soil layers than in the topsoil, which in combination with oxygen [SOURCE: Page_21]

490   limitations could enhance C sequestration as a result of low mineralization rates (Taylor et al., [SOURCE: Page_21]

491   2002; Rumpel and Kögel-Knabner, 2011). C from roots is generally retained more in the soil [SOURCE: Page_21]

492   than C from aboveground litter, which is more affected by physicochemical processes and also [SOURCE: Page_21]

493   due to structural composition differences between leaves and roots (Rasse et al., 2005; Schmidt [SOURCE: Page_21]

494   et al., 2011; Menichetti et al., 2015). Further studies are needed to assess whether the [SOURCE: Page_21]

495   management of Eucalyptus plantations in very deep soils could be an effective option to help [SOURCE: Page_21]

496   mitigate the increase in CO$_2$ in the atmosphere. [SOURCE: Page_21]

497           Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_21]

498   The fine root phenology of coppice-managed Eucalyptus trees under contrasting water supply [SOURCE: Page_21]

499   regimes revealed unexpected low rates of root mortality. The early growth of the sprouts after [SOURCE: Page_21]

500   cutting the trees benefited from the root system established over the previous rotation cycle, [SOURCE: Page_21]

501   and the asynchrony of fine root growth depending on depth highlighted tree plasticity in [SOURCE: Page_21]

502   response to soil conditions. Establishing deep root systems in tropical planted forests could help [SOURCE: Page_21]

503   trees withstand the long drought periods expected under climate change in many tropical [SOURCE: Page_21]

504   regions. Our study suggested that coppice management might be an interesting option in [SOURCE: Page_21]

505   tropical Eucalyptus plantations, both to improve tree tolerance to drought and store carbon at [SOURCE: Page_21]

506   great depth in the soil. [SOURCE: Page_21]
