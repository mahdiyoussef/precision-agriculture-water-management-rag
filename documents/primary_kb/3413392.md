### 1. Introduction

Groundwater resources have significantly contributed to economic development and demographic [SOURCE: Page_3]

growth in the past half century (Shah et al., 2007). However, this growth has occurred at the cost of [SOURCE: Page_3]

increasing pressures on these water resources (Foster and Loucks, 2006; OSS, 2008; Jago-on et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

2009). For example, high abstraction rates for groundwater resources have led to the depletion of [SOURCE: Page_3]

aquifer levels (Ebraheem et al., 2003; Kamel et al., 2006). Although these effects have long been [SOURCE: Page_3]

observed, pressures on groundwater resources have drastically increased in recent years due to human [SOURCE: Page_3]

activities and rapid changes in land use. These pressures have been further intensified by government [SOURCE: Page_3]

subsidies and easier access to drilling technologies (Hong et al., 2003; Zu et al., 2003; Wang et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

2007). [SOURCE: Page_3]

Four types of arid and semiarid agrosystems depend entirely on groundwater resources (Shah [SOURCE: Page_3]

et al., 2007): i) arid agricultural systems, such as in the Middle East and North Africa; ii) industrial [SOURCE: Page_3]

agricultural systems, such as in Australia, southern Europe, and the western United States; iii) [SOURCE: Page_3]

smallholder farming systems, such as in South Asia and the plains of Northern China; and iv) extensive [SOURCE: Page_3]

pastoralism supported by groundwater, such as in Sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America. Public [SOURCE: Page_3]

concerns are related to aquifer depletion and subsequent problems that induce desertification: poor [SOURCE: Page_3]

agricultural management, water salinization and soil degradation (Feng et al., 2008; OSS, 2008; Saiko [SOURCE: Page_3]

and Zonn, 2000; Zammouri et al., 2007; Huang and Pang, 2000). In the Mediterranean region, most [SOURCE: Page_3]

oasian agrosystems with palm groves rely on deep fossil groundwater resources. These agrosystems [SOURCE: Page_3]

constitute ancestral home lands and take part in cultural identities. [SOURCE: Page_3]

To improve water resource management, deep fossil aquifers have been studied intensively in [SOURCE: Page_3]

attempts to provide diagnoses, prognostics, and potential solutions. Most studies have been limited to [SOURCE: Page_3]

examinations of hydrogeological processes and modeling the water budgets of regional aquifer systems [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Ebraheem et al., 2003; Kamel et al., 2006; Tang et al., 2007a,b; Zammouri et al., 2007; Hu et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

2007). Nevertheless, studying the evolution of deep fossil aquifers requires consideration of human [SOURCE: Page_3]

influences, which allows communities to be prepared for adaptation (Rapport and Singh, 2006; OSS, [SOURCE: Page_3]

2008). The challenge is to develop integrated approaches and evaluation tools, in order to characterize [SOURCE: Page_3]

the causes and impacts of deep aquifer depletion (van Cauwenbergh et al., 2007; Ibanez et al., 2008). [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

The biophysical and socioeconomic features of a given agrosystem, which result from public [SOURCE: Page_4]

policies, collective management and individual behaviors, can be determined using synthetic [SOURCE: Page_4]

indicators(Zalidis et al., 2004; van Cauwenbergh et al., 2007; Salvati and Zitti, 2009; Singh et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

2009). The latter can be designed using causal frameworks, such as DPSIR (Driving forces - Pressures [SOURCE: Page_4]

- States - Impacts - Responses), which was developed to define socioecological indicators (OECD, [SOURCE: Page_4]

1998; EEA, 1999). The DPSIR framework has been successfully applied to address various [SOURCE: Page_4]

environmental issues, such as the sustainability of coastal areas (Bidone and Lacerda, 2004; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Karageorgis et al., 2005), strategies for integrated water resource management at the catchment level [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Giupponi et al., 2004), and, more generally, management issues related to seascapes and landscapes [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Svarstad et al., 2008; Jago-on et al., 2009). Nevertheless, applying DPSIR to the management of deep [SOURCE: Page_4]

fossil aquifers is not straightforward because diagnostic data are limited. Furthermore, the causes and [SOURCE: Page_4]

impacts of deep aquifer depletion are neither obvious nor easy to assess (Konikow and Kendy, 2005; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Foster and Loucks, 2006; Shah et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_4]

The aim of this paper is to analyze the causalities between the management of deep fossil [SOURCE: Page_4]

aquifers and the functioning of oasian agrosystems by applying the DPSIR framework. The deep fossil [SOURCE: Page_4]

aquifers considered in this study have provided a water supply for the oasian agrosystems of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

Nefzawa region. On the one hand, these aquifers have experienced complex governance and [SOURCE: Page_4]

management issues in relation to technological development, market liberalization of the date palm [SOURCE: Page_4]

sector, and interferences between public policies and individual strategies. On the other hand, the [SOURCE: Page_4]

Nefzawa region has experienced (i) changing policies for the management of land and non-renewable [SOURCE: Page_4]

deep aquifer water resources, (ii) investments by national programs for agricultural modernization, and [SOURCE: Page_4]

(iii) environmental and socioeconomic monitoring, which provides long-term datasets. [SOURCE: Page_4]

### 2.1.1. Biophysical characteristics

The Nefzawa region is located in Kebili Governorate, southern Tunisia. It extends over a 2500 km² [SOURCE: Page_4]

area, from 33.3 N to 33.8 N latitude and from 8.5 E to 9.0 E longitude. Oases are scattered around [SOURCE: Page_4]

Chott el-Djerid (Figure 1). The climate is arid (Sarfatti, 1988). Precipitation is concentrated in the [SOURCE: Page_4]

winter, with annual values less than 100 mm. [SOURCE: Page_4]

In the vicinity of Chott el-Djerid, most soil from Nefzawa presents saline surface crusts. The [SOURCE: Page_5]

variability in soil salinity mainly results from hydrological processes in relation to topography. Thus, [SOURCE: Page_5]

oases are established over sand dunes, where shallow saline watertables result from poor natural [SOURCE: Page_5]

drainage driven by outflows towards Chotts (El Fekih and Pouget, 1966), and from the inefficiency of [SOURCE: Page_5]

artificial drainage systems (Luedeling et al., 2005; Marlet et al., 2009; Zammouri et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_5]

The water supply for Nefzawa stems from North Western Sahara Aquifer System (NWSAS), [SOURCE: Page_5]

which includes Complex Terminal (CT) and Continental Intercalary (CI) aquifers (Zammouri et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

2007). CT extends 670,000 km², with a depth between 50 and 500 m. CI covers a 1,100,000 km² area [SOURCE: Page_5]

and is located between 800 and 2500 m deep. CI geothermal water temperature is between 60 and [SOURCE: Page_5]

70°C. Water resources were poorly quantified before 1970. Since then, the NWSAS use has been [SOURCE: Page_5]

planned and monitored, as a result of government initiatives that aimed to improve water resource [SOURCE: Page_5]

management (ERESS, 1972). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Oasian crops within Nefzawa have long consisted of irrigated areas characterized by a canopy [SOURCE: Page_5]

stratification that included a lower herbaceous layer, a middle fruit growing layer, and an upper date [SOURCE: Page_5]

palm layer. The lower herbaceous layer is devoted to fodder crops with soil-enriching capacities [SOURCE: Page_5]

through nitrogen binding. The Nefzawa oasian crops are characterized by traditional irrigation, where [SOURCE: Page_5]

the main practice is flooding. Artesian or pumped water from NWSAS is brought by hydroagricultural [SOURCE: Page_5]

infrastructures, such as cooling towers, ditches and water distribution networks. [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 2.1.2. Socioeconomic context

In the distant past, oases were established on artesian springs. Access to water resources was collective, [SOURCE: Page_5]

management rules of traditional communities via farmer assemblies that regulated water partitioning [SOURCE: Page_5]

and resolved user conflicts (Trousset, 1987). In 1885, the Tunisian Government appropriated springs, [SOURCE: Page_5]

thus dispossessing inhabitants from their ancestral and collective ownership (Attia, 1965). The end of [SOURCE: Page_5]

conflicts between inhabitants as well as emigration movements since the beginning of the 1970s [SOURCE: Page_5]

(Sghaier, 2001). [SOURCE: Page_5]

The DPSIR framework was designed to characterize causalities between social, economic and [SOURCE: Page_7]

that feedback the driving forces, pressures, states and impacts. For a given ecosystem, DPSIR can be [SOURCE: Page_7]

applied from different perspectives (e.g., water, soil or vegetation resources, resource quantity or [SOURCE: Page_7]

### 3.1.1. Driving forces

When addressing the management of deep fossil aquifers within the Nefzawa oases, the driving forces [SOURCE: Page_9]

can be differentiated into demography, market constraints, public regulations and farmers’ strategies [SOURCE: Page_9]

for water access and land tenure. [SOURCE: Page_9]

The Nefzawa population doubled over the last 30 years. This growth, combined with [SOURCE: Page_9]

improvements in lifestyle, has resulted in increasing pressures on water resources. On the other hand, [SOURCE: Page_9]

emigration has led to the lack of labor force in the agricultural sector as well as agricultural investments [SOURCE: Page_9]

from emigrates (Bou Ali, 1988; Sghaier, 2001). [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

Market liberalization for the export of palm dates has oriented farmers’ practices toward the [SOURCE: Page_10]

cultivation of remunerative varieties. Various local species have disappeared due to their [SOURCE: Page_10]

inappropriateness for the market, and have been replaced by Deglet Nour variety. The latter now [SOURCE: Page_10]

represents 75% of date production within Nefzawa. Opportunities on the Deglet Nour export market [SOURCE: Page_10]

have led to the illicit expansion of irrigated areas with increasing rates of water abstraction. [SOURCE: Page_10]

The Tunisian Government implemented land privatization policies to promote individual [SOURCE: Page_10]

investment and ownership. These policies have facilitated land tenure and the expansion of irrigated [SOURCE: Page_10]

areas, with consequences to ownership rights for water resources. In an arid context where water [SOURCE: Page_10]

possession have prevailed over land ownership (Trousset, 1987), access to land implies legal and / or [SOURCE: Page_10]

illegal water mobilization. Thus, encouraging land possession led to violations of protection laws for [SOURCE: Page_10]

deep aquifers. Figure 3 shows that from 1970 to 2008 (i) the oasian harvested area in southern Tunisia [SOURCE: Page_10]

increased fivefold, and (ii) the total water abstraction increased fivefold. Partial information for water [SOURCE: Page_10]

abstraction is due to difficulties in monitoring actual abstraction, especially from illicit wells. [SOURCE: Page_10]

### 3.2.1. Public policies: the APIOS program

### 4. Discussion and conclusion

The Nefzawa oasian agrosystems rely on deep fossil aquifers for their agricultural water supply. The [SOURCE: Page_14]

resulting exploitation of these aquifers, increased fourfold between 1970 and 2000 (OSS, 2008), and [SOURCE: Page_14]

led to quantitative and qualitative degradations of water resources. Public policies were initially [SOURCE: Page_14]

13 [SOURCE: Page_14]

oriented toward aquifer exploitation for economic growth and then reshaped toward aquifer [SOURCE: Page_15]

preservation. However, it was not possible to obtaining significant advances in water resource [SOURCE: Page_15]

management because of widespread individual behaviors, such as expanding irrigated areas and [SOURCE: Page_15]

establishing illicit wells for additional irrigation. Farmers with private expansions now beseech the [SOURCE: Page_15]

authorities to maintain illicit areas, which are productive but threatened by salinization, waterlogging [SOURCE: Page_15]

and summer water shortage. This is tolerated by the authorities because of strong social pressure and [SOURCE: Page_15]

the lack of alternative solutions. The competitive use of water resources on regional and oasis scales is [SOURCE: Page_15]

also observed at the transnational level because of the extent of the NWSAS over Tunisia, Algeria and [SOURCE: Page_15]

Libya. [SOURCE: Page_15]

The failure of public policies devoted to regulating the use of water resources from deep fossil [SOURCE: Page_15]

aquifers highlights both the lack of consideration for individual initiatives when establishing such [SOURCE: Page_15]

policies and the long-term uncertainties when implementing them. From this perspective, the Nefzawa [SOURCE: Page_15]

oasian agrosystems constitute a case in which farmers’ practices are driven by various factors (cultural [SOURCE: Page_15]

perceptions of access to land and water resources, demography and technological development), with [SOURCE: Page_15]

subsequent changes in the actors’ behaviors that mitigate public actions. [SOURCE: Page_15]

From the perspective of groundwater management, the DPSIR approach used here facilitates [SOURCE: Page_15]

understanding of the causalities between several biophysical and socioeconomic components and [SOURCE: Page_15]

between different types of interactions. The DPSIR framework also relies on several simplifications [SOURCE: Page_15]

resulting from a condensed representation of a complex system, especially when addressing human [SOURCE: Page_15]

activities and their consequent forcing on natural resources. Methodological improvements are required [SOURCE: Page_15]

that rely on adequate spatiotemporal scales, normalized observations, and deeper characterizations of [SOURCE: Page_15]

agrosystem biophysical and socioeconomic functioning. [SOURCE: Page_15]

From the perspective of evolution scenarios for the Nefzawa oasian agrosystems, another [SOURCE: Page_15]

benefit of implementing DPSIR is the identification of first-order influences for the design of [SOURCE: Page_15]

biophysical and socioeconomic indicators in relation to anthropogenic pressures and agrienvironmental [SOURCE: Page_15]

and economic performance (Zalidis et al., 2004; Bidone and Lacerda, 2004). Below, we propose such [SOURCE: Page_15]

indicators for the Nefzawa oasian agrosystems. [SOURCE: Page_15]
