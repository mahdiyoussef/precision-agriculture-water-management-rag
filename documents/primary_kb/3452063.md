473      3.3. Nitrogen and water Apparent Recovery Efficiency by Difference (ARED) [SOURCE: Page_23]

474   To grasp the added value of input resources (water and nitrogen) on wheat grain yields, the [SOURCE: Page_23]

475   Apparent Recovery Efficiency by Difference (ARED) for the two input resources (Keller [SOURCE: Page_23]

476   and Keller, 1995; Rao et al., 1992) is calculated, for wheat grain yields following different [SOURCE: Page_23]

477   previous crops (wheat, fallow, potato and fava bean) in the two soil water holding [SOURCE: Page_23]

478   capacities (low and high) [Eqs. (12) & (13) section 2.4.1]. ARED was computed for [SOURCE: Page_23]

479   nitrogen in both cases of irrigation (I1 and I0), as well as for irrigation in both cases of [SOURCE: Page_23]

480   nitrogen supply (F$_1$ and F$_0$) (Fig. 2). The separation of means was done using 2-way [SOURCE: Page_24]

481   ANOVA analysis (Tukey test). [SOURCE: Page_24]

482   Concerning fertilization, wheat grain yields increased the most because of nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_24]

483   fertilization (ARED N) when cultivated in a wheat-wheat rotation, whether irrigated or not, [SOURCE: Page_24]

484   in low WHC soil (Fig. 2i & 2iii) (4.61 and 2.79, respectively) or in high WHC (Fig. 2ii & [SOURCE: Page_24]

485   2iv) (4.3 and 2.46, respectively). This means that for each 1 kg of added N, the increase in [SOURCE: Page_24]

486   grain yields for wheat in the wheat-wheat rotation is greater than that observed in other [SOURCE: Page_24]

487   rotations. In other words, wheat grain yields in a wheat-wheat rotation will be more [SOURCE: Page_24]

488   sensitive to lower fertilization, and thus more likely to decrease than wheat grain yields in [SOURCE: Page_24]

489   other rotations. The lowest slope observed for wheat grain yields was in the case of a wheat- [SOURCE: Page_24]

490   fava bean rotation, conforming to several studies that show that fava bean is an excellent [SOURCE: Page_24]

491   previous crop (Angus et al., 2015; Plaza-Bonilla et al., 2017; Yau et al., 2003), which partly [SOURCE: Page_24]

492   reduces the dependence of the main crop (winter wheat in this case) on nitrogen fertilization [SOURCE: Page_24]

493   (Voisin et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_24]

494   In the case of wheat-fallow and wheat-potato rotations, the results were more nuanced. In [SOURCE: Page_24]

495   low WHC soil, fallow land allows for a better use of N than potato as a previous crop, as [SOURCE: Page_24]

496   soil nutrients are rebalanced and soil biota is re-established (Plaza-Bonilla et al., 2017). On [SOURCE: Page_24]

497   the other hand, potato becomes a better previous crop than fallow land in high WHC soils. [SOURCE: Page_24]

498   This is due to the ability of these soils to store more water and facilitate the flux of nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_24]

499   to the roots by mass flow, for the next crop. [SOURCE: Page_24]

500   Concerning irrigation, the results are quite surprising as in low WHC (Fig. 2v), wheat in a [SOURCE: Page_24]

501   wheat-wheat rotation requires less water (in terms of irrigation) than in other rotations, [SOURCE: Page_24]

502   which contradicts other studies that show that wheat is more sensitive to irrigation (thus [SOURCE: Page_24]

503   more dependent) in a wheat-wheat rotation type (Gu et al., 2002; Musick et al., 1994; [SOURCE: Page_25]

504   Zhang and Oweis, 1999). This result can be explained by larger periods of fallow land than [SOURCE: Page_25]

505   in the case of wheat-wheat rotations (maximum of 3 months of fallow). Therefore leading [SOURCE: Page_25]

506   to larger amounts of evaporated water. This evaporated water, however, becomes less [SOURCE: Page_25]

507   important in high WHC (Fig. 2vi), in which more water is stored to its high capacity. In [SOURCE: Page_25]

508   coherence with the literature (Passioura and Angus, 2010), well-fertilized wheat (F$_1$) [SOURCE: Page_25]

509   becomes more water reactive in a wheat-wheat rotation than other rotations. On the other [SOURCE: Page_25]

510   hand, wheat in a wheat-fava bean rotation becomes more reactive to water if nitrogen is [SOURCE: Page_25]

600   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_29]

601   When considering high resource-use efficiency, high system productivity (protein and [SOURCE: Page_29]

602   profit) as well as low economic risk in terms of system sustainability as a whole package, [SOURCE: Page_29]

603   it is clear that there is no comprehensible optimal scenario. Depending on our simulation [SOURCE: Page_29]

604   results, the productivity (protein and net profit) of the different wheat-based cropping [SOURCE: Page_29]

605   systems in two different soil WHC types, taking into consideration risk and wheat [SOURCE: Page_29]

606   efficiency results, are plotted on a conceptual guide-map (Fig. 6). In addition, the [SOURCE: Page_29]

607   productivity frontier is displayed to understand the best attainable scenarios. [SOURCE: Page_29]

608 [SOURCE: Page_29]

609   Using this conceptual guide-map (Fig. 6) is essential for comparing the performance of the [SOURCE: Page_29]

610   different wheat-based cropping systems, but also to identify the possible levers to improve [SOURCE: Page_29]

611   the performance of these systems: [SOURCE: Page_29]

612   - Preserving deep soils for wheat cultivation: Such a strategy is achieved by combatting [SOURCE: Page_29]

613   ongoing soil degradation, especially in dry and sub-dry areas. The presence of many typical [SOURCE: Page_29]

614   cereal area plains in the Mediterranean region with low soil water holding capacity results [SOURCE: Page_29]

615   in grain yield reduction due to post-anthesis terminal drought where a strong relationship [SOURCE: Page_29]

616   was found between actual evapotranspiration in the grain filling phase and the final grain [SOURCE: Page_30]

617   yield. (Karrou and Oweis, 2012). As an example, the soil in the Medjerda-Tunisia (Souissi [SOURCE: Page_30]

618   et al., 2017), Saïs-Morocco (Mohamed et al., 2018) and the Bekaa plain of Lebanon [SOURCE: Page_30]

619   (Darwish et al., 2006b) typical cereal plains are more than 60% characterized by a low [SOURCE: Page_30]

620   water holding capacity. [SOURCE: Page_30]

621   Deep soils in arid area are currently mostly dominated by cereal crops (especially durum [SOURCE: Page_30]

622   and soft wheat), but a wide range of irrigated crops can also be observed, such as [SOURCE: Page_30]

623   vegetables, orchards and fodder crops which represent at least 30% of the total cultivated [SOURCE: Page_30]

624   area (Caiserman et al., 2019). These crops are less sensitive to the depth of the soil than [SOURCE: Page_30]

625   cereal crops. Therefore, keeping deep soils for cereals could be a leverage for policy- [SOURCE: Page_30]

626   makers in order to increase their production, input-use efficiency, and reduce the economic [SOURCE: Page_30]

627   risk of low relative productivity by at least 48%, 35% and 38% respectively, as shown in [SOURCE: Page_30]

628   this study. [SOURCE: Page_30]

629   - Reduction of the areas dedicated to wheat-fallow cropping systems (WF in Fig.5): such a [SOURCE: Page_30]

630   cropping system is characterized by low economic and nutritional performances compared [SOURCE: Page_30]

631   to other cropping systems. This explains why this system has gradually disappeared from [SOURCE: Page_30]

632   arid areas, and only exists for those practicing multiple activities (MoA, 2010). For those [SOURCE: Page_30]

633   farmers, the advantage of this system is that it requires very few inputs (particularly in high [SOURCE: Page_30]

634   WHC soils) and especially involves very little risk compared to other cropping systems. [SOURCE: Page_30]

635   Today, in the Mediterranean region, even if the areas dedicated to this type of rotation are [SOURCE: Page_30]

636   less common than those dedicated to other rotation types, half of the land is left [SOURCE: Page_30]

637   uncultivated each year (López-Bellido and López-Bellido, 2003). Most of the current [SOURCE: Page_30]

638   intensification policies in dry areas encourage the mobilization of more inputs by totally or [SOURCE: Page_30]

639   partially subsidizing access to irrigation water, fertilizers, seeds, etc. By promoting wheat- [SOURCE: Page_31]

640   based alternative systems other than wheat-fallow, it is potentially possible, according to [SOURCE: Page_31]

641   our results, to increase rotational (2 year) protein production by at least 50%, at the Bekaa [SOURCE: Page_31]

642   plain level. [SOURCE: Page_31]

643   - Intensification of wheat cultivation by increasing the amount of inputs: as expressed [SOURCE: Page_31]

644   before, this lever is the most used by policy-makers and farmers to increase wheat [SOURCE: Page_31]

645   production (Pala et al., 2007; Sadras, 2004). The guide-map (Fig. 6) shows that the [SOURCE: Page_31]

646   intensification by increasing inputs is not effective in all cropping systems and the effects [SOURCE: Page_31]

647   on rotational performance, efficiency and risk are not consistent with all cropping systems. [SOURCE: Page_31]

648   Unfortunately, most farmers manage wheat cultivation regardless of the rotation type [SOURCE: Page_31]

649   (Armengot et al., 2011), by considering intensified wheat management systems, presented [SOURCE: Page_31]

650   in a wheat-wheat rotation as a reference pathway to increase productivity (Balkovič et al., [SOURCE: Page_31]

651   2014). Such means, which are encouraged by policy-makers, are not always reasonable as [SOURCE: Page_31]

652   the efficiency of wheat in utilizing the resources decreases dramatically in different dry [SOURCE: Page_31]

653   areas in the Mediterranean (Ben Zekri et al., 2018; Giménez et al., 2016; Ryan et al., 2007; [SOURCE: Page_31]

654   Souissi et al., 2017; Yau et al., 2003). [SOURCE: Page_31]

655   - Switching from wheat-wheat rotation to wheat-legume crops (fava bean in our study): [SOURCE: Page_31]

656   The wheat-legume rotation has shown better productivity than the wheat-wheat system, [SOURCE: Page_31]

657   significantly higher efficiency (nitrogen and water) and much lower economic risk. Such [SOURCE: Page_31]

658   findings were not very surprising as diversified rotations with catch crops usually yield [SOURCE: Page_31]

659   high NUE (Beaudoin et al., 2005; Hansen et al., 2015; Moreau et al., 2012). Growing winter [SOURCE: Page_31]

660   wheat with low inputs leads to a small sacrifice in terms of productivity, which may be a [SOURCE: Page_31]

661   reason why farmers prefer intensified wheat-wheat cropping systems. Apart from [SOURCE: Page_31]

662   fertilization, mechanization limitations in legumes cultivation and the dependence on [SOURCE: Page_32]

663   labour (weeding, harvesting) and establishments (e.g. storage warehouses), which would [SOURCE: Page_32]

664   be pricey if not already owned by the farmer, are also obstacles preventing farmers from [SOURCE: Page_32]

665   an easy switch to wheat-legume rotations. Moreover, fava beans production is a local [SOURCE: Page_32]

666   product, which is sold locally and thus linked to the national level. This could be attributed [SOURCE: Page_32]

667   to the low-trust that is given by farmers regarding national and local markets fearing [SOURCE: Page_32]

668   “unlawful speculation”. Instead, many of them would prefer investing in wheat cultivation [SOURCE: Page_32]

669   knowing in advance that the prices will not witness any change (even if negligible), as the [SOURCE: Page_32]

670   government buys the grain yield. [SOURCE: Page_32]

671   Indeed, wheat farmers in Lebanon as well as in the MENA region do over-fertilize their [SOURCE: Page_32]

672   crops for several reasons. First, farmers tend to believe that applying high rates of N would [SOURCE: Page_32]

673   decrease their economic risk by increasing the grain weight and the final grain yield, and [SOURCE: Page_32]

674   second because they do benefit from stable prices due to the support system at the national [SOURCE: Page_32]

675   level. This issue (over-fertilization) has been widely reported in Tunisia (Cheikh M’hamed [SOURCE: Page_32]

676   et al., 2014; Thabet et al., 2010), Egypt and Morocco (FAO, 2018). In another study, it was [SOURCE: Page_32]

677   also reported that the over nitrogen fertilization all over the Mediterranean is highly [SOURCE: Page_32]

678   impacting, negatively, the soil and water quality in the area in addition to reducing the [SOURCE: Page_32]

679   economic yield (the huge N input represents 15% of the input cost in Morocco) (Heng et [SOURCE: Page_32]

680   al., 2007). On the other hand, a study in Italy has suggested the increase of nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_32]

681   application to up to 200 kg ha-1 (similar to the rates applied in the Bekaa plain) for higher [SOURCE: Page_32]

682   grain weight and better yield, taking into account the high environmental impacts (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_32]

683   nitrogen leaching) (Abad et al., 2004). [SOURCE: Page_32]

684   Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_32]

685   Broadly, as frequently cited, increased production as well as increasing the efficiency in [SOURCE: Page_33]

686   using the resources are the main requirements for feeding a vastly growing and changing [SOURCE: Page_33]

687   world (Godfray et al., 2010). Many farmers, who work under small profit margins seek [SOURCE: Page_33]

688   high production as a primary goal like many industrialized systems, which definitely trade [SOURCE: Page_33]

689   off against ecosystem values and environmental aspects (Foley et al., 2005). Such an [SOURCE: Page_33]

690   approach eventually leads to what we witness today in terms of negative environmental [SOURCE: Page_33]

691   consequences and resource depletion (Pimentel, 2005), as well as negative social impacts [SOURCE: Page_33]

692   (Marks et al., 2010). The results of our research showed that careful considerations should [SOURCE: Page_33]

693   be coupled with recommending a specific cropping system, especially at field level. No [SOURCE: Page_33]

694   optimal scenario was found (rotation and management) that may simultaneously guarantee [SOURCE: Page_33]

695   low risk, significant protein production, large net profit, and high resource-use efficiency [SOURCE: Page_33]

696   (NUE and WUE), at least for the rotations simulated in this study. Several studies have [SOURCE: Page_33]

697   analyzed one crop (or one cropping system) in relation to its productivity and efficiency. [SOURCE: Page_33]

698   Our results, nevertheless, by allowing the farmers and policy-makers to categorize existing [SOURCE: Page_33]

699   systems in terms of their performance and risk, indicate that at field level, a wheat-legume [SOURCE: Page_33]

700   rotation in which wheat is cultivated under semi-intensive and/or extensive agricultural [SOURCE: Page_33]

701   management is very recommended, especially for those who cannot bear high-risk systems, [SOURCE: Page_33]

702   securing both high efficiency in terms of resource-use and great protein production. In [SOURCE: Page_33]

703   future research, anticipating our results, we intend to upgrade this study to the farm level, [SOURCE: Page_33]

704   where more criteria and parameters may come across to propose a whole integrated system [SOURCE: Page_33]

705   that is profitable, non-risky and sustainable, overpowering food security deterioration and [SOURCE: Page_33]

706   nevertheless efficient in terms of resource-use efficiency. [SOURCE: Page_33]
