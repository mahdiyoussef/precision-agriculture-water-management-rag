Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

This study presents a critical overview of reported essential oil (EO) extractions from citrus [SOURCE: Page_3]

peel wastes (CPW), including harmonized data on the various citrus species and cultivars. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Harmonization is vital to enable sustainable management practices. The review only includes [SOURCE: Page_3]

eco-efficient extraction techniques. In total, the review contains 66 quantified examples using [SOURCE: Page_3]

i) mechanical cold press ii) thermal extraction with water or steam media iii) thermal [SOURCE: Page_3]

microwave-assisted extraction iv) other innovative methods (such as ultrasound). The [SOURCE: Page_3]

technologies were assessed for their potential use in cascading production to achieve [SOURCE: Page_3]

economies of scope, particularly considering the use of extraction residues for subsequent [SOURCE: Page_3]

fermentation to produce various products from energy carriers to enzymes. Two techniques [SOURCE: Page_3]

were found insufficient for direct use in fermentation. Cold press extracts an inadequate amount [SOURCE: Page_3]

of EO (average yield 2.85% DW) to ensure suitable fermentation, while solvent extraction [SOURCE: Page_3]

contaminates the residues for its subsequent use. Extractions using water media, such as [SOURCE: Page_3]

hydrodistillation and microwave-assisted hydrodistillation (average EO yield 2.87% DW), are [SOURCE: Page_3]

feasible for the liquid-based fermentation processes, such as submerged fermentation. Steam [SOURCE: Page_3]

extraction is feasible for any type of fermentation. Our review highlighted solvent-free [SOURCE: Page_3]

microwave extraction (average EO yield 5.29% DW) as the most effective method, which [SOURCE: Page_3]

provides a high yield in a short extraction time. We also uncovered and discussed several [SOURCE: Page_3]

inconsistencies in existing yields and energy consumption published data. [SOURCE: Page_3]

### 1. Introduction

Citrus fruits, with a production of over 100 million tonnes per year, represent the largest fruit [SOURCE: Page_4]

million tonnes worldwide (Mahato et al., 2019), mainly composed of inedible citrus peel waste [SOURCE: Page_4]

either landfilled, incinerated, composted or in some regions partly used as animal feed and [SOURCE: Page_4]

pectin production (Martín et al., 2018). There are several factors that limit the use of CPW for [SOURCE: Page_4]

composting: a very low nitrogen content preventing fast decomposition (Mahato et al., 2019), [SOURCE: Page_4]

feed is coherent with top priorities in the updated food waste hierarchy (Teigiserova et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

2020), low pH of 3.4, anti-nutritional properties (Martín et al., 2018; Ani et al., 2015), and [SOURCE: Page_4]

potential costs for transportation (Zema et al., 2018a) prevents widespread use of CPW in [SOURCE: Page_4]

animal feed. From a circular bioeconomy perspective, the next best option is to use CPW for [SOURCE: Page_4]

material recycling to extract high-value compounds (Teigiserova et al., 2020), leading to more [SOURCE: Page_4]

2018a). Its removal is necessary before biological treatment, as demonstrated by (Calabrò et [SOURCE: Page_5]

essential oils content. It can be used for a variety of applications in the food, pharmaceutical, [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 2. Methods

2.1 Literature review methodology [SOURCE: Page_7]

A comprehensive literature search restricted to quantified studies for limonene extraction was [SOURCE: Page_7]

performed. The keywords "citrus", "waste", "value-added", "biorefinery", "food waste", [SOURCE: Page_7]

"chemical", and limonene* were used in the Google Scholar search engine and limited to the [SOURCE: Page_7]

English language. The keywords were used in combination with the Boolean operator "AND" [SOURCE: Page_7]

and the multi-character wildcard "*" search for limonene as it can have multiple variations, [SOURCE: Page_7]

such as d-limonene. There was no period limit for chosen articles, however, most relevant [SOURCE: Page_7]

research included is published after 2005. The abstracts and result sections were screened, and [SOURCE: Page_7]

the literature without quantitative data and those including hazardous materials like hexane [SOURCE: Page_7]

were excluded. Additional literature sources, applying green chemistry principles, were added [SOURCE: Page_7]

through the snowballing technique, i.e., by adding the literature from the article's reference [SOURCE: Page_7]

section. Overall, 18 sources were identified for a detailed review process. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Supporting Information, Table SI.1, includes more details on the extraction methods reported [SOURCE: Page_7]

in this review, including data on temperature, pressure, duration, and energy. The recalculation [SOURCE: Page_7]

of yields is also included. The structure of data allows easy manipulation and selection of [SOURCE: Page_7]

feedstock and green methods. Table SI.2 includes data excluded from this review that reported [SOURCE: Page_7]

removal efficiency rather than actual yields but include verified EO extraction methods. [SOURCE: Page_7]

2.2 Selection and harmonization procedure for extraction technologies [SOURCE: Page_7]

In order to select technologies according to the green chemistry and harmonize their results, a [SOURCE: Page_7]

step-wise approach was chosen, shown in Figure 2. First, we build on general green chemistry [SOURCE: Page_7]

principles (Anastas and Eghbali, 2010; Poliakoff et al., 2002), which seek to reduce hazards [SOURCE: Page_7]

and design a safer process across all chemical life cycle stages, employing rules for waste [SOURCE: Page_7]

prevention, energy, and reaction efficiency. Following these principles, we excluded [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

technologies using toxic ingredients, such as hexane, a solvent used for EO extraction (Choi et [SOURCE: Page_8]

al., 2015; Wikandari et al., 2015). Hexane is restricted under international regulations such as [SOURCE: Page_8]

REACH (EC 1907/2006) or IPPC (96/61/EC) (Ozturk et al., 2019). Besides following the rule [SOURCE: Page_8]

"to reduce or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances" (Mulvihill et al., 2011), [SOURCE: Page_8]

we further assessed the elimination of waste by evaluating the use of residues from EO [SOURCE: Page_8]

extraction for subsequent fermentation processes. The chosen technologies are aligned with [SOURCE: Page_8]

previous findings on green extraction methods (Putnik et al., 2017; Sharma et al., 2017; [SOURCE: Page_8]

González-Rivera et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_8]

Secondly, the composition of citrus peels was reported as a different citrus cultivar and varieties [SOURCE: Page_8]

from the same citrus fruit, which can have different EO yields and different EO compositions [SOURCE: Page_8]

(including limonene content). Therefore, not only the type of fruit but also the cultivar and [SOURCE: Page_8]

varieties represent crucial information. Additionally, geographical aspects such as soil quality, [SOURCE: Page_8]

nutrient availability, temperature, climate, rainfall also influence the EO content and the [SOURCE: Page_8]

phytochemical composition. However, these aspects were excluded from data reporting in this [SOURCE: Page_8]

study due to a lack of information. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Thirdly, the units of the reported yields were assessed. The authors report EO extraction in two [SOURCE: Page_8]

ways, either as an extraction efficiency and as an extraction yield. Extraction efficiency refers [SOURCE: Page_8]

to how much (in %) of the limonene or EO have been removed by applied technology. For [SOURCE: Page_8]

example, the steam distillation method efficiency was 70% limonene removal (Martín et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

2018). More details on removal efficiencies can be found in Supporting Information Table SI.2. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Such reporting of results cannot be used to know how much of the product we can extract. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Without knowing the initial and final concentration of limonene (or EO), the information is [SOURCE: Page_8]

insufficient to calculate the actual yields (de la Torre et al., 2019; Martín et al., 2018; Boluda- [SOURCE: Page_8]

Aguilar et al., 2010). The EO extraction yields are reported in several ways A) per fresh [SOURCE: Page_8]

biomass (i) weight per fresh weight substrate in %, (ii) volume of extract per fresh weight [SOURCE: Page_8]

7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

substrate), (iii) per whole fruit (for cold pressing), B) per dry biomass C) weight per weight of [SOURCE: Page_9]

solvent used (in case of supercritical CO$_2$ extraction). The comparison between high yield [SOURCE: Page_9]

reported per dry versus per fresh basis should not be made, or should at least be highlighted to [SOURCE: Page_9]

make the reader aware of such discrepancies. This is evident in the study by Ciriminna et al. [SOURCE: Page_9]

(2017), where the yields based on dry weight can be five times higher than those reported per [SOURCE: Page_9]

fresh weight. We harmonized these results using the moisture content of peels reported by [SOURCE: Page_9]

authors. When the dry matter content of the peels was not directly mentioned, 20% DW was [SOURCE: Page_9]

used, representing the typical average value as reported in Boluda-Aguilar and López-Gómez [SOURCE: Page_9]

(2013), Chen et al. (2016), and Pourbafrani et al. (2010). The yields reported per whole fruit [SOURCE: Page_9]

were adjusted to represent peel fraction. Additionally, the data that included the volume of EO [SOURCE: Page_9]

has been recalculated using density reported previously (Kamal et al., 2011). More details can [SOURCE: Page_9]

be found in Supporting Information, Table SI.1. [SOURCE: Page_9]

Lastly, we also included data on the scale of the process (small laboratory, small pilots, and [SOURCE: Page_9]

industrial-scale) and data on energy use when reported. [SOURCE: Page_9]

2.3 Extraction methods [SOURCE: Page_9]

The analysis of 18 sources included 66 examples that can be categorized according to [SOURCE: Page_9]

extraction methods into four categories a) mechanical - cold press; b) thermal with water or [SOURCE: Page_9]

steam media (hydrodistillation, steam distillation, steam explosion); c) microwave-assisted [SOURCE: Page_9]

thermal extraction, d) and other innovative methods: such as supercritical CO$_2$, bio-solvent [SOURCE: Page_9]

extraction, and ultrasound extraction. [SOURCE: Page_9]

Cold press is one of the oldest oil extraction methods (Karaman et al., 2015), where the oils are [SOURCE: Page_9]

released by applying mechanical pressure via tapered screw press (Ferhat et al., 2007), using [SOURCE: Page_9]

needles to lacerate the oil glands in the peels (Mahato et al., 2019). The resulting oil fraction is [SOURCE: Page_9]

a watery emulsion (Mahato et al., 2019), which is then centrifuged to recover the citrus EO [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

(Ferhat et al., 2007). Without any heat application, EO retains most of the volatile compounds [SOURCE: Page_10]

and waxes, which are important for their aromatic properties (Rassem et al., 2016; Sawamura, [SOURCE: Page_10]

2010). [SOURCE: Page_10]

Among the reviewed literature, several technologies applied steam or water media as the [SOURCE: Page_10]

primary extraction method, namely hydrodistillation, steam distillation, steam explosion, and [SOURCE: Page_10]

instantaneous controlled pressure drop. They share a similarity in the physicochemical [SOURCE: Page_10]

principle of applying simultaneous internal and external heat and mass transfer to the peels [SOURCE: Page_10]

(Berka-Zougali et al., 2012; Mahato et al., 2019). In hydrodistillation, a long contact of water [SOURCE: Page_10]

and biomass can contaminate EO with waste products from hydrolysis (Berka-Zougali et al., [SOURCE: Page_10]

2012) and with biopolymers solid residues, such as polyphenols or insoluble cellulosic matter [SOURCE: Page_10]

(González-Rivera et al., 2016). Applying steam to the CPW releases the essential oil droplets [SOURCE: Page_10]

(captured in the steam) that are separated after vapor is condensed (Sahraoui et al., 2011). Novel [SOURCE: Page_10]

methods try to shorten the treatment time while increasing the EO yield by applying pressure, [SOURCE: Page_10]

such as steam explosion (Negro et al., 2016) and instantaneous controlled pressure drop (or [SOURCE: Page_10]

D.I.C) (Rezzoug and Louka, 2009). D.I.C is a patented method used for drying-texturation in [SOURCE: Page_10]

food items (Rezzoug and Louka, 2009). After applying a vacuum (at 50mbar), the pressure is [SOURCE: Page_10]

increased, and steam is applied, and then the mixture is quickly decompressed (Rezzoug and [SOURCE: Page_10]

Louka, 2009). [SOURCE: Page_10]

Microwave-assisted extractions are relatively new methods, mostly done on the laboratory [SOURCE: Page_10]

scale with a few small-scale commercial applications (Milestone reactor; Bustamante et al., [SOURCE: Page_10]

2016) and pilot-scale reactors (Milestone 75 L pilot plant, Filly et al., 2014). There are several [SOURCE: Page_10]

types of EO extracting microwaves using (i) water, (ii) steam, and (iii) in-situ water, all [SOURCE: Page_10]

performing with reduced extraction time compared to conventional methods. Applying water [SOURCE: Page_10]

and steam is similar to previously described methods, but the improved process performance [SOURCE: Page_10]

is a key advantage (Bustamante et al., 2016; Ciriminna et al., 2014). It can also be combined [SOURCE: Page_10]

9 [SOURCE: Page_10]

with ultrasound technology, where the ultrasonic probe is applied simultaneously with a coaxial [SOURCE: Page_11]

microwave antenna (González-Rivera et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_11]

Finally, microwave energy can be applied to CPW without using any solvent or media. Besides [SOURCE: Page_11]

using the term Solvent-free microwave extraction (SFME), the authors in the reviewed [SOURCE: Page_11]

literature use several other terms for this technique: [SOURCE: Page_11]

•   "Microwave Hydrodiffusion and Gravity (MHG)" (Bousbia et al., 2009; Boukroufa et [SOURCE: Page_11]

al., 2015), when gravity is used for condensation of the essential oil [SOURCE: Page_11]

•   "Microwave dry distillation" (Ferhat et al., 2016) [SOURCE: Page_11]

•   "solventless MW-assisted extraction approach (SMWAE)" (González-Rivera et al., [SOURCE: Page_11]

2016) [SOURCE: Page_11]

•   "Microwave-accelerated distillation (MAD)" (Ferhat et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_11]

The agreement on the terminology could further advance the research efforts and push the [SOURCE: Page_11]

scale-up of this technology further. Therefore, we refer to all of them as SFME, henceforward. [SOURCE: Page_11]

All of these techniques use in-situ water of the plant material, which is heated and evaporates [SOURCE: Page_11]

(Chemat et al., 2015), releasing EO during the break-down of the cell wall due to internal [SOURCE: Page_11]

pressure (Negro et al., 2016). Solvent-free technologies are further aligned with the green [SOURCE: Page_11]

chemistry principle as they avoid the use of auxiliary substances (e.g., solvents, separation [SOURCE: Page_11]

agents, and so forth) (Poliakoff et al., 2002) and thus have improved reaction mass efficiency [SOURCE: Page_11]

(mass of final product divided to the mass of reactants ratio) (Mulvihill et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_11]

Further methods include solid-liquid solvent extraction using bio-solvents or supercritical fluid [SOURCE: Page_11]

and ultrasound method. Solvent extraction is a simple method but can lead to the loss of most [SOURCE: Page_11]

volatile compounds and therefore change the quality of EO (Berka-Zougali et al., 2012). The [SOURCE: Page_11]

green solvent included in this study follows Environmental, Health, and Safety (EHS) [SOURCE: Page_11]

parameters (Ozturk et al., 2019), while CO$_2$ takes advantage of supercritical fluid being the [SOURCE: Page_11]

10 [SOURCE: Page_11]

intermediate between liquid and gas for their density and viscosity (Mira et al., 1999). Lastly, [SOURCE: Page_12]

ultrasound Clevenger Extraction employs an ultrasonic probe without using any solvent or [SOURCE: Page_12]

water, where frequency allows EO liberation (Pingret et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_12]

3.1 Citrus peel composition [SOURCE: Page_12]

called flavedo. The essential oils are mainly contained in the flavedo part and are either absent [SOURCE: Page_12]

or present in minimal amounts in the albedo (Ferhat et al., 2007). The thickness of the peels [SOURCE: Page_12]

influences the moisture content of the peel and the EO yields, as albedo's sponge-like quality [SOURCE: Page_12]

can soak up the oils. A high CPW moisture content implies that more energy is needed for the [SOURCE: Page_12]

extraction of EO. Certain cultivars and varieties of orange have one of the lowest moisture [SOURCE: Page_12]

content in peels (Pera orange 66% - Lima orange 70%), while Sweet Lime peels can have up [SOURCE: Page_12]

to 79% (Barros et al., 2012). Therefore, knowing the variety and cultivar is crucial information [SOURCE: Page_12]

when comparing the yields. Three authors reported yields for several varieties of citrus; [SOURCE: Page_12]

however, we chose to report one variety per main citrus group, i.e., one per lemon, lime, orange, [SOURCE: Page_12]

and grapefruit. The common cultivars reported are Eureka (Citrus limon L.), Villa França [SOURCE: Page_12]

(Citrus limon L.), Marsh Seedless (Citrus paradisi L.), Tarocco (Citrus sinensis L.), Valencia [SOURCE: Page_12]

late (Citrus sinensis L.), Washington Navel (Citrus sinensis L.), Tangelo Seminole (Citrus [SOURCE: Page_12]

paradisi Macf.) (Ferhat et al., 2016; Bousbia et al., 2009), while Bousbia et al. (2009) [SOURCE: Page_12]

additionally reported on lime (Citrus aurantifolia (Chrism.) Swing) and Ferhat et al. (2016) [SOURCE: Page_12]

report on Rhobs-el-arsa (Citrus medica L.), sweet orange Sorbonne (Citrus aurantium), and [SOURCE: Page_12]

sour orange Bouquetier de Nice (Citrus paradisi). Bustamante et al. (2016) present data on [SOURCE: Page_12]

orange Navel Navelate, Midknight, and Valencia Late cultivars; Verna variety of lemons; [SOURCE: Page_12]
