12   In the dry tropics, leaf activity of dominant woody species in the dry season is fundamental for [SOURCE: Page_2]

ecosystem vitality. Prediction of the potential impact of climate change on leaf phenology of these [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   species is a major challenge in Tropical Ecology. However, the environmental controls of inter-annual [SOURCE: Page_2]

variation of leaf flushing and leaf fall are poorly understood. We focused on a comparison between the [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   two major drivers considered in the dry tropics: soil water availability and atmospheric conditions. We [SOURCE: Page_2]

performed a 5 year phenological study of 39 individual trees of Acacia tortilis ssp. raddiana in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   northern Sahelian zone. Our data show that a significant inter-annual variation, up to 2.0 months, [SOURCE: Page_2]

affects both the timing of the peaks of leaf flush and leaf fall. The full canopy stage lasted between 5 [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   and 8 months, broadly including the rainy season and the “cool” dry season, the timing of leaf flush [SOURCE: Page_2]

peak varying from one month before the first rains to one month after. The trees have access to a deep [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   water table (31 m) which had no significant inter-annual variation. Predictions of canopy fullness from [SOURCE: Page_2]

upper soil water availability (SWA), temperature, relative humidity and vapor pressure deficit (VPD), [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   were compared applying polynomial logistic regressions pooling all the years. We found that [SOURCE: Page_2]

atmospheric conditions and particularly VPDmax (midday value) were far better predictors than SWA. [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   Simulations based on VPDmax, significantly predicted the inter-annual variations both in the timing of [SOURCE: Page_2]

leaf flush peak (RMSE = 0.5 month) and in the timing of leaf fall peak (RMSE = 0.6 month). We [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   conclude that the inter-annual variation of canopy phenology is mainly a fine tuning to atmospheric [SOURCE: Page_2]

conditions. [SOURCE: Page_2]

## INTRODUCTION

36   In dry tropical regions such as Sahelian Africa, leaf activity of dominant woody species in the dry [SOURCE: Page_3]

season is a keystone resource for domestic herbivores, wildlife and ecosystems as a whole (Le [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   Houérou 1989). Prediction of the potential impact of climate change on the timing of leaf flushing and [SOURCE: Page_3]

leaf fall, i.e. leaf phenology, is a new and major challenge in Tropical Ecology. [SOURCE: Page_3]

40           Authors are in agreement on a primary genetic control and an influence of day length on leaf [SOURCE: Page_3]

phenology (Halevy and Orshan 1973; Reich and Borchert 1984; Borchert 1994 a&b; Reich 1995; [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   Williams et al. 1997, Morellato et al. 2000). The controversy is over precisely which environmental [SOURCE: Page_3]

control can best explain inter-annual and inter-site variations in the timings of leaf flush and leaf fall. [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   The hypothesis that has gained most support is that of control by soil water availability through tree [SOURCE: Page_3]

water status (Borchert 1994 a,b,c; Eamus and Prior 2001). However, several irrigation experiments in [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   the field have shown little impact of soil water availability on leaf phenology and a number of authors [SOURCE: Page_3]

have suggested that evaporative demand may also be an important driver (Wright and Cornejo 1990; [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   Duff et al. 1997; Myers et al. 1998). [SOURCE: Page_3]

In conducting a five and a half years study on 39 individuals of Acacia tortilis in the Sahelian zone, we [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   have chosen to investigate the issue by using an inter-annual analysis, which is rare in the bibliography [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Poupon 1979, Corlett and Lafrankie 1998). The Sahelian zone sensu stricto represents one of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   driest savannas with 200-400 mm y-1 of rainfall and with high inter-annual variability (C.V. = 37%), [SOURCE: Page_3]

potential evaporation is in excess of 2000 mm y-1 and groundwater is usually located deeper than 30 m [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   (Le Houerou 1989). Acacia tortilis (Forsk.) Hayne ssp. raddiana (Savi) Brenan, is a dominant tree of [SOURCE: Page_3]

the area which is classified as semi-evergreen or semi-deciduous (Breman and Kessler 1995, Le [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   Floc’h and Grouzis 2003), i.e. the loss of leaves which takes place in the late dry season is and its rate [SOURCE: Page_3]

varies with years, sites and individuals. The tree regularly exhibits leaf flush before the onset of rains. [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   It is known that its roots can reach the deep water table (Lhote 1961; Deans et al. 2004) in common [SOURCE: Page_3]

with numerous woody species of low-rainfall areas. However, high seasonal fluctuations of predawn [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   leaf water potential attest to the influence of soil water availability near the soil surface on tree water [SOURCE: Page_3]

status (Berger et al. 1996). In this study, we focused on the annual attainment and maintenance of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   full canopy stage in the tree population. The full canopy stage of a tree is assessed when the proportion [SOURCE: Page_4]

of the branches supporting green expanded leaves represents more than 50 % of those in the canopy. [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   Such simple phenological assessment at a monthly interval is a good indicator of the seasonal [SOURCE: Page_4]

transpiration and activity of the tree (Do and Rocheteau 2003). The timings when foliation rose above [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   or fell beneath the 50 % value defined the timings of leaf flushing and leaf fall peaks, respectively. [SOURCE: Page_4]

These timings allow the calculation of full canopy stage duration. Using concurrent measurements of [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   climatic conditions and soil water availability, the study had two objectives. The first objective was to [SOURCE: Page_4]

assess the inter-annual variation in the timings of leaf flushing and leaf fall and by consequence the [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   changes in the full canopy stage duration and period in the year. The second objective was to [SOURCE: Page_4]

determine which environmental variable can best explain inter-annual change in the timings of leaf [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   flushing and leaf fall peaks. In this water-controlled biome, we focused on a comparison between soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

water availability and the following atmospheric variables: temperature, relative humidity and vapour [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   pressure deficit which is a combination of the former variables and the driving force of water loss. [SOURCE: Page_4]

## METHODS

76   Location, climate and vegetation [SOURCE: Page_4]

The study was carried out in northern Senegal, near Souilene Village (16°20’ N, 15°25’ W) located 20 [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   km south of Dagana in the grazing land ecosystem of the sandy Ferlo. Annual rainfall averages 280 [SOURCE: Page_4]

mm, mean annual temperature is 28.7°C and average maximum and minimum temperatures are, [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   respectively: 41 and 22 °C for the hottest month (May) and 31 and 14 °C for the coldest month [SOURCE: Page_4]

(January). The sparse woody community is dominated by three species: Acacia tortilis and Balanites [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   aegyptiaca (L.) Del, for the tree layer (90 individuals ha-1) and Boscia senegalensis (Pers.) Lam for the [SOURCE: Page_4]

shrub layer (50 individuals ha-1). The soil is very deep and sandy (90 % sand). [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   Population and measurements [SOURCE: Page_4]

The studied population included 39 mature individuals located in a 1 ha area that has been protected [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   since 1989. Trunk diameter, which was measured 30 cm above the soil, varied from 5 to 45 cm, with [SOURCE: Page_4]

70 % of the population having diameters between 15 and 30 cm. Tree height ranged from 5 to 10 m. [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   Local microclimate was automatically monitored by a datalogger (21X, Campbell Scientific Ltd, [SOURCE: Page_4]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

Leics, UK), which recorded hourly values of air temperature, relative humidity, incoming short wave [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   radiation and rainfall. The water Vapour Pressure Deficit (VPD) was calculated from air temperature [SOURCE: Page_5]

(T) and relative humidity (RH): [SOURCE: Page_5]

92                   VPD = es(T) - e          and,     e = RH * es(T) [SOURCE: Page_5]

where es(T) is the saturation vapour pressure at air temperature T and e, the actual vapour pressure. [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   Soil moisture was measured to a depth of 4 meters by neutron probe (Solo 25, Nardeux S.A., Les Ulis, [SOURCE: Page_5]

France) using six access tubes. The soil wetting front rarely exceeded one meter depth. Soil water [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   availability (SWA) is expressed for the layer 0-100 cm deep, calculated between moisture contents at [SOURCE: Page_5]

field capacity and the driest moisture contents which were less than those prevailing at a water [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   potential of –1.5 MPa. The soil profile down to the water table was augered by the Geological [SOURCE: Page_5]

Department of Dakar University (Dr Abdoulaye Faye). Samples were taken every 0.5 m. Actual [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   moisture content and moisture equivalent to –1.5 MPa were determined in the laboratory. Over the [SOURCE: Page_5]

period June 1995- August 2000, phenological observations and soil measurements were carried out [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   every month in the dry season and twice a month in the rainy season, except in August 1995 and 1997. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Climatic data were not available between 13 and 18 April 1996 and between 29 September and 10 [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   October 1996. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Assessment of phenological stages [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   For each tree, a phenological stage for the foliage was allocated according to the 6 following [SOURCE: Page_5]

categories taking into account the proportion of branches bearing green expanded leaves in the canopy [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   (Grouzis and Sicot 1980): 0, leafless, 0%; 1, leaf buds opening, <10 %; 2, start of leaf expansion, 10- [SOURCE: Page_5]

50 % of the branches; 3, full canopy, > 50 %; 4, start of leaf senescence, changing colour, <50 %; 5, [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   full leaf senescence or fall, <10 %. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Data analysis [SOURCE: Page_5]

112           To express canopy fullness, the phenological stages were converted to their midrange [SOURCE: Page_5]

percentage value of branches bearing green expanded leaves in the canopy: 0, 0 %; 1, 5 %; 2, 30 %; 3, [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   75 %; 4, 30 %; 5, 5%. Thus the maximum value of canopy fullness is 75 by definition. [SOURCE: Page_5]

The timings of leaf flush or leaf fall peaks were calculated for each tree by linear interpolation such as [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   the time when the canopy fullness rose above or fell below the 50 % value in the long term (> 1 [SOURCE: Page_5]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

month). Time is expressed in numerical values of months (mo.), e.g. 6.5 for the middle of June. [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   Because phenological cycles are displayed across two years, we use for simplicity’s sake, the year of [SOURCE: Page_6]

the rainy season to name the phenological cycle, e.g. 1998 for 1998/99. Timings of leaf fall peak [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   located at the beginning of the second year are expressed as greater than month 12, e.g. 13.5 for the [SOURCE: Page_6]

middle of January. The duration of full canopy was calculated for each tree by difference between the [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   timings of leaf fall peak and leaf flush peak. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Year effects on the duration of full canopy and the timings of leaf flush peak and leaf fall peak [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   were tested using a repeated-measures analysis of variance (SAS proc. Mixed, SAS Institute Inc. [SOURCE: Page_6]

1999). Potential influence of environmental variables (edaphic or atmospheric) on changes in canopy [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   fullness, were investigated by regression analysis between values of canopy fullness and each variable, [SOURCE: Page_6]

considering all trees and pooling data for all of the years. For atmospheric variables (temperature, [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   relative humidity and vapour pressure deficit), the average value for the 15 days preceding [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   of rainfall were used. Since at the tree level, canopy fullness values were qualitative and ordinal [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   regressions with a cumulative logit link (SAS proc. Genmod, SAS Institute Inc. 1999). This model [SOURCE: Page_6]

relates the cumulative log of odds of the response variable to the explanatory variable in a linear form [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   (Agresti 1984). We assessed the validity of the regression by computing the significance of each [SOURCE: Page_6]

variable using Wald tests (Agresti 1984). Because we wanted to compare the predictive power of [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   environmental variables, the c statistic was computed (Hosmer and Lesmehow 2000). It measures the [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   1. A 0.5 value means that the model is no better than assigning observations randomly into outcome [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   predictive power. The analysis gives the values of the explanatory variable (environmental variable) [SOURCE: Page_6]

## 240 DISCUSSION

242   The full canopy stage of A. tortilis sp. raddiana lasts between 5 and 8 months, broadly including the [SOURCE: Page_10]

rainy season and the “cool” dry season. The seasonal period and duration of full canopy stage is in [SOURCE: Page_10]

244   agreement with general description of its leaf phenology in Sahelian Africa (Grouzis and Sicot 1980, [SOURCE: Page_10]

Diouf and Zaafouri 2003). As with the majority of species in the seasonally dry tropics, the defoliation [SOURCE: Page_10]

246   phase was centered around the hot dry season. [SOURCE: Page_10]

The first main result of this study was large inter-annual variation in the start and end of the [SOURCE: Page_10]

248   full canopy stage on the same site, more than 2 months in each case. One consequence was a 25 % [SOURCE: Page_10]

shortfall of the stage duration for two of the years (1996 and 1997). Such variation confirms that [SOURCE: Page_10]

250   genetics and day length cannot be the unique controlling factors of canopy phenology and that other [SOURCE: Page_10]

factors have complementary influence on the timings of leaf flush and leaf fall peaks. We should insist [SOURCE: Page_10]

252   that here we deal with the major timing of leaf flush and not initial leaf flush which is often discussed [SOURCE: Page_10]

in the literature (Borchert 1994c). Initial leaf flush was generally observed between April and May. [SOURCE: Page_10]

11 [SOURCE: Page_11]

254   Finally we discuss which factors influence the timing of the massive leaf flush which follows the [SOURCE: Page_11]

initial leaf flush. [SOURCE: Page_11]

256            The second main result was that atmospheric conditions and particularly VPDmax could [SOURCE: Page_11]

explain inter-annual change of canopy fullness better than upper or deep SWA. The influence of [SOURCE: Page_11]

258   annual rainfall on canopy phenology through upper SWA is a current hypothesis in the Sahelian zone : [SOURCE: Page_11]

higher rainfall would induce a larger residual upper soil water availability in the dry season which [SOURCE: Page_11]

260   could delay the timing of defoliation and would advance the time of leaf flushing peak. Our results do [SOURCE: Page_11]

not support this hypothesis despite a large inter-annual variation in rainfall (from 146 mm in 1997 to [SOURCE: Page_11]

262   367 mm in 1999). It was particularly notable that three month after rainy season, upper SWA reached [SOURCE: Page_11]

similar and very small values in all years. How was this possible? The phenological stage or canopy [SOURCE: Page_11]

264   fullness percentage does not describe leaf number or area which were two times higher for the wettest [SOURCE: Page_11]

years, 1998 and 1999 than for 1997 (Goudiaby 2003). Biomass of grass was also greater at the end of [SOURCE: Page_11]

266   the wettest rainy seasons. These two factors involve greater water uptake, which can explain how [SOURCE: Page_11]

SWA reached similar small values. [SOURCE: Page_11]

268            The third main result was that atmospheric conditions and particularly VPDmax predicted a [SOURCE: Page_11]

significant part of the inter-annual variations in both the timing of leaf flush peak and of leaf fall peak. [SOURCE: Page_11]

270   We conclude that these inter-annual variations of canopy phenology were mainly controlled by [SOURCE: Page_11]

atmospheric conditions. Comparable data on this point are scarce in the literature although the possible [SOURCE: Page_11]

272   influence of relative humidity on leaf flush was often hypothesized. One reason may be that [SOURCE: Page_11]

phenological studies on the same individuals, which last for more than one or two years, are rare in the [SOURCE: Page_11]

274   tropics. Another reason could be that until now, VPD was not always considered as a climatic variable [SOURCE: Page_11]

influencing savanna tree phenology (De Bie et al. 1998, Chidumayo 2001). Moreover, the conditions [SOURCE: Page_11]

276   of water constraint (rainfall amount, evaporative demand and depth to the water table) in the [SOURCE: Page_11]

ecosystem studied here were more extreme than in savanna situations detailed elsewhere in the [SOURCE: Page_11]

278   literature (Eamus and Prior 2001). However, an inter-annual analysis with “only” 5 to 6 years cannot [SOURCE: Page_11]

definitively demonstrate that VPD is a major proximal cue of canopy phenology in the dry tropics. [SOURCE: Page_11]

280   Nevertheless, the results give a new and clear insight on the influence of atmospheric conditions. [SOURCE: Page_11]

12 [SOURCE: Page_12]

What would be the adaptative significance of such response to atmospheric conditions? This [SOURCE: Page_12]

282   behaviour leads to maximization of the duration of canopy fullness below a VPD threshold between [SOURCE: Page_12]

4.5 and 4.9 kPa. Such behaviour maximizes the duration of photosynthetic activity under a threshold [SOURCE: Page_12]

284   of evaporative demand and does not reduce water loss. Field observation at the terminal branch scale [SOURCE: Page_12]

in 1997 and 1998 showed, that the annual phenological pattern corresponds to three main cohorts of [SOURCE: Page_12]

286   leaves (Goudiaby, 2003). The first cohort begins between April and May, the second cohort begins in [SOURCE: Page_12]

July and the last begins between December and January. The cohort of July expressed the longest leaf [SOURCE: Page_12]

288   life span around 6-7 months. The life span of the initial and final cohorts was very short and variable [SOURCE: Page_12]

depending upon the year. Leaf stomata are known to be very sensitive to VPD. We hypothesize that [SOURCE: Page_12]

290   the response of the two extreme cohorts of leaves to climatic conditions could explain inter-annual [SOURCE: Page_12]

variation of canopy phenology. [SOURCE: Page_12]

292   Do these results mean that soil water availability has no influence on leaf phenology of Acacia tortilis? [SOURCE: Page_12]

We do not support this. Firstly, leaf activity of trees during large periods of the dry season is only [SOURCE: Page_12]

294   possible because trees have access to extra-annual water in the deep soil. Secondly, the existence of [SOURCE: Page_12]

large spatial variation in canopy deciduousness at the landscape scale suggests a strong effect of SWA. [SOURCE: Page_12]

296   It was particularly notable at the border of the Senegal river, near Dagana city, where the water table is [SOURCE: Page_12]

close to the top soil that the tree population behaves like an evergreen species. Finally, we believe that [SOURCE: Page_12]

298   the average phenological pattern fits the prevailing local SWA’s, annual rainfall amount and depth to [SOURCE: Page_12]

the water table. While the inter-annual variation of canopy phenology is a fine tuning to atmospheric [SOURCE: Page_12]

300   conditions. [SOURCE: Page_12]

In conclusion, if such large inter-annual variation of canopy phenology is common to other dominant [SOURCE: Page_12]

302   species of the seasonally dry tropics, which is fairly possible, the consequences for the potential [SOURCE: Page_12]

impact of climate change are important and not only rainfall should be considered. [SOURCE: Page_12]
