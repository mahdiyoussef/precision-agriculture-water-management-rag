19    Abstract: [SOURCE: Page_2]

20       This study presents a decision support tool that evaluates the environmental efficiency of reusing [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   treated wastewater for agricultural irrigation, among other options. The developed tool is published [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   as open source at https://doi.org/10.18167/DVN$_1$/YLP1BA. The objective of this decision support tool [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   is to facilitate the interpretation of the Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) results by progressively reducing [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   the non-discriminatory impacts to solve the difficulty of making a decision with a large number of [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   criteria. This framework was applied to a representative case of reuse of reclaimed water for vine [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   irrigation at the Murviel-Les-Montpellier experimental site (Hérault, France). It was then generalized [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   through modeling assumptions to consider different reuse scenarios. [SOURCE: Page_2]

43       1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

44        Agricultural production is highly dependent on water and increasingly subject to water risks. [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   Indeed, irrigated agriculture is the largest user of water in the world. Globally, about 70% of freshwater [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   resources are consumed for crop irrigation [1]. By 2050, irrigated food production is expected to [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   increase by more than 50% due to population growth [1]. However, freshwater resources continue to [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   decline due to climate change, groundwater pollution, and aquifer salinization. [SOURCE: Page_4]

49        In the context of water scarcity, agriculture plays an important role in sustainable management [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   of freshwater resources and mobilization of alternative water such as treated wastewater and [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   desalinated water. Reclaimed water is a key alternative water source that has the advantage of being [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   available most of the time, especially during drought periods [2]. Moreover, the nutrient content [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   (nitrogen and phosphorous) in reclaimed water can reduce the use of chemical fertilizers and enhance [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   crop productivity [3]. Reclaimed water reuse in agriculture is a common practice in the Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   countries and other arid and semi-arid regions where freshwater resources are scarce [3, 4]. For [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   example, in Italy and Spain, between 8 and 12% of treated municipal wastewater is reused for [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 of 30 [SOURCE: Page_5]

57   irrigation [5]. In Tunisia, reuse of treated water for agricultural purposes is promoted by the [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   government and accounts for 20% of wastewater effluents [6]. [SOURCE: Page_5]

59        While reclaimed wastewater reuse has benefits for environmental sustainability, it also poses [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   risks [3]. In this context, Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) provides a quantitative multicriteria approach [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   to evaluate the environmental performance of processes throughout their life cycle. Numerous LCA [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   studies have been conducted to evaluate the environmental impacts caused by either wastewater [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   treatment systems [7,8] or by crop systems irrigated conventional water [9-12]. However, few evaluate [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   the environmental impacts of total crop systems irrigated with reclaimed water. Recently, Romeiko [9] [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   investigated the impact of irrigating corn, soybean, and wheat crops systems with reclaimed and ground [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   water sources in northern China. Wastewater was treated by biological processes (anaerobic-anoxic-oxic) [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   followed by chlorine disinfection prior to discharge. The author uses a combination of experimental [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   measurements and modeling datasets to reflect the specific regional conditions of the study, and he [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   considers 1 kg of grain as a functional unit (FU). Compared to groundwater resources, reuse showed to [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   be beneficial for the majority of the environmental impacts, such as global warming, acidification, ozone [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   depletion, and harmful for non-cancer impacts. Likewise, Moretti et al. [11], based on experimental data of [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   field work, compared the environmental performance of nectarine orchards irrigated in the Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   coastal region by two different water sources: (i) treated municipal wastewater by membrane filtration and, [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   (ii) surface water. The functional unit used is "1 kg of nectarines". The authors found that replacing [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   groundwater with reclaimed water limited eutrophication. However, reuse performed worse for climate [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   change, humans and freshwater toxicity mainly affected by the wastewater treatment phase. Miller-Robbie [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   et al. [12] evaluated greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and energy use of spinach crop systems in an [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   Indian urban farm irrigated with three water qualities: Groundwater, untreated wastewater, and treated [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   wastewater. It was showed that reuse in agriculture can reduce GHG emissions. Rodriguez-Garcia et [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   al.[13] evaluated assessed the eutrophication and global warming impacts of six different typologies of [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   WasteWater Treatment Plants (WWTP) coupled with different treated wastewater reuse scenarios [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   (agriculture, industral, groundwater recharge, etc.). The plants that reuse treated wastewater for irrigation [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   include tertiary treatments (UV disinfection or filtration process). The authors used two different functional [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   units, one based on volume (𝑚3 ) and the other on eutrophication reduction (kg 𝑃𝑂43− removed). The results [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   of the two FU were different. For the volumetric FU (𝑚3 ), the results showed that the potential [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   eutrophication of agricultural reuse is worse compared to the other scenarios, while it seems to be [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   beneficial for Global Warming Potential (GWP) compared to the acquifer recharge. The study by Meneses [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   et al. [14] compared the environmental impacts of producing, for irrigation purposes, 1𝑚3 of : non-potable [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 of 30 [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   water (for agricultural irrigation and urban uses), potable water and desalinated water. Actual operational [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   data from a WWTP in the Mediterranean region were used. The results showed that the agricultural reuse [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   is the most environmentally friendly option, as it reduces the use of fertilizers and thus the energy [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   consumption for their production. Similarly, Muñoz et al. [15] studied the life cycle impacts of tobacco in [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   Spain irrigated with three different water sources including groundwater, treated municipal wastewater, [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   and desalinated water. They found that irrigation with reclaimed water offred potential environmental [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   benefits, compared to the use of desalinated water, especially in terms of eutrophication, aquatic [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   ecotoxicity and energy use. In the same vein, Azeb et al. [16] analyzed the impact of irrigation with recycled [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   water and farmers’ practices in greenhouse cucumber cultivation. Compared to groundwater irrigation, the [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   results show that fertilizers have the highest impact on life cycle analysis in the case of reused water. The [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   authors explain this by the fact that farmers do not use the optimal fertilizer doses in terms of irrigation [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   water quality. [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   Moreover, among other options , the environmental efficiency of reclaimed water reuse depends on the [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   water-energy nexus, which links local water availability to water treatment impacts (as shown by Maeseele [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   et al. [17]). [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   These studies [9, 11-16] provided important outcomes that allow a better understanding of the potential [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   benefits and main sources of environmental damage from reuse of treated water in agriculture. However, [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   the results depend on the location and characteristics of the experimental field. There is still a lack of a [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   broad and systematic overview of the potential environmental impacts of reclaimed water reuse in [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   irrigation, including the following aspects: Wastewater treatment technologies, fertilizers reduction, water [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   availability, distance between the WWTP and the field, and energy mix. [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   The objective of this study is to enhance this knowledge and to highlight the environmental trade-offs [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   between conventional and recycled water as an irrigation source. LCA and decision rules were used in this [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   work to develop an intelligent tool to support decision making for the appropriate irrigation water source [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   depending on the context. The entire system of reuse in agriculture is considered: from wastewater [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   treatment to crop irrigation. [SOURCE: Page_6]

115        2. Materials and Methods [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   The methodology of LCA is normalized according to ISO 14040. It is an iterative four-stage process: [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   (1) goal and scope definition; (2) inventory analysis; (3) impact assessment; and (4) interpretation. [SOURCE: Page_6]

206            reclaimed wastewater meets microbiological standards. Available technologies for [SOURCE: Page_10]

207            wastewater reclamation include from simple sand filtration to advanced oxidation processes [SOURCE: Page_10]

208            and reverse osmosis. The choice of the most appropriate technology or combination of [SOURCE: Page_10]

337   LCA requires the processing, calculation and analysis of a wide range of information. The use of LCA [SOURCE: Page_17]

338   software facilitates these different phases, ensuring transparency and traceability. SimaPro is a [SOURCE: Page_17]

339   calculation software that links inventory data, informed by the LCA producer, to the environmental [SOURCE: Page_17]

340   damage caused by the inventoried substances. It was developed by the Dutch and is a world leader [SOURCE: Page_17]

341   in the implementation of LCA. SimaPro software version 9.1.1.1 integrates several evaluation [SOURCE: Page_17]

342   methods (Recipe, ILCD, Usetox...) and various life cycle inventory databases (ICV) (ecoinvent, ELCD, [SOURCE: Page_17]

345   analyzed several methodologies for life-cycle impact assessment to reach a consensus on the [SOURCE: Page_17]

349   to mid-point category and then from the mid-point category to the damage results. To limit the [SOURCE: Page_17]

512   5. Discussions [SOURCE: Page_26]

513   The results of the decision support tool has shown that the environmental benefits of agriculture [SOURCE: Page_26]

514   wastewater reuse over conventional water remain case-by-case dependent and no general [SOURCE: Page_26]

5 of 30 [SOURCE: Page_27]

515   conclusion can be drawn. The decision on reuse depends on the characteristics of the treatment [SOURCE: Page_27]

516   technology, especially tertiary treatment, and the specifics of the site. [SOURCE: Page_27]

517   Tertiary treatment of wastewater is not only a disinfection step, but can also prevent operational [SOURCE: Page_27]

518   problems due to clogging of drippers with biofilms. However, the complexity of the technology (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_27]

519   UF treatment) and the use of chemicals (e.g., chlorination) could increase the environmental impacts [SOURCE: Page_27]

520   of the reuse scenarios compared to groundwater and surface irrigation. The environmental benefits [SOURCE: Page_27]

521   of treated wastewater over conventional water sources are related to the reuse of treated wastewater, [SOURCE: Page_27]

522   which reduces the use of fertilizers. If the wastewater is not reused, it will be discharged into the [SOURCE: Page_27]

523   aquatic environment where it will affect freshwater and marine eutrophication. Thus, reuse can be a [SOURCE: Page_27]

524   double-edged sword. [SOURCE: Page_27]

525   Conventional water sources also have negative impacts on the environment through (i) the energy [SOURCE: Page_27]

526   consumption required to extract and transport water, (ii) emissions from the production and use of [SOURCE: Page_27]

527   fertilizers and (ii) the release of TWW into the environment. In addition, on the one hand, overuse of [SOURCE: Page_27]

528   conventional water sources is the cause of water quality (i.e., salinization) and quantity degradation. [SOURCE: Page_27]

529   On the other hand, surface and groundwater can also be polluted by human activities and wastewater [SOURCE: Page_27]

530   discharges, which can lead to contamination of water resources by pathogens. The problem is not in [SOURCE: Page_27]

531   contrast with wastewater treated by tertiary treatment, which is a disinfection step. The consideration [SOURCE: Page_27]

532   of pathogens in urban water is still relatively complex in LCA, as shown by [24]. [SOURCE: Page_27]

533   The aim of this work is to provide decision makers with a tool to decide on the efficiency of reuse [SOURCE: Page_27]

534   depending on the specifics of the case considered. However, the lack of data on country-specific [SOURCE: Page_27]

535   water scarcity could affect the accuracy of the results. Endpoint indicators could be used to consider [SOURCE: Page_27]

536   the impact of water scarcity, as shown in [17]. Thus, decision makers can improve their decisions by [SOURCE: Page_27]

537   considering water availability at the site in question. [SOURCE: Page_27]

538   In addition, the local electricity mix can also influence the environmental efficiency of TWW reuse. [SOURCE: Page_27]

539   Likewise, endpoint impact indicators could provide more useful information on the impact of the [SOURCE: Page_27]

540   energy-water mix than midpoint indicators [17]. The integration of endpoint impacts will be the subject [SOURCE: Page_27]

541   of our future work to improve the proposed decision tool. [SOURCE: Page_27]

544   irrigation water source depending on the context. It uses LCA impacts data generated by SimaPro [SOURCE: Page_27]

545   and predefined decision rules programmed in Excel. This tool is available as open source at [SOURCE: Page_28]
