Abstract:                                Filtration is essential in drip irrigation systems to remove physical contaminants carried by water that can clog the emitters, cause wear, or foul components of the system, affecting its performance and lifespan. Automatic flushing strainer-type filters initiate and terminate discrete flushing cycles that are activated automatically by means of differential pressure. The objective of this study is to investigate the hydraulic performance and flow behaviour of an automatic flushing strainer-type filter operated with clean water, using experimental and numerical approaches, to optimise the dimensions of its filter housing and to increase the range of operating flow rates. Pressure drop curves were determined for the filter housing and the filter system equipped with five models of filter elements (woven and non-woven elements). Excessive pressure drop in the filter housing and low filtration rates were identified as the main drawbacks of the original filter system. Numerical simulations enabled the identification of the most critical regions in terms of pressure losses near the transitions between the inlet and outlet segments of the pipe. Four designs of filter housing were simulated to evaluate the possibilities of optimising the filter housing dimensions using a constant filtering area. Larger inlet and outlet diameters combined to a filter housing shorter and wider were improvements in the filter housing dimensions that enabled to decrease the pressure drop in the filter and/or increase the range of operating flow rates. The results provided useful information for enhancing the hydraulic performance of the filtration system. [SOURCE: Page_2]

48   1   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_8]

49   Filtration involves the physical separation of one or more components from a [SOURCE: Page_8]

50   suspension in a fluid by passage through or across a barrier (filter medium) that is [SOURCE: Page_8]

51   permeable only to some of these components (Purchas & Sutherland, 2002). In [SOURCE: Page_8]

52   irrigation systems, particularly in microirrigation, filtration is important to prevent the [SOURCE: Page_8]

53   intake of particles and sediments that can accumulate along pipes and other [SOURCE: Page_8]

54   components, resulting in the clogging of emitters. Under typical irrigation conditions, [SOURCE: Page_8]

55   complete removal of all suspended particles cannot be achieved (ISO9912-1, 2004). [SOURCE: Page_8]

56   Practical and economic limitations only allow for the removal of larger particles, and [SOURCE: Page_8]

57   consequently, sediments can be found in irrigation lines (Oliveira et al., 2020; Puig- [SOURCE: Page_8]

58   Bargués & Lamm, 2013; Ravina et al., 1992) [SOURCE: Page_8]

59   Strainer-type, or screen filters, are devices that contain one or more filter elements. The [SOURCE: Page_8]

60   strainer-type filter elements consist of a perforated plate, screen, mesh, or a combination [SOURCE: Page_8]

61   of these, intended to retain suspended solids larger than the aperture size specified by [SOURCE: Page_8]

62   the manufacturer. The screen can be made of steel, nylon, polypropylene, or nonwoven [SOURCE: Page_8]

63   materials. The material and characteristics of the screen directly affect the fluid flow [SOURCE: Page_8]

64   and performance of the filter (Sparks & Chase, 2016; Sutherland, 2008). In irrigation [SOURCE: Page_8]

65   applications, the filter performance mainly refers to hydraulic performance (i.e., [SOURCE: Page_8]

66   pressure drop, filtration rate), removal efficiency of suspended particles, resistance to [SOURCE: Page_8]

67   corrosion, and backwash effectiveness when automatic mechanisms are part of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

68   filtration system. For woven wire cloths, the filtration performance is influenced by [SOURCE: Page_8]

69   filter cloth specifications such as wire diameter, shape and material, type of weave (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_8]

70   plain Dutch weave, twill Dutch weave, reverse Dutch weave), aperture size, and mesh [SOURCE: Page_8]

71   count. The performance of nonwoven fabrics is influenced by the material, porosity, [SOURCE: Page_8]

72   permeability, thickness, pore size, and mass per unit area of the fabric (Ribeiro et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

73   2004, 2008). For media filters, the requirements for evaluating several characteristics [SOURCE: Page_9]

74   related to filter performance are standardized in ASAE S539 (ASABE, 2017). [SOURCE: Page_9]

75   The filtration for irrigation systems may consist of primary and secondary filters. The [SOURCE: Page_9]

76   primary filter may serve several plots and consist of a media filter, screen or disc filter, [SOURCE: Page_9]

77   most often incorporating a self-cleaning mechanism. Screen or disc filters manually [SOURCE: Page_9]

78   cleaned, can be installed as secondary downstream safety filters at the inlet of plots [SOURCE: Page_9]

79   (Pizarro Cabello, 1996; Ravina et al., 1997). Screen filters are suitable for removing [SOURCE: Page_9]

80   suspended solid particles, but problems may arise when algal debris are part of the [SOURCE: Page_9]

81   contaminants. Algal material tends to intertwine between the screen mesh and removal [SOURCE: Page_9]

82   is difficult when the packing becomes dense (Nakayama et al., 2007). If the irrigation [SOURCE: Page_9]

83   water contains a high concentration of suspended solid particles, sand separators or [SOURCE: Page_9]

84   settling basins should be installed upstream of the filtration system (Keller & Bliesner, [SOURCE: Page_9]

85   2000). [SOURCE: Page_9]

86   Some screen filters have flushing cycles that are automatically activated, and they are [SOURCE: Page_9]

87   called automatic flushing strainer-type filters (ISO9912-3, 2013). Automatic flushing [SOURCE: Page_9]

88   filters initiate and terminate discrete flushing cycles that are activated automatically by [SOURCE: Page_9]

89   means of differential pressure or at regular intervals of time or filtered volume [SOURCE: Page_9]

90   (Nakayama et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_9]

91   Several studies have emerged in recent years with the intention of improving designs for [SOURCE: Page_9]

92   energy consumption, and understanding the flow behaviour and performance of filters, [SOURCE: Page_9]

93   mainly media filters (Arbat et al., 2011; Bové et al., 2017; Bové, Arbat, Pujol, et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

94   2015; Mesquita et al., 2017, 2019; Pujol et al., 2020; Solé-Torres et al., 2019). Many [SOURCE: Page_9]

95   studies have used computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to investigate, design, and [SOURCE: Page_9]

96   improve irrigation equipment (Camargo et al., 2020). Although experimental data will [SOURCE: Page_9]

97   always be necessary for the validation of numerical simulations and to confirm the [SOURCE: Page_9]

98   performance of irrigation equipment, numerical simulations can be useful to reduce [SOURCE: Page_10]

99   resources related to development costs. [SOURCE: Page_10]

100   Mathematical models based on dimensional analysis have been developed to predict [SOURCE: Page_10]

101   head losses of filters for irrigation systems operated with tap water (Yurdem et al., [SOURCE: Page_10]

102   2008, 2010), water with suspended solids, and effluents (Duran-Ros et al., 2010; Puig- [SOURCE: Page_10]

103   Bargués et al., 2005; Zong et al., 2015). Some of these models can describe filter [SOURCE: Page_10]

104   clogging as a function of the characteristics of the filter, water, and flow. CFD was used [SOURCE: Page_10]

105   to estimate fluid flow characteristics and pressure losses in different parts of sand filters, [SOURCE: Page_10]

106   as well as to propose improvements in the design of filter components aiming for better [SOURCE: Page_10]

107   hydraulic performance (Arbat et al., 2011; Bové, Arbat, Pujol, et al., 2015; Mesquita et [SOURCE: Page_10]

108   al., 2017, 2019). CFD studies on strainer-type filters are scarce in the literature on [SOURCE: Page_10]

109   irrigation engineering. [SOURCE: Page_10]

110   The objective of this study was to investigate the hydraulic performance and flow [SOURCE: Page_10]

111   behaviour of an automatic flushing strainer-type filter operated with clean water, using [SOURCE: Page_10]

112   experimental and numerical approaches, to optimise the dimensions of its filter housing [SOURCE: Page_10]

113   and to increase the range of operating flow rates. [SOURCE: Page_10]

115   2.1    Filter [SOURCE: Page_10]

116   The automatic flushing strainer-type filter model FA-20 manufactured by Iavant [SOURCE: Page_10]

117   Filtering Systems, Brazil, was investigated (Fig. 1). The filter housing is made of steel, [SOURCE: Page_10]

118   and an electrostatic powder coating is applied over its inner and outer surfaces. The inlet [SOURCE: Page_10]

119   and outlet internal diameters are 80 mm, and the total surface area of the filtration [SOURCE: Page_10]

120   element is 272,376 mm^2. [SOURCE: Page_10]

121   This filtration system operates differently from other automatic flushing strainer-type [SOURCE: Page_10]

122   filters. In this filter, the operating water flows from the outer to the inner surface of the [SOURCE: Page_10]

123      filter element; thus, residues gradually accumulate over the external surface of the [SOURCE: Page_11]

124      element. The automatic flushing mechanism is activated by a differential pressure [SOURCE: Page_11]

125      threshold with a default value of 50 kPa. When the flushing routine is activated, the [SOURCE: Page_11]

126      flushing valve is opened, and the electric motor coupled to a gearbox rotates the filter [SOURCE: Page_11]

127      element. When the filter element rotates, its outer surface rubs against the brushes, [SOURCE: Page_11]

128      which facilitates the detachment of solid material accumulated during the filtration [SOURCE: Page_11]

129      routine. Parallel to the brushes, there is a narrow cavity that is connected to a flushing [SOURCE: Page_11]

130      pipe. The differential pressure between the inside of the filter and the atmosphere is [SOURCE: Page_11]

131      converted into a high velocity through the flushing pipe, which removes the material [SOURCE: Page_11]

132      accumulated over the external surface of the filter element. [SOURCE: Page_11]

## 464   4   CONCLUSIONS

465   The hydraulic performance and flow behavior of an automatic flushing strainer-type [SOURCE: Page_25]

466   filter operated with clean water were investigated using experimental and numerical [SOURCE: Page_25]

467   approaches. For the original filtration system equipped with woven elements, the [SOURCE: Page_25]

468   experimental results indicated that the pressure drop due to the filter housing was [SOURCE: Page_25]

469   dominant and represented more than 86% of the total pressure drop in the filtering [SOURCE: Page_25]

470   system. Similarly, for non-woven elements, more than 53% of the total pressure drop [SOURCE: Page_25]

471   was caused by the filter housing. Excessive pressure drop in the filter housing and low [SOURCE: Page_25]

472   filtration rates were identified as the main opportunities for improving the original [SOURCE: Page_25]

473   filtration system. [SOURCE: Page_25]

474   CFD simulations were performed to predict values of pressure drop of the original filter [SOURCE: Page_25]

475   housing operated with clean water. Numerical simulations enabled the identification of [SOURCE: Page_25]

476   the most critical regions in terms of pressure losses near the transitions between the inlet [SOURCE: Page_25]

477   and outlet segments of the pipe. [SOURCE: Page_25]

478   Keeping the filtering area constant, four designs of filter housing were simulated to [SOURCE: Page_25]

479   evaluate possibilities for optimizing the filter housing dimensions. Larger inlet and [SOURCE: Page_25]

480   outlet diameters combined to a filter housing shorter and wider were improvements in [SOURCE: Page_26]

481   the filter housing dimensions that enabled to decrease the pressure drop in the filter [SOURCE: Page_26]

482   and/or increase the range of operating flow rates. When comparing the proposed models [SOURCE: Page_26]

483   of filter housing (models B, C and D) against the original design (model A) at the flow [SOURCE: Page_26]

484   rate of 100 m^3 h-1 (i.e., filtration rate of 375 m^3 m^2 h-1), the decrease in pressure drop [SOURCE: Page_26]

485   varied from 27.6% to 67.6% according to the combination of filter element and model [SOURCE: Page_26]

486   of filter housing. [SOURCE: Page_26]

487   The CFD simulations indicates that the filter housing design of an existing automatic [SOURCE: Page_26]

488   strainer-type filter can be improved to reduce pressure loss and/or increase the range of [SOURCE: Page_26]

489   operating flow rates. The obtained results provide useful information for planning [SOURCE: Page_26]

490   enhancements of the filtration system and for building prototypes for further [SOURCE: Page_26]

491   experimental evaluation. The best size of the filter housing depends on the target [SOURCE: Page_26]

492   nominal flow rate, which is defined by the manufacturer according to their market [SOURCE: Page_26]

493   strategies. For development purposes, numerical simulations may reduce the number of [SOURCE: Page_26]

494   prototypes manufactured for preliminary evaluations, thereby decreasing investment, [SOURCE: Page_26]

495   time, and labour requirements. [SOURCE: Page_26]
