The assessment of water travel time through the vadose zone is known to be critical for a proper [SOURCE: Page_4]

estimation of the hydrologic response time of water bodies to changes in land use management [SOURCE: Page_4]

and global changes. In this study, the hydraulic properties of fifteen samples displaying [SOURCE: Page_4]

contrasted lithologies (soil, powdery limestone, calcareous sand, limestone rock) and extracted [SOURCE: Page_4]

from three cored boreholes drilled throughout the vadose zone of a vulnerable limestone aquifer [SOURCE: Page_4]

were first determined in the laboratory. Three 23 m-deep vadose zone profiles were then [SOURCE: Page_4]

reconstituted with the HYDRUS-1D software for the numerical simulation of water flow and [SOURCE: Page_4]

the estimation of the water travel time using unimodal and bimodal approaches. The measured [SOURCE: Page_4]

hydraulic properties, meteorological and water table level data were used as input for a virtual [SOURCE: Page_4]

bromide tracing experiment undertaken over a period of 55 years (1966-2020). The results [SOURCE: Page_4]

showed that the experimental hydraulic properties of the samples were more accurately [SOURCE: Page_4]

described with a dual porosity approach, since the latter allowed a precise representation of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

bimodal characteristic of most of the samples. The water flow and travel time simulated using [SOURCE: Page_4]

unimodal or bimodal models for describing the vadose zone hydraulic properties were largely [SOURCE: Page_4]

different. The impact of the vadose zone lateral heterogeneities on the simulated water flow and [SOURCE: Page_4]

### 1. Introduction

An increasing number of Earth’s Critical Zone (CZ) networks and observatories have emerged [SOURCE: Page_5]

in recent years (Gaillardet et al., 2018; Guo and Lin, 2016; Zacharias et al., 2011), stimulated [SOURCE: Page_5]

by societal challenges like the prediction of natural hazards, carbon storage or water resources [SOURCE: Page_5]

management. These observatories are dedicated to deciphering the complex hydrological and [SOURCE: Page_5]

biogeochemical processes which occur at various temporal and spatial scales in the CZ (Bogena [SOURCE: Page_5]

et al., 2018; Brantley et al., 2018; Galle et al., 2018; Jensen and Refsgaard, 2018; Li et al., 2018; [SOURCE: Page_5]

Molénat et al., 2018; O’Geen et al., 2018; Pisinaras et al., 2018; Seyfried et al., 2018). The [SOURCE: Page_5]

Vadose Zone (VZ), which extends from the soil surface down to the aquifer, is considered as a [SOURCE: Page_5]

key component of the CZ and is of major importance in the hydrological and biogeochemical [SOURCE: Page_5]

cycles. Consequently, an increasing body of work aims at improving the knowledge of the VZ [SOURCE: Page_5]

functioning, which includes numerous complex and coupled physical, geochemical, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

microbial processes (Aldana et al., 2021; Arora et al., 2019; Vereecken et al., 2016). This [SOURCE: Page_5]

constitutes a fundamental milestone for a proper assessment of groundwater recharge and in [SOURCE: Page_5]

managing contamination issues for the preservation of aquifers. This is especially true since the [SOURCE: Page_5]

increasing use of groundwater resources worldwide and the degradation of the quality of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

water resources caused by intensive agriculture have led to the enforcement of several stringent [SOURCE: Page_5]

regulatory measures in several countries geared towards achieving a good chemical status for [SOURCE: Page_5]

all water bodies in years ahead (Vero et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_5]

In this context, the estimation of the water travel time through the VZ is crucial as it constitutes [SOURCE: Page_5]

an important component of the total time lag defining the inherent hydrologic response delay [SOURCE: Page_5]

3 [SOURCE: Page_5]

of water bodies to a change in land management practices. This response time is often [SOURCE: Page_6]

conceptualized as consisting in both a vertical (within the VZ) and a lateral (within the saturated [SOURCE: Page_6]

zone) component (Sousa et al., 2013). The knowledge of the water travel time through the VZ [SOURCE: Page_6]

is considered as a prerequisite for quantifying the recharge rate and the time of migration of [SOURCE: Page_6]

contaminants from the soil surface down to the aquifer (Mattern and Vanclooster, 2010). [SOURCE: Page_6]

However, the water travel time in the VZ is known to be highly variable in space and time. Its [SOURCE: Page_6]

quantification is recognized as highly complex and influenced by several factors including the [SOURCE: Page_6]

size and geometry of the VZ, the properties of the VZ materials, the physical, chemical and [SOURCE: Page_6]

biological processes governing water flow and solute transport within the VZ and the pattern [SOURCE: Page_6]

of the meteorological inputs (Jeong et al., 2017; Leij and van Genuchten, 2002; Sousa et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

2013; Sprenger et al., 2016; Vero et al., 2014). Numerous experimental methods are currently [SOURCE: Page_6]

used to estimate the water travel time through the VZ, such as lysimeters (mainly focused on [SOURCE: Page_6]

the soil) or field experiments using radioactive or stable isotopes, chloride, nitrate, bromide or [SOURCE: Page_6]

fluorobenzoate as tracers (Asadollahi et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2019; Ju et al., 2020; Sprenger [SOURCE: Page_6]

et al., 2016; Turkeltaub et al., 2018). However, these experimental approaches are known to be [SOURCE: Page_6]

fairly expensive, labor intensive, time consuming and may also have limited predictive [SOURCE: Page_6]

capacity. As a consequence, numerical models are being increasingly used for the study of water [SOURCE: Page_6]

flow, solute transport and travel time assessments within the VZ (Bouraoui and Grizzetti, 2014; [SOURCE: Page_6]

Konikow, 2011; Schoups et al., 2008; Szymkiewicz et al., 2019; Vero et al., 2014). These [SOURCE: Page_6]

models need an estimation of the VZ hydrodynamic parameters and may partly rely on [SOURCE: Page_6]

pedotransfer functions (Patil and Singh, 2016; Schaap et al., 2001; Szymkiewicz et al., 2018; [SOURCE: Page_6]

Vereecken et al., 2010; Vero et al., 2017; Zhang and Schaap, 2019). Some authors used inverse [SOURCE: Page_6]

modeling approaches based on transient measurements of water content, matric head and tracer [SOURCE: Page_6]

content in the VZ (Haws et al., 2005; Jacques et al., 2002; Sprenger et al., 2016, 2015; Varvaris [SOURCE: Page_6]

et al., 2021a, 2021b). However, hydraulic data acquisition is mostly focused on the soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

4 [SOURCE: Page_6]

compartment and the numerical simulations run for the estimation of the travel time are often [SOURCE: Page_7]

limited to the use of a unimodal approach, relying notably on the Mualem-van Genuchten model [SOURCE: Page_7]

to describe the VZ hydraulic properties (van Genuchten, 1980). [SOURCE: Page_7]

The main objective of this work was to provide a new methodological approach for the study [SOURCE: Page_7]

of the water flow and the estimation of the water travel time at the scale of a highly [SOURCE: Page_7]

heterogeneous VZ. To this end, the hydraulic properties (water retention and hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_7]

conductivity curves) of fifteen samples taken from three cored boreholes and representative of [SOURCE: Page_7]

the lithologies (soil, powdery limestone, calcareous sand, limestone rock) encountered within [SOURCE: Page_7]

the VZ of a vulnerable limestone aquifer (Beauce, France) were first determined in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

laboratory. These experimental data were then used for the parameterization of analytical [SOURCE: Page_7]

models describing the measured hydraulic properties using single (unimodal) and dual porosity [SOURCE: Page_7]

(bimodal) approaches. The travel time within the VZ has been estimated by means of a virtual [SOURCE: Page_7]

bromide tracing experiment performed over a period of 55 years using the HYDRUS-1D [SOURCE: Page_7]

software (Šimůnek et al., 2016). The appearance of the initial breakthrough, the peak [SOURCE: Page_7]

concentration and the total exit of the tracer at the maximum water table level of the aquifer [SOURCE: Page_7]

were considered as indicators of the water travel time. The impact of the choice of the analytical [SOURCE: Page_7]

model used to describe the experimental hydraulic properties, the vertical and lateral [SOURCE: Page_7]

heterogeneity of the VZ and the meteorological conditions on the simulation of the water flow [SOURCE: Page_7]

and travel time through the VZ has been explored. [SOURCE: Page_7]

### 2.1. The Beauce limestone aquifer

The multi-layered Beauce limestone aquifer is located in the center of France and extends over [SOURCE: Page_7]

9700 km^2 (de Frutos Cachorro et al., 2017). It is mainly composed of limestones from upper [SOURCE: Page_7]

Oligocene to lower Miocene but also consists in clay, marl, powdery limestone, calcareous sand [SOURCE: Page_7]

or chalk facies depending on the location. This mostly unconfined aquifer is spread between [SOURCE: Page_7]

5 [SOURCE: Page_7]

the Seine (northeast) and the Loire (southwest) rivers and constitutes one of the largest [SOURCE: Page_8]

groundwater reservoirs in France with an average water stock of 20 billion m^3 and high inter- [SOURCE: Page_8]

annual variations (Le Coz, 2000). The Beauce represents the main cereal-producing region in [SOURCE: Page_8]

Europe (Graveline, 2020). Its land use consists essentially in agriculture (74%), 50% of which [SOURCE: Page_8]

is irrigated. Indeed, the region is one of the driest in France with an annual average precipitation [SOURCE: Page_8]

of 600 mm (Lejars et al., 2012). This results in an intense irrigated farming of between 120 000 [SOURCE: Page_8]

and 240 000 ha that withdraws from the groundwater resource between 150 and 450 million m^3 [SOURCE: Page_8]

of water per year (Graveline, 2020). The sensitivity of the water resource to natural recharge [SOURCE: Page_8]

and the observation of an overall lowering of the water table level since the early 1990s have [SOURCE: Page_8]

led to the implementation of several local water policy measures to control water withdrawals [SOURCE: Page_8]

in connection with the French Water Law (Légifrance, 1992), which is now the translation of [SOURCE: Page_8]

the European Water Framework Directive (European Commission, 2000). Over the past [SOURCE: Page_8]

decades, the Beauce intensive agriculture has also impacted the groundwater quality with [SOURCE: Page_8]

nitrates and pesticides (notably atrazine and bentazon) whose concentrations still exceed [SOURCE: Page_8]

regulatory limits (DDT, 2016) despite government policies and regulatory measures that have [SOURCE: Page_8]

been put into effect since the early 1990s (European Commission, 2009, 1991). [SOURCE: Page_8]

### 2.2. Location, sampling and description of the VZ materials

The experimental site is located at Villamblain, 30 km northwest of Orléans (France) (DMS [SOURCE: Page_8]

coordinates: X = 48°1'5.131"; Y=1°34'55.333"). Three cored boreholes (B$_1$, B$_2$ and B$_3$) [SOURCE: Page_8]

separated by a maximum distance of 10.8 m from each other (Figure 1) have been drilled in [SOURCE: Page_8]

March 2017 from 0 to 20 m deep. The soil formation (0.0-1.5 m deep) was drilled by hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_8]

percussion while the other VZ materials (1.5-20.0 m deep) were taken by rotary drilling with a [SOURCE: Page_8]

three-annular compartments corer to avoid sample contamination by the drilling fluid (water). [SOURCE: Page_8]

As stated by Aldana et al. (2021), compaction caused by hydraulic percussion led to unreliable [SOURCE: Page_8]

hydraulic properties data for the soil samples. Consequently, additional soil samples were [SOURCE: Page_8]

6 [SOURCE: Page_8]

collected manually from the ground surface after digging a pit at a few meters from the three [SOURCE: Page_9]

boreholes (Figure 1) and down to a depth of 1 m. [SOURCE: Page_9]

The VZ lithologies were described based on visual examination of the undisturbed core [SOURCE: Page_9]

samples. It revealed a high vertical (within a single borehole) and lateral (between the three [SOURCE: Page_9]

boreholes) lithological heterogeneity (Aldana et al., 2021; Isch et al., 2020). A soil with a [SOURCE: Page_9]

thickness between 0.9 and 1.8 m was observed at the top of the VZ. This soil is typical of the [SOURCE: Page_9]

Beauce region and is referred to as a loamy clay Calcisol (Duval and Isambert, 1992; Michot et [SOURCE: Page_9]

al., 2003) according to the French soil reference system (Baize and Girard, 2009), or a [SOURCE: Page_9]

Hypereutric Cambisol with a silt loam texture according to the World Reference Base for Soils [SOURCE: Page_9]

(IUSS Working Group WRB, 2015). Three main soil layers were observed during the visual [SOURCE: Page_9]

description of the core samples and the soil pit (0.0-0.3 m; 0.3-0.6 m; > 0.6 m) (Aldana et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

2021; Isch et al., 2020). The thickness of the soil layers was in good agreement with that [SOURCE: Page_9]

described in other studies carried out near the experimental site (Michot et al., 2003; Ould [SOURCE: Page_9]

Mohamed et al., 1997). The soil is developed on a highly heterogeneous Miocene lacustrine [SOURCE: Page_9]

fragmented powdery limestone facies (referred to as “powdery limestone” in this work) that has [SOURCE: Page_9]

been cryoturbated in its upper part during the Quaternary (Michot et al., 2003; Ould Mohamed [SOURCE: Page_9]

and Bruand, 1994) and which also contains calcareous sand interbeds (Aldana et al., 2021). The [SOURCE: Page_9]

thickness of this layer is from 5.2 to 6.8 m. Finally, the last main stratigraphic facies is a 12.2 [SOURCE: Page_9]

to 13.4 m thick massive, fractured or weathered hard rock (Pithiviers limestone) (Aldana et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

2021;                  Schnebelen                   et                 al.,                 1999). [SOURCE: Page_9]

### 3.1.1. Experimental hydraulic properties

The hydraulic properties of a majority of the samples concerned by this work have already been [SOURCE: Page_21]

discussed below. [SOURCE: Page_21]

The experimental hydraulic properties (water retention and hydraulic conductivity) of the SA [SOURCE: Page_21]

due to the slight increase in clay content observed with depth (Aldana et al., 2021; Ould [SOURCE: Page_21]

the three fragmented powdery limestone samples (PB, PC and PD) were close despite a  [SOURCE: Page_21]

observed from pF 0.0 to 3.0 for sample PC (Figure 3b). The cryoturbated unit (PA), which was [SOURCE: Page_21]

between pF 2.0 and 3.0 than the three other powdery limestone samples (Figure 3b). Its  [SOURCE: Page_21]

values were also in good agreement with those reported by Michot et al. (2003) over the range [SOURCE: Page_21]

of pF 1.0-4.2. Water retention of calcareous sand interbeds (IA and IB samples) was the lowest [SOURCE: Page_21]

among the soft VZ materials for pF > 1.0 (except IA vs PC) (Figure 3c). IB sample displayed a [SOURCE: Page_21]

lower water retention capacity than IA (except at saturation) because of its higher sand content [SOURCE: Page_22]

As already pointed out by Aldana et al. (2021), the hydraulic properties of the hard limestone [SOURCE: Page_22]

rock samples were highly heterogeneous (Figure 3d). Their 𝜃𝑠 values were the lowest of all the [SOURCE: Page_22]

### 3.2. Simulation of water flow and estimation of travel time within the B$_2$ profile

Since a majority of the samples were taken from the B$_2$ profile (Table 2), the influence of the [SOURCE: Page_31]

choice of the analytical model (MVG or DBM) on the simulation of water flow and the [SOURCE: Page_31]

estimation of TT within the VZ was studied on this profile. [SOURCE: Page_31]

The B$_2$ water content profiles simulated at the date of the drilling (14 March 2017) using the [SOURCE: Page_31]

MVG and DBM models were in relatively good agreement with the experimental measurements [SOURCE: Page_31]

made on the undisturbed core samples on that same date (R² ≥ 0.892, Figure 4b). The [SOURCE: Page_31]

differences in  between the two models were rather small and the main disparities [SOURCE: Page_31]

(< 0.05 cm^3/cm^3) were observed within the soil (0-1 m deep), the powdery limestone facies [SOURCE: Page_31]

(from 2.0 to 3.5 m deep and from 5.5 to 6.6 m deep) and the saturated zone (below 18 m deep). [SOURCE: Page_31]

Overestimations of experimental  by the models were noted in the powdery limestone material [SOURCE: Page_31]

PB (2 m deep) and in limestone rock materials (RC, RD and RE) from 10 to 15 m deep. [SOURCE: Page_31]

Underestimations of experimental  by the models were observed in massive (RB, 8.5 m deep) [SOURCE: Page_31]

and downstream weathered limestone rock materials (RF, 16 m deep). [SOURCE: Page_31]

Since the Beauce limestone aquifer is known for its highly permeable soils and its vertical soil [SOURCE: Page_31]

water flow regime (Bruand et al., 1997; Flipo et al., 2012), no run off was observed during the [SOURCE: Page_31]

whole simulation period (55 years and a total rainfall of 35180 mm). The matric head values [SOURCE: Page_31]

simulated by the DBM model in each soil layer displayed more fluctuations than those of the [SOURCE: Page_31]

MVG model (Figure 5 and Figure B.3). The soil water status simulated by the DBM model [SOURCE: Page_31]

was drier (lower mean values of h) and characterized by more pronounced drying and wetting [SOURCE: Page_31]

cycles than for the MVG model (Figure B.3 and Table A.3). The mean downward water flux [SOURCE: Page_31]

simulated within the soil was higher for the DBM model than for the MVG model (Table A.3). [SOURCE: Page_31]

This could be attributed to the higher cumulative ETR (actual evapotranspiration) simulated [SOURCE: Page_31]

with the MVG model (30210 mm) compared to the DBM model (28009 mm), which resulted [SOURCE: Page_31]

in a lower quantity of water infiltrating in the VZ profile for the MVG model over the simulation [SOURCE: Page_31]

29 [SOURCE: Page_31]

period. This was also induced by the higher soil K values observed near saturation (pF < 1.5) [SOURCE: Page_32]

for the DBM model compared to the MVG model (except SC) (Figure 3a, Figure B.1a and [SOURCE: Page_32]

Figure B.2a). As a result, the bromide transport simulated within the soil by the DBM model [SOURCE: Page_32]

The mean water content and matric head values simulated by the models within the deep VZ [SOURCE: Page_32]

wetter VZ profile (higher mean values of h) compared to the MVG model. Although the [SOURCE: Page_32]

saturated hydraulic conductivity of the powdery limestone (PB, PC and PD for MVG and PB and [SOURCE: Page_32]

MVG model, these were actually less frequent than for the DBM model due to the higher K [SOURCE: Page_32]

near saturation (pF < 1) of the powdery limestone and rock materials and the more dynamic [SOURCE: Page_32]

water regime of the latter. In accordance with the lower mean downward water flux simulated [SOURCE: Page_32]

along the VZ profile (Table A.3), the bromide transport simulated with the MVG model from [SOURCE: Page_32]

the DBM model (Figure 6b-d). [SOURCE: Page_32]

Finally, the mean TTI, TTP and TTE (calculated from all the TT values simulated for each input [SOURCE: Page_32]

year over the same period of time) were always higher for the MVG model than for the DBM [SOURCE: Page_32]

for TTP and 14 years for TTE (Table 3). The year of input corresponding to the minimum and [SOURCE: Page_32]

30 [SOURCE: Page_32]

maximum TTI, TTP and TTE were relatively close between the two models (Figure B.5 and [SOURCE: Page_33]

Table 3). The most significant variations in TT simulated between the two models were noted [SOURCE: Page_33]

### 3.3.2. Impact of the meteorological conditions

As previously underlined by some authors (Sprenger et al., 2016; Vero et al., 2014), [SOURCE: Page_36]

Maximum differences in the TT indicators according to input years for each VZ profile varied [SOURCE: Page_36]

from 7.0 (B$_1$) to 7.2 (B$_3$) years for TTI, from 8.2 (B$_1$) to 9.5 (B$_3$) years for TTP and from 7.4 [SOURCE: Page_36]

In this study, the numerical simulation of water flow and the estimation of water travel time [SOURCE: Page_43]

through the highly heterogeneous VZ of a limestone aquifer was undertaken over a period of [SOURCE: Page_43]

55 years. The parameterization of the model (HYDRUS-1D) was based on hydraulic properties [SOURCE: Page_43]

laboratory measurements performed on fifteen samples representative of the lithologies [SOURCE: Page_43]

heterogeneous VZ profiles were reconstituted in HYDRUS-1D based on three boreholes drilled [SOURCE: Page_43]

in March 2017. Daily rainfall, ETP and water table level data were used as inputs for the [SOURCE: Page_43]

implementation of a virtual bromide tracing experiment made over the 55 years. The arrival [SOURCE: Page_43]

time of the initial breakthrough (TTI), the peak (TTP) and the total exit of the bromide [SOURCE: Page_43]

concentration (TTE) at the maximum water table level were considered as suitable indicators of [SOURCE: Page_44]

The results obtained in this work highlighted that the experimental hydraulic properties of the [SOURCE: Page_44]

samples were more accurately described by the DBM model. The latter allowed a precise [SOURCE: Page_44]

description of the bimodal characteristic of the VZ materials, i.e., macro- and micro-porosity [SOURCE: Page_44]

porosity for the limestone rock. The choice of the analytical model used to describe the VZ [SOURCE: Page_44]

hydraulic properties (MVG or DBM) showed a significant influence on the simulation of the [SOURCE: Page_44]

TTE of 31.5 years (min 26.6 – max. 39.0) were found for the VZ of the Beauce limestone aquifer [SOURCE: Page_44]
