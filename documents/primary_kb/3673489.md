60       I. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

61 [SOURCE: Page_4]

62         Karst aquifers are major water resources, supplying approximately 25% of the world’s [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   population (Ford and Williams, 2007). Karst landscapes, associated with anthropogenic [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   activities, are foremost concerns in determining karst water quality hazards specifically in [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   terms of contamination. Since the beginning of the year 2000, researches on the issue of karst [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   aquifers being vulnerable to water quality deterioration has increased. These are presented in [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   the surface mapping studies (Andreo et al., 2009; Kavouri et al., 2011; Kazakis et al., 2015; [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   Marin et al., 2015; Kazakis et al., 2018; Ollivier et al., 2019) and on the results of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   analyses of the water temporal evolution at the outlets of karst systems (Pronk et al., 2006; [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   Goldscheider et al., 2010; Jung et al., 2014; Schiperski et al., 2015; Doummar and Aoun, [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   2018; Yang et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_4]

72         Exokarstic landforms such as sinkholes or sinking stream allow punctual infiltration of [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   pollutants (Fournier et al., 2007; Kavouri et al., 2011; Huneau et al., 2013; Sivelle and Labat, [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   2019). Fast infiltration and rapid transport of water can occur through fissure and conduits. [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   This is characteristic of karst aquifers, and contribute to having incidences of contamination [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   (Vesper and White, 2004; Geyer et al., 2007; Hillebrand et al., 2012; He et al., 2010; Bauer [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_4]

78         Identifying water resource hazards implies understanding the movement of water from [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   different origins which can be done through the analysis of spring’s natural and artificial [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   responses (Massei et al., 2002; Pronk et al., 2006; Heinz et al., 2009; Goldscheider et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   2010; Ender et al., 2018; Sivelle and Labat, 2019; Goeppert and Goldscheider, 2019). [SOURCE: Page_4]

82         Firstly, in infiltrations, identifying water origins are important. DOC and NO$_3$- can be [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   used as infiltration markers. These tracers can be used in assessing the flow of water from [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   soil or unsaturated zone (Celle-Jeanton et al., 2003; Mahler and Garner, 2009; Mudarra and [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   Andreo 2011; Mudarra et al., 2011). Specifically, DOC in groundwater is used to assess a [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   water origin (Batiot et al., 2003; Charlier et al., 2011) and as a complementary marker of [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   turbidity in identifying fecal contamination (Pronk et al., 2007). In a rural context, the input [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   of nitrogen, organic or inorganic matter, can lead to an accumulation of NO$_3$- in soils and [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   unsaturated zone (Thomas et al., 2016). Leaching of these may result to a nitrate increase at [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   the outlet of kart systems (Rowden et al., 2001; Stueber and Criss 2005; Pronk et al., 2008; [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   Pu et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_5]

92         The common use of NO$_3$- and DOC at the outlet of a karst system with high resolution [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   monitoring will not only help in identifying the water origins but also facilitates in evaluating [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   the identified water quality hazards (punctual infiltration and diffuse infiltration). This [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   brought to the second and third concerns: (2) which type of water perturbation needs water [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   quality treatment and particularly, (3) which type hosts bacteria? In these lines, particles and [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   bacteria were used as supplementary parameters. [SOURCE: Page_5]

98         As for the particles, these can be resuspended due to a pressure wave induced by [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   percolating storm water reaching the phreatic water level (pulse-through turbidity). These can [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   also be introduced into the aquifer from the Earth surface (flushthrough turbidity) as a result [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   of either slow percolation or a more rapid point recharge such as from sinking streams. The [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   nature of turbidity can be determined looking at the particles if these are of autochthonous [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   (originating from the weathering of material) or allochthonous (originating from the Earth’s [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   surface) origin (Massei et al., 2002, 2003; Valdes et al., 2005; Fournier et al., 2007; [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   Schiperski et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_5]

106         Particle is an important subject matter as it can induce environmental problems due to [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   its ability to sorb bacteria. There are studies which have demonstrated the increased survival [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   of microorganisms when they are associated with particles (Pommepuy et al., 1992; [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   Palmateer et al., 1993). Some research results suggest that turbidity indicates microbial [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   pathogen presence such as E. coli, enterococcus or total coliforms (Ryan and Meiman, 1996; [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   Nebbache et al., 1997; Pronk et al., 2006, 2009; Goldscheider et al., 2010). However, others [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   did not observed the same findings stating that turbidity is not always accompanied by [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   surface contaminants (Dussart-Baptista et al., 2003; Pronk et al., 2007, 2009; Heinz et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   2009). In these lines, this study proposed a tool that can determine the type of turbidity that is [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   accompanied by contaminants (e.g. NO$_3$- and microbial pathogens). [SOURCE: Page_6]

116         The proposed method was applied to the Toulon springs in Périgueux, France. The [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   Toulon springs, as several karst springs, are a water resource being influenced by diffuse [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   pollution and punctual infiltration (Lastennet et al., 2004; Lorette et al., 2018). The [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   hydrogeological catchment is mainly rural (agriculture). There is no livestock and few [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   residential areas (less than 3 000 houses). The Toulon springs are experiencing an increase of [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   nitrate for over 60 years. This is a foremost concern as it serves as a major source of drinking [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   water for approximately 55 000 people. [SOURCE: Page_6]

123         The main objective of this research is to develop a method using high-resolution [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   monitoring that will facilitate in determining origins of water flowing at the outlet of karst [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   system during floods like in the Toulon. These origins are associated with water quality [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   hazards. Specifically, this study would like to: (i) identify the types water quality hazards that [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   springs like in the Toulon are facing brought about by the movement of water from different [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   origins (proposing a conceptual model of transfer of contaminants) and (ii) propose a [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   conceptual model/framework explaining the relationships between NO$_3$- and DOC in this [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   karst environment. [SOURCE: Page_6]

137       2.1 Local geology and hydrogeology [SOURCE: Page_7]

138 [SOURCE: Page_7]

139         The Toulon springs supply drinking water to the city of Périgueux (France) since 1832. [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   These consist of the Abîme Spring and the Cluzeau Spring, originating from the same karst [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   conduit. The average discharge is 450 L s-1, making it as one of the most important water [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   resources in the Dordogne County. The springs are vauclusian type, water reaches the surface [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   through localized faults at the extrados of an anticline fold oriented at N145° (Lastennet et [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   al., 2004). [SOURCE: Page_7]

145         Geology of the area is composed of carbonated rocks from upper Jurassic and upper [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   Cretaceous (Von Stempel, 1972; Lorette, 2019). At the bottom, the upper Jurassic formations [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   (Oxfordian and Kimmeridgian) consist of dolomitic limestone with a thickness varying from [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   100 m to 150 m. At the top, the upper Cretaceous formations (Turonian, Coniacian and [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   Santonian) is made up of limestone with a thickness varying from 200 m to 250 m. At the top [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   of the carbonate rocks is an epikarst, surmounted by a soil layer of few meters. The Toulon [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   karst system also presents fold and faults with a principal direction NW-SE (Fig. 1). [SOURCE: Page_7]

152         Hydrogeological context, on the other hand, considers two main multilayered aquifers: [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   (i) unconfined karstic upper Cretaceous aquifer: Turonian, Coniacian and Santonian aquifers [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   and (ii) confined karstic upper Jurassic aquifer: Oxfordian and Kimmeridgian aquifers. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   two main aquifers are separated by Cenomanian marls, considered impermeable. Spatial [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   thickness (4-20 meters) and facies variations (marls-sand) over the study area induced an [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   additional difficulty in understanding the relationship of the two main aquifers. [SOURCE: Page_7]

213      3. Material and methods [SOURCE: Page_11]

214 [SOURCE: Page_11]

215      3.1 Hydrological Approach [SOURCE: Page_11]

216 [SOURCE: Page_11]

217        The sampling strategy aims to identify the water quality hazards of the Toulon springs [SOURCE: Page_11]

218   in relation to the movement of water from different origins. High resolution monitoring of [SOURCE: Page_11]

219   hydrochemical parameters combined with particle sampling measurement and bacteriology [SOURCE: Page_11]

220   sampling were performed during the first flood events of each cycles: (i) February 2017 for [SOURCE: Page_11]

221   the 2016-2017 cycle and (ii) December 2017 for the 2017-2018 cycle. The first floods are [SOURCE: Page_11]

222   identifiable as these were associated with important rainfall after a long low–water period. [SOURCE: Page_11]

223   Nitrate, turbidity, and DOC were monitored in the Toulon springs. These parameters show [SOURCE: Page_11]

224   similar trends at each first flood of the cycle. The flood of February 2017 was chosen to [SOURCE: Page_11]

225   establish the links between the bacterial contamination and other monitored parameters. Once [SOURCE: Page_11]

226   relationships are established, an innovative method was developed to identify the origin of [SOURCE: Page_11]

10 [SOURCE: Page_11]

227   the water flowing in the karst outlet during flood events. Once the NO$_3$- and DOC trend is [SOURCE: Page_12]

228   known, this can be applied for the first flood of every hydrological cycles (for example [SOURCE: Page_12]

229   December 2017). [SOURCE: Page_12]

231      3.2 Continuous data monitoring and discrete sampling [SOURCE: Page_12]

232 [SOURCE: Page_12]

233        Since March 2016, turbidity, NO$_3$-, and DOC are measured at the Toulon springs. [SOURCE: Page_12]

234   Turbidity is measured using an optical sensor (Solitax sc TLine, HACH) every 10 minutes, [SOURCE: Page_12]

235   with a relative precision of 1%, and a range of measurement of 0.001 NTU to 4 000 NTU. [SOURCE: Page_12]

236   Nitrate and DOC are measured every 6 minutes using a UV-visible scanning [SOURCE: Page_12]

237   spectrophotometer (Spectro::lyser, S::CAN), with a precision of 0.1 mg L-1 and 0.05 mg L-1, [SOURCE: Page_12]

238   respectively, and a range of measurement of 0 to 46 mg L-1 and 0 to 133 mg L-1, respectively. [SOURCE: Page_12]

239   To avoid measurement errors due to matrix interferences when turbidity increases, a manual [SOURCE: Page_12]

240   calibration of the spectro::lyser was made with laboratory measurements (Lorette, 2019). [SOURCE: Page_12]

241        Simultaneously, hydrodynamic data (rainfall and discharge) were also monitored. [SOURCE: Page_12]

242   Rainfall was measured at the Toulon springs hydrogeological catchment every 15 minutes. [SOURCE: Page_12]

243   Only accumulated rainfall over a day for the central station is presented in this article. [SOURCE: Page_12]

244   Finally, discharge was measured in a gauging station every 10 minutes at the Toulon springs, [SOURCE: Page_12]

245   with an accuracy of 0.5%. [SOURCE: Page_12]

246        From 08/02/17 to 24/02/17, 12 sampling campaigns were performed to analyze the [SOURCE: Page_12]

247   bacteriology water samples (Escherichia coli, total coliforms, and enterococcus) and particles [SOURCE: Page_12]

248   (particle size distribution, observation, and chemistry) in the water. Bacteriology water [SOURCE: Page_12]

249   samples were taken using HDPE 500 mL bottles with thiosulfate and kept at 4°C before the [SOURCE: Page_12]

250   analysis on the same day of sampling. To sample particle, an HDPE bottle of 2 L is [SOURCE: Page_12]

11 [SOURCE: Page_12]

251   immerged and filled up in the Toulon spring. These bottles were kept in darkness until the [SOURCE: Page_13]

252   analysis is performed. [SOURCE: Page_13]
