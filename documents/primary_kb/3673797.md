1    Linking deliberative evaluation with integrated assessment and 2    modelling: a methodological framework and its application to 3    agricultural water management 4 5    Abstract: 6   Dealing jointly with both systemic and social complexity is a challenge that we face with sustainability 7   issues. However, joining these two aspects of complexity is more a “post-normal science” call than an 8   effective practice. Roughly speaking, analytical-systemic approaches focus on the complex-system 9   side, while deliberative approaches deal with the multi-actor side. Our framework for evaluating 10   different alternative actions, called the S2CE, is intended to reconcile the two approaches. It takes as 11   premises: the recognition of plural values, which makes competing actions only weakly comparable; 12   and the existence of irreducible uncertainties, which gives a heuristic status to the future. The 13   framework hybridises an integrated assessment and modelling exercise, with a deliberative multi- 14   criteria evaluation one, and concludes with a reflexive stage. Application to agricultural water 15   management illustrates the strength of this cross-fertilization. First, the use of computer simulations 16   has been found to enrich collective deliberation by helping stakeholders form and transform their value 17   judgments, and by raising critical questions about options that appeared consensual. Second, the 18   deliberative evaluation informed the integrated assessment and modelling by revealing some model 19   limitations, along with the model’s unequal capacity to reflect the claims of different stakeholders. 20 21   Keywords: post-normal science and technologies, multi-criteria evaluation, integrated assessment 22   and modelling, agricultural water management, collective deliberation, science-policy interface, 23   transdisciplinarity. 24 [SOURCE: Page_3]

25       1. Introduction: clarifying the challenge 26   “Normal” science and “command and control” management have already attracted half a 27   century of criticism, due to their inability to provide satisfactory answers to sustainability 28   issues. In reaction, post-normal science has offered an appealing science-policy philosophy 29   to approach sustainability-related problems, including GMOs and climate change. Post- 30   normal science is supposed to apply “when facts are uncertain, values in dispute, stakes 31   high and decisions urgent” (Funtowicz & Ravetz, 1990). One of its leading principles is to 32   break the dichotomy between facts (“hard”, “unquestionable”) and values (“soft”, 33   “unscientific”) by producing knowledge and evaluating its social robustness within extended 34   peer communities (Funtowicz & Ravetz, 1993). Unravelling and communicating uncertainties 35   is another founding principle of the post-normal science approach (van der Sluijs, 2002). 36   Post-normal science has found fertile ground for development within diverse fields and for 37   different purposes (Turnpenny, Jones, & Lorenzoni, 2011). Applied to complex systems, it 38   has helped to renew the conception of modelling for decision-making (Allison, Dickson, 39   Fisher, & Thrush, 2018; Haag & Kaupenjohann, 2001). It has also been taken up by social- 40   ecological economists to support an alternative view of sustainability that departs from 41   standard environmental economics (Frame & Brown, 2008; Funtowicz & Ravetz, 1994; 42   Giampietro, Mayumi, & Munda, 2006; Munda, 2004) and promotes deliberative processes for 43   reaching collective choices (Paavola, 2007; Vatn, 2009; Zografos, 2015). Modellers of social- 44   ecological systems and social-ecological economists mainly adopt the idea that sustainability 45   problems are “wicked”, but with different standpoints. The former adopt a systemic-analytical 46   approach and put the emphasis on interconnections between problems and between scales 47   (changes at one scale can trigger sometimes drastic changes at other scales, e.g. Tschakert 48   et al., 2014). The latter, favouring deliberative approaches, focus instead on radical 49   uncertainties and the weakly-comparable representations across people (Frame & O’Connor, 50   2011; Martinez-Alier, Munda, & O’Neill, 1998). 51    Different standpoints imply different drawbacks, when evaluating different options for change. 52    Modelling and scenario exploration, as typical methods in analytical-systemic approaches, 53    help surfacing feedback loops and the range of potential effects of a change. However the 54    model embodies a shared representation of the social-ecological system under scrutiny (Kok, 55    Rothman, & Patel, 2006; March, Therond, & Leenhardt, 2012; Tschakert et al., 2014; 56    Vervoort, Kok, van Lammeren, & Veldkamp, 2010). This representation is often monolithic 57    and offers little room for the confrontation of different value judgments. On the other hand, in 58    deliberative approaches to sustainability issues, systemic complexity is often largely 59    overlooked. To avoid comparing “apples and oranges”, social-ecological economists deal 60    with fragmented and competing representations of the problem at stake. The analysis of the 61    interplay between different issues is too narrowly limited to the consideration of trade-offs 62    between stakeholders’ preferences. Overall, systemic-analytical and deliberative approaches 63    should be combined in order to avoid choosing between envisioning counter-intuitive effects 64    of changes and tackling social dilemmas. 65    It is not a new epiphany that systemic and deliberative approaches should be linked better to 66    meet post-normal challenges (Allain, Plumecocq, & Leenhardt, 2017; Bond, Morrison- 67    Saunders, Gunn, Pope, & Retief, 2015; Rauschmayer & Wittmer, 2006). However, 68    transforming this claim into practice remains problematic. This is what we want to focus on in 69    this article. Before putting forth our argument, we would like to clarify some challenges that, 70    in our opinion, explain the difficulty of linking the two approaches. 71    First of all, the role of modelling appears to be a major bone of contention. Analytical 72    approaches accommodate modelling very well, with for instance the Integrated Assessment 73    and Modelling (IAM) community. Models are seen as tools to foster social learning (Pahl- 74    Wostl et al., 2007) as well as to communicate uncertainties, trade-offs and cross-scale 75    effects (Hamilton, ElSawah, Guillaume, Jakeman, & Pierce, 2015; Pahl-Wostl, Schlumpf, 76    Büssenschütt, Schönborn, & Burse, 2000). Involving stakeholders has become a routine 77    practice, although the stages and degrees of stakeholder involvement vary widely among 78    cases (Voinov et al., 2016). The aim is to assemble different sources of knowledge, either in 79    the form of “soft” models used as intermediary objects or as integrated computer models 80    which connect together different sub-models originally used for other purposes. 81    However, models (especially computer models) suffer from a long history of “technologies of 82    hubris” (Jasanoff, 2003) and are therefore considered cautiously, if not suspiciously, by 83    social scientists for deliberation purposes. Most of the critics come from the accentuation of 84    framing biases, i.e. when the solution to a problem is already incorporated in its formulation, 85    with model-based indicators (Elgert, 2013), as well as the lack of reflection on ambiguity, in 86    the sense of coexistence of equally-legitimate representations of the same system 87    (Brugnach, Dewulf, Henriksen, & van der Keur, 2011). Petersen et al. (2011) add to it the 88    impracticability of models for assessing innovative or marginal discourses: “Using rigorous 89    methods such as scenario design and system-bounded models only partially allowed for 90    incorporation and processing of the creativity and out-of-the box ideas that were generated 91    by stakeholders.” (p. 376). 92   Second, the conception of “integration” is another matter of silent dispute. In many respects, 93   the integration promoted in analytical approaches is incompatible with the treatment of 94   incommensurabilities – the absence of a common metric to compare different issues - 95   emphasized by social-ecological economists (Frame & O’Connor, 2011; Martinez-Alier et al., 96   1998; Munda, 2004). In IAM, for instance, integration covers different domains, e.g. issues, 97   stakeholders, disciplines, processes and models, and scales (Jakeman & Letcher, 2003) – a 98   list that varies across authors. Integration is generally materialized by a conceptual model, 99   which is a formal representation depicting “our current understanding about the structure and 100   workings of a system” (Argent et al., 2016). To a lesser extent, the same occurs with 101   multicriteria analysis, when conceived out of a deliberative framework. A series of attributes 102   or criteria portrays different aspects of a problem that vary in different directions: the analysis 103   consists of assessing trade-offs and producing a synthetic evaluation. In this case, the 104   multicriteria structure reflects an analytical decomposition of the problem, not the 105   juxtaposition of stakeholders’ representations. [SOURCE: Page_4]

106   However, with integration comes the spectre of a single problem representation, hence mode 107   of valuation, that heterodox economists, and among them social-ecological economists, 108   condemn (Spash, 2009). While integration is also a pivotal topic in ecological economics, it 109   has a specific focus on “problem compressions” (Giampietro, 2003) and the way values are 110   articulated (Martinez-Alier et al., 1998; Vatn, 2009). The different evaluation methods 111   promoted (e.g. social multi-criteria evaluation or deliberative multi-criteria evaluation) have in 112   common their investigation of the social structure of a problem as well as the diversity of 113   values attached to it (De Marchi, Funtowicz, Lo Cascio, & Munda, 2000; Stagl, 2006). In the 114   words of (Frame & Brown, 2008): “the notion of integration is dialogic and multi-perspectival; 115   it is not to be imagined that the dialogue will be one that combines many voices and reduces 116   them to a single, consensual view” (p. 226). 117   Those distinct perspectives on integration resonate with the way participation is conceived in 118   deliberative versus analytical frameworks. For instance, many IAM scientists consider that 119   one crucial aspect of integration is among different knowledge and viewpoints (Hamilton et 120   al., 2015; van Asselt & Rijkens-Klomp, 2002). To this end, they try to gather experts from 121   different disciplines and local actors to contribute to the development of a conceptual model 122   or of scenarios to assess (Leenhardt et al., 2012; Miller, O’Leary, Graffy, Stechel, & Dirks, 123   2015; Tschakert et al., 2014). They use different participatory techniques and artefacts to 124   access more remote or less formalized information held by non-scientists (e.g. farmers) and 125   to lift “the barriers to integration” (Tress, Tress, & Fry, 2007). In contrast, in social-ecological 126   economics, deliberation (rather than participation) has a political meaning. It constitutes an 127   institution that articulates incommensurable values (Vatn, 2009) and not a tool to bring 128   together the different pieces of the same puzzle. Concretely, this means that the artefacts of 129   a deliberative exercise (indicators, scenarios, models, etc.) support the expression and 130   confrontation of value judgments (Allain, Plumecocq, & Leenhardt, 2018; Frame & O’Connor, 131   2011) and are not just descriptors providing a holistic view of the situation. Hence, integration 132   and participation, which seem to flow naturally from a post-normal science endeavour, do not 133   have the same meaning in analytical and deliberative exercises. 134   The good news is that there are signs of rapprochement between the different communities; 135   hence start of reconciliation between analytical-systemic and deliberative approaches. In this 136   respect, the work of Salliou et al. (2017) that positions itself as Companion Modelling is a fine 137   illustration. Using Bayesian belief networks to represent perceived causal links between 138   orchard pest management and fruit quality, the modellers chose to have one parametrization 139   of the model per actor. Based on these, they compared the different actors’ views on how 140   different management scenarios could impact the fruit production. Using different models 141   rather than a single integrated one generated dialogue about ambiguity, which was very 142   close to the clarification of incommensurabilities advocated by social-ecological economists. 143   It would also be a caricature to state that the promoters of deliberation who endorse a radical 144   pluralism perspective, are “anti-modelling”. Frame and Brown (2008) do not close the door to 145   modelling in their review of “post-normal technologies”, especially if it takes place in the 146   framework of explorative futures studies. Information and Communication Technologies are 147   also used to having different stakeholders handle diverse knowledge sources, including 148   numerical models, to elaborate their value judgments (Guimarães Pereira, & Funtowicz, 149   2006). Recently, Douguet et al. (under review) proposed to use their deliberation support tool 150   in conjunction with a scenario simulation model, but so far with extensive leeway in the 151   articulation of the two tools. 152   Our introduction has now come full circle. To sum up: both systemic-analytical and 153   deliberative approaches offer valuable insights for the management of wicked problems. 154   Their combination is however challenging because the approaches to modelling and 155   integration differ; some initiatives already show a trend towards rapprochement in order to 156   embrace the part of complexity (systemic or social) that each of them lacks. So, in practice, 157   how do we simultaneously envision potential surprising effects and social dilemmas when 158   comparing different options for change? In this paper, we propose a methodological 159   framework called “S2CE” that explicitly links integrated assessment and modelling of social- 160   ecological systems with deliberative evaluation. Our framework is about how to perform this [SOURCE: Page_5]

161   combination under both the radical pluralism and complex system hypotheses, and how to 162   analyse the results of such an operation. We end with an application in the field of 163   agricultural water management that provides insights for discussing the method. [SOURCE: Page_6]

164      2. The methodological framework: Evaluation embracing social and 165         systemic complexity (S2CE) 166 167   The S²CE reflects the fact that our evaluation (E) framework was designed to embrace both 168   social and systemic aspects (“S2”) of complex problems (“C”). This framework builds upon the 169   strengths of integrated assessment and modelling, on the one hand, and social/deliberative 170   evaluation, on the other. It acknowledges, as premises: 171   - the coexistence of different value systems that offer different representations of a problem 172   (value pluralism); 173   - yet, the possibility to compare competing alternatives in relative terms (“weak 174   comparability”, Munda, 2004); 175   - the systemic nature of social-ecological problems, which implies that their components are 176   interdependent, provoking either rigidities or surprises when implementing changes 177   (uncertainty); 178   - yet, the relevance, for present action, of exploring contrasting scenarios of change that are 179   however meaningless for the future (in line with Sardar, 2010, who states that futures studies 180   are "futureless"). 181   S2CE has many proximities with the Integraal framework for sustainability evaluations 182   (O’Connor, Small, & Wedderburn, 2010) , but adds to it specific stages linked to the use of 183   simulation models (Leenhardt et al., 2012), i.e. translation of narratives into simulation inputs 184   and customization of simulation outputs for use by non-experts. The Integraal framework, as 185   a typical example of deliberative evaluation, puts to light convergences and divergences 186   among stakeholder judgments, which is a way of formalizing social complexity. However, 187   non-linear or cascading effects have nearly no chance to integrate stakeholders’ 188   argumentation unless they become legible elsewhere. IAM or other systemic-analytical 189   approaches can generate this legibility. S2CE is therefore an extension of the deliberative 190   evaluation loop with a method focusing on system’s complexity, here IAM. One peculiar 191   feature of S2CE is to give indicators a double status: that of arguments (for the deliberative 192   process) and that of descriptors of changes (for the purpose of systemic analysis). 193   As a result, S2CE consists of the following 8 stages (Fig. 1): 194      1. Problem structuring: the problem space, theoretically infinite, is turned into a finite 195         number of alternatives, stakeholders and issues. It acquires the structure of a social- 196         choice problem. 197      2. Translation for modelling: alternatives and issues, generally expressed in the form 198         of narratives during the previous stage, are translated into model inputs (e.g. 199         scenarios) and outputs (e.g. indicators to obtain), most of the time quantitative. 200      3. Simulation: although this stage looks like “the computer works”, it actually entails a 201         trial-and-error process, as the people implementing simulations must check the 202         coherence of the outputs, and in many cases launch new simulations with reviewed 203         input files or model code. 204      4. Processing and customization: model outputs are turned into indicators, i.e. an 205         object with meaningful information that facilitates learning about a situation and 206         forming a value judgment about it (Allain, Plumecocq, et al., 2018). 207      5. Integrated assessment: alternatives are assessed using a subset of indicators 208         chosen for their ability to unravel trade-offs, uncertainties and surprising effects. In 209         addition, the underlying mechanisms explaining the differences among indicators and 210         among alternatives are analysed, potentially following complementary simulations. 248       3. Application to agricultural water management 249   Agricultural water management is an illustrative case of sustainability issues. First, it is one of 250   the topics generating the most intense debates and conflicts throughout the world, even in 251   temperate regions and established democracies (Temper, Delbene, Martinez-Alier, & 252   Rodriguez-Labajos, 2015). Second, water management systems involve numerous and 253   radical uncertainties (Pahl-Wostl et al., 2007) Third, water management systems have 254   encountered multiple failures, whether social, ecological, or both (Budds, 2009; Walker et al., 255   2002), which make changes all the more urgent. 256   Calls for a change of paradigm have for more than 20 years sustained the political discourse 257   of an “Integrated Water Resource Management”, yet persistent difficulties have been 258   experienced in effectively democratizing water governance and switching the focus from 259   increasing water supply towards regulating water demand. In South Western France, where 260   our case study is situated, there are still strong legacies of the old technocratic paradigm. 261   This is reflected in the prominence of the “Minimum Flow Requirement” indicator (Fernandez, 262   2014), which currently structures all water management interventions, from the zoning for 263   water policies to the operational decisions to release water stored in public dams or to restrict 264   irrigation withdrawals. It is also evidenced in the persisting idea that water crises in 265   agricultural areas, especially in a context of climatic change, could be solved with more water 266   storage (Debril & Therond, 2012). 267   With no formalized demand from stakeholders, but building upon long-term collaboration 268   between our research team and water managers, we developed the S2CE framework while 269   reflecting on the specific situation of a “red spot” watershed in our region. This watershed 270   combines severe water imbalance and severe social conflicts. In this section we reconstruct 271   the stages of the S2CE that were implemented there. [SOURCE: Page_8]
