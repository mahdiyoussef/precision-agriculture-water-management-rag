15   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   In the context of organic waste management, near infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) is [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   being used to offer a fast, non-destructive, and cost-effective characterization system. [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   However, cumbersome freeze-drying steps of the samples are required to avoid water’s [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   interference on near infrared spectra. In order to better understand these effects, [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   spectral variations induced by dry matter content variations were obtained for a wide [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   variety of organic substrates. This was made possible by the development of a [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   customized near infrared acquisition system with dynamic highly-resolved simultaneous [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   scanning of near infrared spectra and estimation of dry matter content during a drying [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   process at ambient temperature. Using principal components analysis, the complex [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   water effects on near infrared spectra are detailed. Water effects are shown to be a [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   combination of both physical and chemical effects, and depend on both the [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   characteristics of the samples (biochemical type and physical structure) and the [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   moisture content level. This results in a non-linear relationship between the measured [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   signal and the analytical characteristic of interest. A typology of substrates with respect [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   to these water effects is provided and could further be efficiently used as a basis for the [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   development of local quantitative calibration models and correction methods accounting [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   for these water effects. [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

38 [SOURCE: Page_3]

39          A growing number of solid organic waste treatment processes such as anaerobic [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   digestion, composting or pyrogaseification are currently being developed and [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   industrialized. Usually, organic wastes cover a wide range of physical characteristics [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   and bio-chemical compositions, making substrate characterization a key issue in [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   optimizing any of these processes. Recently, near infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) has [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   been used to offer a fast, non-destructive, and cost-effective waste characterization [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   system in the anaerobic digestion context (Charnier et al., 2016; Fitamo et al., 2017; [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   Godin et al., 2015; Lesteur et al., 2011; Mayer et al., 2013; Mortreuil et al., 2018) and [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   composting context (Albrecht et al., 2008; Galvez-Sola et al., 2010; Vergnoux et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   2009). However, a freeze-drying step is always required, due to strong interferences in [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   the near infrared region related to the presence of water in the substrates (Lobell and [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   Asner, 2002; Williams, 2009). Not only is this drying step cumbersome and impedes any [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   online application, but the volatilization process that takes place during drying makes [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   some characteristics (volatile fatty acids) impossible to predict directly. Though some [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   applications have been developed for the characterization of liquid samples with the [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   presence of water, these are usually restricted to a limited moisture content range, as [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   well as one substrate type (Jacobi et al., 2009; Stockl and Lichti, 2018). In fact, near [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   infrared spectroscopy is sensitive to numerous factors including the spectrometer lamp [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   temperature (Sánchez et al., 2003), sample presentation (Sørensen et al., 2014), light [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   penetration depth (Padalkar and Pleshko, 2015), sample particle size distribution (Igne [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   et al., 2014), sample temperature (Sánchez et al., 2003), and moisture content (Lobell [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   and Asner, 2002). All these interfering factors need to be accounted for in order to build [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   robust quantitative calibrations (Acharya et al., 2014; Zeaiter et al., 2004). Furthermore, [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   these factors may interact together, leading to more complexity for their correction. [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   Indeed, for example, a close relationship between moisture effects and temperature has [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   been outlined (Renati et al., 2019; Wenz, 2018), leading to account for both factors in [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   conjunction (Hans et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_4]

66 [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   The effect of moisture content on near infrared spectra has been described for a wide [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   variety of different matter types including soil (Bogrekci and Lee, 2006; Bowers and [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   Hanks, 1965; Chang et al., 2005; Knadel et al., 2014; Lobell and Asner, 2002; Sudduth [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   and Hummel, 1993; Wu et al., 2009), crops (Gaines and Windham, 1998; Gergely and [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   Salgó, 2003; Peiris et al., 2016; Popineau et al., 2005; Williams, 2009), food (Büning- [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   Pfaue, 2003), plants (Carter, 1991), wood (Giordanengo et al., 2008), pharmaceuticals [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   (Igne et al., 2014), object models (Reeves, 1995, 1994; Wenz, 2018), and water- [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   dominant systems (Muncan and Tsenkova, 2019). In addition, though not focused on [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   the analysis of moisture content effects in NIRS, some studies use NIRS to monitor [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   drying or hydration processes where moisture content varies (Caponigro et al., 2018; [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   Raponi et al., 2017). However, no study has yet analyzed and compared moisture [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   content effects in one comprehensive experiment with a wide variety of biochemical and [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   physical types. Better understanding water effects and how they relate to the substrate [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   properties appears as key for the development of robust calibrations models on wet [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   substrates. Indeed, groups could then be used for building local models, an approach [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   which has been shown to be successful for BMP prediction on plant biomasses (Godin [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_5]

84 [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   The main effect of moisture content variations on NIR spectra usually put forward in [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   studies relates to the apparition of three broad OH absorbance bands (detailed further [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   on); but one major effect of water relates to physical effects (ie. changes in scattering). [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   This is why, when speaking about water effects, an important aspect to have in mind [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   concerns the measurement mode. For transparent liquid samples such as pure water or [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   clear suspensions, transmission or transflexion mode is usually preferred (Pasquini, [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   2003), while for solid samples like powders, diffuse reflection appears most suitable. [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   When studying large moisture content variations, one substrate may cover various [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   states from a clear suspension, to a sludge-type material, to a powder when fully dried. [SOURCE: Page_5]

94 [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   Because near infrared spectra contain both physical information (such as granulometry) [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   and chemical information (compound concentration of interest), a pre-processing step is [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   commonly used to maximize the chemical information in the spectra. This is done by [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   getting rid of baseline effects due to scattering (referred to additive and multiplicative [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   effects), as well as using spectral derivation to deconvolve the peaks. A wide variety of [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   pre-processing techniques are used (Rinnan et al., 2009; Zeaiter and Rutledge, 2009), [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   sometimes even in combination (Roger et al., 2020). However, these pre-processing [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   steps may bring important artefacts (Rabatel et al., 2019) in the spectra when applied [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   inappropriately. As well, some pre-processing steps such as derivation may deport the [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   chemical information on shifted peak positions which can make the assignment of [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   bands more complicated (Oliveri et al., 2019). Nevertheless, such pre-processing steps [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   will most likely remain necessary when building quantitative models. [SOURCE: Page_6]

107 [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   In the context of highly diverse matter types, water effects are expected to vary at least [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   according to the biochemical characteristics. Exploring such differences in effects is the [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   aim of this article. A customized air-drying system was built, allowing the simultaneous [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   monitoring of samples’ moisture content and acquisition of near infrared spectra during [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   drying. Using this system, spectral variations related to moisture content variations were [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   obtained for a large variety of substrates. A principal component analysis was used to [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   explore the various effects. The aim of this global PCA was to identify major groups of [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   substrates in regards to water effects. This was done by analyzing the scores’ kinetics [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   of each substrate during drying in relation with the interpretation of each component [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   loadings using band assignments (Williams and Antoniszyn, 2019; Workman and [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   Weyer, 2012). Because the aim of the study was to explore the water effects, including [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   baseline modifications related to scattering effects, data analysis was done on the raw [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   spectra, without any prior pre-processing steps. [SOURCE: Page_6]

124          The study was conducted on       = 89 substrates chosen to represent a wide range [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   order to provide control samples with simplified water effects due to limited water [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   paper, aluminum, plastic). Because these packaging materials were found dry at their [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   original state, samples were wetted artificially by adding water at the start of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   experiment. [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   For each substrate, 50 g of fresh matter (initial mass before drying,     ) were sampled [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   and manually ground (to obtain a mixture with elements below 1 cm) for further drying [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   and NIR analysis. To determine dry matter content before and after drying (respectively [SOURCE: Page_7]

137        and      ), two replicate samples of 5-10 g were weighed before and after 48h of [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   2.2. Drying system [SOURCE: Page_7]

140          The drying system used (Figure 1) was a customized system consisting in a [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   closed tube loop, with an internal circulation of air generated by a peristaltic pump [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   (Masterflex N°77521-47 6-600 RPM, with a head #7018-52) set at 500 RPM [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   corresponding to a generation of a flow speed of 2000 ml min-1. A strong desiccant [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   (sodium hydroxide) was used to enable drying of the gas phase and therefore the [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   substrate: indeed, sodium hydroxide allows to bring the relative humidity at about 8% at [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   25°C (Greenspan, 1976). The drying circuit was connected to a hermetic spectrometer [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   sampling cup in which the waste sample was placed. The sampling cup was placed [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   over the spectrophotometer for continuous automatic near infrared acquisitions; and the [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   desiccant was weighed continuously using a precision balance (Ohaus Traveler [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   TA502), to enable the measurement of loss of water during drying. In addition, two [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   temperature probes were installed on the system to monitor both the temperature inside [SOURCE: Page_8]

152   the sample cup chamber and the room temperature, for investigation of temperature- [SOURCE: Page_8]

153   induced spectral variations. Before closing the system and launching the acquisition, the [SOURCE: Page_8]

154   circuit was flushed with nitrogen gas to limit oxidative reactions on the substrates. Using [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   this drying system, substrates were dried during time periods varying from 12 hours to [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   72 hours. [SOURCE: Page_8]

16 [SOURCE: Page_16]

565 [SOURCE: Page_27]

566   It was shown that one of the main effects of water on near infrared spectra concerns [SOURCE: Page_27]

567   global changes in scattering due to water’s crucial role in biomolecules’ structure and [SOURCE: Page_27]

572   properties vary according to the chemical composition of substrates. For example, the [SOURCE: Page_27]

573   presence of fat may form emulsions leading to decreased scattering levels during [SOURCE: Page_27]

574   drying, while suspensions or porous media formed by solid ligno-cellulosic component [SOURCE: Page_27]

575   show increased scattering levels during drying. As well, the presence of soluble [SOURCE: Page_28]

576   components such as sucrose may lead to transparent solutions with important forward [SOURCE: Page_28]

577   scattering levels. A complex interaction between chemical composition and physical [SOURCE: Page_28]

578   scattering properties has therefore been outlined. [SOURCE: Page_28]

579   Secondly, a strong overlap of water OH absorbance bands has been highlighted and [SOURCE: Page_28]

580   shown in the second and third components, masking other more minor OH absorbance [SOURCE: Page_28]

581   bands present in carbohydrates, fatty acids, or alcohols. [SOURCE: Page_28]

582   Thirdly, two different spectral patterns related to water’s chemical interaction (ie. water [SOURCE: Page_28]

583   state) were identified in the third and eighth components. Indeed, it was shown in the [SOURCE: Page_28]

584   third component that small differences between the first overtone absorbance band at [SOURCE: Page_28]

585   1430 nm and the second combination absorbance band at 1940 nm is associated with [SOURCE: Page_28]

586   the presence of simple carbohydrates or proteins, both of these molecules forming [SOURCE: Page_28]

587   important interactions with water. In addition, in the eighth component, as drying occurs, [SOURCE: Page_28]

588   a shift of the OH absorbance bands from high energy vibrations to lower energy [SOURCE: Page_28]

589   vibrations was highlighted for most substrates. [SOURCE: Page_28]

590   Fourthly, the fourth component was found linearly dependent of dry matter content in [SOURCE: Page_28]

591   most substrates. However, it was shown that the rate of this dependence differed over [SOURCE: Page_28]

592   substrates depending on its physical properties. [SOURCE: Page_28]

593   Finally, different components related to the substrates’ chemical composition were [SOURCE: Page_28]

594   found. Indeed, the fifth, sixth and seventh components differentiated substrates based [SOURCE: Page_28]

595   on carbohydrates levels, as well as fat content levels. This is promising in regards to the [SOURCE: Page_28]

596   possibility of developing calibrations on high moisture content substrates as there is still [SOURCE: Page_28]

28 [SOURCE: Page_28]

597   information related to the chemical composition: wet substrates spectra are not “just [SOURCE: Page_29]

598   water spectra”. [SOURCE: Page_29]

599   4. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_29]

600   The present study investigated the complexity of water effects in near infrared [SOURCE: Page_29]

601   spectroscopy and highlighted the close dependency with the biochemical and physical [SOURCE: Page_29]

602   characteristics of samples. [SOURCE: Page_29]

603   A customized acquisition system allowed to obtain a unique dataset comprising NIR [SOURCE: Page_29]

604   spectral variations related to water content modifications in standard conditions [SOURCE: Page_29]

605   (ambient temperature/humidity) with no heating nor chemical altering (oxidation, [SOURCE: Page_29]

606   Maillard reactions). Such water spectral variations were obtained on a very wide variety [SOURCE: Page_29]

607   of biochemical types (including carbohydrate substrates, protein substrates, fat [SOURCE: Page_29]

608   substrates as well as packaging materials), allowing a comprehensive analysis of the [SOURCE: Page_29]

609   water effects in near infrared spectroscopy. [SOURCE: Page_29]

610   A detailed analysis of the dataset using principal component analysis revealed water’s [SOURCE: Page_29]

611   complex effects, combining both physical and chemical effects. The fact that water [SOURCE: Page_29]

612   effects depend both on the dry matter content range and the nature of the substrates [SOURCE: Page_29]

613   (both biochemical composition, and physical structure) leads to important challenges for [SOURCE: Page_29]

614   its correction in the context of organic waste characterization. These results encourage [SOURCE: Page_29]

615   future research on the correction of water effects to focus on the development of local [SOURCE: Page_29]

616   and clustered approaches, to correct water effects within groups of substrates with [SOURCE: Page_29]

617   common physical properties and dry matter content range. [SOURCE: Page_29]
