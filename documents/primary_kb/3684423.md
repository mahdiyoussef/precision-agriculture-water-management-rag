175      3.1. Random Forest [SOURCE: Page_11]

176   Random forest models (Breiman, 2001) are an ensemble learning method for both [SOURCE: Page_11]

177   classification and regression. A forest, which is an ensemble of randomized decision trees, is [SOURCE: Page_11]

178   built and trained based on a bootstrap approach. Individual trees are built using the principle [SOURCE: Page_11]

179   of recursive partitioning. “The feature space is recursively split into regions containing [SOURCE: Page_11]

180   observations with similar response value” (Strobl et al., 2009). The predictions of the [SOURCE: Page_11]

181   individual trees are finally averaged to give one single prediction. [SOURCE: Page_11]

182      3.2. Mapping model: the quantile regression forest [SOURCE: Page_11]

183   In this study, we use one of the most commonly used algorithms in DSM studies, namely, the [SOURCE: Page_11]

184   quantile regression forest algorithm (QRF; Meinshausen, 2006), which is an extension of [SOURCE: Page_11]

185   Breiman’s random forests (RF; 2001). For the regression, RF provides an ensemble prediction [SOURCE: Page_11]

186   based on n regression trees. For every tree, the algorithm integrates random features by [SOURCE: Page_11]

187   randomly selecting a subset of features to be split. While RF provides solely the conditional [SOURCE: Page_11]

188   mean, QRF supplies the whole conditional distribution of the target variable by keeping all [SOURCE: Page_11]

189   observations at the terminal nodes and can infer estimates for the conditional quantiles [SOURCE: Page_11]

190   (Meinshausen, 2006). More details on QRF can be found in Meinshausen (2006). [SOURCE: Page_11]

191   QRF was run with the ranger package, which is a fast implementation of Breiman’s random [SOURCE: Page_11]

192   forest and Meinshausen’s quantile regression forest (Wright and Ziegler, 2015). [SOURCE: Page_11]

409   5.    Discussion [SOURCE: Page_24]

410        5.1. Evaluation protocol 23 [SOURCE: Page_24]

411   The evaluation protocol that was applied in this study consisted of a 10-fold cross validation [SOURCE: Page_25]

412   approach with 20 repetitions, with the reference data being the soil profiles at punctual sites [SOURCE: Page_25]

413   with the observed properties (textural fractions, coarse fragments and soil thickness) used to [SOURCE: Page_25]

414   produce a local estimation of the SAWC using the pedotransfer functions. This protocol [SOURCE: Page_25]

415   ensured both, an evaluation of the SAWC predictions from independent samples and a [SOURCE: Page_25]

416   comprehensive consideration of the mapping errors of all SAWC components. To our [SOURCE: Page_25]

417   knowledge, this is the first time that these two conditions were fulfilled in an evaluation [SOURCE: Page_25]

418   protocol dealing with the SAWC, which makes the comparisons with previous papers dealing [SOURCE: Page_25]

419   with SAWC (e.g. Poggio et al, 2010; Hong et al, 2013; Leenaars et al, 2018) difficult. [SOURCE: Page_25]

420   However, our evaluation protocol had two main limitations. First, the evaluation sites were [SOURCE: Page_25]

421   not characterized by the real SAWC measurements, and the fine earth water retention was [SOURCE: Page_25]

422   estimated with the pedotransfer functions. This did not allow us to account for the PTF errors, [SOURCE: Page_25]

423   as Román Dobarco et al. (2019) did in their study. However, these authors showed that PTF [SOURCE: Page_25]

424   errors played a minor role in comparison with the mapping errors of the SAWC components. [SOURCE: Page_25]

425   This should be even more true with the addition of the ST mapping errors in the evaluation [SOURCE: Page_25]

426   protocol in comparison with the protocol of Román Dobarco et al (2019). Furthermore, the [SOURCE: Page_25]

427   introduction of the PTF error in our error propagation models following the technique [SOURCE: Page_25]

428   proposed by Román Dobarco et al. (2019) did not modify the ex-ante uncertainty evaluation [SOURCE: Page_25]

429   of the SAWC paper (result not shown in this paper). However, a full evaluation of the SAWC [SOURCE: Page_25]

430   mapping would be preferable, which means investing in costly field and laboratory [SOURCE: Page_25]

431   measurements or finding accurate and inexpensive proxies for the SAWC (Coulouma et al, [SOURCE: Page_25]

432   2020). [SOURCE: Page_25]

433   A second limitation of our evaluation protocol was that SAWC mapping was evaluated at the [SOURCE: Page_25]

434   site level, which was not representative of the decision-making units of the end-user and [SOURCE: Page_25]

435   represents the worst case scenario for assessing the soil property prediction quality. Ideally, [SOURCE: Page_25]

24 [SOURCE: Page_25]

436   the evaluation should be performed for areal units (Bishop et al, 2015), which would produce [SOURCE: Page_26]

437   more realistic results that would be in accordance with the visual evaluation of the map (see [SOURCE: Page_26]

438   further). However, evaluating the SAWC from the areal units would require data collection [SOURCE: Page_26]

439   that cannot be reasonably envisaged. Consistency checking involving data available at larger [SOURCE: Page_26]

440   spatial support and closely related with AWC could be an alternative (Vanderlinden, 2005) [SOURCE: Page_26]

441      5.2.   Error propagation model [SOURCE: Page_26]

442   Following Román Dobarco et al. (2019), an error propagation model using a first-order Taylor [SOURCE: Page_26]

443   expansion was developed for the ex-ante estimation of SAWC mapping uncertainty. This [SOURCE: Page_26]

444   model was, to some extent, more complete than the one developed by Román Dobarco et al. [SOURCE: Page_26]

445   (2019) in that it considered the error in the soil thickness maps and the correlations between [SOURCE: Page_26]

446   the error in the SAWC component maps that were not considered in Román Dobarco et al. [SOURCE: Page_26]

447   (2019). This model was obtained by selecting an inference trajectory that included separate [SOURCE: Page_26]

448   mapping of the soil layer thicknesses, which allowed easy application of the first-order Taylor [SOURCE: Page_26]

449   expansion. The results revealed that the consideration of the error correlations had an impact [SOURCE: Page_26]

450   on the final result if they reached a given level, which was the case for the error correlations [SOURCE: Page_26]

451   between the soil layer maps (Table 4). The results showed that the ex-ante estimation of [SOURCE: Page_26]

452   uncertainty was only slightly biased (differences with nominal values of 90% less than 1 for [SOURCE: Page_26]

453   two out of three SAWC maps), which corresponded to much smaller uncertainty estimation [SOURCE: Page_26]

454   biases than those obtained by Román Dobarco et al. (2019). We also verified that the RMSE [SOURCE: Page_26]

455   obtained from the validation protocol was closely related to the predicted uncertainty (Figure [SOURCE: Page_26]

456   4), which, to our knowledge, has not yet been verified. We note, however, that the error [SOURCE: Page_26]

457   propagation model built in this study did not consider the PTF errors. This error could be [SOURCE: Page_26]

458   easily added by following the procedure proposed by Román Dobarco et al. (2019). [SOURCE: Page_26]

459      5.3. General performances of the SAWC predictions [SOURCE: Page_26]

25 [SOURCE: Page_26]

460   Although the SAWC map of the Languedoc-Roussillon region exhibited expected and [SOURCE: Page_27]

461   pedologically sound soil patterns, poor results were obtained from the evaluation protocol [SOURCE: Page_27]

462   (Table 6). This was likely related to the difficulty of mapping the two most critical [SOURCE: Page_27]

463   components of the SAWC, namely, the coarse fragments and the soil layer thicknesses (Table [SOURCE: Page_27]

464   5). As observed by Vaysse et al. (2015), the soil thickness and coarse fragments were [SOURCE: Page_27]

465   characterized by a pre-eminence of short-scale variations that could not be captured by a [SOURCE: Page_27]

466   DSM model using a so sparse soil spatial sampling according to the soil layer depths (Table [SOURCE: Page_27]

467   1). A denser spatial sampling is therefore necessary in this situation. Furthermore, the coarse [SOURCE: Page_27]

468   fragment data were obtained from visual observations of the soil profile, which carry a greater [SOURCE: Page_27]

469   uncertainty than the ones of other soil properties that are measured in a laboratory. More [SOURCE: Page_27]

470   accurate field protocols for measuring the proportion of coarse fragments (Algayer et al, [SOURCE: Page_27]

471   2019) are required to improve this situation. [SOURCE: Page_27]

472   It is also important to notice that biases were important components of the SAWC prediction [SOURCE: Page_27]

473   errors (between 15 and 30% as shown on table 6), which generated an overall SAWC [SOURCE: Page_27]

474   overestimation.   This overestimation can be related with the important positive biases [SOURCE: Page_27]

475   observed for the predictions of the thicknesses of the three first soil layers (table 5). Such [SOURCE: Page_27]

476   biases should be caused by the difficulties of the Random Forest algorithm to deal with the [SOURCE: Page_27]

477   important subset of locations having null soil layer thicknesses. Dealing with zero-inflated [SOURCE: Page_27]

478   input datasets of regression models is a well-known problem in ecology (Martin et al, 2005). [SOURCE: Page_27]

479   Specific regression approaches adapted to zero-inflated datasets (Savage et al, 2015) should [SOURCE: Page_27]

480   be applied to mitigate this problem. [SOURCE: Page_27]

481   Conversely, the last soil layer exhibited a negative bias (table 5) that can be related with the [SOURCE: Page_27]

482   unbalanced distribution of soil thicknesses in the set of sampling locations (figure 3). Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_27]

483   a very small proportion of deep soils were sampled because of the low maximum observation [SOURCE: Page_27]

484   depths (only 15% of the soil profiles had maximum observation depths greater than 120 cm). [SOURCE: Page_27]

26 [SOURCE: Page_27]

485   This too small proportion generated the underestimation of the deepest soil layers thicknesses [SOURCE: Page_28]

486   since, the random forest algorithm is known to behave like an interpolator and to smooth the [SOURCE: Page_28]

487   outliers (Song, 2015). A more balanced sampling of the ST across the study region is [SOURCE: Page_28]

488   therefore necessary. [SOURCE: Page_28]

491          •   We developed a DSM model that mapped the SAWC values and provided an ex [SOURCE: Page_28]

492              ante local estimation of the prediction uncertainty. For the first time, this [SOURCE: Page_28]

493              uncertainty model took into account all SAWC component mapping errors for all [SOURCE: Page_28]

494              soil layers. [SOURCE: Page_28]
