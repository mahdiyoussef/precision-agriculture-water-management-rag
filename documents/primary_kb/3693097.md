4   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

5   Eutrophication is one of the main causes of the degradation of lake ecosystems. Its intensification during the [SOURCE: Page_2]

6   last decades has led the stakeholders to seek for water management and restoration solutions, including those [SOURCE: Page_2]

7   based on modelling approaches. This paper presents a review of lake eutrophication modelling, on the basis [SOURCE: Page_2]

8   of a scientific appraisal performed by researchers for the French ministries of Environment and Agriculture. [SOURCE: Page_2]

9   After a brief introduction presenting the scientific context, a bibliography analysis is presented. Then the [SOURCE: Page_2]

10   main results obtained with process-based models are summarized. A synthesis of the scientist recommenda- [SOURCE: Page_2]

11   tions in order to improve the lake eutrophication modelling is finally given before the conclusion. [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   1    Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   Eutrophication of aquatic ecosystems refers to an ecological state, where biological processes driven by in- [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   creased nutrient loading from their watershed conducts to proliferation of primary producers (phytoplankton, [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   aquatic plants, cyanobacteria), hypoxia or even anoxia and loss of biodiversity. Worldwide, eutrophication is [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   one of the main causes of the degradation of the ecological state in inland and coastal waters. Eutrophication [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   has been observed since the beginning of the twentieth century in industrialized countries (e.g. Le Moal et [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   al., 2018; Moss, 2012; Takolander et al., 2017; Yao et al., 2018). However, during the last decade, eutrophi- [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   cation has become a more pregnant societal issue, encompassing many different stakes, from ecological to [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   economic and political, which must be tackled at the scale of the concerned territories by the involved stake- [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   holders. [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   Lakes and reservoirs are part of the hydrological cycle of their watershed. Fluxes are permanently exchanged [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   between the lake and the atmosphere, the ground and surface waters. Lakes play an essential role in the bio- [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   geochemical cycles of continental watersheds. Two processes drive these biogeochemical cycles, particularly [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   Carbon, Nitrogen and Phosphorus cycles: the primary production and the settling of particulate matter. [SOURCE: Page_2]

1 [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   In lakes, the current velocities are generally low and the water residence time high. Therefore, lakes provide [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   us with a time-integrated response to the external forcing. The analysis of their time trajectory can allow us [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   to better understand the biological and physical, local and global pressures that they face (Perga et al., 2016; [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   Schindler, 2009). [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   Lake ecosystems are particularly sensitive to nutrient loading from their catchment because of the thermal [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   stratification of the water column during the period when the primary production is maximal (spring and [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   summer). The thermal stratification divides the water column into two layers: the upper layer, the warmer [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   and lighted epilimnion, where primary production occurs, and a colder deeper layer, the hypolimnion. Ther- [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   mal stratification occurs in all lakes but extending on different time scales. In shallow lakes, thermal stratifi- [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   cation hardly lasts more than a few hours or days. The main morphometric (depth, volume) and hydrological [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   (discharge of the tributaries, surface and land-use in the catchment) characteristics will determine the lake [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   vulnerability to eutrophication. [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   In the early 1960s, lakes were the first water bodies where eutrophication effects were observed, mainly on [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   the water resources used for drinking water supply (e.g. Dillon and Rigler, 1974a; Vollenweider and [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   Kerekes, 1982). Actually, lake eutrophication started in industrialized countries since the 1930s as it is indi- [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   cated by recent outcomes based on paleo-limnological studies. This is the case in Europe, for example for [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   Lake Bourget (France) where eutrophication appeared around 1933 (Giguet-Covex et al., 2010; Jenny et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   2013). [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   In the 1960-1980s, lake eutrophication was widely described, in particular in symposia devoted to this topic [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   like the Madison Symposium (National Academy of Sciences, 1969) or the Symposium of the American [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   Society of Limnology and Oceanography (ASLO) in 1971. Historically, the description of lake eutrophica- [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   tion was more directed to deep lakes, located in the Northern hemisphere (United States, Canada, and West- [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   ern Europe) and Australia. The general acceptation of lake trophic state classification, “oligotrophic”, [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   “mesotrophic” and “eutrophic” follows the studies of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and De- [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   velopment (OECD) in the late 1970s (Vollenweider, 1975; Vollenweider and Kerekes, 1982). [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   The main effects of lake eutrophication are an increase of phytoplankton biomass, where cyanobacteria fre- [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   quently dominate, a decrease of water transparency and a clear difference between surface layers, rich in [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   oxygen, and the deoxygenated hypolimnion (Dodds, 2006; Wetzel, 2001). Hypoxia in the hypolimnion leads 2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   to an internal phosphorus load released by the sediment, which in turn amplifies the eutrophication of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   system (Dodds, 2006). [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   Eutrophication has been one of the main threats for water quality in lakes and reservoirs since the 1960s. In [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   spite of number of research works conducted during the last five decades, eutrophication remains a major [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   concern worldwide (Smith et al., 2006). More than 40% of lakes are eutrophic and affected by algal blooms [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   (Bartram et al., 1999). The issues to be addressed still regard interactions between nutrients, principally [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   phosphorus which is generally the main cause of lake eutrophication, and the ecological functioning. New [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   manifestations of eutrophication have emerged during the last two decades (Anderson et al., 2012; Le Moal [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   et al., 2018; Pomati et al., 2017). Among the phytoplankton species, cyanobacteria have gained an increased [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   interest, due to the worldwide occurrence of their blooms. The increasing frequency of cyanobacteria blooms [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   might be linked to climate change. [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   The management and restoration solutions to control eutrophication must be supported by scientific out- [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   comes, including modelling approaches. Since the 1970s and the awareness of the negative impact of eutro- [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   phication on lake ecosystems, numerical modelling was considered as an interesting tool (Imboden, 1974; [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   Vollenweider, 1975; Vollenweider and Kerekes, 1982). Many models were developed, often to obtain pre- [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   dictive tools used as support for lake management and to define targets of nutrient loading reduction by the [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   catchment. The model key state variables are those that link primary production to nutrients, principally [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   phosphorus, nitrogen and sometimes silica (De Senerpont Domis et al., 2014; Reynolds et al., 2001). [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   Cyanobacteria growth also raises new modelling issues to represent processes such as nitrogen fixation, up- [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   take of nutrient organic forms and mobility of the cells. [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   In order to support public decision-making, issues raised by public entities, for example a ministry, can be [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   addressed in the form of a multidisciplinary critical assessment of available scientific knowledge, called col- [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   lective scientific expertise. In 2016, two French ministries respectively in charge of ecology and agriculture, [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   initiated a collective scientific expertise (Expertise Scientifique Collective, ESCo in French), bringing to- [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   gether scientific knowledge available on the eutrophication issue (Pinay et al., 2017). The objective was to [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   improve the consistency and effectiveness of public action on eutrophication control. One chapter of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   scientific expertise report was devoted to eutrophication modelling (Crave et al., 2017). This chapter in- [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   cluded five sections respectively entitled “Modelling of non-point nutrient loading in the catchment”, “Mod- 3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   elling in-stream processes”, “A focus on sediment transport modelling”, “Modelling eutrophication in coastal [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   ecosystems” and “Modelling eutrophication in lake ecosystems”. A review of marine eutrophication models [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   derived from this scientific expertise study can be found in (Ménesguen and Lacroix, 2018). The paper pre- [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   sented hereafter is based on the chapter focusing on lake eutrophication modelling and including a large re- [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   view of modelling studies. [SOURCE: Page_5]

133   2.2   Modelling objectives [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   Several purposes have led to the development of lake eutrophication models. Although eutrophication mod- [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   els are not only used for management purposes but also for research (Simonsen and Dahl-Madsen, 1978; [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   Vanhuet, 1992), we will focus in this paper mainly on the management objectives. In the keyword analysis, [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   the words “management”, “simulation”, “prediction” and “restoration” appear respectively, in 54, 49, 31 and [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   22 references of the bibliographic corpus, which represents 12%, 11%, 7% and 5% of the references. This [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   shows how lake eutrophication is a concerning problem for which practical solutions are needed. [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   In the 1970s, the OECD model (Vollenweider, 1975), that has been calibrated on a data set coming from [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   more than 200 lakes of several continents, was used to predict the eutrophication response to phosphorus [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   loading. The nutrient load limits in the USA and Canada have been established from this model. [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   Some models have been used to assess the conditions for achieving regulatory or management objectives. [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   For example, the 1D vertical model DYRESM-CAEDYM was used to compute the level of phosphorus in- [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   put reduction necessary to make the ecological status of Lake Ravn (Denmark) comply with the requirements [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   of the European Water Framework Directive (Trolle et al., 2008). [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   Models are also developed to perform numerical experiments. Recently, the sharp increase in the number of [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   reservoirs worldwide (World Commission on Dams, 2000), for irrigation, drinking water production and [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   hydroelectricity, has led researchers to investigate the effects of eutrophication on the carbon and greenhouse [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   gas cycle in these water bodies (Chanudet et al., 2012; Stepanenko et al., 2016). Models are also used to [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   predict the functioning and the impact of a reservoir that is expected to be built (Inkala et al., 1998). Con- [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   versely, some papers focus on the simulation of the impact of climate changes on lake eutrophication (Elliott [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   and Defew, 2012; Hassan et al., 1998; Schwefel et al., 2016). It includes the increase of temperature (Mark- [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   ensten et al., 2010) and the intensification of extreme climatic events such as floods (Brito et al., 2017) or [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   storms (Schwalb et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   Model of lake eutrophication are also often used to predict the evolution of variables of interest. In the short [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   term (one or two weeks), the objective can be to predict algal blooms, particularly cyanobacteria blooms. It [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   makes it possible to anticipate the measures to be taken depending on the water body use: recreational activi- [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   ties such as bathing, production of drinking water, etc. (Huang et al., 2015, 2012; W. Li et al., 2014; Reckna- [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   gel et al., 2016). Over longer time-scales, models are used to predict the response of lake ecosystems to cli- [SOURCE: Page_7]

162   sometimes with a special focus on cyanobacteria dynamics (Howard and Easthope, 2002; Markensten et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

172   2.3     Model typology [SOURCE: Page_8]

173   The scientific community early recognized the value of mathematical modelling for the study of lake ecosys- [SOURCE: Page_8]

174   tems. Researchers have developed numerous models of various types. Recent reviews of lake modelling de- [SOURCE: Page_8]

175   velopments can be found in (Bhagowati and Ahamad, 2018a; Jørgensen, 2010b). Depending on how the [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   ecosystem is represented mathematically, we can distinguish several classes of models (see for example [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   (Jørgensen and Fath, 2001; Mooij et al., 2010a)). Thus, models are generally first separated in two classes: [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   empirical models and mechanistic models (Fornarelli et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_8]

179   2.3.1    Empirical models [SOURCE: Page_8]

180   Empirical models, also called data-oriented models (Afshar et al., 2012) or data-driven models (Elshorbagy [SOURCE: Page_8]

181   and Ormsbee, 2006) are derived from the available data sets. They mainly consist in statistical relationships [SOURCE: Page_8]

182   (called transfer functions) between predictor variables and response variables of interest. The first empirical [SOURCE: Page_8]

183   models that were developed were regression models that assume linear relationships between the variables as [SOURCE: Page_8]

184   it is the case for the nutrient-chlorophyll relationships given in (Dillon and Rigler, 1974a; Reckhow, 1993). [SOURCE: Page_8]

185   Empirical models present several advantages: they require little a priori knowledge about the ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_8]

186   processes and the data themselves; few data and “simple mathematics are needed” and these models are ge- [SOURCE: Page_8]

187   neric and “give good predictions for a group of lakes” (Vanhuet, 1992). However, when applied to an indi- [SOURCE: Page_8]

188   vidual lake, empirical models often lead to large uncertainties. According to Vanhuet (1992), “empirical [SOURCE: Page_8]

189   models are often applied if only mean annually values are of interest”. However, due to the rapid progress in [SOURCE: Page_8]

190   available computational power, and to the increasing use of high frequency measurement systems based on [SOURCE: Page_8]

7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

191   in situ sensors, interest in empirical models is still growing. These models are used for different purposes, for [SOURCE: Page_9]

192   example to re-analyze statistical relationships between nutrients and chlorophyll in different contexts (Brown [SOURCE: Page_9]

193   et al., 2000; Huszar et al., 2006). New statistical approaches are also proposed to improve the quality of the [SOURCE: Page_9]

194   models. For example, quantile regression is applied instead of ordinary least square regression in ( Xu et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

195   2015) and a Bayesian approach is used to link nutrient and chlorophyll concentrations in (Freeman et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

196   2009). Bayesian model averaging has also been proposed to predict the occurrence of harmful algal blooms [SOURCE: Page_9]

197   (Hamilton et al., 2009). However, these models provide results associated with a very large level of uncer- [SOURCE: Page_9]

198   tainty and over time scales too long to study the short-term response to a decrease in nutrient inputs by the [SOURCE: Page_9]

199   watershed; they therefore lack precision for use by managers (Reynolds and Elliott, 2010). [SOURCE: Page_9]

200   Statistical models of the 1970s and 1980s have evolved significantly over the last decade towards "data- [SOURCE: Page_9]

201   driven" models. Such models need large datasets that were not available in the past, but that lake monitoring [SOURCE: Page_9]

202   systems are now able to collect. Artificial neural networks (ANNs) are now commonly used to study lake [SOURCE: Page_9]

203   eutrophication (Chen and Liu, 2014; Ieong et al., 2015; Millie et al., 2006; Rankovic et al., 2010), because of [SOURCE: Page_9]

204   “their recognized capability in predicting highly nonlinear and complex relationships” (Fornarelli et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

205   2013). However, if ANNs provide interesting prediction tools, their construction is a “highly demanding [SOURCE: Page_9]

206   task, particularly when dealing with short and noisy ecological data” (Chaves and Kojiri, 2007; Fornarelli et [SOURCE: Page_9]

207   al., 2013; Kim et al., 2012). Other data-driven models using machine learning techniques such as tree-based [SOURCE: Page_9]

208   models (Fornarelli et al., 2013; Jung et al., 2010; Peretyatko et al., 2012), support vector machines (Diaz [SOURCE: Page_9]

209   Muniz et al., 2015; Garcia-Nieto et al., 2018; Yunfeng Xu et al., 2015) or random forests (Hallstan et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

210   2012) are also proposed. For short-term (day to weeks) forecast, such data-driven models give good results, [SOURCE: Page_9]

211   but for the simulation of long-term prospective scenarios, they hardly can be used as it is necessary to in- [SOURCE: Page_9]

212   clude knowledge about the driving processes. [SOURCE: Page_9]

213   2.3.2   Mechanistic models [SOURCE: Page_9]

214   Mechanistic models, also called process-oriented models (Arhonditsis and Brett, 2004), process-based mod- [SOURCE: Page_9]

215   els (Fornarelli et al., 2013) or theoretical models (Vanhuet, 1992), are based on the a priori knowledge of the [SOURCE: Page_9]

216   driving processes of the ecosystem. Most of them consist in a set of differential or difference equations [SOURCE: Page_9]

217   which describe in details the biogeochemical processes and whose solution is obtained by numerical meth- [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

218   ods. Several review papers focusing on mechanistic models have been published (e.g. Anagnostou et al., [SOURCE: Page_10]

219   2017; Arhonditsis and Brett, 2004). [SOURCE: Page_10]

220   In the 1980s, mechanistic models often compute the steady-state concentration of variables, leading to [SOURCE: Page_10]

221   nonlinear static relationships between variables of interest (e.g. Imboden, 1974; Schnoor and O’Connor, [SOURCE: Page_10]

222   1980; Vollenweider, 1975). These models are called “input-output” models (Vollenweider, 1975) or black- [SOURCE: Page_10]

223   box models (Teruggi and Vendegna, 1986) and are sometimes considered as empirical models whereas their [SOURCE: Page_10]

224   structure is directly deduced from dynamic equations of biogeochemical processes. The data that generally [SOURCE: Page_10]

225   come from a group of lakes, are only used to identify the model parameters with statistical methods. This [SOURCE: Page_10]

226   approach has been widely used to estimate the trophic state of numerous lakes (e.g. Jones and Bachmann, [SOURCE: Page_10]

227   1976; Dillon and Rigler, 1974b). As they are based on a steady-state assumption, input-output models fail to [SOURCE: Page_10]

228   “predict the dynamic trend of eutrophication in a large reservoir with temporal variation in inflow and stor- [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   age” (Afshar et al., 2012). Moreover, these models “do not describe the biological variables which are of [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   main interest” and “do not take into account the influence of biological processes on the model parameters” [SOURCE: Page_10]

231   and some “important interactions between several phytoplankton limiting factors, light, phosphorus, nitro- [SOURCE: Page_10]

232   gen” (Simonsen and Dahl-Madsen, 1978). [SOURCE: Page_10]

233   Most mechanistic models are dynamic. Mooij et al. (2010) make the distinction between “minimal dynamic [SOURCE: Page_10]

234   models”, “complex dynamic models” and “structurally dynamic model”. Minimal dynamic models are sim- [SOURCE: Page_10]

235   ple and composed of only a few differential or differences equations; they “often generate a hypothesis about [SOURCE: Page_10]

236   a possible cause of a phenomenon that would not easily be arrived at intuitively” (Mooij et al., 2010a). On [SOURCE: Page_10]

237   the contrary, complex dynamic models (e.g. CAEDYM, PCLake, DELWAQ) include numerous variables [SOURCE: Page_10]

238   and processes and are designed to be used as a virtual reality for the simulation of the whole ecosystem. [SOURCE: Page_10]

239   They are therefore often coupled with a detailed hydrodynamic model and validated on field data. A com- [SOURCE: Page_10]

240   parison between nine complex dynamic models commonly used to study eutrophication can be found in [SOURCE: Page_10]

241   (Anagnostou et al., 2017). Structurally dynamic models have then been proposed to add flexibility in the [SOURCE: Page_10]

242   structure of the models (Jørgensen, 2015). The idea is to make the model parameters vary over time, based [SOURCE: Page_10]

243   on the expert knowledge or by optimization of a well-chosen goal function. Kalman filter (Huang et al., [SOURCE: Page_10]

244   2013) is based on similar idea: by addition of a “correction” term in the equations of the model, it enables to [SOURCE: Page_10]

245   adjust the model variables to the measurements. Finally, we can mention the particular case of the BLOOM 9 [SOURCE: Page_10]

246   model (Los, 1980) which also relies on optimization techniques: it is based on the computation of the opti- [SOURCE: Page_11]

247   mal species composition via linear programming. [SOURCE: Page_11]

248   In the above-mentioned models, the ecosystem is described at the population level. The need to take into [SOURCE: Page_11]

249   account some specific features of the populations, such as size distribution or other traits, led the researchers [SOURCE: Page_11]

250   to use the individual-based approach (DeAngelis and Mooij, 2005). As it is « too computationally demanding [SOURCE: Page_11]

251   to model all individuals separately » (Mooij et al., 2010a), derived approaches that are computationally more [SOURCE: Page_11]

252   efficient, have been proposed, such as super-individual models, physiologically structured population mod- [SOURCE: Page_11]

253   els, stage-structures biomass models, and trait-based models. Mention should be made of other approaches [SOURCE: Page_11]

254   such as energy system language (e.g. Rivera et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_11]

255   Mechanistic models have been applied to many lakes (e.g. Cui et al., 2016; Hou et al., 2004; Takkouk and [SOURCE: Page_11]

256   Casamitjana, 2016), and their contribution to the study of the spatio-temporal dynamics of ecosystems is [SOURCE: Page_11]

257   widely recognized. In particular, hydrodynamic processes are represented accurately. However with regard [SOURCE: Page_11]

258   to biological processes, because of the complexity of the process interplay, and the lack of knowledge, [SOURCE: Page_11]

259   mechanistic models still need to be improved. Mechanistic models are already complex and involve a large [SOURCE: Page_11]

260   set of parameters (sometimes more than 100) to be calibrated. As a consequence, the outcomes of simulated [SOURCE: Page_11]

261   scenarios are hampered with great uncertainty (Gal et al., 2014). The calibration itself is a difficult task be- [SOURCE: Page_11]

262   cause it necessitates the use of extensive data sets (Elshorbagy and Ormsbee, 2006) and because the simula- [SOURCE: Page_11]

263   tions, particularly when coupled with 3D hydrodynamic models, are computationally demanding. Limita- [SOURCE: Page_11]

264   tions concerning their application at the decision-making level have been emphasized (Fornarelli et al., [SOURCE: Page_11]

265   2013). Nevertheless, deterministic models remain best suited to simulate the spatio-temporal response of lake [SOURCE: Page_11]

266   ecosystems to forcing change scenarios. Moreover the increase of the computational power should facilitate [SOURCE: Page_11]

267   the use of such models in the future. [SOURCE: Page_11]

268   Note that mechanistic models are often deterministic but the stochastic nature of the events and pathways [SOURCE: Page_11]

269   leading to eutrophication should encourage the use of stochastic components (Kutas and Toth, 1985; Sarkar [SOURCE: Page_11]

270   and Chattopadhayay, 2003; Wang and Qi, 2018). [SOURCE: Page_11]

272   2.3.3   From box models to 3D models [SOURCE: Page_11]

10 [SOURCE: Page_11]

273   To reproduce accurately the ecosystem dynamics, mechanistic models are frequently coupled with a hydro- [SOURCE: Page_12]

274   dynamic model that can be quite simple, simulating only the water temperature and the regime of tempera- [SOURCE: Page_12]

275   ture stratification and mixing or more complex, simulating also the current velocities and patterns. [SOURCE: Page_12]

276   The spatial dimension of the coupled models varies from zero (box models): in that case “the water body of a [SOURCE: Page_12]

277   lake or of each box within a lake is represented as a completely mixed stirred tank reactor” (Nyholm, 1978), [SOURCE: Page_12]

278   to three : the spatial heterogeneity of the ecosystem is taken into account in all three dimensions. If a box [SOURCE: Page_12]

279   model alone cannot account for the lake spatial heterogeneity, this can be obtained using a set of intercon- [SOURCE: Page_12]

280   nected box models (Muraoka and Fukushima, 1986; Nyholm, 1978). For example, Smiths lake has been [SOURCE: Page_12]

281   divided into 11 boxes, each of them is represented as a well-mixed reactor, connected to its neighbouring [SOURCE: Page_12]

282   boxes through fluxes (Everett et al., 2007). For the vertical dimension, some models simulate the thermal [SOURCE: Page_12]

283   stratification by using 2-box models with one box for the epilimnion and one box for the hypolimnion (Frisk, [SOURCE: Page_12]

284   1982; Imboden, 1974; Imboden and Gächter, 1978; Rodriguez Reartes et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_12]

285   Vertical 1D models are also frequently used to simulate the thermal stratification and its influence on phyto- [SOURCE: Page_12]

286   plankton, nutrient and oxygen dynamics (Bell et al., 2006; Fennel et al., 2007; Trolle et al., 2008). 2D mod- [SOURCE: Page_12]

287   els, more rarely used, are developed for large but shallow systems where thermal stratification is negligible [SOURCE: Page_12]

288   (Fragoso et al., 2008; Huang et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2008). 3D models appeared in the early 2000s (Kuo [SOURCE: Page_12]

289   and Thomann, 1983), but it was in the 2010s, due to the increase of computational power and of in situ [SOURCE: Page_12]

290   measurements, that 3D models have been increasingly applied on lakes (Carraro et al., 2012; Deus et al., [SOURCE: Page_12]

291   2013; Leon et al., 2011; Schwalb et al., 2015; Soulignac et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_12]

292   The coupling of models can be a difficult task. The Framework for Aquatic Biogeochemical Models [SOURCE: Page_12]

293   (FABM) has been developed to facilitate the coupling between several hydrodynamic models and ecological [SOURCE: Page_12]

294   models (Trolle et al., 2011a). [SOURCE: Page_12]

403   2.5   Model selection [SOURCE: Page_16]

404   The previous paragraphs give an overview of the different types of models that are used for the modelling of [SOURCE: Page_16]

405   lake eutrophication. We can then wonder how to choose among these different types of models. Answering [SOURCE: Page_16]

406   to this question is not simple, but general trends can nevertheless help our choice. The selection of the model [SOURCE: Page_16]

407   type and structure will depend on the available knowledge and data, and on the modelling objective. With [SOURCE: Page_16]

408   little knowledge about the ecosystem, only empirical models can be considered. Regression models can be [SOURCE: Page_16]

409   obtained from few data and are useful to estimate mean annual values. On the other hand, data-driven models [SOURCE: Page_16]

410   based on machine learning techniques require a large amount of data and manage to make short-term predic- 15 [SOURCE: Page_16]

411   tion. On the opposite, process-based models will be considered only if sufficient knowledge about the proc- [SOURCE: Page_17]

412   esses of the ecosystem is available. Data are nevertheless also required for such models, especially for the [SOURCE: Page_17]

413   calibration of the model parameters. However, the results of process-based models can also be used for [SOURCE: Page_17]

414   qualitative studies. In that case, the model outputs are validated by confrontation with the already acquired [SOURCE: Page_17]

415   knowledge. Finally, when both knowledge and data are available, we can consider mixed approaches. For [SOURCE: Page_17]

416   example, we can use a Kalman filter technique that requires data to adjust the parameters of a process-based [SOURCE: Page_17]

417   model. Concerning the structure of the model, the variables to be represented are the variables of interest for [SOURCE: Page_17]

418   the study, namely the variables that we want to simulate and the variables on which they depend. In the same [SOURCE: Page_17]

419   way, we will represent the processes of interest and the ones that we assume to have an impact on the system [SOURCE: Page_17]

420   we intend to represent. The time-scale of the targeted simulations will also determine the processes to be [SOURCE: Page_17]

421   included.For example, it may be useless to take into account daily variations of some processes if we are [SOURCE: Page_17]

422   interested on the annual variations of the ecosystem. Finally, depending on the level of ecological description [SOURCE: Page_17]

423   (individuals, species, populations, etc.), different types of models can be considered: individual based- [SOURCE: Page_17]

424   models, population equations, meta-ecosystems. [SOURCE: Page_17]

472   3.2   Cyanobacteria dynamics [SOURCE: Page_19]

473   Cyanobacteria strongly impair lake ecological functioning. Moreover they cause significant public health [SOURCE: Page_19]

474   problems. Modelling their dynamics and the processes that control them are therefore the subject of many [SOURCE: Page_19]

475   studies (Belov and Giles, 1997; Carraro et al., 2012; Elliott, 2012; Guven and Howard, 2006; Hense and [SOURCE: Page_19]

476   Beckmann, 2006). According to many authors (e.g. Carey et al., 2012; Gkelis and Zaoutsos, 2014; New- [SOURCE: Page_19]

477   combe, 2012; O’Neil et al., 2012; Paerl and Huisman, 2008; Paerl and Paul, 2012), the ongoing climate [SOURCE: Page_19]

478   change is likely to result in an increase in global cyanobacterial biomass as well as in occurrences of blooms. [SOURCE: Page_19]

479   Guven and Howard (2006) provide a review and critical analysis of 12 main models of cyanobacteria devel- [SOURCE: Page_19]

480   opment in lake ecosystems. Two modelling approaches are discussed: deterministic models and neural net- [SOURCE: Page_19]

481   work models. These models generally focus on describing either the biological processes involved in the [SOURCE: Page_19]

482   growth of biomass or the processes involved in the motion of cells. Few people combine all the aspects that [SOURCE: Page_19]

483   affect the dynamics of cyanobacteria. [SOURCE: Page_19]

484   Migration in the water column is a characteristic process of cyanobacteria: the rate of settling or flotation in [SOURCE: Page_19]

485   the water column varies according to their density, which itself varies according to the amount of carbohy- [SOURCE: Page_19]

486   drates and volume of vacuoles in the cells. This is a very complex, non-linear process, with many feedbacks. [SOURCE: Page_19]

487   It involves the intensity of photosynthesis, which is itself a function of the intensity of light in the water col- [SOURCE: Page_19]

488   umn, and therefore of the location of cyanobacteria in the water column. In addition, the different species of [SOURCE: Page_19]

489   cyanobacteria form colonies whose morphometric characteristics affect the mobility. The models depict the [SOURCE: Page_19]

490   migration process with different levels of complexity. [SOURCE: Page_19]

491   In Kromkamp and Walsby (1990), the density change depends on two simultaneous processes: an increase [SOURCE: Page_19]

492   controlled by the light intensity received by the cells and a decrease as a function of time. The parameter [SOURCE: Page_19]

18 [SOURCE: Page_19]

493   values of the equations were obtained from experimental data of density change of a cyanobacterium species [SOURCE: Page_20]

494   (Planktothrix aghardii) as a function of time and light intensity. The results show that the size of the colonies [SOURCE: Page_20]

495   influences the migration rate but not the depth reached in the water column. The biomass vertical location [SOURCE: Page_20]

496   fluctuates according to the rise of the floating colonies and the settling of others towards the bottom. Phos- [SOURCE: Page_20]

497   phorus deficient cells migrate to greater depths than light-limited cells. [SOURCE: Page_20]

498   Howard et al. (1996) propose a cyanobacterial growth and transport model that accounts for, on the one [SOURCE: Page_20]

499   hand, the photosynthesis production, the cell density variation and the vertical migration of cells and on the [SOURCE: Page_20]

500   other hand, the turbulent mixing in the water column that also affects cyanobacteria motion. [SOURCE: Page_20]

501   Belov and Giles (1997) present a dynamic model of cyanobacteria motion based on the properties of regula- [SOURCE: Page_20]

502   tion of cell buoyancy under simplified conditions: an isothermal water column where nutrients are not limit- [SOURCE: Page_20]

503   ing factors. The main factor controlling cell mobility is the nycthemeral cycle of light. The article focuses on [SOURCE: Page_20]

527   For management applications, models are often integrated into warning systems to predict short-term phyto- [SOURCE: Page_21]

528   plankton blooms, particularly to improve operational control of cyanobacterial blooms (Shimoda and Arhon- [SOURCE: Page_21]

531   In order to predict algal blooms in Lake Taihu, the EcoTaihu model has been integrated into a Windows [SOURCE: Page_21]

532   software platform (Zhang et al., 2013). The results of the simulations generally show a good agreement with [SOURCE: Page_21]

533   the measurements. But the authors also point out that no model is able to integrate all ecological processes. [SOURCE: Page_21]

534   The forecast discrepancies are not only due to the model but also to data uncertainties and because modelling [SOURCE: Page_21]

535   results and field measurements are not directly comparable. [SOURCE: Page_21]

537   3.4    Ecosystem response to local management strategies [SOURCE: Page_21]

538   Eutrophication models can be used to study prospective scenarios and compare the impact of local manage- [SOURCE: Page_21]

539   ment alternatives. Several management scenarios of Los Molinos reservoir (Argentina) were tested with a [SOURCE: Page_21]

540   model describing the dynamics of nutrients and phytoplankton (Rodriguez Reartes et al., 2016). After cali- [SOURCE: Page_21]

541   bration and validation of the model, scenario simulation results highlighted the impact on water quality of [SOURCE: Page_21]

542   livestock husbandry practices and of insufficient wastewater treatment, especially septic tanks. According to [SOURCE: Page_21]

543   the model simulations, the combination of wastewater treatment in the reservoir direct watershed and meas- [SOURCE: Page_21]

544   ures to reduce nutrient loading (N and P) from livestock would be the most beneficial to preserve the quality [SOURCE: Page_21]

545   of the lake. [SOURCE: Page_21]

20 [SOURCE: Page_21]

546   The effects of different actions to reduce eutrophication of a lake in Sweden (reduction of nutrient inputs, [SOURCE: Page_22]

547   biomanipulation, herbicide application) were analysed with a vertical 1D biogeochemical model (Pers, [SOURCE: Page_22]

548   2005). According to the simulation results, the best effects are obtained with a nutrient reduction and bioma- [SOURCE: Page_22]

549   nipulation of the fish species. [SOURCE: Page_22]

550   The comparison of different prospective scenarios should include a reference scenario. This is not the case in [SOURCE: Page_22]

551   most of the performed modelling studies. This is due to the lack of sufficiently long-term historical data sets. [SOURCE: Page_22]

552   Moreover, it is particularly complex to achieve a reliable assessment of the environmental conditions prior to [SOURCE: Page_22]

553   the onset of eutrophication. For total phosphorus, reference values of the watershed loading can be estimated [SOURCE: Page_22]

554   by different approaches (export coefficients, morpho-edaphic indices, paleolimnological estimates). But the [SOURCE: Page_22]

555   results of these different methods have rarely been compared. A study performed on 35 sub-alpine lakes in [SOURCE: Page_22]

556   northern Italy (Salerno et al., 2014) highlighted the need of deterministic watershed models to estimate the [SOURCE: Page_22]

557   total reference phosphorus inputs. [SOURCE: Page_22]

558   An expected outcome of the prospective simulations regards the level of nutrient loading by the watershed [SOURCE: Page_22]

559   that must not be overpassed to avoid eutrophication of a lake. But even when the targets to be achieved are [SOURCE: Page_22]

560   derived from modelling results, the authors remind that these values must be considered with caution as the [SOURCE: Page_22]

561   models are not able to anticipate accurately the changes in the trophic web that would occur in response to [SOURCE: Page_22]

562   nutrient reduction (e.g. Trolle et al., 2008). [SOURCE: Page_22]

563   Modelling studies mainly focus on the advantages of the modelling approach developed and its transferabil- [SOURCE: Page_22]

564   ity to other systems. The model is generally implemented on a particular study site for which a sufficient data [SOURCE: Page_22]

565   set exists. Despite the transferability of process-based models, they must be implemented in a configuration [SOURCE: Page_22]

566   adapted to the characteristics of each lake in order to estimate the required threshold of nutrient loading [SOURCE: Page_22]

567   (Trolle et al., 2008). Moreover, due to the complexity of the ecological functioning of lake ecosystems, many [SOURCE: Page_22]

568   processes are not taken into account or are very simplified in the models (role of sediment, change of phyto- [SOURCE: Page_22]

569   plankton assemblages, mobility of certain species, etc.). When recommendations are made, the authors are [SOURCE: Page_22]

570   very cautious and they immediately nuanced the recommendations. Thus, the total phosphorus loading which [SOURCE: Page_22]

571   would allow Lake Ravn (Denmark) to achieve the objectives of good ecological status required by the Euro- [SOURCE: Page_22]

572   pean Framework Directive on Water, was estimated thanks to a vertical 1D model (Trolle et al., 2008). The [SOURCE: Page_22]

573   authors recommend a reduction of at least 50% of the total phosphorus inputs by the watershed. In parallel, 21 [SOURCE: Page_22]

574   they raise awareness on the possible cascade effects within the food web, not simulated by the model, and [SOURCE: Page_23]

575   which could lead to an unpredictable evolution of the ecosystem. [SOURCE: Page_23]

577   3.5   Ecosystem response to global changes [SOURCE: Page_23]

578   A common application of models is the study of expected effect of global changes, especially climate [SOURCE: Page_23]

579   change. Climate change will impact precipitation patterns, wind speeds, solar radiation, and air temperature [SOURCE: Page_23]

580   (e.g. Arnell and Gosling, 2013; Beniston et al., 2007; European Environment Agency, 2012). Most models [SOURCE: Page_23]

581   simulate the effect of climate change on different aspects of lake functioning related to eutrophication: [SOURCE: Page_23]

582   mainly oxygen concentrations (Fang and Stefan, 1997, 2009), phytoplankton growth (Hassan et al., 1998) [SOURCE: Page_23]

583   and the dominance of cyanobacteria (e.g. Elliott, 2012). The consideration of climate change is mainly based [SOURCE: Page_23]

584   on two approaches: (1) the use of outputs from regional climate models treated by downscaling techniques to [SOURCE: Page_23]

585   obtain meteorological forcing at the studied lake level; or (2) a meteorological forcing sensitivity analysis [SOURCE: Page_23]

586   where actual measured meteorological data are modified by a range of factors. [SOURCE: Page_23]

587   Very often the analysis of the influence of climate change, in particular of global warming, focuses on [SOURCE: Page_23]

588   cyanobacteria. Global warming is supposed to have a positive effect on the increase of cyanobacteria bio- [SOURCE: Page_23]

589   mass (e.g. Carey et al., 2012; Newcombe, 2012; Paerl and Paul, 2012; Posch et al., 2012; Wagner and [SOURCE: Page_23]

590   Adrian, 2009). Nevertheless, very few reliable predictive simulations of the dominance of cyanobacteria [SOURCE: Page_23]

591   have still been performed (Elliott, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_23]

592   In Lake Windermere (Great Britain), the effect of an increase in water temperature and phosphorus loading [SOURCE: Page_23]

593   on phytoplankton has been studied with the PROTECH model (Elliott, 2011). The results show an earlier [SOURCE: Page_23]

594   spring peak of diatoms (2-3 days/°C). The biomass of diatoms, which is controlled mainly by light is little [SOURCE: Page_23]

595   changed. On the other hand, the biomass of cyanobacteria increases with the increase of the two factors stud- [SOURCE: Page_23]

596   ied. These two factors act in synergy, the predominant influence being due to phosphorus inputs. The domi- [SOURCE: Page_23]

597   nance of cyanobacteria is enhanced in late summer. Temperature impacts mainly succession and seasonal [SOURCE: Page_23]

598   phytoplankton community whereas nutrient inputs affect the amount of produced biomass. [SOURCE: Page_23]

599   Howard and Easthope (2002) used the predictions of the HADCM$_2$ climate model over a period of 90 years, [SOURCE: Page_23]

600   until the end of the 21st century, to simulate the evolution of the growth of the cyanobacterium Microcystis [SOURCE: Page_23]

22 [SOURCE: Page_23]

601   in Farmoor Lake (Great Britain). The main meteorological variables used are the monthly averages of wind [SOURCE: Page_24]

602   speed, cloud cover and solar radiation. The results do not show an overall increase in the annual biomass of [SOURCE: Page_24]

603   cyanobacteria. On the other hand, the growth period of cyanobacteria would be longer with higher biomass [SOURCE: Page_24]

604   than currently in spring (May) and autumn (September). These results only quantify the direct effect of cli- [SOURCE: Page_24]

605   mate change because the long-term change in rainfall on loading from the watershed is not taken into ac- [SOURCE: Page_24]

606   count. [SOURCE: Page_24]

607   The effect of global warming predicted until the end of the 21st century has been studied in three lakes in [SOURCE: Page_24]

608   New Zealand (Trolle et al., 2011b). A regional climate model based on the IPCC A$_2$ scenario predicts an [SOURCE: Page_24]

609   average annual increase in air temperature of about 2.5 ° C in the 2100s in the studied lake region. The future [SOURCE: Page_24]

610   climate conditions are described in a very simplified way; only the change of air temperature is taken into [SOURCE: Page_24]

611   account. The average annual increase in air temperature forecast for the year 2100 has been added to the [SOURCE: Page_24]

612   average daily temperatures over the period 2002-2007. The morphometric and trophic characteristics of the [SOURCE: Page_24]

613   three modelled lakes are different. The first is quite deep (33.5m) and oligo-mesotrophic, the second shallow [SOURCE: Page_24]

614   (13.5m) and eutrophic and the third very shallow (2.5m) and very eutrophic. Cyanobacteria are present only [SOURCE: Page_24]

615   in the second lake. Modelling results show an increase in phytoplankton biomass in summer and an increase [SOURCE: Page_24]

616   in the dominance of cyanobacteria in the second lake. The average annual biomass increases in the oligo- [SOURCE: Page_24]

617   mesotrophic and eutrophic lakes but decreases in the highly eutrophic lake. The phytoplankton biomass de- [SOURCE: Page_24]

618   creases in winter and spring, especially diatoms. [SOURCE: Page_24]

619   A recent analysis of ten modelling studies (Elliott, 2012) on the impact of climate change on cyanobacteria [SOURCE: Page_24]

620   has shown an increase in the relative abundance of cyanobacteria with an increase in water temperature, an [SOURCE: Page_24]

621   increase in the residence time of the water and an increase in nutrient loading by the catchment. [SOURCE: Page_24]

622   The direct effects of climate change mainly relate to phenology of phytoplankton dynamics but little to the [SOURCE: Page_24]

623   annual amount of total biomass. These changes in phytoplankton succession may result in nitrogen limitation [SOURCE: Page_24]

624   in some lakes, and thus favour the dominance of nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria. In addition, warmer water in [SOURCE: Page_24]

625   spring can increase nutrient uptake by the phytoplankton community, which in some lakes causes nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_24]

626   limitation later in the year, potentially favourable for some nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria. The increase in the [SOURCE: Page_24]

627   relative abundance of cyanobacteria may also impact the higher levels of the ecosystem food web due to the [SOURCE: Page_24]

628   low grazing of cyanobacteria by zooplankton. 23 [SOURCE: Page_24]

629   3.6      Coupled models of lake and watershed [SOURCE: Page_25]

630   Coupling watershed modelling and lake response modelling is a key issue for an efficient use of models in [SOURCE: Page_25]

631   eutrophication control. But to our knowledge, few research results are still available. [SOURCE: Page_25]

632   Most often, the coupling between watershed model and lake model is not effective. A hydrological model of [SOURCE: Page_25]

633   Lake Cayuga watershed (USA) has provided estimates of nutrient loading by lake tributaries (Haith et al., [SOURCE: Page_25]

634   2012). But this watershed model is not coupled with a model of the lake itself. This is also the case for Lake [SOURCE: Page_25]

635   Gucheng watershed in China (Huang et al., 2009) or for Lake Tahoe watershed (USA) (Riverson et al., [SOURCE: Page_25]

636   2013). [SOURCE: Page_25]

637   Since the 2000s, integrated modelling of the watershed and the lake has been undertaken. Lake watershed [SOURCE: Page_25]

638   modelling is conducted with the objective of providing the lake model with input data to then simulate the [SOURCE: Page_25]

639   lake response to watershed loadings. But the limits of these coupled models come from the cumulative un- [SOURCE: Page_25]

640   certainties of the hydrological model and the lake model. If the inflows are fairly well simulated, the main [SOURCE: Page_25]

641   uncertainties are due to errors in computing nutrient concentrations in the lake tributaries (Liu et al., 2006; [SOURCE: Page_25]

642   Silva et al., 2016; van Puijenbroek et al., 2004). [SOURCE: Page_25]

643   Carraro et al. (2012b) propose for Lake Pusiano (Northern Italy) a coupled model of loading by the water- [SOURCE: Page_25]

644   shed (SWAT model) and a deterministic model of the lake response (DYRESM-CAEDYM model). The [SOURCE: Page_25]

645   results obtained for different scenarios are discussed and compared, but no quantified recommendation for a [SOURCE: Page_25]

646   percentage of reduction in loadings or concentrations to be achieved is given. [SOURCE: Page_25]

647   Very few studies have focused on coupling climate, watershed and lake models. To study phytoplankton [SOURCE: Page_25]

648   biomass in a large shallow lake, Lake Malaren (Sweden), a regional climate model, a hydrological model, [SOURCE: Page_25]

649   and a lake model were used in sequence (Markensten et al., 2010). In this lake, warming increases the dura- [SOURCE: Page_25]

650   tion of phytoplankton production, slightly increases total biomass, and modifies the phytoplankton composi- [SOURCE: Page_25]

651   tion to the advantage of nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria. According to the authors, the change in the hydro- [SOURCE: Page_25]

652   logical regime and therefore the nutrient loading to the lake drives the dominance of cyanobacteria. [SOURCE: Page_25]

653   The aquatic continuum, from headwaters to the ocean, is at the heart of current research on biogeochemical [SOURCE: Page_25]

654   processes in inland waters. This is reflected in the topic of the special issue of Limnology and Oceanography, [SOURCE: Page_25]

655   a reference journal in the field, entitled "Headwaters to oceans: ecological and biogeochemical contrasts [SOURCE: Page_25]

24 [SOURCE: Page_25]

656   across the aquatic continuum" (Xenopoulos et al., 2017). In this area, progress on knowledge integration is [SOURCE: Page_26]

657   rapid and modelling of the continuum, still in its infancy, should make significant progress in the coming [SOURCE: Page_26]

658   years. [SOURCE: Page_26]

660   4    Discussion and perspectives [SOURCE: Page_26]

661   The development of lake eutrophication models is still a growing field in limnology. However several au- [SOURCE: Page_26]

662   thors complain that the performances of the biological models remain relatively low (Mooij et al., 2010; [SOURCE: Page_26]

663   Shimoda and Arhonditsis, 2016). According to Trolle et al., (2011a), the problem is not the “lack of knowl- [SOURCE: Page_26]

664   edge about ecosystem functioning, but rather the limited extent of open communication, inadequate collabo- [SOURCE: Page_26]

665   ration and lack of suitable structure to support the aquatic scientific modelling community”. In their paper, [SOURCE: Page_26]

666   these authors argue for the adoption of good practices for developing, using and sharing models effectively [SOURCE: Page_26]

667   and correctly, which should help improving the models. Mooij et al. (2010) identified two challenges for [SOURCE: Page_26]

668   making further progress in lake ecosystem modelling: to avoid developing more models largely following [SOURCE: Page_26]

669   the concept of others (‘reinventing the wheel’) and to avoid focusing on only one type of model, while ignor- [SOURCE: Page_26]

670   ing new and diverse approaches that have become available (‘having tunnel vision’)” (Mooij et al., 2010a). [SOURCE: Page_26]

671   This suggests to merge different modelling approaches, such as 1D hydrodynamic-ecological model and [SOURCE: Page_26]

672   super individual-based model (Makler-Pick et al., 2011), or machine learning models and physical dynamical [SOURCE: Page_26]

673   models (Crisci et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_26]

674   To improve the models, the complexity of the predictive ecosystem models should also be increased, because [SOURCE: Page_26]

675   “there is a lot of knowledge that we are not using” (Hellweger, 2017). The most striking example is the al- [SOURCE: Page_26]

676   most systematic use of the Monod function for representing the growth of a phytoplankton population [SOURCE: Page_26]

677   whereas the use of internal (vs. external) nutrient quota (Droop, 1968) could be represented (Hellweger, [SOURCE: Page_26]

678   2017). Another example is the use of Michaelis-Menten kinetics for nutrient uptake by phytoplankton, which [SOURCE: Page_26]

679   does not enable to represent the variable stoichiometry of biomass and of nutrient uptake (Flynn, 2010, 2005; [SOURCE: Page_26]

680   Smith et al., 2014) whereas size- and trait-based kinetics give promising results (Fiksen et al., 2013; Smith et [SOURCE: Page_26]

681   al., 2014). In the same way, the process of mineralization of the organic matter by bacteria, which has been [SOURCE: Page_26]

682   proved to be important for the cyanobacteria dynamics, is often represented as a simple chemical reaction [SOURCE: Page_26]

683   with a rate either constant or dependent on the bacteria concentration at best (Li et al., 2014). Xu et al. (2015) 25 [SOURCE: Page_26]

684   also claim that the use of more advanced statistical methods, such a quantile regression, can improve the [SOURCE: Page_27]

685   models of lake eutrophication. [SOURCE: Page_27]

686   Even if the relationship between complexity and uncertainty of models is not so clear (Håkanson, 1995; [SOURCE: Page_27]

687   Hellweger, 2017; Jørgensen, 1990), it is obvious that the outcomes of simulated scenarios are limited by [SOURCE: Page_27]

688   great uncertainty (Arhonditsis and Brett, 2004). Some authors have attempted to quantify model errors due to [SOURCE: Page_27]

689   uncertainty (Bennett et al., 2013; Hongping and Yong, 2003; Reckhow, 1994; Scavia et al., 1981; Snodgrass, [SOURCE: Page_27]

690   1985; Stow et al., 1997; Van Straten and Keesman, 1991), but few models include thorough uncertainty [SOURCE: Page_27]

691   analyses (Hongping and Yong, 2003). [SOURCE: Page_27]

692   This uncertainty partly comes from the large set of parameters (sometimes more than 100) to be calibrated. [SOURCE: Page_27]

693   To quantify the effect of parameter uncertainties on the model outputs, sensitivity analysis can be conducted [SOURCE: Page_27]

694   (Arhonditsis et al., 2007; Brett et al., 2016; Janse et al., 2010; Yi et al., 2016). However, as for general uncer- [SOURCE: Page_27]

695   tainty analysis, sensitivity analysis is not always performed. Two articles (Arhonditsis and Brett, 2004; Shi- [SOURCE: Page_27]

696   moda and Arhonditsis, 2016) have investigated whether the conventional methodological procedures for [SOURCE: Page_27]

697   sensitivity analysis, calibration and validation, are reported in the papers or not. They show that only half of [SOURCE: Page_27]

698   the papers present sensitivity analysis results and that “manual” or “trial and error” calibration is the most [SOURCE: Page_27]

699   applied calibration procedure. Very few papers use automatic calibration procedures that are either based on [SOURCE: Page_27]

700   optimization or on Monte Carlo and Bayesian inference. Moreover these papers only deal with 0D or 1D [SOURCE: Page_27]

701   models. [SOURCE: Page_27]

702   Another part of output uncertainty comes from the model structure itself. To reduce it, several “ensemble [SOURCE: Page_27]

703   approaches” have been proposed, similar to those used for weather forecasts. In (Gal et al., 2014; Nielsen et [SOURCE: Page_27]

704   al., 2014) an ensemble of model runs obtained with several “equivalent” set of parameters are used to im- [SOURCE: Page_27]

705   prove the model predictive power. In (Trolle et al., 2014), the same scenario is simulated using an ensemble [SOURCE: Page_27]

706   of models. Bayesian model averaging (BMA) can also be used to improve the predictive performance of the [SOURCE: Page_27]

707   models (Hamilton et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_27]

708   Another effective way is to conduct cross-comparisons of similar models on the same study sites. This type [SOURCE: Page_27]

709   of comparative studies has been performed recently for 1D models (Bruce et al., 2018; Perroud et al., 2009; [SOURCE: Page_27]

710   Stepanenko et al., 2012, 2010). To our knowledge very few attempts have been made for hydrodynamic- [SOURCE: Page_27]

711   ecological models (Trolle et al., 2014) and none for 3D models. 26 [SOURCE: Page_27]

712   Nevertheless, to perform such studies, portability of the model software is required. This type of concern is [SOURCE: Page_28]

713   just starting to be addressed by the modeller community. A Fortran-based Framework for Aquatic Biogeo- [SOURCE: Page_28]

714   chemical Models (FABM - Bruggeman and Bolding, 2014; Trolle et al., 2011) enables to easily couple hy- [SOURCE: Page_28]

715   drodynamic models with ecological models, whereas the project “Framework for Interoperable Freshwater [SOURCE: Page_28]

716   Models” (FIFM) has investigated flexible computer-based frameworks for integrating freshwater models [SOURCE: Page_28]

717   (Elliott et al., 2014). The Database Approach To Modelling (DATM -Mooij et al., 2014) enables to auto- [SOURCE: Page_28]

718   matically implement the same model under different frameworks. In order to facilitate the comparison of [SOURCE: Page_28]

719   models or the application of one model on several lakes, it is also necessary to share the codes and the data [SOURCE: Page_28]

720   sets (Mooij et al., 2010a). Just as importantly, the datasets must be of good quality and enriched with meta- [SOURCE: Page_28]

721   information (such as the type of sensor used and their accuracy). This is mandatory for the good interpreta- [SOURCE: Page_28]

722   tion and use of the data (Mooij et al., 2010a). The use of high-frequency sensors (Brentrup et al., 2016; Car- [SOURCE: Page_28]

723   raro et al., 2012; Soulignac et al., 2017), as well as satellite images (Curtarelli et al., 2015; Dash et al., 2011; [SOURCE: Page_28]

724   Tyler et al., 2006; Yacobi, 2006), will also enrich significantly the data sets. Using such enriched and good- [SOURCE: Page_28]

725   quality data sets can improve the model performances. Indeed, the more data we use for calibration, com- [SOURCE: Page_28]

726   parison with model outputs or to feed machine learning models, the greater the accuracy of the model will [SOURCE: Page_28]

727   be. [SOURCE: Page_28]

729   5     Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_28]

730   Early lake eutrophication models in the 1970s were empirical models linking in-lake phosphorus and chlo- [SOURCE: Page_28]

731   rophyll concentrations, as well as models of watershed nutrient loading and in-lake chlorophyll concentra- [SOURCE: Page_28]

732   tion. These models, very easy to implement, were useful for providing targets of nutrient and chlorophyll [SOURCE: Page_28]

733   concentrations, required for reducing or controlling lake eutrophication. Dynamic deterministic models have [SOURCE: Page_28]

734   been then developed, whose characteristics are very diverse as regards the spatial dimension (well-mixed [SOURCE: Page_28]

735   system, 1D vertical to 3D models), the sediment exchange with the water column, the complexity of ecologi- [SOURCE: Page_28]

736   cal functioning and the hydrodynamics. Most of these models represent the pelagic zone of the lake. The [SOURCE: Page_28]

737   most represented state variables include phytoplankton, considered as a single group or divided into main [SOURCE: Page_28]

738   groups, nutrients (phosphorus and nitrogen) and oxygen. Among the phytoplankton groups, cyanobacteria [SOURCE: Page_28]

739   have been increasingly represented since the mid-2000s. The dynamics of macrophytes and their possible 27 [SOURCE: Page_28]

740   competition with phytoplankton are accounted for in some rather recent models. One notable development [SOURCE: Page_29]

741   over the past decade has been the modelling of phytoplankton dynamics based on the functional traits of [SOURCE: Page_29]

742   dominant species. The objectives of the modelling studies are frequently related to the assessment of the [SOURCE: Page_29]

743   expected mid-and long-term evolution of lake eutrophication under the influence of global changes (nutri- [SOURCE: Page_29]

744   ents, urbanization, climate…). Among these changes, the effects of global warming are the most frequently [SOURCE: Page_29]

745   addressed. At these time scales, a main barrier that models have to face, regards the evolution of the phyto- [SOURCE: Page_29]

746   plankton succession and its cascading effects on lake ecological functioning. Recent evolutions show a [SOURCE: Page_29]

747   strong coupling between modelling and acquisition of new types of experimental data (remote sensing, high [SOURCE: Page_29]

748   frequency field sensors, biology molecular data…). A strong trend seen in papers published in the last dec- [SOURCE: Page_29]

749   ade is the increasing number of application of 3D models coupled with satellite images of phytoplankton [SOURCE: Page_29]

750   biomass spatial distribution. [SOURCE: Page_29]
