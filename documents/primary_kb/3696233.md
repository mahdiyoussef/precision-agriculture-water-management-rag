1   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

2          The clogging of drippers due to the development of biofilms reduces the benefits and [SOURCE: Page_2]

3   is an obstacle to the implementation of drip irrigation technology in a reclaimed water [SOURCE: Page_2]

4   context. The narrow section and labyrinth geometry of the dripper channel results the [SOURCE: Page_2]

5   development of a heterogeneous flow behaviours with the vortex zones which it enhance the [SOURCE: Page_2]

6   fouling mechanisms. The objective of this study was to analyse the influence of the three [SOURCE: Page_2]

7   dripper types, defined by their geometric and hydraulic parameters, fed with reclaimed [SOURCE: Page_2]

8   wastewater, on the biofouling kinetics and the bacterial communities. Using optical coherence [SOURCE: Page_2]

9   tomography, we demonstrated that the inlet of the drippers (mainly the first baffle) and vortex [SOURCE: Page_2]

10   zones are the most sensitive area for biofouling. Drippers with the lowest Reynolds number [SOURCE: Page_2]

11   and average cross-section velocity v (1 l.h-1) were the most sensible to biofouling, even if [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   detachment events seemed more frequent in this dripper type. Therefore, dripper flow path [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   with larger v should be consider to improve the anti-clogging performance. In addition, the [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   dripper type and the geometry of the flow path influenced the structure of the bacterial [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   communities from dripper biofilms. Relative abundancy of filamentous bacteria belonging to [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   Chloroflexi phylum was higher in 1 l.h-1 drippers, which presented a higher level of [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   biofouling. However, further research on the role of this phylum in dripper biofouling is [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   required. [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   Keywords: Drip irrigation; biofilm; flow behaviours; Optical Coherence Tomography; [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   High-throughput DNA sequencing [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   The scarcity of water resources is driving the development of water-saving irrigation [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   techniques that enable integrated management of water resources. The reuse of reclaimed [SOURCE: Page_2]

1 [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   wastewater (RWW) is an appropriate way to alleviate the problem of scarce water resources [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   especially in arid and semi-arid regions (Worako, 2015). Using RWW has several advantages [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   including reducing pressure on freshwater resources and on the need for nutrients (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   nitrogen, phosphorus) for plant growth (Lazarova and Bahri, 2005) and can improve crop [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   yields (Wang et al., 2013). Drip irrigation is the most water efficient (up to >90%) and safest [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   technique for irrigation using RWW (Lamm et al., 2007). Drippers are usually composed of a [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   labyrinth-channel and an outlet basin compartment. The geometry of the channel promotes [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   the dissipation of the turbulent kinetic energy due to the zigzag flow path and allows water to [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   be delivered in drip form in an optimum quantity for crop growth. However, the labyrinth [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   channel is narrow (cross-section of around 1 mm) (Zhang et al., 2010) and thus sensitive to [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   clogging (Liu and Huang, 2009; Niu et al., 2013) which reduces drip irrigation advantages by [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   reducing irrigation uniformity (Dosoretz et al., 2010) and by increasing maintenance (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   cleaning, replacing irrigation lines). [SOURCE: Page_3]

37          The process leading to clogging of the drippers is complex and the phenomenon is [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   influenced by several parameters including water quality (concentration of suspended solids [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   and chemical composition) (Lamm et al., 2007; Oliveira et al., 2017), the geometry of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   dripper (Li et al., 2019; Wei, 2011) as well as biological processes (Katz et al., 2014), the [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   latter are the most difficult to control when RWW is used (Lamm et al., 2007). Moreover, [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   biofilms in distribution networks can harbour contaminants and may impact the quality of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   water released to agroecosystems (Jjemba et al., 2010). Therefore, identifying and [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   understanding the mechanisms leading to biofilm development is necessary to optimize [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   control strategies. Previous studies have shown that flow velocity has an impact on the growth [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   of biofilm in both drip-irrigation pipes (Li et al., 2012; Mahfoud et al., 2009) and in drippers [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   (Gamri et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

48          Previous studies have been carried out on the effect of hydrodynamics on the [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   development of biofilm in straight pipe systems. Thus, an increase in flow rate or shear stress [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   tends to promote the development of a thin and dense biofilm in the pipe wall (Lehtola et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   2006; Percival et al., 1999). However, the flow behaviour in a dripper is more complex and [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   heterogeneous than in pipes, where the flow is simplified, due to the narrow labyrinth flow [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   path. This milli-labyrinth channel induces non-isotropic conditions by the development of [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   turbulent regime with a main high velocity flow and vortex zones in the channel corners (Al- [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   Muhammad et al., 2019, 2016; Wei et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2007). Qian et al., (2017) [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   studied the development of fouling in a milli-fluidic system fed with reclaimed wastewater [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   using the Optical coherence tomography (OCT).OCT method allows to monitor biofilm [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   formation non-invasively at a mesoscale (µm to mm range) and without staining (Derlon et [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   al., 2012; Dreszer et al., 2014; Qian et al., 2017; Wagner et al., 2010; West et al., 2016). They [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   reported that fouling occurs mainly in inlet channel regions and in low velocity vortices areas [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   as found by Ait-Mouheb et al., (2018). [SOURCE: Page_4]

62          Flow behaviour not only influences the development of biofilms but also the microbial [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   composition of the biofilms. Previous research on biofilms has shown that hydrodynamic [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   conditions can drive the microbial community of the biofilms (Besemer, 2015; Hou et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   2020; Rickard et al., 2004). Rochex et al., (2008) shown that increased shear stress reduced [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   bacterial diversity and slow down biofilm maturation and tend to maintain a young biofilm in [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   a Taylor-Couette reactor. However, in the context of the use of RWW, few authors have [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   examined the influence of dripper parameters (i.e. flow rate, cross section, and geometry of [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   the milli-channel) on microbial composition. Previous studies have described the microbial [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   communities present in biofilms of different drippers by analysing phospholipid-derived fatty [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   acids (PLFAs) (Yan et al., 2010, 2009; Zhou et al., 2017) and indicated that dripper flow rate [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   influenced the PLFAs distribution. These authors showed that three to seven types of PLFAs [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   associated with Gram-negative bacteria were common in biofilms inside drippers, suggesting [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   a significant role for these bacteria in biofilm development. However, in order to develop [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   effective biofilm control strategies, deeper community characterization is required. Recent [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   study used high-throughput sequencing to describe the bacterial communities present in [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   dripper biofilms to improve knowledge on fouling and to identify the microorganisms [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   responsible for fouling (Xiao et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_5]

79          The objectives of the present study were to determine the effect of 3 commercial [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   drippers with different discharges (1) on biofouling in the dripper channel and (2) on the [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   bacterial communities in biofilms formed in drip irrigation systems fed by RWW. A non- [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   destructive microscopic time-monitoring observation system was developed to study biofilms [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   in commercial drippers with different flow rates (1, 2 and 4 l.h-1) and known cross-sections. [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   The combined use of the Optical Coherence Tomography method and high-throughput [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   sequencing made it possible to monitor the development of the biofilm under different [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   hydraulic conditions while assessing the impact of these conditions on microbial structure. [SOURCE: Page_5]
