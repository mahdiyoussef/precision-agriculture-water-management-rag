83      The works on ontologies have increased recently not only in computer science [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   but also in various domains, including agri-food related domains. This is driven [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   by the need to communicate and share knowledge with common understanding. [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   2.1. Scientific data organization and integration [SOURCE: Page_5]

89      Over the recent years researchers have faced significant problems to manage [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   scientific data due to their increasing volume and complexities. This causes [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   researchers to spend quite a bit of time to manage and integrate scientific data [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   rather than to directly focus on their analysis [7] /* BRIGITTE: AJOUT REF [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   PHENOME */. Scientific data are generally collected from measurements di- [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   rectly linked to real-world phenomena [8]. Within the agri-food domain, cross- [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   disciplinary scientific data are required to explore complex and temporal aspects [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   of food quality and the impact of practices and operations. [SOURCE: Page_5]

97      The need for a more adaptable mechanism to organize scientific data has [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   been addressed in the literature, both in non-ontological based approaches such [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   as, LabKey Server [9] and SciPort [10]; and in ontological based approaches [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   such as proposed in [11], [7], [12], [3], [13] and [14]. Some of these approaches [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   are targeted to specific scientific domains while the others are developed to be [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   more generic and extensible. The non-ontological approach mostly relies on data [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   models, such as database or XML schema, where attributes and relationships [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   of domain concepts are captured in standardized structures. The ontological [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   approach has some additional advantages in terms of data interoperability and [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   knowledge reasoning [15, 16, 17, 18]. [SOURCE: Page_6]

107      To the best of our knowledge, we did not find an ontological approach to [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   represent scientific experiments as well as observational data which fulfill all our [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   needs. An ontology of scientific experiments, the EXPO [19], has been proposed [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   to formalize the generic concepts of experimental design, methodology and result [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   representation. However, this ontology does not provide a clear explanation [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   about scientific data representation. Neither does this ontology describe how to [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   manage a set of experiments in which several interrelated experiments have to [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   be conducted in a given order. The Extensible Observation Ontology (OBOE) [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   has been developed to serve as a formal and generic conceptual framework for [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   describing the semantics of observational data sets (i.e., data sets consisting [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   of observations and measurements) [7]. The basic concepts of the observational [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   model consists of five classes and six properties [3]. This ontology can be applied [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   to various types of observations. Nevertheless, it is more suited to representing [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   scientific data which are generated from measurement by sensors. It does not [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   provide other types of observational data such as expert judgements or results [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   from a calculation procedure. [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   2.2. Agri-food ontologies [SOURCE: Page_6]

124      In order to fulfill the needs of representing complex knowledge, sharing [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   common understanding within or across scientific domains and reusing domain [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   knowledge in agri-food sectors, some ontologies have been proposed, either very [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   general or more specific. Some researchers focused on building ontologies to con- [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   ceptually model agricultural practices related to crop productions, such as, hilly [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   citrus production ontology [20], precision agriculture ontology [21], crop-pest [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   ontology [22] and potato ontology [23]. Meanwhile, some works concentrating [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   on food taxonomy have also been carried out, such as FOODS (Food-Oriented [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   Ontology-Driven System) [24] and wine classification [25]. [SOURCE: Page_7]

133      The valid definition of concepts and a complete taxonomy for agricultural [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   practices and food processing are important to model agri-food experiments. [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   Most of the above ontologies refer to the terms or concepts listed in AGROVOC. [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   AGROVOC is a multi-lingual vocabulary developed by the Food and Agricul- [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   ture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations and covers divers areas includ- [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   ing food, nutrition, agriculture, fisheries, forestry, environment etc [26]. Some [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   ontologies extend these concepts in order to accommodate their data sources [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   according to the target domains, such as shown in [20],[22], and [23]. [SOURCE: Page_7]

141      Another part of the agri-food domain that recently attracted the attention [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   of researchers is related to food traceability. It is driven by a growing interest [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   in developing systems for the food supply chain. Some essential works in this [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   domain have been carried out, such as The Food Track and Trace Ontology [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   (FTTO) [27] and TraceALL [28]. Even though the FTTO is dedicated to food [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   traceability management, this ontology provides representative food concepts [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   that also become valuable resources for food processing experiments. This on- [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   tology includes four core concepts: actor, food product, process and service [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   product. [SOURCE: Page_7]

150      The ontologies mentioned above have proven useful in their own domain. [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   However those ontologies mostly cover one sub-domain, for example only agricul- [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   tural practices or only food processing. The question of data integration across [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   complementary sub-domains often has not been discussed yet in the Agri-food [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   3. The global Agri-food experiment ontology design approach [SOURCE: Page_8]

156      In this section, we explain how we built an ontology network (AFEO), start- [SOURCE: Page_8]

157   ing from two complementary existing ontologies (AEO and OFPE), in order [SOURCE: Page_8]

158   to integrate heterogeneous data sources, both from field experiments and from [SOURCE: Page_8]

159   food processing processes. To design AFEO ontology, we have followed the [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   NeOn methodology [6]. NeOn provides methodological support for constructing [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   an ontology network (AFEO in our case), when different pre-existing resources [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   must be re-engineered in order to be reused for a new purpose. NeOn per- [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   mitted us to design iteratively the AFEO ontology network based on some of [SOURCE: Page_8]

164   the nine scenarios proposed in the methodology which cover commonly occur- [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   ring situations. The section is divided into three parts. The first part defines [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   the requirements associated with the desired result, the AFEO ontology net- [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   work. The second part presents the scope of both selected ontology resources [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   (AEO and OFPE) as inputs of the ontology integration process. The third part [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   describes the ontological transformations used to integrate AEO and OFPE on- [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   tologies into AFEO in order to fulfill the requirements. Main OWL 2 (Web [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   Ontology Language, second edition) ontological constructs used in this paper [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   are presented in [29]. [SOURCE: Page_8]
