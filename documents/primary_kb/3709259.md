21                                              Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   Identifying locally adapted and adopted efficient practices can be a step towards the [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   agroecological transition of irrigated plains of the Southern Mediterranean region. However, [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   these types of practices - often little known - are drowned within a wide range of practices. [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   The objective of this study was, first, to elaborate a method to describe this diversity in a [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   semi-arid irrigated landscape, at both the plot and farm scales; second, to show the main [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   characteristics of these types of crop management; and third, to question their sustainability [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   according to agroecological principles. To do so, this paper focused on Southern [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   Mediterranean irrigated cropping systems as i) studies on management routes are scarce with [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   regard to this region; ii) irrigated landscapes are known for their high level of natural, [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   technical and financial constraints, often favoring more intensive farming practices regarding [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   chemical inputs. A series of semi-directive interviews were conducted in order to assess [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   farmers’ management of chili-pepper-based (Capsicum annuum) cropping systems, a [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   widespread crop in the Merguellil plain, Central Tunisia. The "Typ-iti” method, combining [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   multivariate analysis, clustering and association rules, was used to characterize the diversity [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   of technical management routes (TMR) of these systems. The environmental sustainability [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   was qualitatively accessed by classifying the clusters of TMRs obtained according to their [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   potential impact on natural resources. Then, these management routes were qualitatively [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   associated to some farm structural indicators, to analyze their diversity at the farm level. [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   These enabled to distinguish a gradient of farming practices and characteristics, respectively [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   at both plot and farm scales relative to environmental impacts. The study showed that some [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   agroecology-compatible TMRs coexisted with conventional TMRs for chili-pepper-based [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   cropping systems. Our method characterized three main groups: i) a group of intensive TMRs [SOURCE: Page_2]

44   regarding chemical input powered especially by tenant farmers, ii) an intermediary group iii) [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   a moderate group powered by land owners. Throughout these main groups, seven types of [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   TMRs were described. These were always comprised of both agroecology and non- [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   agroecology-compatible technical operations. This coexistence of diversely impacting [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   practices challenges agroecology in multiple ways. Fertilization management appeared as a [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   major issue in the study zone, often resulting in high applied doses. These findings could [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   allow actors in charge of agriculture to better focus their action. However, the study did not [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   take into account the outputs of the studied systems, so that their productivity and [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   environmental impacts have not yet been assessed in a quantitative manner. For future [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   studies, the paragons obtained from our analysis are typical cases that could be studied for [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   such a purpose. [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   1   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   The Southern Mediterranean region is vulnerable to climatic hazards and at a high risk of [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   severe degradation of both biodiversity and agricultural production, due to the combination of [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   climate change and conventional farming practices (Migliorini et al., 2018; MedECC, 2020). [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   New patterns of action would be required to address these issues simultaneously (Caron et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   2014), with an acute need to implement new agricultural models combining biodiversity [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   conservation, climate change adaptation and food production. Agroecology, which is the [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   production of significant amounts of food, valorizing ecological processes and ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   services and integrating them as essential factors in the improvement of practices (Gliessman, [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   1990; Altieri, 2008. Wezel et al., 2014), with its three pillars i) efficiency increase, ii) [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   substitution of inputs, iii) system redesign or diversification (Hill, 1998; Wezel et al., 2014; [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   Duru et al., 2015; Ci and Ma, 2016), could be a pathway for a more sustainable agriculture in [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   the Southern Mediterranean region (Horlings and Marsden, 2011). Agroecology suggests that [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   actions to achieve this goal must be adapted to each agrosystem and take into account local [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   conditions. [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   Although recent literature is appearing on the subject (for example, de Lattre-Gasquet et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   2017; Ameur et al., 2020), little is still known on the potential of agroecological concepts to [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   address these issues in this region of the world, in particular within irrigated areas. In the [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   North African irrigated areas, agriculture is strongly based on the use of external productive [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   factors (especially chemical inputs). Efficiency increases and input substitutions could appear [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   as a first step of action towards a more sustainable agriculture. This can start by a widespread [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   adoption of local efficient and environmental-friendly farming practices. In this paper, it is [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   hypothesized that strong social, economic and environmental constraints in irrigated areas [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   (Jamin et al., 2011) might enable a diversity of farming practices. Could more environmental- [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   friendly farming practices arise from this local diversity? In what extent are these practices [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   already present or how can they be developed in Mediterranean irrigated systems? In a first [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   step to answer these questions, the objective of this study was to i) describe the possible [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   diversity of farming practices in a typical North African irrigated area and ii) identify the [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   existence of environmental-friendly management routes, and the types of farmers [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   implementing them. The paper seeks to provide a complete and comprehensive description of [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   existing farming practices at the plot and farm-scale, enhancing their diversity, and putting [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   forward in particular potential agroecological practices, but does not undertake a quantitative [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   evaluation of their agronomic or environmental impacts. [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   Access to irrigation is a recurrent demand of smallholder farmers in North Africa, due to [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   expected productivity and income increases, compared to rainfed systems. Progress has been [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   made in this matter, with the development of individual pumping systems giving access to the [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   aquifers. The number of pumping wells has indeed increased drastically in North Africa, [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   irrigating more than 1.75 million ha in Algeria, Morocco and Tunisia (Leduc et al., 2004; [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   Kuper et al., 2017). The Merguellil plain in Central Tunisia is a typical example. In parallel, [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   public policies sought to encourage water economy and have promoted the development of [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   modern irrigated systems, resulting in a wide adoption of drip irrigation. But through [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   fertigation, the latter also facilitates increased applications of nutrients, at a certain [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   environmental cost due to pollutions (Burkhalter and Gates Timothy, 2005; Poussin et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   2008; Laib et al., 2018). However, it has been shown that the modern irrigated systems could [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   also enable a better management of nutrients and inputs (Benouniche et al., 2014; Al-Ghobari [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   and Dewidar, 2018; Sandhu et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   In addition, smallholder farmers such as those in the Merguellil plain, can design [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   environmental-friendly farming practices, more adapted to their local social, economic and [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   environmental conditions, allowing them to achieve their production goals, depending on [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   neither chemical inputs nor pesticides (Altieri, 2008). To achieve this, farmers often combine [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   different strategies as part of a usual household management scheme (Pinto-Correia et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   2017). Thus, the farmer makes choices through diverse ways of farming. So, it is fair to ask [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   whether diversity is one of the main characteristics of these farming practices at the territorial [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   scale. [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   In this paper, we elaborate a method to describe the diversity of farming practices in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   Merguellil plain – a location of multiple researches, characterized by its dynamic and diverse [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   agricultural system (Azizi et al., 2017) – at both the plot and farm scales, by analyzing local [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   practices. This can be achieved by characterizing existing technical management routes [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   (Renaud-Gentié et al., 2014; Salou et al., 2017), noted TMRs, and identifying a gradient [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   between chemically-intensive and more environmental-friendly TMRs. [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   TMRs are the logical and ordered succession of technical operations (TO) aiming to control [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   the environment and obtain a given production (Sébillotte, 1974). So far, investigations that [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   integrate all the TMRs steps to describe existing cropping systems in small-scale household [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   agriculture are scarce (Renaud-Gentié et al., 2014, Czyrnek-Delêtre et al., 2018), even the [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   more in Mediterranean irrigated areas. Multiple studies characterizing farming systems and [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   crop management practices exist (Bellon et al., 2001; Köbrich et al., 2003; Maton et al., 2005; [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   Poussin et al., 2008; Blazy et al., 2009; Ibidhi et al., 2018). These studies used either [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   statistical analysis or participatory and expert-based methods. These methods have limits as [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   they i) only identify the most relevant character of each TMR; and ii) do not describe the [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   logical links between TOs (Renaud-Gentié et al., 2014). For instance, only quantitative data [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   on farming practices were used to describe the diversity of coffee-based agroforestry systems [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   in Costa-Rica (Meylan et al., 2013). Another study, examining the variability of farm [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   structure and farming practices, highlighted both low input and intensive systems, but was [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   limited to the analysis of water and N use, pest and crop residue management (Caballero, [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   2001). [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   The paper, focused on chili-pepper (Capsicum annuum) as a cropping system model, proceeds [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   in the following way. First, it develops the approach used to assess farmers’ practices. Then, it [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   describes the technical operations, their interconnexions within TMRs, and their (non- [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   )agroecological orientation and (non-)association to farm types. Finally, it discusses this [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   diversity of farming practices in the light of co-existing irrigation, pollution and agroecology [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   stakes, and how these conditions challenge the implementation of agroecological practices in [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   Southern Mediterranean irrigated areas. [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   2.1    Study site [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   The study took place in the downstream plain of the Merguellil catchment in Central Tunisia [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   (Fig. 1). The latter, covering over 700 km^2, is part of the large and flat Kairouan alluvial plain [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   which extends almost over 3000 km^2 (Leduc et al., 2007). The climate is semi-arid with 300 [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   to 500 mm of total annual rainfall. The downstream plain is almost entirely used for [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   agricultural purposes with a combination of irrigated and, to a lesser extent, non-irrigated [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   agriculture. Most agricultural water withdrawal, consuming 80% of the total extracted water [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   per year (Poussin et al., 2008), takes place in this downstream zone where a very high density [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   of drilling is observed (Massuel et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   Cropping systems are characterized by a wide variety of crops, including olive groves and [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   fruit orchards, alone or intercropped; watermelon (Citrullus lanatu) and melon (Cucumis [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   melo), tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), chili-pepper (Capsicum annuum) and other winter and [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   summer vegetables (Poussin et al., 2008; Ameur et al., 2020). Farm structures are also diverse [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   as far as total area, level of equipment and cropping systems are concerned. The agricultural [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   sector is very dynamic, characterized by significant changes in farms’ production structures [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   and orientation during the last decade. These changes were stimulated by i) a facilitated water [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   access for initially rainfed farms; ii) the adoption of irrigated horticultural crops for some [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   farmers; iii) the reduction of annual crops, replaced by irrigated olive groves and fruits [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   orchards for others (Azizi et al., 2017). As one of the most widespread crops, present in most [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   of cropping systems, pepper-based cropping systems were used to represent the irrigated [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   horticultural crops in this area. [SOURCE: Page_8]

566   ecological-based TMRs might have social, economic and even agronomic coasts. For [SOURCE: Page_30]

567   instance, agroforestry might generate addition income but reduce cultivated area of green [SOURCE: Page_30]

568   market crops. The use of homemade organic fertilizers can contribute to reduce the [SOURCE: Page_30]

569   production costs but may require increased workload. The use local varieties most resistant to [SOURCE: Page_30]

30 [SOURCE: Page_30]

570   pest attacks can also reduce production but in return, can also lead to lower yields. In [SOURCE: Page_31]

571   addition, resource-use efficiencies highly depend on farmers’ irrigation and fertilization [SOURCE: Page_31]

572   strategies with have not been studied in detail. It thus remains to evaluate whether the design [SOURCE: Page_31]

573   of ecological-based TMRs, based on the agroecology-compatible practices found, might be [SOURCE: Page_31]

574   viable on these three axes through an agroecological assessment. More in-depth socio- [SOURCE: Page_31]

575   economic, environmental and agronomic assessments of current and future TMRs, taking into [SOURCE: Page_31]

576   account the whole production systems need to be undertaken. [SOURCE: Page_31]

577   Furthermore, our results support those observed earlier by de Lattre-Gasquet et al. (2017) [SOURCE: Page_31]

578   describing some trends in favor of agroecological uses of land in Tunisia. The findings could [SOURCE: Page_31]

579   allow stakeholders in charge of agriculture to better focus their action, to support smallholder [SOURCE: Page_31]

580   farming through agroecology. Indeed, with such a diversity of farming practices, it is [SOURCE: Page_31]

581   important to differentiate and adapt the support policies. The study can also contribute to a [SOURCE: Page_31]

582   broader field of research on regional diversity of cropping practices. The article defined the [SOURCE: Page_31]

583   key TOs related to each TMR and the types of farms implementing them. On the one hand, [SOURCE: Page_31]

584   the regional representativity of these different chili-pepper TMRs could be more easily [SOURCE: Page_31]

585   obtained by carrying rapid interviews on these key variables on a larger sample. On the other [SOURCE: Page_31]

586   hand, the method could also be applied to the other major regional crops. The findings are [SOURCE: Page_31]

587   also interesting for further research as far as previous studies of modeling or assessment of the [SOURCE: Page_31]

588   environmental impact of the practices in the study zone, did not take into account this [SOURCE: Page_31]

589   diversity (Pradeleix et al., 2012; Massuel et al., 2017; Jouini et al., 2018) or only partly [SOURCE: Page_31]

590   (Pradeleix et al., 2018). Although the study evaluated neither the agronomic nor [SOURCE: Page_31]

591   environmental performances of the TMRs, one simplistic assumption could be that the less [SOURCE: Page_31]

592   environmental-friendly TMRs mostly linked to hybrid varieties lead to higher yields. Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_31]

593   “hybrid varieties are far more productive than local varieties” according to farmers. This [SOURCE: Page_31]

594   statement has been confirmed by additional investigations (Chloé Morel, unpublished report, [SOURCE: Page_31]

31 [SOURCE: Page_31]

595   2018; Akakpo et al., unpublished data, 2019) showing that in general, yields range from less [SOURCE: Page_32]

596   than 12 T.ha-1 for the chili-pepper local varieties to up to 70 T.ha-1 for hybrid varieties. [SOURCE: Page_32]

597   Hence, cluster 1, 2, and 3, mostly associated to hybrid varieties can be considered as most [SOURCE: Page_32]

598   productive, generating in general, up to 70 T.ha-1. Inversely, cluster 4, 5, 6 and 7 can be [SOURCE: Page_32]

599   considered as much less productive, less than 12 T.ha-1, as these clusters were basically [SOURCE: Page_32]

600   associated to local varieties. However, a mix of (non)-environmental-friendly TMRs were [SOURCE: Page_32]

601   applied to both varieties. So, in terms of agro-environmental performance of the TMRs, one [SOURCE: Page_32]

602   can speculate that high productivity and low environmental impacts can be achieved in [SOURCE: Page_32]

603   systems applying environmental-friendly TMRs to hybrid varieties. Indeed, the current study, [SOURCE: Page_32]

604   based on interviews, could not precisely take into account the outputs of the studied systems, [SOURCE: Page_32]

605   in particular yields. The qualitative assessment of the impact of TMRs and the addition of [SOURCE: Page_32]

606   scores of TOs different in nature and with potentially very different types of impact on the [SOURCE: Page_32]

607   environment are a limit of the study. To have objective and realistic conclusions, this [SOURCE: Page_32]

608   assessment requires precise monitoring of TMRs, in particular of irrigation and fertilization [SOURCE: Page_32]

609   strategies. The paragons are typical cases that could be studied for such a purpose. [SOURCE: Page_32]

610   5   Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_32]

611   Returning to the hypothesis formulated at the beginning of this study, we can state that at the [SOURCE: Page_32]

612   territorial scale, farming practices in the Merguelil irrigated plain are very diversified. One of [SOURCE: Page_32]

613   the most significant findings to emerge from this study is that technical management routes of [SOURCE: Page_32]

614   chilli-pepper crop are very contrasted. In a qualitative manner, the findings enable to answer [SOURCE: Page_32]

615   the main question of the study by stating that environmental-friendly technical management [SOURCE: Page_32]

616   routes exist within the conventional agriculture known for this region. Yet, farmers seem to [SOURCE: Page_32]

617   have a common problem of fertilization management and pesticide use. The study challenges [SOURCE: Page_32]

618   research in agroecology in semi-arid irrigated landscapes. It gives a broader hypothesis for [SOURCE: Page_32]

619   further research to reassess paradigms tending to oppose irrigated agriculture to agroecology. [SOURCE: Page_32]

32 [SOURCE: Page_32]

620   Further research might investigate the cumulative environmental impact of the studied TMRs, [SOURCE: Page_33]

621   and their relationship to yield diversity. However, this poses a methodological problem, as it [SOURCE: Page_33]

622   requires costly monitoring often difficult to implement in real and poorly gauged farming [SOURCE: Page_33]

623   conditions. [SOURCE: Page_33]
