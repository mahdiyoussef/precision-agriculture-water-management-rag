## 1. INTRODUCTION

In Mediterranean countries, dams play a key role in development. Dams are generally built to [SOURCE: Page_3]

provide supplementary water that can satisfy the needs of the population, mainly including [SOURCE: Page_3]

urban, water flood control and rural irrigation demands. Dams favor the regulation of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

hydrologic regimes of rivers and retain sediments (Altinbilek 2002). Nevertheless, they may [SOURCE: Page_3]

also cause considerable water losses by evaporation, thereby increasing the salinity of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

impounded water and lowering the volume of available water needed for groundwater [SOURCE: Page_3]

recharge and alternative uses. Simultaneously, such structures, in turn, may promote the [SOURCE: Page_3]

recharge of aquifers. According to de Vries and Simmers (2002), the aquifer recharge is [SOURCE: Page_3]

achieved via two key processes: the direct infiltration of precipitation through the soil in [SOURCE: Page_3]

outcrop areas and the localized recharge arising from surface water accumulation and [SOURCE: Page_3]

sideways percolation (as is the case in this study). [SOURCE: Page_3]

Localized recharge in reservoir lakes depends on various processes and mechanisms [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Simmers 1998), and several authors have considered this process to be an indirect supply [SOURCE: Page_3]

process (Nazoumou 2002). Thus, recharge can occur in natural lakes or be caused by dams. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Therefore, the recharge process relies on many different phenomena, such as dam releases, [SOURCE: Page_3]

infiltration wells, and irrigation returns. Leakage through geological embankments that [SOURCE: Page_3]

enclose reservoirs can add to silting and evaporation and may result in considerably high [SOURCE: Page_3]

water losses (Mesney 2004) and pose severe threats to the stability and security of dam [SOURCE: Page_3]

structures (Benfetta et al. 2017). The natural recharge by dams is disrupted in some way by [SOURCE: Page_3]

climate change which is increasing from year to year and has short and long-term negative [SOURCE: Page_3]

effects on surface water and groundwater in the northern part of the African continent. Since [SOURCE: Page_3]

among these consequences the irregularity of precipitation, water degradation, stagnation of [SOURCE: Page_3]

sludge in dams, dead slice, drought, and increased extraction of groundwater, etc. (Hamed et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al. 2018) [SOURCE: Page_4]

Leaks occur through materials geological boundaries with a degree of porosity, permeability [SOURCE: Page_4]

or discontinuity sufficient to allow the occurrence of water movement (Kim 2013). Leakage is [SOURCE: Page_4]

considered a negative effect when it promotes the instability of a dike and/or a significant loss [SOURCE: Page_4]

of water; however, leakage can also be considered a positive effect when a nearby aquifer is [SOURCE: Page_4]

recharged. The amount of available research on this subject is limited, but examples of [SOURCE: Page_4]

reservoir leaks, such as leaks in the such as the Negratín Dam (Spain), Gauting (China), [SOURCE: Page_4]

Karoun (Lebanon), and Conqueyrac (France), were cited by Benfetta et al. (2017). Recent [SOURCE: Page_4]

works have focused on studying dam leakage processes by quantifying, qualifying or even [SOURCE: Page_4]

spatially and temporally delimiting leakage terms (Al-Fares 2011; Berhane et al. 2017; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Berhane et al. 2013; Gutiérrez et al. 2015; Kim 2013; Kim and Park 2014; Metwaly et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

2006; Sevil et al. 2017). Research on the processes associated with leakage through geologic [SOURCE: Page_4]

barriers or natural banks is complex due to limited knowledge of the geometry and properties [SOURCE: Page_4]

of subsurface rocks. Moreover, because different causes cause leakage in different dams [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Benfetta et al. 2017), methods must be adapted to the target case study. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Various techniques have been used to study leakage water entering the boundaries of lakes or [SOURCE: Page_4]

dams. Some authors have used isotopic tracers (Alazard 2013; Grünberger et al. 2004; Yi et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al. 2018) to detect the contribution of lake water to underground water. Leakage estimates [SOURCE: Page_4]

have been computed using surface water budgets, Darcy's law or even by correlating reservoir [SOURCE: Page_4]

lake levels with underground water piezometric levels (Bouteffeha, Dages, Bouhlila, & [SOURCE: Page_4]

Molenat, 2015). Likewise, researchers have used geophysical techniques such as vertical [SOURCE: Page_4]

electrical sounding (VES) or electrical tomography (Bièvre et al. 2018) to study flow path [SOURCE: Page_5]

extents and to delineate leakage areas. [SOURCE: Page_5]

In Tunisia, the government constructed numerous small earthen reservoirs for irrigation, [SOURCE: Page_5]

artificial groundwater recharge, and sediment retention. According to the “General Direction [SOURCE: Page_5]

of Dams and Large Hydraulic Works” (DGBGTH) the state office in charge, numerous dams [SOURCE: Page_5]

in the country are suspected to experience leakage (e.g., Beni M'Tir, Kasseb, Barbra, Bou [SOURCE: Page_5]

Heurtma, and El Aroussia). Nevertheless, only a few scientific studies have been performed in [SOURCE: Page_5]

this region (e.g., Alazard 2013; Dassi et al. 2005). The particular case of the Lebna Dam [SOURCE: Page_5]

located in Cap Bon (Tunisia) draws attention. Built to deliver surface water to large coastal [SOURCE: Page_5]

irrigation regions to diminish the overpumping of the coastal aquifer (Gaubi et al. 2017; [SOURCE: Page_5]

Kerrou et al. 2010), the dam quickly showed evidence of noncontrolled recharge occurring [SOURCE: Page_5]

through geological material detected through the modification of piezometric levels. Since its [SOURCE: Page_5]

establishment, the Lebna reservoir has never dried up, indicating that the leakage does not [SOURCE: Page_5]

endanger the surface water supply. Nevertheless, in light of the available evidence [SOURCE: Page_5]

representing this situation, the effectiveness of this recharging system controlled by natural [SOURCE: Page_5]

spills in front of an underground salinization region experiencing sea water intrusion is [SOURCE: Page_5]

uncertain. [SOURCE: Page_5]

The main objectives of this study are to depict the geological barrier formation by employing [SOURCE: Page_5]

fieldwork observations and disclosing the hydrological features of the infiltration processes [SOURCE: Page_5]

across the dam bank. To determine the infiltration rate and the ratio between the increase in [SOURCE: Page_5]

the Lebna reservoir water level and leakage, data collected from the reservoir were used to [SOURCE: Page_5]

## 5 DISCUSSIONS

A geological survey was performed to identify and map a sandy formation outcrop known as [SOURCE: Page_20]

the Hammamet Formation of upper Pliocene age; this material is present on the southern [SOURCE: Page_20]

ridges of the studied reservoir. The position of the formation lies on a layer of Sidi Barka clay [SOURCE: Page_20]

(Lower Pliocene), favoring infiltration when water levels are higher than 15 m.a.s.l. The rise [SOURCE: Page_20]

of the water level to the dam-dumping height of 18 m.a.s.l should increase at the same time [SOURCE: Page_20]

the infiltration surface area and the hydraulic gradient increase. Dam balance calculations [SOURCE: Page_20]

indicated that an average water volume of 3.76 Mm$_3$.y-1 +/- 0.02 infiltrated into the water [SOURCE: Page_20]

table downstream of the dam throughout the 1990-2017 period, with high variations occurring [SOURCE: Page_20]

between wet and dry years. As mentioned by Kerrou et al. (2010) to be coherent with his [SOURCE: Page_20]

modeling of the Korba aquifer, the estimated volume was approximately 3.73 Mm$_3$.y-1 in [SOURCE: Page_21]

2004. [SOURCE: Page_21]

Kerrou et al. (2010) used a unique value as a permanent input to represent the coastal aquifer, [SOURCE: Page_21]

whereas we were able to compute varying recharge estimates that could be entered to [SOURCE: Page_21]

represent scenarios dedicated to forecasting the evolution of saline intrusion under climate [SOURCE: Page_21]

change conditions. The extension and limits of the aquifer downstream of the reservoir were [SOURCE: Page_21]

precisely determined by geophysical means combining VES and ETR methods. Tectonic [SOURCE: Page_21]

feature and facies changes influenced the aquifer layer flow paths from the reservoir to the [SOURCE: Page_21]

sea; for example, the Hamamet Formation presented large thickness variations with clear [SOURCE: Page_21]

piezometric patterns. Areas with large aquifer thicknesses implied low piezometric gradients. [SOURCE: Page_21]

This description of the aquifer limits constitutes added value that creates opportunities for the [SOURCE: Page_21]

simulation and understanding of the responses of the aquifer to recharge and pumping. From [SOURCE: Page_21]

the EC and piezometric evolution data, 4 different areas of interest were discriminated: [SOURCE: Page_21]

1-     Near the reservoir (0-500 m from the south banks), low EC (<1.8 mS.cm-1) and [SOURCE: Page_21]

piezometric levels were connected to the corresponding lake levels with low [SOURCE: Page_21]

gradients, indicating the direct influence of the recharge zone in the [SOURCE: Page_21]

Hammamet Formation. [SOURCE: Page_21]

2-     Downstream (0.5-1 km), tectonic structures narrow the flow paths, and a [SOURCE: Page_21]

higher-gradient zone is found with higher EC values [1.8 to 2.6 mS.cm-1]. The [SOURCE: Page_21]

salinity in this region is typical of mixing between underground water and [SOURCE: Page_21]

reservoir water [SOURCE: Page_21]

3-     Downstream (1-2 km), a thicker aquifer zone with low piezometric gradients [SOURCE: Page_21]

and similar EC values can be found. [SOURCE: Page_21]

4-      At approximately the sea shoreline (distance<3 km), the piezometric gradients [SOURCE: Page_22]

are disturbed, and the EC values are elevated (>2.6 mS.cm-1), indicating [SOURCE: Page_22]

overpumping and salt intrusion. [SOURCE: Page_22]

A combination of VES and ERT techniques was used in this study, but as Kayode, Odukoya, [SOURCE: Page_22]

Adagunodo, and Adeniji (2018) they found that a combination of seismic refraction and [SOURCE: Page_22]

resistivity methods is adequate for obtaining robust information regarding seepage pathways [SOURCE: Page_22]

in and around dams. Similarly, the self-potential (SP) and induced polarization (IP) methods [SOURCE: Page_22]

are known to be sound for monitoring subsurface seepage (Panthulu et al. 2001); thus, we [SOURCE: Page_22]

considered applying these methods in the future near the lake zone, but these methods may be [SOURCE: Page_22]

inadequate for sites with piezometric levels deeper than 4 to 8 meters. In this study, the [SOURCE: Page_22]

geophysical campaign was carried out during the dry season. It is always possible to perform [SOURCE: Page_22]

regular follow-up measurements at different times to detect the resistivity variations on the [SOURCE: Page_22]

banks of the Lebna reservoir. [SOURCE: Page_22]

The uncertainties in our piezometric maps are difficult to reduce; few points were measured [SOURCE: Page_22]

by DGPS methods (9 out of 71), and for the rest of the analyzed points, SRTM 90 and [SOURCE: Page_22]

topographic map data were used, contributing an accuracy of 2 meters. In our experience [SOURCE: Page_22]

incorporating DGPS values with topographic map data, the errors were under 50 cm. Nearly [SOURCE: Page_22]

all piezometric data are collected in traditional wells that are very often affected by pumping [SOURCE: Page_22]

and/or stand as abandoned wells with suspicious water renewal processes. Pumping for [SOURCE: Page_22]

irrigation and water returns were not evaluated in the study. Kerrou et al. (2010) estimated an [SOURCE: Page_22]

annual pumping value of 100 mm for irrigated zones. We assumed that this value was [SOURCE: Page_22]

insufficient for inducing a large water table perturbation. Nevertheless, we recommend [SOURCE: Page_22]

complementary temporal monitoring with a pressure probe to document these effects. In most [SOURCE: Page_22]

previous works that explored the dam leakage phenomenon (Dreybrodt et al. 2002; Zhang et [SOURCE: Page_23]

al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2012; Zhou et al. 2021), piezometric data were used as the main [SOURCE: Page_23]

evidence. In several cases, the data interpretations were based on punctual measurements [SOURCE: Page_23]

recorded downstream of the studied dam and correlated to the water level in the reservoir [SOURCE: Page_23]

(Zhou et al. 2021). Whenever piezometric maps were presented in the works mentioned [SOURCE: Page_23]

above, they were used to describe the flow in general and not the flow perturbations resulting [SOURCE: Page_23]

from leakage flow contributions. This consideration resulted in our study being more focused [SOURCE: Page_23]

In this study, a relation between the water level and infiltration was established, and the [SOURCE: Page_23]

resulting curve included slope changes that corresponded to the limits of sandy layers located [SOURCE: Page_23]

In this case, the resulting equilibrium between the piezometric level and the water level of the [SOURCE: Page_23]

reservoir would explain the decrease in leakage flows. In our case, the reservoir level can be [SOURCE: Page_23]

## 6 CONCLUSION

Water leakage should be considered when constructing of hydraulic structures such as dams, [SOURCE: Page_24]

reservoirs or artificial lakes. This phenomenon can contribute to the study region either [SOURCE: Page_24]

negatively (through water losses or dam breakage) or positively (by contributing to the [SOURCE: Page_24]

recharge of the groundwater), as in our case study. Investigating the geological nature of the [SOURCE: Page_24]

bank layers prior to construction is a major step that can be taken to determine whether [SOURCE: Page_24]

leakage will occur and, if so, the order of magnitude. In cases such as our study, the leakage [SOURCE: Page_24]

volume mentioned (17% of the dam inflow) was not as critical as that reported in Somers et [SOURCE: Page_24]

al. (2016), who suggested a 49% exchange with the subsurface. Nevertheless, this [SOURCE: Page_24]

phenomenon was welcomed, as it represented enhanced contribution to the recharge of an [SOURCE: Page_24]

overexploited aquifer affected by marine intrusions. [SOURCE: Page_24]

Recharge through the percolation of dam water into natural beds represents an artificial input [SOURCE: Page_24]

source to groundwater in arid areas. The choice of method for estimating recharge must be [SOURCE: Page_24]

guided by the research objectives, the data availability and the possibility of acquiring [SOURCE: Page_24]

additional data and the scale of the study area. In this paper, we estimated the groundwater [SOURCE: Page_25]

recharge volume over a 27-year period in a Mediterranean catchment area using a water [SOURCE: Page_25]

balance approach. Electrical resistivity measurements were applied downstream of the Lebna [SOURCE: Page_25]

Dam reservoir to precisely detect the locations where leakage occurred. Based on vertical [SOURCE: Page_25]

electrical surveys and tomography, resistivity methods allowed the detection of high- [SOURCE: Page_25]

resistivity regions corresponding to leakage areas. [SOURCE: Page_25]

The current findings are very useful for stakeholders, especially, to enhance the importance of the [SOURCE: Page_25]

Lebna dam as a source of aquifer recharge for the upstream region. Characterization of the Lebna dam [SOURCE: Page_25]

/ aquifer relationship, improved by a non-controlled leakage of 3.7 Mm$_3$. y-1 from Lebna dam to [SOURCE: Page_25]

upstream aquifer and leakage mapping are calculated and presented for the first time. [SOURCE: Page_25]

The characterizations of water leakage and aquifer (groundwater balance, water level, geometry) are [SOURCE: Page_25]

useful for: i) local and national water management authorities (DGRE and CRDA Nabeul), to improve [SOURCE: Page_25]

sustainable groundwater management strategy, and ii) groundwater uses specially the farms in [SOURCE: Page_25]

upstream dam, to identify the depth of drilling and wells to reach the aquifer and to know the available [SOURCE: Page_25]

groundwater resource. [SOURCE: Page_25]
