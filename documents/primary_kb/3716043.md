### 1. Introduction

The use of reclaimed wastewater for irrigation has become an attractive alternative water resource to [SOURCE: Page_5]

alleviate water scarcity. However, there are several potential environmental- and health-related risks [SOURCE: Page_5]

associated with this practice. One risk is the introduction of antibiotics and their associated antibiotic [SOURCE: Page_5]

resistant bacteria (ARB) and antibiotic resistant genes (ARGs) into the agro(eco)systems (Kampouris et [SOURCE: Page_5]

al., 2021; Marano et al., 2021; Subirats et al., 2019). Bacterial infections are treated with antibiotics, [SOURCE: Page_5]

and, after administration, part of these compounds is excreted unchanged in the urine and feces (Junker [SOURCE: Page_5]

et al., 2006). This is a problematic issue because antibiotic residues can promote resistance in bacteria [SOURCE: Page_5]

during wastewater treatment process (Watkinson et al., 2007). Antibiotic resistance can then be acquired [SOURCE: Page_5]

by soil-bacteria (Gatica & Cytryn, 2013) or even reach the food chain (Christou et al., 2017) when using [SOURCE: Page_5]

reclaimed wastewater for crop irrigation. The dissemination of this resistance in the environment might [SOURCE: Page_5]

end up with inefficiency of antibiotics use (Lucas et al., 2016). For these reasons, the development and [SOURCE: Page_5]

dissemination of antibiotic resistance are today issues attracting great attention from the general public [SOURCE: Page_5]

and has been classified as one of the major challenges for the global health by the WHO (WHO, 2014). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Conventional wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) are currently not designed to fully remove [SOURCE: Page_5]

antibiotics and ARB&Gs, likely due to the cost and difficulty of treating complex waste mixtures. ARGs [SOURCE: Page_5]

have been detected in wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) (Cacace et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2012) [SOURCE: Page_5]

drinking water treatment system (Zhang et al., 2020), surface water (Tang et al., 2016) and groundwater [SOURCE: Page_5]

(Zainab et al., 2020) indicating a broad distribution and contamination of different water environments. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Even if it has been reported that low-energy anaerobic–aerobic treatment reactors, constructed wetlands, [SOURCE: Page_5]

and disinfection processes have shown good removal efficiencies (Barancheshme & Munir, 2019), [SOURCE: Page_5]

ARGs are frequently detected in WWTPs effluents. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Traditionally, it has been suggested that urban WWTPs act as hotspots of ARGs and also mobile genetic [SOURCE: Page_5]

elements (MGEs) (Guo et al., 2017; Rizzo et al., 2013). In a recent review including data from WWTPs [SOURCE: Page_5]

worldwide, a positive correlation between antibiotics and ARGs commonly detected in WWTPs was [SOURCE: Page_5]

observed, except for β-lactam antibiotics and bla genes (Wang et al., 2020b). The most frequently [SOURCE: Page_5]

detected antibiotics are macrolides (clarithromycin, erythromycin, azithromycin and roxithromycin), [SOURCE: Page_5]

3 [SOURCE: Page_5]

quinolones (ofloxacin, ciprofloxacin and norfloxacin), and sulfonamides (sulfamethoxazole), [SOURCE: Page_6]

trimethoprim and tetracycline while bla (blaCTXM, blaTEM), sul (sul^1, sul^2), tet (tetO, tetQ, tetW) and [SOURCE: Page_6]

ermB genes are the ARGs commonly reported in WWTPs (Wang et al., 2020a). [SOURCE: Page_6]

Yet, other recent studies have shown controversial results, suggesting that in fact the core resistome of [SOURCE: Page_6]

biological wastewater treatment units is different from other parts of urban water ecosystems and not [SOURCE: Page_6]

necessarily a hotspot for gene transfer (Munck et al., 2015; Quintela-Baluja et al., 2019). It is therefore [SOURCE: Page_6]

important to study the composition and behavior of microbial communities involved in wastewater [SOURCE: Page_6]

treatment and transport processes, from the WWTP to the downstream receiving water bodies or in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

case of agricultural reuse, to the field and crops. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Biofilms are biological structures made up of bacteria, organic matter and some inorganic matter that [SOURCE: Page_6]

together constitute three-dimensional polymer niches (Flemming & Wingender, 2010). Biofilms may [SOURCE: Page_6]

also be associated with the accumulation of absorbed chemical and pathogenic agents and would thus [SOURCE: Page_6]

act as potential reservoirs of materials that would affect the safety of irrigated food produced. However, [SOURCE: Page_6]

they lead also to self-depuration due the ability to accumulate organic compounds (Huerta et al., 2016; [SOURCE: Page_6]

Ruhí et al., 2016; Wilkinson et al., 2018) at concentrations up to few hundred ng g-1, as well as to degrade [SOURCE: Page_6]

them (Desiante et al., 2021;Writer et al., 2011 Sabater et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_6]

It has been suggested that ARB can proliferate within irrigation pipe-based biofilms (Blaustein et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

2016) and that biofilms in water environments are thought to be hotspots for horizontal gene transfer of [SOURCE: Page_6]

ARGs (Abe et al., 2021). In fact, biofilms are formed when bacteria are under stressed conditions. In [SOURCE: Page_6]

the distribution system, the presence of antibiotics, residual concentration of chlorine will imply a stress [SOURCE: Page_6]

and then will favour biofilm development with growth of bacteria and extracellular polymeric substance [SOURCE: Page_6]

(EPS) production. As in activated sludge system, this bacterial growth and activity could favour gene [SOURCE: Page_6]

transfer. This is a hypothesis that is discussed in the recent paper of Abe et al (2021), but was also [SOURCE: Page_6]

proposed by Pruden et al (2014) for the spread of ARG in biofilm grown in drinking water pipe system [SOURCE: Page_6]

due to opportunistic pathogens growth. Literature on wastewater transport pipes or drip irrigation [SOURCE: Page_6]

systems with reclaimed wastewater is still scarce. A study performed in sewers transporting raw [SOURCE: Page_6]

wastewater showed that community composition rather than antibiotic concentration is the main factor [SOURCE: Page_6]

4 [SOURCE: Page_6]

driving the diversity of the sewage resistome (Auguet et al., 2017). Lequette and coworkers (2020) have [SOURCE: Page_7]

also shown that community composition shifts between different parts of a treated wastewater irrigation [SOURCE: Page_7]

system such as pipes and drippers (Lequette et al., 2020). However, no link has been established between [SOURCE: Page_7]

antibiotic concentration, community structure, biofilm development and ARGs occurrence. [SOURCE: Page_7]

In this work we address some of these questions in an attempt to get a comprehensive overview of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

effect of antibiotics on the dissemination of antibiotic resistance thanks to a deep characterization of [SOURCE: Page_7]

biofilms growing in treated wastewater. Therefore, biofouling, microbial activity, community [SOURCE: Page_7]

composition and abundances of four ARGs and 2 MGEs were monitored in biofilms exposed to a [SOURCE: Page_7]

cocktail of six antibiotics commonly found in wastewater. The role of planktonic bacteria in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

spreading of resistance in interaction with biofilms was also investigated by determining antibiotic [SOURCE: Page_7]

concentration and microbial activity and composition in the liquid phases during the 14 days of [SOURCE: Page_7]

antibiotics exposure. [SOURCE: Page_7]

3.1 Degradation of antibiotics by planktonic and biofilm bacteria. [SOURCE: Page_14]

Belonging to the most frequently detected antibiotics in wastewater (Wang et al., 2020a), CLA, OFX, [SOURCE: Page_14]

Among these six antibiotics, AMX, CEF and OFX have bactericidal activity, whereas CLA, SMX and [SOURCE: Page_14]

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) is defined as the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial that [SOURCE: Page_14]

fact that an antibiotic concentration that kills or inhibits growth of at least some bacteria will, by [SOURCE: Page_14]
