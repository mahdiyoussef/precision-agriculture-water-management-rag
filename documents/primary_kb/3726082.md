25   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   An increasing number of studies demonstrate that tree species biodiversity can affect [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   primary productivity and nutrient cycling in forests due to several factors, such as [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   complementarity, facilitation or selection effects. For instance, resource partitioning [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   in soils has been found to allow a more optimized nutrient uptake in mixed species [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   plots compared with monocultures. However, how these effects will be modified by [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   climate change – such as water availability – is not as well understood, especially in [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   deep soil layers. Therefore, we specifically asked how water availability may [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   influence the effect of tree mixtures on soil microorganism activity by measuring [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   extracellular enzyme activities (EEAs) and available nutrients along the soil profile [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   (down to 90 cm) in a 10-year-old plantation in southwestern France, which [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   manipulates tree species composition (Pinus pinaster and Betula pendula, in [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   monocultures and in mixed plots) and irrigation. Our results showed that EEAs [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   directly depend on tree species composition and water conditions in interaction with [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   soil depth; we found a positive effect of mixing birch and pine on carbon (C)- and [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   nitrogen (N)-related EEAs at an intermediate soil depth (15–30 cm soil layer), while [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   the effect of increasing water availability increased phosphorus (P)-related EEAs [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   mostly in the upper soil layers (0 to 30 cm). However, we found no significant [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   interactive effect between tree diversity and water availability on EEAs, underlying [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   that the negative effect of lower water availability cannot be offset by the positive [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   effect of mixing tree species. Differences in EEAs and available nutrients along the [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   soil profile highlight the importance to look at different soil depths to better [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   understand how nutrient cycling may be affected by increasing tree diversity and [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   changes in water availability. [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

50      Forest biodiversity provides a multitude of ecosystem goods and services [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   (Gamfeldt et al., 2013; Jactel et al., 2017; Liang et al., 2016). However, forests in [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   many areas are threatened by more intense and frequent drought periods (IPCC, 2014; [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   Schwalm et al., 2012), with important negative consequences such as on nutrient [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   cycling (He and Dijkstra, 2014; Schlesinger et al., 2015) or even on tree survival [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   (Hartmann et al., 2018). A well-explored option to mitigate these negative effects is to [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   increase tree stand diversity (Lebourgeois et al., 2013; Pretzsch et al., 2013). Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   mixed-species forests generally have been found to be more productive than pure [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   stands (Baeten et al., 2019; Cardinale et al., 2007; Liang et al., 2016; Zhang et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   2012) and are more stable to climatic fluctuations due to overyielding, species [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   asynchrony and favorable species interactions (Jucker et al., 2014a; Kardol et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   2018; Morin et al., 2014). However, recent studies have shown that the positive effect [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   of tree diversity on forest productivity might be annihilated under drought conditions [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   (Jactel et al., 2018). In addition, not all tree species mixtures are more resistant to [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   water stress (Grossiord, 2019). Although many studies on biodiversity effects focused [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   on aboveground plant productivity, only a few addressed belowground productivity [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   and soil functioning (Alberti et al., 2017; Brassard et al., 2013; Ma and Chen, 2016). [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   Furthermore, we know little about how limiting water conditions may affect [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   biodiversity benefits and if their interactive effects may affect soil microbial activity [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   and biogeochemical cycling in mixed forests. [SOURCE: Page_4]

70      An important step of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P) cycling is the [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   decomposition of organic matter that derives from leaf and root litter (Hättenschwiler [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   et al., 2005; Zechmeister-Boltenstern et al., 2015). Among the main mechanisms [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   involved in decomposition, one process is based on extracellular enzymes [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   (Sinsabaugh et al., 2002). Soil microorganisms release extracellular enzymes to [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   acquire energy and resources by degrading neighboring soil organic matter (Burns, [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   1982; Burns et al., 2013). Studying soil extracellular enzyme activities (EEAs) can [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   thus give information on potential belowground microbial mineralization processes [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   and how microbial communities drive C, N, and P cycling in soils (Allison et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   2007; Fanin et al., 2016a; Henry, 2013). Soil EEAs are measured to assess microbial [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   responses to global changes, including precipitation changes in varying ecosystems [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   (Henry, 2013). EEAs have been found to be negatively affected by water stress [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   (Brockett et al., 2012; Schlesinger et al., 2015), including in forests (Sardans and [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   Peñuelas, 2005). A recent global meta-analysis concluded that drought led to a [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   significant decrease in urease and phenol oxidase activity, while an increase in [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   precipitation significantly increased N-related enzyme activities (Xiao et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   However, these effects may vary with soil depth due to water runoff dynamics and [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   infiltration from topsoil to deeper soils. Topsoil is thus expected to be more positively [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   affected by an increase in precipitation due to its increase in soil moisture, a major [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   factor determining EEAs in soils (Brockett et al., 2012), whereas deeper soils may be [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   less affected by precipitation, have less varying moisture and thus less effect on EEAs [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   (Fanin et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_5]

92      Tree species may also have important effects on EEAs due to their varying litter [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   qualities (Weand et al., 2010). Recently, Alberti et al. (2017) found a positive [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   synergistic effect of tree diversity on EEAs, due to the influence of varying litter traits [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   on soil C and N mineralization. These results suggest that an increase in tree species [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   richness may influence biogeochemical cycling due to stimulation of microbial [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   activity (Hacker et al., 2015; Steinauer et al., 2014). Because tree species diversity [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   may positively influence microbial biomass and activity (Chen et al., 2019; Lucas- [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   Borja et al., 2012; Thakur et al., 2015; West et al., 2006), and that both microbial [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   biomass and substrate pools decrease with depth (Agnelli et al., 2004; Fierer et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   2003), there is a need to study the effect of tree diversity on EEAs along the soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   profile (Herold et al., 2014; Loeppmann et al., 2016; Stone et al., 2014). The diversity [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   effects may also directly depend on root biomass along the litter-soil continuum. [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   Mixing tree species with contrasting root systems may lead to niche partitioning along [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   the soil profile (Brassard et al., 2013), which can benefit exploration of the total soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   volume (Schmid and Kazda, 2002) and total nutrient uptake (Richards et al., 2010). In [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   addition, facilitation mechanisms between associated species, such as hydraulic lift [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   (Zapater et al., 2011), may be especially advantageous in mixed stands during drought [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   events. Thus, investigating how tree diversity may offset the negative effects of [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   drought along the soil profile would provide new insights into how biodiversity [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   effects on nutrient cycling may change in a future climate. [SOURCE: Page_6]

112      In this study, our primary objective was to investigate the interactive effect [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   between tree diversity and water availability on available nutrients and EEAs along [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   the soil profile. In practice, we sampled soils at five depths in an experimental [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   plantation where both tree diversity and water availability (through irrigation) were [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   manipulated and tested three hypotheses. First, we tested the hypothesis (H$_1$) that [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   EEAs would be higher in a two-species mixture than in the corresponding pure plots [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   since mixing different litter qualities may lead to more synergistic litter [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   decomposition (Chapman et al., 2013; Cornwell et al., 2008). Specifically, we [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   hypothesized that the effect of mixing trees on EEAs should be higher in the topsoil [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   layer, where the effect of tree species identity is highest (Augusto et al., 2002) and [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   where the organic matter quantity is highest and richest in nutrients (Jobbágy and [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   Jackson, 2001; Melillo et al., 1989). Secondly, we hypothesized (H$_2$) an increase in [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   water supply would have a positive effect on EEAs by alleviating the water constraint [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   (Sardans and Peñuelas, 2005; Xiao et al., 2018). We anticipate that the effect of [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   increasing water availability should be highest in the topsoil, mainly because [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   microbial communities at the soil surface are often more subjected to water stress than [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   those living in deeper soil horizons (Fanin et al., 2019). Finally, we hypothesized (H$_3$) [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   that the positive effect of mixing two tree species on EEAs would be strongest in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   plots experiencing the dry summers. This is because mixing tree species with [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   contrasting root exploration strategies may favor drought resistance through [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   facilitation mechanisms (Bello et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_7]

288          All of the statistical analyses were done using R software (version 3.5.1). First, [SOURCE: Page_13]

289   extreme values were removed following the outlier labelling rule with a conservative [SOURCE: Page_13]

290   tuning parameter of g = 2.2 (Hoaglin and Iglewicz, 1987). On average, 1.25% of data [SOURCE: Page_13]

292   presenting values per soil column. [SOURCE: Page_13]

293          Mixed models were used to assess the effect of the following fixed effect [SOURCE: Page_13]

294   factors: tree species composition (pure birch, pure pine, mixed birch-pine), water [SOURCE: Page_13]

394   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_17]

395          By using a 10-year-old plantation which manipulates tree species composition [SOURCE: Page_17]

396   and irrigation in southwestern France, we investigated the effect of mixing birch and [SOURCE: Page_17]

397   pine, water availability, and their interaction on seven hydrolytic extracellular enzyme [SOURCE: Page_17]

398   activities (EEAs) and available nutrients along the soil profile. Our results are partly [SOURCE: Page_17]

16 [SOURCE: Page_17]

399   in agreement with our two first hypotheses, thereby demonstrating that EEAs directly [SOURCE: Page_18]

400   depend on tree species composition and water conditions. In contrast to our last [SOURCE: Page_18]

401   hypothesis, we did not find any effect of the interaction between water availability [SOURCE: Page_18]

402   and species mixing on EEAs, underlying that the negative effect of lower water [SOURCE: Page_18]

403   availability cannot be offset by the positive effect of mixing tree species. [SOURCE: Page_18]

404 [SOURCE: Page_18]

405      4.1. Effect of mixing tree species [SOURCE: Page_18]

406          In agreement with our first hypothesis, we found that mixing birch and pine [SOURCE: Page_18]

407   had a positive effect on C- and N-related EEAs in interaction with soil depth (Table [SOURCE: Page_18]

408   2). However, contrary to our expectations that this effect should be more pronounced [SOURCE: Page_18]

409   in the topsoil (Alberti et al., 2017), we observed a strong synergistic increase in C- [SOURCE: Page_18]

410   related and N-related EEAs in the 15–30 cm soil layer compared to both birch and [SOURCE: Page_18]

411   pine monocultures (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). While the litter quality was significantly higher in [SOURCE: Page_18]

412   birch than in pine plots (lower C:N ratio, Table 1), the EEAs in the pure birch plots [SOURCE: Page_18]

413   were only slightly higher than those in the pure pine plots (Fig. 1). Although we [SOURCE: Page_18]

414   expected an influence of tree species identity (Purahong et al., 2016) and litter quality [SOURCE: Page_18]

415   on microbial decomposition and EEAs (Sinsabaugh et al., 2002), the little influence of [SOURCE: Page_18]

416   the tree species on EEAs has been found in other studies. For instance, Herold et al. [SOURCE: Page_18]

417   (2014) found no differences between coniferous and deciduous central European [SOURCE: Page_18]

418   forests on six of our measured EEAs. Likewise, Weand et al. (2010) described no tree [SOURCE: Page_18]

419   species effect on C-, N- and P-related enzymes in five single-species plots in an [SOURCE: Page_18]

420   American Northeastern hardwood forest. This was also true for the N- and P-related [SOURCE: Page_18]

421   EEAs studied in five tree species in a temperate secondary forest in Northeastern [SOURCE: Page_18]

422   China (Diao et al., 2020). Therefore, it is unlikely that the increase in EEAs at the [SOURCE: Page_18]

423   mid-soil layer of the mixed birch-pine plot was related to the different and [SOURCE: Page_18]

17 [SOURCE: Page_18]

424   complementary foliar litter quality of the two species. Instead, we interpret our results [SOURCE: Page_19]

425   as a consequence of the higher nutrient demand of mixed stands. [SOURCE: Page_19]

426          Indeed, higher tree productivity in mixed stands may stimulate soil microbial [SOURCE: Page_19]

427   biomass through an increase in root exudate quantity and diversity (Steinauer et al., [SOURCE: Page_19]

428   2016), or favor C allocation to ectomycorrhizal fungi to get N from organic molecules [SOURCE: Page_19]

429   to sustain tree biomass production (Fernandez and Kennedy, 2016; Lindahl and [SOURCE: Page_19]

430   Tunlid, 2015). Alternatively, niche partitioning in root systems or increase in root [SOURCE: Page_19]

431   biomass along the soil profile may have increased nutrient uptake (Jose et al., 2006), [SOURCE: Page_19]

432   which may exacerbate, in turn, nutrient limitation of microorganism communities [SOURCE: Page_19]

433   (Kuzyakov and Xu, 2013). In both cases, this implies that there is likely a strong [SOURCE: Page_19]

434   demand by microorganisms to degrade the soil organic matter (Fatemi et al., 2016) at [SOURCE: Page_19]

435   this middle soil layer in mixed stands, and indicates that the potential effects of tree [SOURCE: Page_19]

436   diversity on EEAs are not only limited to the superficial soil layers. [SOURCE: Page_19]

437          Contrary to C- and N-related EEAs, we found that there was a decrease in the [SOURCE: Page_19]

438   P-related EEA in the 30–60 cm soil layer in the mixed-species plots compared to both [SOURCE: Page_19]

439   birch and pine monocultures (Fig. 1C). This may be due to a shift in the nutrient needs [SOURCE: Page_19]

440   of the microorganisms (Mooshammer et al., 2014) or because the changes in tree [SOURCE: Page_19]

441   species diversity may lead to a shift in the structure of microbial communities (Liu et [SOURCE: Page_19]

442   al., 2018; Pei et al., 2016; Schweitzer et al., 2008). For instance, changes in fungi to [SOURCE: Page_19]

443   bacteria ratios may alter the stoichiometric requirements at the community scale [SOURCE: Page_19]

444   (Fanin et al., 2013), which could explain the reduced and different effect of tree [SOURCE: Page_19]

445   diversity on N- and P-related EEAs. On the other hand, this may be caused by [SOURCE: Page_19]

446   differential root uptake and competition for the available nutrients between [SOURCE: Page_19]

447   microorganisms and both tree and understory roots (Kuzyakov and Xu, 2013; [SOURCE: Page_19]

448   Richardson and Simpson, 2011). This hypothesis is supported by the tendency of [SOURCE: Page_19]

18 [SOURCE: Page_19]

449   available N and P to decrease and increase respectively in mixed plots (Fig. S$_3$), [SOURCE: Page_20]

450   although the overall effect of tree species for the entire soil column was non- [SOURCE: Page_20]

451   significant (Table 2). Taken together, these findings suggest that there is a shift in [SOURCE: Page_20]

452   microbial C:N:P stoichiometry from monocultures to the two-species mixture along [SOURCE: Page_20]

453   the soil profile. [SOURCE: Page_20]

455       4.2. Effect of water availability [SOURCE: Page_20]

456           In accordance with our second hypothesis, we found an increase in both N- [SOURCE: Page_20]

457   and P-related EEAs with irrigation. Overall, EEAs were lower in the control blocks [SOURCE: Page_20]

458   (Table S$_3$), which were subject to the long dry summers of southwestern France (Fig. [SOURCE: Page_20]

459   S$_1$). Although we sampled in early spring, i.e. several months after the irrigation had [SOURCE: Page_20]

460   been switched off, we still found differences in soil moisture between control and [SOURCE: Page_20]

461   irrigated blocks (Table 1). The legacy of sub-optimal water conditions for the past [SOURCE: Page_20]

462   eight years in the control blocks may have led to a decrease in both microbial biomass [SOURCE: Page_20]

463   (Brockett et al., 2012) and tree and understory root biomass (Table S$_2$; Brunner et al., [SOURCE: Page_20]

464   2015). The lower EEAs in these dry control blocks could thus be due to fewer [SOURCE: Page_20]

465   microorganisms and roots, leading to less root exudation and enzyme stimulation [SOURCE: Page_20]

466   (Zhang et al., 2019). This interpretation is supported by our RDA results, where tree [SOURCE: Page_20]

467   fine root biomass points in the same direction as soil moisture (Fig. 5), demonstrating [SOURCE: Page_20]

468   a positive covariation between these variables. The drought episodes experienced by [SOURCE: Page_20]

469   control blocks and not by irrigated blocks, at various periods during the three [SOURCE: Page_20]

470   summers (Fig. S$_1$), may have caused a significant change in the composition of root [SOURCE: Page_20]

471   exudates, both during these drought periods and after rewetting (Gargallo-Garriga et [SOURCE: Page_20]

472   al., 2018). Alternating periods of drying and rewetting, and changes in root exudation, [SOURCE: Page_20]

473   can also have an influence on microbial hotspots and thus microbial abundance and [SOURCE: Page_20]

19 [SOURCE: Page_20]

474   activity (Kuzyakov and Blagodatskaya, 2015), which could help explain lower EEAs [SOURCE: Page_21]

475   in control blocks. [SOURCE: Page_21]

476          In line with our expectations, the effect of irrigation was higher in the topsoil [SOURCE: Page_21]

477   for the P-related EEA (Fig. 3). This is probably because microbial communities [SOURCE: Page_21]

478   benefit more of increasing humidity in soil layers that are more subjected to drought [SOURCE: Page_21]

479   and where the substrate is relatively nutrient rich (Fanin et al., 2019). However, this [SOURCE: Page_21]

480   effect was relatively constant throughout the soil column for N-related EEAs. This [SOURCE: Page_21]

481   result is in line with the idea that there is a shift in the requirements of microbial [SOURCE: Page_21]

482   communities at the community scale along the soil profile (Fanin et al., 2013; [SOURCE: Page_21]

483   Mooshammer et al., 2014), and may explain the variability in nutrient availability [SOURCE: Page_21]

484   from the top to the bottom soil layers (Fig. 4). Indeed, irrigation had a negative effect [SOURCE: Page_21]

485   on available N in the topsoil. This is probably due to important N uptake by trees and [SOURCE: Page_21]

486   the understory, coupled with a leaching of soluble mineral N-forms (NH$_4$+ and NO$_3$-) [SOURCE: Page_21]

487   into the deeper soil layers, where irrigation had a positive effect on available N (Table [SOURCE: Page_21]

488   2, Fig. 4A). In contrast, the available P was generally low and unaffected by the [SOURCE: Page_21]

489   different factors (Table 2, Fig. 4B). The lack of significant increase in available P [SOURCE: Page_21]

490   within the 0–30 cm soil layer may be due to a strong and rapid absorption by tree [SOURCE: Page_21]

491   roots in the topmost layer. High understory root biomass in deeper soils (Table S$_2$) [SOURCE: Page_21]

492   and a general decrease in P desorption along the soil profile (Achat et al., 2013), may [SOURCE: Page_21]

493   explain the decrease in available P along the soil profile. The differing effects of water [SOURCE: Page_21]

494   availability on available nutrients point towards a strong influence of organic matter [SOURCE: Page_21]

495   quality and associated microorganism communities to explain the EEAs’ responses. [SOURCE: Page_21]

496          Furthermore, because C-related enzymes were not as affected as other EEAs [SOURCE: Page_21]

497   by irrigation, the result reinforces the idea that changes in organic matter quality are [SOURCE: Page_21]

498   also important drivers of EEAs under low water availability. The lower litterfall and [SOURCE: Page_21]

20 [SOURCE: Page_21]

499   forest floor C:N ratios in dry blocks compared to irrigated blocks support this [SOURCE: Page_22]

500   hypothesis (Table 1). Indeed, higher tree growth rates when water conditions are [SOURCE: Page_22]

501   favourable reduce available N input to soils, with further consequences on N [SOURCE: Page_22]

502   limitations in our study system. Along the entire soil profile, we found less available [SOURCE: Page_22]

503   N in the non-irrigated blocks (Table S$_3$). Taken together, these results highlight that [SOURCE: Page_22]

505   direct effects of water availability and indirect effects through tree growth on organic [SOURCE: Page_22]

506   matter quality and nutrient availability. [SOURCE: Page_22]

508      4.3. Effect of the interaction between mixing tree species and water [SOURCE: Page_22]

509           availability [SOURCE: Page_22]

510          Although we hypothesized that mixing birch and pine could mitigate the [SOURCE: Page_22]

511   effects of lower water availability on nutrient availability and EEAs, we did not find [SOURCE: Page_22]

512   such a compensatory effect (Table 2). Studies at larger scales have shown that [SOURCE: Page_22]

513   biodiversity may become more beneficial as water limitations increase (Lebourgeois [SOURCE: Page_22]

517   during drought years, and Jactel et al. (2018) found high overyielding due to mixing [SOURCE: Page_22]

518   tree species at wet sites at the stand level. Additionally, we suggest that the lack of a [SOURCE: Page_22]

519   significant interaction effect between mixing birch and pine trees and water [SOURCE: Page_22]

520   availability might be due to the spatial segregation of the individual effects of these [SOURCE: Page_22]

521   two factors. While mixing the two tree species tended to increase EEAs in the mid- [SOURCE: Page_22]

522   soil layer, irrigation tended to increase EEAs and decrease available N in the top soil. [SOURCE: Page_22]

523   Moreover, we collected our samples in March, when the control blocks were not [SOURCE: Page_22]

21 [SOURCE: Page_22]

524   under water stress and the irrigation treatment had not yet begun for the year (Table [SOURCE: Page_23]

525   1). [SOURCE: Page_23]

527   consequence of the lack of differences between the tree species themselves. Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_23]

528   we did not see different patterns of the effect of water availability on EEAs between [SOURCE: Page_23]

529   the two monocultures. This might be surprising given that Pinus pinaster is a fairly [SOURCE: Page_23]

530   more drought tolerant species compared to Betula pendula (Choat et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_23]

532   vulnerability P50: Pinus pinaster = -2.8 to -3.7 Mpa, Betula pendula = -2.2 to -2.4 [SOURCE: Page_23]

533   Mpa (Choat et al., 2012; Delzon, unpublished data)), and both species’ roots similarly [SOURCE: Page_23]

535   which could explain the small effects of species identity and, in turn, of mixing [SOURCE: Page_23]

536   species. Additional analyses including more tree species and more sampling dates are [SOURCE: Page_23]

537   needed to further evaluate the relationship between EEAs and tree diversity, as well as [SOURCE: Page_23]

538   intra annual temporal variation of EEAs and biodiversity effects (Cardinale et al., [SOURCE: Page_23]

539   2012). [SOURCE: Page_23]

540 [SOURCE: Page_23]

541         4.4. Environmental effects and larger implications for nutrient cycling [SOURCE: Page_23]

542            The RDA gives us new insight into how environmental variables may have an [SOURCE: Page_23]

543   effect on different enzyme activities (Fig. 5). Available N was negatively correlated [SOURCE: Page_23]

544   with the tree species’ fine root biomass along the first axis, likely due to rapid nutrient [SOURCE: Page_23]

545   absorption by roots, which led to N-depletion in the topsoil where roots thrive (Giehl [SOURCE: Page_23]

546   and Wirén, 2014). N-related EEAs may have been stimulated by this low N [SOURCE: Page_23]

547   availability in order to maintain high growth rates (Kaye and Hart, 1997), or by tree [SOURCE: Page_23]

548   root exudation (Renella et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2019). Indeed, N-related EEAs were [SOURCE: Page_23]

22 [SOURCE: Page_23]

549   also positively correlated to tree species’ fine root biomass (Fig. 5). To a lesser extent, [SOURCE: Page_24]

550   the P-related enzyme was negatively correlated to available P, likely due to similar [SOURCE: Page_24]

551   feedback mechanisms. [SOURCE: Page_24]

552              Interestingly, we also found that C-related EEAs were strongly related to [SOURCE: Page_24]

553   available P. This implies that when P availability is high, microorganisms are strongly [SOURCE: Page_24]

554   limited by C, inciting them to produce more C-related enzymes to get access to the [SOURCE: Page_24]

555   energy produced by decomposing organic matter (Fanin et al., 2017, 2016b). This [SOURCE: Page_24]

556   hypothesis was supported by the significant positive correlation between available P [SOURCE: Page_24]

557   and the Cenz:Penz ratio across all species and water availability treatments (Fig. 6B), [SOURCE: Page_24]

558   which we did not find between the available N and the Cenz:Nenz ratio (Fig. 6A). This [SOURCE: Page_24]

559   is likely because alleviating P limitation generates a new constraint for [SOURCE: Page_24]

560   microorganisms and thus the necessity to acquire C from organic molecules in these [SOURCE: Page_24]

561   P-poor podzol soils (Achat et al., 2009; Augusto et al., 2017). This relationship was [SOURCE: Page_24]

562   true regardless of species composition, water availability, or soil depth, suggesting [SOURCE: Page_24]

563   that P limitation plays a more important role in controlling C-related enzymes. Thus, [SOURCE: Page_24]

564   potential C sequestration is likely to be limited by P availability in these nutrient-poor [SOURCE: Page_24]

565   forests. [SOURCE: Page_24]

566 [SOURCE: Page_24]

567       4.5. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_24]

568              This study revealed that water supply and mixing pine and birch trees could [SOURCE: Page_24]

569   have important long-term effects on soil extracellular enzyme activities (EEAs). First, [SOURCE: Page_24]

570   from a management perspective, our results suggest that overall C sequestration in our [SOURCE: Page_24]

571   system is likely to be constrained by P availability, regardless of water availability. [SOURCE: Page_24]

572   Second, we found C-N-P decoupling effects as a result of tree species mixing or [SOURCE: Page_24]

573   improved water availability, which may alter microorganism stoichiometry and [SOURCE: Page_24]

23 [SOURCE: Page_24]

574   belowground C, N, P fluxes. Finally, the lack of interactive effects between mixing [SOURCE: Page_25]

575   birch and pine, and water supply, suggests that the negative effect of lower water [SOURCE: Page_25]

576   availability on microbial activity cannot be offset by the positive effect of mixing [SOURCE: Page_25]

577   these two species. The next step will be to sample at different seasons to investigate [SOURCE: Page_25]

578   the possible temporal variations of EEAs and to measure other belowground processes [SOURCE: Page_25]

579   such as protein depolymerisation and mineralization, in order to assess the dynamics [SOURCE: Page_25]

580   of interactive effects between water availability and tree diversity over seasons and [SOURCE: Page_25]

581   disentangle underlying mechanisms. [SOURCE: Page_25]
