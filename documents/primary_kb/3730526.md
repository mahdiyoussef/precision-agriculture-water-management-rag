24   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   Understanding the range of approaches available for assessing the impacts of [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   agricultural changes at landscape scale is important when addressing local to global [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   issues. Using a topic modelling approach, we reviewed the literature on impact [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   modelling of agricultural landscapes. A search in Web of Science using the keywords [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   model, agricultural systems and landscape yielded 1,975 hits, of which 514 papers met [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   our selection criteria. The most salient terms fell within six groups: change, scale, [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   pollution, biodiversity, practices and terms on biophysical/regulatory conditions. We [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   identified four main topics: water quality, water quantity/energy crops, biodiversity and [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   Integrated Assessment. Water management issues were more likely to be covered in [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   North American researches, while issues related to Integrated Assessment were mainly [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   covered in European studies. We found no relationship between topic and model type. [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   We conclude that future integrated studies should consider the diversity of agricultural [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   systems in governance of water and biodiversity issues. [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   Spatial expansion and intensification of agriculture in recent decades has had [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   tremendous environmental impacts on agricultural landscapes (Foley et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   These landscapes are defined as systems in which interactions occur between farmers [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   and their natural and social resources, including management of fields, field margins and [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   associated semi-natural habitats (Benoit et al., 2012). Modern agriculture is contributing [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   to water degradation, increased energy use and greenhouse gas emissions, together [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   with widespread pollution and loss of biodiversity (Foley et al., 2011). These impacts [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   take place beyond the field and farm levels, necessitating a landscape approach if they [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   are to be addressed properly at the relevant scale(s), through relevant research [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   disciplines and methods. Impacts resulting from spatial interactions need to be [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   considered at larger scales than point effects. This can be watershed scale when [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   considering water quality or quantity (e.g. Frey et al., 2013; Gungor and Guncu, 2013; [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   Fan and Shibata, 2015; Carvalho-Santos et al., 2016) or landscape scale when [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   considering spatial flows of pathogens (e.g. Hossard et al., 2013), nectar-foraging species [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   (e.g. Baveco et al., 2016) etc. Many studies have examined sustainability at field or farm [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   scale (e.g. Zahm et al., 2008; Pelzer et al., 2012; Craheix et al., 2016), but upscaling their [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   results to larger spatial or temporal scales may be difficult and produce uncertain [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   conclusions (Dargaard et al., 2003). However, such upscaling may be necessary to [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   understand the impact of different land uses, crops and/or cropping techniques on [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   landscape performance, sustainability and ecosystem services (Tscharntke et al., 2005). [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   Because of the multiple temporal and spatial scales involved, assessment of agricultural [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   landscapes is challenging and requires modelling approaches to study system changes [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   and their impacts. Modelling allows the complex processes occurring to be simplified, in [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   order to explore the impacts of possible changes (land use, crop, practices) that cannot [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   be distinguished in the real world (e.g. Legg, 2004; Skelsey et al., 2010). Different [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   modelling approaches can be used for designing and/or assessing landscape [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   performance in the face of change, depending on the topic studied and available [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   knowledge. These approaches may involve empirical models (e.g. Bennett et al., 2014), [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   process-based models (e.g. Santhi et al., 2014), optimisation models (e.g. Huang et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   2012), agent-based models (e.g. Brady et al., 2012), statistical models (e.g. Gottschalk et [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   al., 2007) or a combination of these types of models (e.g. Schonhart et al., 2016). In such [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   studies, the objective in designing alternatives is to compare their impacts with [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   appropriate indicators, with simulation models being used to predict values for these [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   indicators (Clavel et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   Different methods can be used to design alternatives, ranging from simulation studies [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   (comparable     to    sensitivity   analyses   on   cropping    practices   and/or    their [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   proportion/location) to participatory approaches. Scenarios, which describe “possible [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   futures that reflect different perspectives on past, present and future developments” [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   (Van Notten, 2005), are currently used in participatory approaches. They usually include [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   a description of the initial situation (for comparison with alternatives) and often also the [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   drivers/causes of change (Dockerty et al., 2006; Alcamo and Henrichs, 2008), which can [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   be social, economic (e.g. policies) and/or physical or ecological (e.g. climate change). The [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   design of alternative landscapes can be performed by the research team (e.g. Babel et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   2011) or in a participatory approach involving stakeholders (e.g. Hossard et al., 2018), [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   depending on the topic studied, the model used for assessment and the preferences of [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   the research team. [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   Understanding the range of approaches available for impact modelling at landscape [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   scale is important when exploring potential opportunities to efficiently address local to [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   global problems. In the first instance, bibliometric analyses have been performed, [SOURCE: Page_4]

123   2. Methods [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   2.1. Literature search and study selection [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   The literature search was conducted in April 2017 and involved entering keywords in [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   the Clarivate Analytics’ Web of Science (formerly operated by the Institute for Scientific [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   Information) without a time frame limitation. The search was limited to the “Article” [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   document type and to the “English” language. For “Topics”, the following search [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   equation was used: “model* AND (agri* OR agro* OR crop* OR farm*) AND (landscape* [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   OR watershed* OR (water NEAR catchment*)) AND (scenar* OR alternative*)”. This [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   initial search yielded 1,975 hits, spanning from 1978 to 2017. We then excluded papers [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   based on article abstracts (1,461 studies) when they did not match our selection criteria, [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   which were: (1) use of a model, i.e. a simplified representation of the system, as a tool to [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   design or assess future agricultural landscape(s), (2) a focus on agricultural systems [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   (including farming practices and/or agricultural organisation, explaining why we chose [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   not to use “land use*” as a key word), (3) resolution at landscape scale (i.e. beyond the [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   farm level) and (4) with outcomes on alternative agricultural systems (thus excluding [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   papers focusing only on the effects of climate change). We did not specify the type of [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   impact (e.g. pollution, nitrates) as our objective was to gain a general overview of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   literature, without focusing on a specific impact as done in previous studies (e.g. water [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   in Zare et al., 2017; biodiversity in Liu et al., 2011). Since our focus was on agricultural [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   landscape changes explicitly including cropping practices, or their organisation in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   landscape, we excluded pure land cover/land use studies that provide limited [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   descriptions of agricultural practices, by (1) our search equation with specific [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   agricultural terms and (2) excluding remaining studies (920) in the initial 1,975 paper [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   dataset (eligibility step, excluded as “No agriculture”) (Figure 1). [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   We then manually excluded general papers lacking a case study application, e.g. reviews [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   without a case study (12 papers) (Figure 1). Our final dataset thus comprised 514 [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   individual papers, which were all read by the research team. The list and references of [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   the 514 papers are available online, together with the LDA R code and the groups’ [SOURCE: Page_8]

153   2.2. Overview of bibliometric analysis and topic modelling [SOURCE: Page_10]

154   Records of the 514 papers were manually downloaded from Web of Science into Zotero [SOURCE: Page_10]

155   (BibTeX format). The dataset was then exported from Zotero (www.zotero.org) as a [SOURCE: Page_10]

156   JSON file (http://www.json.org/) for analysis with R software version 3.3.3 (R Core [SOURCE: Page_10]

157   Team, 2017) using “jsonlite” R package (Ooms, 2014). Metadata, including journal name, [SOURCE: Page_10]

158   year of publication and main author country, were analysed to determine trends in [SOURCE: Page_10]

159   publication with regard to time periods, journals and geographical distribution (related [SOURCE: Page_10]

160   to author country, not case study country). [SOURCE: Page_10]

161   In addition to metadata analyses, publication abstracts were analysed using a topic [SOURCE: Page_10]

162   modelling approach. Topic models, proposed in the late 1990s (Hofman, 1999; [SOURCE: Page_10]

163   Papadimitriou et al., 2000), are models used to assess the frequency of occurrence of [SOURCE: Page_10]

164   terms in a dataset of papers from the literature (Grün and Hornik, 2011). The Latent [SOURCE: Page_10]

165   Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) model (Blei et al., 2003) is designed for topic modelling. It is a [SOURCE: Page_10]

166   probabilistic model based on the hypothesis that each article is characterised by one or [SOURCE: Page_10]

167   more topics, and that each topic is characterised by a unique multinomial distribution of [SOURCE: Page_10]

168   terms (D’Amato et al., 2017; Weinshall et al., 2013; Yau et al., 2014). The LDA model thus [SOURCE: Page_10]

169   allows identification of topics reflecting relevant information on the relations and [SOURCE: Page_10]

170   similarities in structure between papers in a dataset (Weinshall et al., 2013). Topics are [SOURCE: Page_10]

171   assumed to be uncorrelated in the LDA model (Grün and Hornik, 2011) and it allows [SOURCE: Page_10]

172   journal articles to contain more than one topic (Blei et al., 2003). As a Bayesian model, [SOURCE: Page_10]

173   LDA requires information on the a priori distribution of model parameters, called “prior [SOURCE: Page_10]

174   distribution”, which can be informative or non-informative, depending on the modeller [SOURCE: Page_10]

175   choice.   Using the Bayes’ theorem, the prior distribution is updated to obtain the [SOURCE: Page_10]

176   “posterior distribution” (a probability distribution), which is based on both the prior [SOURCE: Page_10]

10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

177   distribution and the information gathered in the data. Thus, the posterior distribution is [SOURCE: Page_11]

178   conditional on the data used. [SOURCE: Page_11]

180   2.3. LDA procedure for main dataset topics [SOURCE: Page_11]

181   Three main steps have to be performed before estimating the LDA of a dataset (Grün [SOURCE: Page_11]

182   and Hornik, 2011). The first step is to pre-process data with tokenisation and stemming [SOURCE: Page_11]

183   to build a dataset dictionary, in order to ensure relevant analyses (Grün and Hornik, [SOURCE: Page_11]

184   2011). Tokenisation is applied to separate so-called “tokens” (e.g. words), by removing [SOURCE: Page_11]

185   punctuation characters, numbers, converting to lower-case and removing stop words, [SOURCE: Page_11]

186   using the “tm” R package (Feinerer and Hornik, 2017). Stemming is applied to reduce [SOURCE: Page_11]

187   each word to its root grammatical form, in order to increase inter-paper comparability [SOURCE: Page_11]

188   (i.e. homogeneity of terms), e.g. “chang” would be the root for changes, change, changing, [SOURCE: Page_11]

189   etc (Appendix A). In addition, in our LDA analysis we deleted the term “model” from the [SOURCE: Page_11]

190   dictionary, as it was the only mandatory term in our search equation (see section 2.1.). [SOURCE: Page_11]

191   We then analysed the resulting dictionary, to further homogenise tokens by merging [SOURCE: Page_11]

192   redundant terms (e.g. “plough” and “plow”; “tillage” and “till”; “actor” and “stakeholder”; [SOURCE: Page_11]

193   see Appendix A for the entire list). Potentially misleading terms were manually checked [SOURCE: Page_11]

194   in each paper to avoid unintentional mergers (e.g. catchment relating to non-water [SOURCE: Page_11]

195   applications). Very infrequent words, i.e. terms occurring less than five times, were also [SOURCE: Page_11]

196   removed from the analysis (as in D’Amato et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_11]

197   In the second step, the number of topics (k) included in the dataset has to be chosen [SOURCE: Page_11]

198   before running LDA. This number is often set a priori by the user, based on assumptions [SOURCE: Page_11]

199   on the dataset structure (e.g. on research fields in Kane et al., 2016). In the present [SOURCE: Page_11]

200   study, we hypothesised that topics would correspond to the main sustainability issues [SOURCE: Page_11]

201   (environmental, economic, social), but that these could be further split into sub-issues [SOURCE: Page_11]

11 [SOURCE: Page_11]

202   (e.g. water quality and water quantity both consider the environmental aspect of [SOURCE: Page_12]

203   sustainability) or according to scientific discipline (e.g. economics, agronomy, hydrology [SOURCE: Page_12]

204   etc.). Given this uncertainty, we opted not to specify the number of topics a priori, but to [SOURCE: Page_12]

205   set it according to the estimation strategy proposed by Taddy (2012). This method finds [SOURCE: Page_12]

206   the “best” number of topics within the minimum and maximum user-defined number of [SOURCE: Page_12]

207   topics. The “best” number of topics is that maximising the Bayes factor computed with [SOURCE: Page_12]

208   marginal likelihood calculations (Taddy, 2012), i.e. maximising the posterior [SOURCE: Page_12]

209   distribution over the possible instances of topics over words (Uto et al., 2017). To [SOURCE: Page_12]

210   analyse our dataset of 514 papers, we set the possible number of topics between 2 and [SOURCE: Page_12]

211   51 (where 51 corresponded to mean number of 10 papers per topic), using the “topics” [SOURCE: Page_12]

212   function in the “maptpx” R package (Taddy, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_12]

213   The third step to be performed before LDA estimation concerns the (paper) sampling [SOURCE: Page_12]

214   method and thus the value specification for the parameters of the prior distributions [SOURCE: Page_12]

215   (Grün and Hornik, 2011). We used a collapsed Gibbs sampler (e.g. D’Amato et al., 2017) [SOURCE: Page_12]

216   and set the distribution parameters as suggested in Taddy (2012). Gibbs sampling is a [SOURCE: Page_12]

217   Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithm used to obtain a sequence of observations based [SOURCE: Page_12]

218   on a multivariate probability distribution, which is particularly useful for calculating the [SOURCE: Page_12]

219   posterior distribution of a Bayesian network (Geman and Geman, 1984). [SOURCE: Page_12]

220   The LDA model for main topics was then fitted using the “lda.collapsed.gibbs.sampler” of [SOURCE: Page_12]

221   the “lda” R package (Chang, 2015) using 2500 iterations (as in D’Amato et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_12]

222   This LDA-Gibbs approach provides estimates of posterior probability of association [SOURCE: Page_12]

223   between journal articles and topic, and terms and topic. It thus provides the probability [SOURCE: Page_12]

224   for (1) allocation of the journal articles to each topic and (2) allocation of individual [SOURCE: Page_12]

225   terms to each topic (‘topic keywords’). Topic keywords can be either generic to the [SOURCE: Page_12]

226   entire dataset or specific to one (or a few) topic(s). Keyword specificity to one topic is [SOURCE: Page_12]

12 [SOURCE: Page_12]

227   measured based on computation of the “relevance” of a given term to a given topic [SOURCE: Page_13]

228   (Sievert and Shirley, 2014). Sievert and Shirley (2014) define the relevance of a term w [SOURCE: Page_13]

229   to a topic t as a function of a weight parameter lambda (λ) ranging between 0 and 1. λ [SOURCE: Page_13]

230   determines the (user-defined) weight given to the probability of a term w under topic k [SOURCE: Page_13]

231   relative to its lift: relevance(term w | topic t) = λ * (p(w|t) + (1- λ)*(p(w|t)/p(w) (Sievert [SOURCE: Page_13]

232   and Shirley, 2014). A high value of λ results in keywords common to the entire dataset, [SOURCE: Page_13]

233   while a low value results in topic-specific keywords (Sievert and Shirley, 2014). λ is [SOURCE: Page_13]

234   chosen a priori by the user. In this study, we mainly used a value of λ = 0.6, as [SOURCE: Page_13]

235   recommended by Sievert and Shirley (2014), although lower values of λ were also [SOURCE: Page_13]

236   employed (0.1 step) to look for specificities of methods and models, especially for highly [SOURCE: Page_13]

237   specific terms (λ = 0). Topic results are available online for further exploration [SOURCE: Page_13]

238   (http://shin-r.innovation.inra.fr/review_LH_PC/), with the possibility of choosing [SOURCE: Page_13]

239   different λ values and visualising relevant terms according to the chosen λ value. [SOURCE: Page_13]

240   In addition, our analyses included a list of salient terms in the whole dataset. The [SOURCE: Page_13]

241   saliency of a term refers to the frequency of keywords in the dataset, using word [SOURCE: Page_13]

242   distinctiveness (Chuang et al., 2012). The analyses of salient and topic-specific keywords [SOURCE: Page_13]

243   were performed using the “LDAvis” R package (Sievert and Shirley, 2015), which also [SOURCE: Page_13]

244   calculates the distance between topics using Jensen-Shannon divergence (Sievert and [SOURCE: Page_13]

245   Shirley, 2014). This inter-topic distance approximates the between-topic semantic [SOURCE: Page_13]

246   relationship, using multidimensional scaling. [SOURCE: Page_13]

674   potential amount of services (Mitchell et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_35]

675   Most papers in our dataset were linked to one dominant topic. Less frequent topic [SOURCE: Page_35]

676   combinations were studies of water quality together with biodiversity (Topics 1 and 4) [SOURCE: Page_35]

677   and studies of biodiversity and water quantity/energy crops (Topics 3 and 4). Although [SOURCE: Page_35]

678   we excluded papers focusing only on the effects of climate change, this theme emerged [SOURCE: Page_35]

679   as a driver of scenarios in Topic 3 (Water quantity/energy crops). Thus, in our dataset, [SOURCE: Page_35]

680   biodiversity was apparently not assessed in studies linking climate change and [SOURCE: Page_35]

681   agricultural practices, the two main factors that actually threaten biodiversity. Different [SOURCE: Page_35]

682   studies have assessed biodiversity responses to climate change (e.g. Bellard et al., 2012), [SOURCE: Page_35]

683   or to agriculture and their potential conflicts (e.g. Henle et al., 2008). Attempts to study [SOURCE: Page_35]

684   their joint effects appear to have focused mostly on land use change, without detailing [SOURCE: Page_35]

685   agricultural practices. Several studies have considered habitat loss, but with less [SOURCE: Page_35]

686   attention to spatial (e.g. fragmentation in the landscape) or practice change (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_35]

687   management intensity) (de Chazal and Rounsevell, 2009), and with a limited number of [SOURCE: Page_35]

35 [SOURCE: Page_35]

688   taxa considered (Chopin et al., 2019). Future studies of alternative agricultural [SOURCE: Page_36]

689   landscapes thus would need to enlarge the vision on biodiversity, e.g. by increasing the [SOURCE: Page_36]

690   number of taxa (Chopin et al., 2019) and including detailed population characteristics [SOURCE: Page_36]

691   and their climate drivers, together with potential agricultural practices to be applied in [SOURCE: Page_36]

692   these futures. This will require integrated approaches, as agricultural practices are [SOURCE: Page_36]

693   determined by a set of drivers, including (but not limited to) climate change. [SOURCE: Page_36]

694   Participatory approaches would thus be also required for this issue. [SOURCE: Page_36]

695   Finally, our review did not identify hot topics and methods used in agricultural studies. [SOURCE: Page_36]

696   For instance, “resilience” was not identified as a top word among our four topics, [SOURCE: Page_36]

697   appearing in only five of the 514 papers in our dataset. However, resilience is gaining [SOURCE: Page_36]

698   increasing attention in agricultural research to characterise the relationship between [SOURCE: Page_36]

699   agricultural outputs and perturbation, the two main parameters being global warming [SOURCE: Page_36]

700   and price volatility (Urruty et al., 2016). Agronomists study agricultural resilience at [SOURCE: Page_36]

701   farm to country scale (Urruty et al., 2016). The landscape scale tends to be studied by [SOURCE: Page_36]

702   researchers in ecology, looking at e.g. spatial resilience (location, connectivity, [SOURCE: Page_36]

703   complexity) (Cumming, 2011), land use management and habitat (Tscharntke et al., [SOURCE: Page_36]

704   2005), or by economists looking at land use patterns and the resilience of agricultural [SOURCE: Page_36]

705   returns (Abson et al., 2013). In those ecology-based studies, agricultural practices are [SOURCE: Page_36]

706   often simplified, characterised as categories of land use and disregarding the level of [SOURCE: Page_36]

707   decision, i.e. the farm. This indicates that the landscape agronomy approach called for by [SOURCE: Page_36]

708   Benoit et al. (2012) has not yet fully emerged. This approach is necessary for [SOURCE: Page_36]

709   disaggregating land use and better characterising the diversity of cropping systems and [SOURCE: Page_36]

710   landscape diversity, as highlighted by Chopin et al. (2017). It is particularly important [SOURCE: Page_36]

711   for issues requiring coordination of agriculture-related actions at the landscape scale [SOURCE: Page_36]

712   (e.g. erosion in Souchère et al., 2010), or collective governance of e.g. water resources [SOURCE: Page_36]

36 [SOURCE: Page_36]

713   (e.g. Murgue et al., 2015) or integrated crop-livestock systems (e.g. Moraine et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_37]

714 [SOURCE: Page_37]

715   5. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_37]

716   In this review, we distinguished four main topics covered by studies modelling the [SOURCE: Page_37]

717   impacts of agricultural changes at landscape scale. These were: water quality, water [SOURCE: Page_37]

718   quantity/energy crops, biodiversity and integrated assessment. We found very few [SOURCE: Page_37]

719   publications on these topics in South America and Africa, despite the fact that hot topics [SOURCE: Page_37]

720   like water scarcity in Africa are likely to increase with climate change, calling for [SOURCE: Page_37]

721   collective governance at scales beyond field and farms. Similarly, issues like GMs and [SOURCE: Page_37]

722   emerging weed resistance, particularly in South America, call for spatially explicit [SOURCE: Page_37]

723   methods for coordinating actions at medium spatial scales. Finally, although we found [SOURCE: Page_37]

724   abundant North American and European studies on modelling agricultural changes and [SOURCE: Page_37]

725   impacts at landscape scale, hot topics like pesticides (EU) and diversification (USA) did [SOURCE: Page_37]

726   not emerge, despite their critical impacts at landscape scale for e.g. water quality, water [SOURCE: Page_37]

727   quantity and pest control. This indicates an urgent need for integrated studies [SOURCE: Page_37]

728   considering the diversity of agricultural and cropping systems in governance of the [SOURCE: Page_37]

729   collective issues of water quality, water quantity and biodiversity. [SOURCE: Page_37]
