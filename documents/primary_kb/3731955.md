Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

For many smallholder farmers in East and Southeast Asia, farming represents a decreasing share of their [SOURCE: Page_2]

income-generating activities, leading to increasing limits to investing family labour in farming. This [SOURCE: Page_2]

process has been particularly marked in regions where farmers face limited access to land, water and [SOURCE: Page_2]

markets. This study asks whether irrigated farms in central Thailand are on a similar pathway. It analyses [SOURCE: Page_2]

how these farms have evolved to face existing constraints and their future prospects. The farms have [SOURCE: Page_2]

adapted to constraints including lack of fresh water in the dry season, floods, and sometimes insufficient [SOURCE: Page_2]

labour. In almost all farming families, farming is still the main income-generating activity for at least [SOURCE: Page_2]

one household member. Most of the farmers involved in fish and shrimp farming had already identified [SOURCE: Page_2]

a successor in their family or considered it was possible. This was also the case of rice farmers who had [SOURCE: Page_2]

made quite high investments in their farms, most of whom cultivated relatively large areas. However, [SOURCE: Page_2]

another group of rice farmers had become trapped in a vicious circle of low farm profitability, the [SOURCE: Page_2]

increasing age of the farmers and the fact their farms held little attraction for younger generations. Thai [SOURCE: Page_2]

agricultural policies did not provide sufficient means to break this cycle. The future of these vulnerable [SOURCE: Page_2]

rice farms thus appears to be uncertain. Breaking this cycle may require a comprehensive approach that [SOURCE: Page_2]

explicitly addresses the issue of farm structure. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Key words: ageing farmers; farming households; irrigation schemes; rice; Thailand [SOURCE: Page_2]

### 1. Introduction

Rural inhabitants in East and Southeast Asian countries increasingly obtain their income from off-farm [SOURCE: Page_2]

sources. This is the case in low, medium and high income countries in the region (Haggblade et al., [SOURCE: Page_2]

2010; Rigg et al., 2016) and is taking place not only thanks to increased opportunities in non-agricultural [SOURCE: Page_2]

sectors but also because of the difficulty of maintaining or increasing incomes from farming. Many [SOURCE: Page_2]

1 [SOURCE: Page_2]

studies describe such processes in situations where farm characteristics and the environment in which [SOURCE: Page_3]

farms evolve limit the prospects for obtaining an income, these being small size, lack of irrigation water, [SOURCE: Page_3]

or limited market access (Fishman et al., 2013; Hu and Rahman, 2015; Pritchard et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_3]

Far fewer studies have investigated the extent to which better endowed farms in East and Southeast [SOURCE: Page_3]

Asia, in particular larger farms and farms with access to irrigation, are on the same pathway towards a [SOURCE: Page_3]

diminished role of agriculture as a source of income in farming families. What is happening on these [SOURCE: Page_3]

farms needs to be analysed to understand the evolution of the agricultural sector in these countries as a [SOURCE: Page_3]

whole, especially as irrigated areas usually play a major role in supplying inputs to agricultural value [SOURCE: Page_3]

chains and in supporting food security at national level. [SOURCE: Page_3]

This topic is particularly relevant in Thailand, where the average farm is quite big (3.1 ha, according to [SOURCE: Page_3]

the 2013 agricultural census) compared with other countries in East and Southeast Asia (Rigg et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

2016). Several studies have underlined the intensity and rapidity of the decline in the economic role of [SOURCE: Page_3]

agriculture in farming households with limited access to land and irrigation water in Thailand. Gödecke [SOURCE: Page_3]

and Waibel (2011), Nilsen (2014), Rigg et al. (2012) and Rigg et al. (2019) investigated agrarian changes [SOURCE: Page_3]

in villages in the Northern and Northeast Regions, where farming is mostly rainfed and where the [SOURCE: Page_3]

average farm (between 1.9 and 2.3 ha in these studies) is smaller than the national average. [SOURCE: Page_3]

The image that emerges from these studies can be outlined as follows. First, ageing of the farming [SOURCE: Page_3]

population is even faster than at national scale. In the villages studied by Rigg et al. (2012), the average [SOURCE: Page_3]

age of people whose main activity was farming increased from 36 in 1983 to 55 in 2008. Second, young [SOURCE: Page_3]

people are moving out of the villages. Gödecke and Waibel (2011) and Nilsen (2014) identified a wide [SOURCE: Page_3]

gap in the age pyramid at village level, as most people aged between 20 and 40 had moved to cities to [SOURCE: Page_3]

find work. Third, household members increasingly find opportunities to work in factories and still come [SOURCE: Page_3]

home every day (Rigg et al., 2008; Shirai and Rambo, 2017; Shirai et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_3]

All these changes have reduced the availability of family labour, and farming households consequently [SOURCE: Page_3]

increasingly choose farming systems and practices that require less family labour, e.g., planting trees, [SOURCE: Page_3]

mechanisation or the use of paid labour (Formoso, 2016; Podhisita, 2017). In areas located not far from [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

the frontiers of Thailand, hired labourers often come from neighbouring countries (Barney, 2012; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Rungmanee, 2015). These changes in the organisation of farming households (in terms of spatial [SOURCE: Page_4]

structure and labour) have enabled them to adapt to the constraints and opportunities in both urban and [SOURCE: Page_4]

rural areas. However, most of these changes reflect a trend towards less intensive farming (Rigg, 2019). [SOURCE: Page_4]

More generally, the increasing labour constraints can limit farmers’ capacity to or interest in testing and [SOURCE: Page_4]

implementing more productive or more environmentally sustainable farming practices (Bhandari and [SOURCE: Page_4]

Mishra, 2018; Phastraporn, 2019). [SOURCE: Page_4]

This description of farm dynamics is representative of what is happening to the majority of the farms in [SOURCE: Page_4]

these regions. Nevertheless, in rainfed areas of the North and Northeastern Regions of Thailand, some [SOURCE: Page_4]

farms are actively engaged in changes involving the intensive use of family labour (Piotrowski et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

2013; Plews-Ogan et al., 2017). Moreover, macro indicators do not show that the agricultural sector has [SOURCE: Page_4]

become “dormant”. Thailand remains a major producer and exporter of agricultural products (Thailand [SOURCE: Page_4]

Board of Investment, 2017). Many farms successfully access high-value markets (Kersting and Wollni, [SOURCE: Page_4]

2012) and powerful agro-industries have emerged (Briones and Rakotoarisoa, 2013). The contribution [SOURCE: Page_4]

of agriculture to the growth of Thailand’s domestic product has not changed much over the past two [SOURCE: Page_4]

decades (from 9.1% in 1996 to 8.6% in 2016, according to the World Bank, 2018). However, there have [SOURCE: Page_4]

been few recent studies of agricultural dynamics in areas better endowed with resources in Thailand. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Formoso (2016) studied rice farms with access to irrigation in the Northeast Region and reported that [SOURCE: Page_4]

agriculture was still the main income-generating activity of several farms but did not detail the [SOURCE: Page_4]

characteristics of these farms. By contrast, Rigg (2019) reported that in a village located in an irrigated [SOURCE: Page_4]

area of the Central Region, almost all the villagers surveyed said farming was no longer their main [SOURCE: Page_4]

occupation. [SOURCE: Page_4]

The present study focuses on irrigated areas of central Thailand. Its aim was to understand whether the [SOURCE: Page_4]

engagement of members of farm households in farming has declined in a similar way to that described [SOURCE: Page_4]

in areas of Thailand where farms have limited access to land and water. Taking into account the diversity [SOURCE: Page_4]

in the size and types of production of farms in the study area, our analysis addresses three issues: (i) [SOURCE: Page_4]

how farming systems have adapted to key constraints and changes in the environment and within the [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

farms, thus enabling – or not – households to earn their livelihood farming; (ii) what are the future [SOURCE: Page_5]

prospects of these farms, especially in terms of the engagement of family members in farming; and (iii) [SOURCE: Page_5]

to what extent ongoing policies and local initiatives to support agriculture help farming households deal [SOURCE: Page_5]

with prevailing constraints and thus help sustain their engagement in farming. [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 2.1.    Study area

The study was conducted in the Bang Pakong River delta. This area belongs to a broader zone around [SOURCE: Page_5]

Bangkok which also includes the deltas of the Mae Klong and Chao Phraya Rivers. These delta areas [SOURCE: Page_5]

are not clearly defined in hydrological terms. This zone represents the largest irrigated area in Thailand [SOURCE: Page_5]

and is mainly dedicated to growing rice. Intensive shrimp farming in coastal areas around Bangkok [SOURCE: Page_5]

started in the 1980s (Szuster, 2006), but faced a series of crises in the 1990s and in the 2000s, in [SOURCE: Page_5]

particular because of diseases. This activity later recovered and expanded thanks to the development of [SOURCE: Page_5]

new production techniques and to the introduction of shrimp species capable of adapting to low salinity [SOURCE: Page_5]

levels (Lebel et al., 2010). This enabled the extension of shrimp breeding to inland areas. Today, shrimps [SOURCE: Page_5]

are the main agricultural product in the downstream part of the Bang Pakong River Basin (Seekao and [SOURCE: Page_5]

Pharino, 2016). [SOURCE: Page_5]

The main role of the Ministry of Agriculture and Cooperatives in this zone is to manage water, i.e. to [SOURCE: Page_5]

provide irrigation water in the dry season and address flood problems in the rainy season. Four main [SOURCE: Page_5]

national agricultural policies have been implemented in the delta region around Bangkok in the past [SOURCE: Page_5]

three decades. The first involved a series of schemes to support rice prices on the domestic market but [SOURCE: Page_5]

this support stopped almost completely after the military coup in 2015 (Ricks, 2018). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Second, reduction of the extent of land under rice and the promotion of other crops were declared [SOURCE: Page_5]

national objectives in the Fourth National Plan (1977-1981) and were strongly reaffirmed in the Seventh [SOURCE: Page_5]

National Plan (1992-1996) because of the low profitability of rice farming and because of water [SOURCE: Page_5]

shortages (Sirisup and Kammeier, 2003; Srimanee and Routray, 2012). A major diversification [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

programme was implemented in the Chao Phraya River Basin from 1993 to 1999, but had limited results, [SOURCE: Page_6]

the main causes being floods, labour shortages and difficulties related to the marketing of non-rice [SOURCE: Page_6]

products (Sirisup and Kammeier, 2003). [SOURCE: Page_6]

Third, from 2000 on, the Ministry of Agriculture and Cooperatives promoted an approach aimed at [SOURCE: Page_6]

developing a “self-sufficiency economy” for small-scale farms (Egery, 2014). According to this [SOURCE: Page_6]

approach, small-scale farmers should avoid intensive monocropping (related to contract farming, for [SOURCE: Page_6]

instance). Rather, small-scale farmers should produce both for home consumption and for the market, [SOURCE: Page_6]

they should produce multiple crops, and use limited amounts of pesticides and chemical fertilizers. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Fourth, in 2015, the government announced a new policy that was almost completely opposed to [SOURCE: Page_6]

previous ones. This policy aimed at helping small-scale farms improve the production and marketing of [SOURCE: Page_6]

one crop (or animal). Groups of farmers were invited to work together in “large-scale schemes” and in [SOURCE: Page_6]

partnership with public organisations to reduce production costs thanks to economies of scale, to [SOURCE: Page_6]

increase yields and negotiate better prices. In the rice sector, the official objective was to reduce [SOURCE: Page_6]

production costs by 20% and increase farm sales by 20% (Department of Agricultural Extension, 2016). [SOURCE: Page_6]

This was to be achieved thanks to training, enhanced collective action between farmers and improved [SOURCE: Page_6]

connection with markets (e.g., certification or contracting with agro-industries). [SOURCE: Page_6]

The main river in Prachinburi Province is the Prachinburi River, which becomes the Bang Pakong River [SOURCE: Page_6]

after the confluence with the Nakhon Nayok River. The study areas are four irrigated zones that receive [SOURCE: Page_6]

water from the Prachinburi and Bang Pakong Rivers (Figure 1). These four zones were selected because [SOURCE: Page_6]

they include the main farming systems in the downstream part of the Prachinburi River basin. Farmers [SOURCE: Page_6]

in Bang Pla Ra Subdistrict concentrate on fish and shrimp farming and farmers in Bang Taen Subdistrict [SOURCE: Page_6]

on rice and fish farming. In the two other zones, farmers concentrate on rice production. Many farmers [SOURCE: Page_6]

in the Dong Krathong Yam Subdistrict have purchased agricultural machinery, such as a tractor, and [SOURCE: Page_6]

farm relatively large areas (more than 13 ha). Farmers in Bang Kung Subdistrict farm much smaller [SOURCE: Page_6]

areas. Each study area receives irrigation water through a network of canals connected to the Prachinburi [SOURCE: Page_6]

and Bang Pakong Rivers. The pumps that pump water from the rivers into the canals are operated by [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

water user associations in Bang Kung, Bang Pla Ra and Dong Krathong Yam Subdistricts and by the [SOURCE: Page_7]

subdistrict local administration in Bang Taen Subdistrict. [SOURCE: Page_7]

### 2.2.     Analytical framework

We characterised farming systems and investigated how the farms had adapted to the constraints they [SOURCE: Page_7]

faced, especially those related to water resources, agricultural equipment and labour. The future [SOURCE: Page_7]

prospects of the farms were delineated using two indicators: the farmers’ own plans for the future and [SOURCE: Page_7]

interest expressed by possible successors in taking over the farm. Preliminary interviews showed that in [SOURCE: Page_7]

many farming households, farming was the main activity of at least one member. Thus, we analysed [SOURCE: Page_7]

whether farm incomes were sufficient for potential successors to be interested in taking over the farm [SOURCE: Page_7]

as their main activity. Possible successors (particularly the farmer’s own children) often compare the [SOURCE: Page_7]

income they expect from farming with the income they could get from non-farming activities. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

economic criterion concerning annual net income is clearly only one among many other criteria they [SOURCE: Page_7]

use to decide whether to take over the farm or not. Other criteria include opportunities to increase their [SOURCE: Page_7]

income in the future, income uncertainty, quality of life, the possibility to send the children to high- [SOURCE: Page_7]

quality schools, the amount of physical effort required, and autonomy in decision making (Ruiz-Salvago [SOURCE: Page_7]

et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

The minimum wage for a full-time job in Thailand is approximately 8,000 baht^1 per month (Thai [SOURCE: Page_8]

National Wage Committee, 2016). Working in factories may involve renting a room or daily transport, [SOURCE: Page_8]

at a cost of approximately 1,500 baht per month. Thus, working in factories may provide a net income [SOURCE: Page_8]

of 6,500 baht per month. We consequently defined an economic threshold of 78,000 bath [SOURCE: Page_8]

(approximatively 2,450 US$) net income per year as the minimum amount that possible successors [SOURCE: Page_8]

would want before accepting to take over the farm, and to work on the farm as their main income- [SOURCE: Page_8]

generating activity. This economic threshold is a very rough approximation, first because of the wide [SOURCE: Page_8]

range of opportunities to obtain a non-farming income and of the variability of the cost of living. Second, [SOURCE: Page_8]

farms with different characteristics do not require the same amount of labour and a more detailed [SOURCE: Page_8]

analysis would involve calculating net income per unit of labour time. [SOURCE: Page_8]

2.3.    Data collection and analysis Twenty-seven farmers were interviewed in each of the four areas, giving a total of 108 farmers (43% of [SOURCE: Page_8]

whom were women). We interviewed the farm holder or, if the farm was run by a couple, one of them. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The farms were chosen from lists of farmers provided by farmers’ groups (e.g., the water users’ [SOURCE: Page_8]

association) to ensure diversity in terms of farm size, agricultural activities and the location of the farm [SOURCE: Page_8]

within the irrigated areas. Interviews first focused on farm characteristics and family structure and [SOURCE: Page_8]

second, on production costs and benefits in 2016. Third, farmers were asked if they had previously faced [SOURCE: Page_8]

problems of insufficient irrigation water, brackish water or floods, and if so, what they had done to [SOURCE: Page_8]

tackle them. Finally, farmers were asked about their plans in the future (in approximately 5 years) and [SOURCE: Page_8]

whether they had already identified a successor within the family to take over their farm. Ten staff [SOURCE: Page_8]

members of the offices of the Ministry of Agriculture and Cooperatives in Prachinburi Province were [SOURCE: Page_8]

also interviewed to understand the policies and local initiatives implemented in our study areas to [SOURCE: Page_8]

support farming activities. The interviews were conducted in 2017. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Farm net incomes were calculated as the difference between gross sales and yearly production costs, but [SOURCE: Page_8]

did not account for past investments in agricultural equipment, since our focus was on the net income [SOURCE: Page_8]

that a successor would expect if he or she took over the farm as it was. Neither did the assessment of [SOURCE: Page_8]

### 3.2.    Adaptation to constraints

Most of the farms we surveyed had adapted their farming practices to brackish water, floods, lack of [SOURCE: Page_12]

labour or lack of agricultural equipment. Concerning brackish water, 52% of the farmers we interviewed [SOURCE: Page_12]

said that the water in the river near their farm was sometimes brackish in the dry season. These farmers [SOURCE: Page_12]

were generally located in downstream areas, Bang Taen and Bang Pla Ra Subdistricts. In their opinion, [SOURCE: Page_12]

the occurrence of brackish water in the river had become increasingly frequent in the dry season over [SOURCE: Page_12]

the past 15 years, especially in March and April. This was due to increased pumping because farmers [SOURCE: Page_12]

had started producing dry season rice. As a result, seawater intruded inland over a much greater distance [SOURCE: Page_12]

from the estuary. [SOURCE: Page_12]

At the individual level, rice farmers had adapted by growing short-duration rice varieties, which can be [SOURCE: Page_12]

harvested before March. Fish and shrimp farmers had dug ponds to store water. At collective level, in [SOURCE: Page_12]

February, the water users’ associations pump water into canals to store it for later use when the water in [SOURCE: Page_12]
