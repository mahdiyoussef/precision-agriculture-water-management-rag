13   Abstract: This paper aims to assess the conditions under which hydraulic projects can be considered [SOURCE: Page_1]

14   as an efficient option, from an environmental point of view, to secure water supply of agricultural [SOURCE: Page_1]

15   areas using the Territorial Life Cycle Assessment (T-LCA) methodology. Firstly, the environmental [SOURCE: Page_1]

16   performance of three theoretical agricultural land-use planning scenarios are defined: (1) a business- [SOURCE: Page_1]

17   as-usual case without irrigation, (2) irrigation with an Inter-Basin Water Transfer (IBWT) and (3) with [SOURCE: Page_1]

18   an Agricultural Reservoir (AR). These are all assessed by computing the territorial eco-efficiency (i.e. a [SOURCE: Page_1]

19   ratio between the services provided by land planning scenarios and their related environmental [SOURCE: Page_1]

20   impacts). Secondly, Territorial Life Cycle Assessment methodology was used to assess the water- [SOURCE: Page_1]

21   energy-infrastructure nexus between the two hydraulic projects. Results indicate that the eco- [SOURCE: Page_1]

22   efficiencies of the scenarios vary according to the service considered and to the type of land use. For [SOURCE: Page_1]

23   land management or economic functions, the scenario without irrigation can perform better, while [SOURCE: Page_1]

1 [SOURCE: Page_1]

24   hydraulic projects are more eco-efficient for functions related to biomass production. The analysis of [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   the water-energy-infrastructure nexus highlights the trade-offs between the two types of project. On [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   Agriculture is essential for humanity to meet its food requirements and employs more than a quarter [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   of the world's population (The World Bank, 2021). However, it generates multiple impacts on the [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   environment. Agricultural practices and soil occupation release between 15% and 24% of global [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions (Vermeulen et al., 2012). On one hand, agriculture is by far the [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   primary consumer of water in the world, reaching 70%-80% of the total consumption in the arid and [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   semi-arid zones (Fereres and Rabanales, 2007), and is one of the main drivers of biodiversity loss, [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   ecosystem destruction and freshwater pollution (Rockström et al., 2020). On the other hand, [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   agriculture faces challenges posed by worldwide issues such as climate change. This global change is [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   expected to induce rises in temperature and atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO$_2$) concentrations, [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   precipitation changes, more frequent occurrences of pests and diseases and of extreme heat and [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   drought, which in turn should affect crop yields and nutritional quality (Lobell and Gourdji, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   Hence, due to the global changes induced by human activity and a growing world population, food [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   security is becoming one of the challenges of the coming century and adaptation solutions must be [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   found (Vermeulen et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   Irrigation contributes to increasing agricultural yields in dryland areas and is one of the main [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   adaptation strategies implemented for agriculture in the face of climate change, as it makes it [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   possible to secure production yields in the face of increasing drought and temperature (Mbow et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   2019). Large-scale planning projects can be implemented to secure the water supply of agricultural [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   territories such as water transfers (Piao et al., 2010) or reservoirs (Gorguner and Kavvas, 2020). [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   These hydraulic infrastructures have a long lifetime, lasting around 50 years (Raluy et al., 2005b). [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   Therefore an ex ante environmental assessment is required to support local decision-making as well [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   as assist territorial planners for the selection of the least impactful alternative for the environment. [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   Indeed, territorial planners require a help-to-decision tool in order to take decisions without [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   regretting the construction of long-lasting infrastructures that present high economical costs and [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   environmental impacts. This assessment should take into account multiple categories of [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   environmental impacts and a lifecycle perspective to avoid pollution transfers within the water- [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   energy nexus (Sharif et al., 2019). Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) is a well-established and recognized [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   methodology to be applied for quantifying the environmental performance of products and services [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   (ISO, 2006a, 2006b). It is a multi-criteria environmental impact assessment method used to quantify [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   the potential impacts of the life cycles of human activities on ecosystems, mineral and fossil [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   resources as well as on human health. [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   Several studies have used LCA to compare the environmental impact of a variety of hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   structures (Byrne et al., 2017) such as water transfers (Muñoz et al., 2010), reservoirs (Ghimire et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   2014), groundwater pumping (Pradeleix et al., 2015), water reuse (Maeseele and Roux, 2021) and [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   desalinization plants (Raluy et al., 2005a). However, these studies calculate impacts for a functional [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   unit of 1 m^3 of water delivered to the end-user. The boundaries stop at the water supply gate. [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   Therefore, they do not grasp the entire range of services provided by irrigation, such as the territorial [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   socio-economic benefits resulting from agricultural yield conservation. These limitations are inherent [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   of the LCA framework which is a product oriented method at a microscale and do not allow for the [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   full integration of the territorial context, and multifunctionality (Loiseau et al., 2018). The choice of [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   water source as well as the functional unit for water supply systems have been found to greatly [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   contribute to the variability of the water supply impacts (Hospido et al., 2012). Moreover, the [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   impacts of hydraulic infrastructures vary according to certain physical parameters such as lifetimes or [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   length of the infrastructures, annual quantity of water supplied and energy consumption (Raluy et [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   al., 2005b). Consequently, it is also paramount to consider the variability in the design of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   hydraulic infrastructure parameters in order to select a specific land planning of water supply. [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   These limitations can be overcome by using Territorial LCA (T-LCA), an adaptation of the conventional [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   LCA framework, to assess the performance of a territory at a mesoscale and an associated land [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   planning scenario while considering its multifunctionality (e.g. economic, social or environmental [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   land use functions) (Loiseau et al., 2013). Compared to a conventional LCA, the starting point of [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   territorial LCA is no longer the definition of a main function for the studied systems, but the [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   definition of the boundaries of a territory, and associated planning scenarios. Subsequently, two [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   types of indicators must be quantified for each of these scenarios, i.e. environmental impacts and a [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   set of services provided. These indicators are then used to compute eco-efficiency ratios. These have [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   been defined by (Seppälä et al., 2005) as the ratio between services provided by the territory and its [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   environmental impacts. These ratios can help compare the environmental performances of different [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   agricultural scenarios such as in dairy farms (Iribarren et al., 2011) and vineyard irrigation (Canaj et [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   al., 2021). Eco-efficiency allows for a trade-off to be identified between economy and environment [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   to achieve a certain level in the environmental performance of a society (Huppes and Ishikawa, [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   2005). [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   A few studies have presently implemented territorial LCA approaches on agricultural areas to support [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   decision-making in the design of land planning scenarios for example in the French Brittany region [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   (Avadí et al., 2016) and Aube department (Borghino et al., 2021) or in the Walloon region of Belgium [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   (Ding et al., 2020). However, none of them have compared the impacts of planning scenarios that [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   integrate the hydraulic infrastructures. [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   From an environmental point of view, the main objective of this study is to assess the conditions [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   under which hydraulic projects can be selected as an efficient option for securing the water supply of [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   agricultural areas. This can be performed thanks to territorial LCA methodology. Generic conclusions [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   are drawn from a theoretical case study, located in the South of France. The case study is a [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   theoretical agricultural perimeter of 700 ha (size of a small municipality), where water resources [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   ought to become a challenging issue for agriculture within the coming next decades due to climate [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   change (Giorgi and Lionello, 2008). Three main planning alternatives (described in detail in section [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   2.1.2) will be compared based on a combination of crops and on the implementation or not of [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   hydraulic infrastructures. The latter rely on the use of surface water resources, distinguishing two [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   types of sources, and their dedicated infrastructure, i.e. a local resource consisting of storm water [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   run-off stored in an Agricultural Reservoir (AR), and an imported resource taken directly from a river [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   through an Inter-Basin Water Transfer (IBWT). These water projects have long lifetimes, and local [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   planners and decision-makers need to identify the “no regret” scenarios. This study also addresses [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   the water-energy-infrastructure nexus, and discusses the design conditions under which hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   projects can be considered, from an environmental point of view, as viable options for securing [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   agricultural territories. [SOURCE: Page_7]

430   4.      Discussion [SOURCE: Page_25]

431   4.1. Supporting decision making [SOURCE: Page_25]

432   This paper proposes a novel approach for defining the conditions under which the environmental [SOURCE: Page_25]

433   performances of a hydraulic project varies higher or lower than another type of project, while taking [SOURCE: Page_25]

434   into account biophysical constraints. The tipping lines that were computed according to the main [SOURCE: Page_25]

435   parameters of the designed inter-basin transfer illustrate that the impacts of this type of project [SOURCE: Page_25]

436   can vary both higher or lower than the impacts of a project based on a local water resource such as [SOURCE: Page_25]

437   an agricultural reservoir. Such studies are necessary for planners to identify the best options within a [SOURCE: Page_25]

438   given context, while other parameters related to agricultural practices or land use patterns could be [SOURCE: Page_25]

439   taken into account in future studies. [SOURCE: Page_25]

440   One main objective of the T-LCA framework is to compare the environmental performance of land [SOURCE: Page_25]

441   planning scenarios. For this purpose, eco-efficiency is a metric for quantifying both the services [SOURCE: Page_25]

442   provided by different scenarios and the resulting environmental impacts (Loiseau et al., 2018). In this [SOURCE: Page_25]

443   study, there is no scenario in which the eco-efficiency remains maximal for the three considered [SOURCE: Page_25]

444   territorial functions. These functions have been defined according to the scientific literature, and [SOURCE: Page_25]

445   other functions could be identified from real case studies in consultation with stakeholders. [SOURCE: Page_25]

446   This study also highlighted that T-LCA can be used by territorial planners to identify trade-offs in the [SOURCE: Page_25]

447   water-energy-infrastructure nexus. Section 3.2 inferred that an environmental analysis should not [SOURCE: Page_25]

448   focus on only one aspect of the water-energy-infrastructure nexus before a decision is taken [SOURCE: Page_25]

449   concerning the construction of a specific type of hydraulic infrastructure. Indeed, unlike the energy [SOURCE: Page_25]

450   and water components of the nexus, the analysis cannot be as straightforward for the infrastructure [SOURCE: Page_25]

451   component because IBWT does not perform better than AR at all the midpoint indicators. [SOURCE: Page_25]

25 [SOURCE: Page_25]

452   4.2. Considering spatio-temporal variability [SOURCE: Page_26]

453   4.2.1. In LCI [SOURCE: Page_26]

454   LCIs are adapted to the French context, particularly for agricultural production and the electricity [SOURCE: Page_26]

455   mix. The relatively low impacts of the water supply in comparison with farming practices are partly [SOURCE: Page_26]

456   due to the use of the French electricity mix, which comprises 70% nuclear-based electricity (ADEME, [SOURCE: Page_26]

457   2018), a low-carbon form of energy. Therefore, by choosing a more carbon-intensive electricity mix, [SOURCE: Page_26]

458   as found in China for example, the impacts of the water supply could increase, thus modifying the [SOURCE: Page_26]

459   results of this study (Leão et al., 2019a). For agricultural production, more detailed modelling would [SOURCE: Page_26]

460   allow for local specificities to be better accounted for, such as the pedo-climatic conditions of the [SOURCE: Page_26]

461   studied territory, or the agricultural practices implemented by the stakeholders. To enhance spatial [SOURCE: Page_26]

462   representativeness, regionalized LCI could be developed based on interviews of local stakeholders as [SOURCE: Page_26]

463   in Borghino et al. (2021) or spatial databases (Nitschelm et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_26]

464   In addition, the inter-annual variation of crop yields has not been considered in this study. Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_26]

465   yield data for grapevines were sourced from 2013 data (Agreste, 2016). Wheat and corn yields were [SOURCE: Page_26]

466   also based on data over an average of 4 years: 2005-2009 for corn (Koch and Salou, 2020) and 2014- [SOURCE: Page_26]

467   2019 for wheat (Nemecek et al., 2019). The required amount of water fluctuates between April and [SOURCE: Page_26]

468   August depending on the irrigation periods of the different crops, with increasing needs from June to [SOURCE: Page_26]

469   August. Yet, the water requirement of the cultivated area was overestimated, hence so was 𝐸𝐸𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴 . [SOURCE: Page_26]

470   Moreover, more water circulates through the IBWT during the irrigation period than during the rest [SOURCE: Page_26]

471   of the year, when agricultural needs are lower. The annual quantity of water circulating through the [SOURCE: Page_26]

472   IBWT was therefore also overestimated. [SOURCE: Page_26]

473   4.2.2. In LCIA [SOURCE: Page_26]

474   Spatial variability was only considered for quantifying the midpoint impact on water resources with [SOURCE: Page_26]

475   the AWARE model, at the sub-watershed scale. This significantly improves the calculation of the [SOURCE: Page_26]

476   impact, since there are strong regional discrepancies in terms of water resource availability. [SOURCE: Page_26]

26 [SOURCE: Page_26]

477   However, temporal variability was not taken into account in this study as annual characterization [SOURCE: Page_27]

478   factors (CF) were chosen for each of the sub-watersheds. Selecting monthly CFs has a significant [SOURCE: Page_27]

479   impact on the Water Scarcity (WS) indicator, as indicated in Table S. 9 of the S.I., where WS [SOURCE: Page_27]

480   calculated with annual or monthly CF are compared. Indeed, for Scenario 2, the consideration of a [SOURCE: Page_27]

481   winter CF for the water filling of the AR can lead to an avoided impact. Yet, these results are [SOURCE: Page_27]

482   preliminary and a better assessment of the local hydrology would be necessary. Indeed, the global [SOURCE: Page_27]

483   impacts of small reservoirs on hydrology are estimated to represent 5% of the mean discharge and [SOURCE: Page_27]

484   44% of the low flow (Habets et al., 2018), and should be fully addressed in AWARE calculations. [SOURCE: Page_27]

485   Spatialized CFs for site-dependent impacts such as eutrophication or acidification could also have [SOURCE: Page_27]

486   been employed for better territorial representativeness (Nitschelm et al., 2016). This could be done [SOURCE: Page_27]

487   by applying new regionalized LCIA methods such as IMPACT World + (Bulle et al., 2019) or LC-IMPACT [SOURCE: Page_27]

488   (Verones et al., 2020), when their full development will be achieved. [SOURCE: Page_27]

489   4.3. Water resource in LCIA methods [SOURCE: Page_27]

490   The impact induced by water resource consumption is crucial for the comparison between [SOURCE: Page_27]

491   performances of the studied scenarios. In the IMPACT World+ method, the terrestrial ecosystems are [SOURCE: Page_27]

492   only affected by water use if it is extracted from the ground because it affects the moisture of soils, [SOURCE: Page_27]

493   which in turn impacts the growth of given plant species (Zelm et al., 2011). Therefore, the impacts on [SOURCE: Page_27]

494   terrestrial ecosystems remain negligible in this study because the irrigation water originates from [SOURCE: Page_27]

495   surface water. In ReCiPe 2016, impacts of water consumption on terrestrial ecosystems are based on [SOURCE: Page_27]

496   the Net Primary Production (NPP) of vascular plants limited by water, and encompass all water [SOURCE: Page_27]

497   bodies (Pfister et al., 2009). For aquatic ecosystems, both endpoint LCIA methods are based on the [SOURCE: Page_27]

498   works of (Hanafiah et al., 2011) where the effects of changes in river discharge on freshwater fish [SOURCE: Page_27]

499   species are considered. Since lotic habitats are most vulnerable to consumptive water use [SOURCE: Page_27]

500   (Vörösmarty et al., 2010), it would also be worthwhile, in the long run, to use the HCP (Habitat [SOURCE: Page_27]

501   Change Potential) midpoint impact category in the study. This comprises the effects of water [SOURCE: Page_27]

502   consumption on instream species (Damiani et al., 2021). In LC-IMPACT, only the impacts of both [SOURCE: Page_27]

27 [SOURCE: Page_27]

503   surface water and groundwater consumption on wetlands are considered (Verones et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_28]

504   Recent investigations propose to consider the impacts on terrestrial ecosystems that entail the [SOURCE: Page_28]

505   conversion of a terrestrial habitat into an aquatic habitat (Dorber et al., 2020). Applied to AR, these [SOURCE: Page_28]

506   new spatialized CFs would increase the impacts of AR on terrestrial ecosystems while having a [SOURCE: Page_28]

507   beneficial effect on aquatic ecosystems. . [SOURCE: Page_28]

508   4.4. Taking into account the effects of global changes [SOURCE: Page_28]

509   Within the next decades, global changes such as climate change are expected to intensify the [SOURCE: Page_28]

510   variations in meteorological parameters (Mbow et al., 2019), which, in turn, could lead to a rise in [SOURCE: Page_28]

511   inter-annual variations of the scenario eco-efficiencies. Therefore, prospective T-LCA of land planning [SOURCE: Page_28]

512   scenarios with long-lasting infrastructures would result in the use of more robust data and in the [SOURCE: Page_28]

513   consideration of potential environmental feedbacks. The effects of the different types of stress [SOURCE: Page_28]

514   induced by the evolution of meteorological conditions on crop development should be taken into [SOURCE: Page_28]

515   account. Prospective crop yields could be sourced from real-life experiments, for example from a [SOURCE: Page_28]

516   phytotron (Niero et al., 2015) or from crop growth models such as Aquacrop (El Chami and Daccache, [SOURCE: Page_28]

517   2015) and CropSyst (Tendall and Gaillard, 2015). Due to the spatio-temporal dynamics of water [SOURCE: Page_28]

518   resources, inter-annual variations of water availability should also be taken into account. A projection [SOURCE: Page_28]

519   of future availability could be sourced from databases as has been done by (Leão et al., 2019b) with [SOURCE: Page_28]

520   the Prospective Water-Supply Mix (P-WSmix). A modification in the future water availability would [SOURCE: Page_28]

521   lead to a difference between the values of present and future AWARE CF. [SOURCE: Page_28]

522   5.      Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_28]

523   This study aimed to assess, from an environmental point of view, the conditions under which [SOURCE: Page_28]

524   hydraulic projects can be considered as an efficient option in order to secure the water supply of [SOURCE: Page_28]

525   agricultural areas. However, as the different scenarios do not provide the same range of services, it is [SOURCE: Page_28]

526   impossible to perform conventional LCA for comparison purposes on the basis of the same functional [SOURCE: Page_28]

527   unit. The present paper demonstrates the feasibility and relevance of the territorial LCA approach as [SOURCE: Page_28]

28 [SOURCE: Page_28]

528   well as the associated eco-efficiency concept for comparing such scenarios. This was achieved by [SOURCE: Page_29]

529   comparing three different theoretical land-planning scenarios, i.e. without irrigation (scenario 0), [SOURCE: Page_29]

530   irrigated by an IBWT (scenario 1) or by an AR (scenario 2). The major highlights and main [SOURCE: Page_29]

531   recommendations and challenges to conduct territorial LCA approaches are listed in the following: [SOURCE: Page_29]

532       •   T-LCA provides eco-efficiency metrics that provide exhaustive information about the [SOURCE: Page_29]

533           environmental performance of land-planning scenarios for three territorial functions in this [SOURCE: Page_29]

534           paper, “agricultural turnover”, “mass of agricultural products” and “land occupation”. [SOURCE: Page_29]

535           Depending on the selected function, the environmental performance of the three scenarios [SOURCE: Page_29]

536           vary. For functions “agricultural turnover” and “land occupation”, scenario 0 has a better [SOURCE: Page_29]

537           eco-efficiency over all the endpoints indicators. For function “mass of agricultural products”, [SOURCE: Page_29]

538           the results are more contrasted : scenario 0 is better on Fossil and Nuclear Energy Use, [SOURCE: Page_29]

539           scenario 1 is better for Human Health and Ecosystems, and scenario 2 is better on Mineral [SOURCE: Page_29]

540           Resource Use. These outputs allow the identification of trade-offs between different [SOURCE: Page_29]

541           functions and environmental impacts, and can support local planners and stakeholders to [SOURCE: Page_29]

542           select scenarios that maximize the main function of their project while limiting the effects on [SOURCE: Page_29]

543           other functions. [SOURCE: Page_29]

544       •   The T-LCA framework identifies trade-offs in the water-energy-infrastructure nexus. Overall, [SOURCE: Page_29]

545           the water for irrigation supplied from IBWT performs better than from AR regarding most of [SOURCE: Page_29]

546           the end-point indicators (Ecosystem, Human Health and Fossil & Nuclear Energy Use), [SOURCE: Page_29]

547           because only a part of its total impact is allocated to the studied territory. Results are less [SOURCE: Page_29]

548           advantageous for the Mineral Resource Use, due to the high amount of cast-iron necessary [SOURCE: Page_29]

549           in IBWT infrastructure. [SOURCE: Page_29]

550       •   Moreover, T-LCA could also apply to the eco-design of large-scale hydraulic infrastructures. [SOURCE: Page_29]

551           Indeed this framework can provide flexible environmental impact assessments (i.e. tipping [SOURCE: Page_29]

552           lines) that take into account several design parameters, thus contributing to the choice of an [SOURCE: Page_29]

553           infrastructure design. [SOURCE: Page_29]

29 [SOURCE: Page_29]

554        •     The main limitation of this study is that the LCIs are built according to static environmental [SOURCE: Page_30]

555              conditions. Therefore the LCIs do not account for the effects of environmental dynamics (i.e. [SOURCE: Page_30]

556              climate, hydrology and soil) nor do they include the possibility of changes in the ecosphere [SOURCE: Page_30]

557              (e.g. climate change or water resource depletion), which are expected to further intensify [SOURCE: Page_30]

558              during the long lifetime of the hydraulic infrastructures. For this reason, it is necessary to [SOURCE: Page_30]

559              involve direct and indirect environmental feedback into the T-LCA framework for the choice, [SOURCE: Page_30]

560              without risk of regret, of a specific type of long-lasting hydraulic infrastructure. [SOURCE: Page_30]
