21   Choosing a suitable process-oriented eco-hydrological model is essential for obtaining reliable simulations 22   of hydrological processes. Determining soil hydraulic and solute transport parameters is another 23   fundamental prerequisite. Research discussing the impact of considering evaporation fractionation on 24   parameter estimation and practical applications of isotope transport models is limited. In this study, we 25   analyzed parameter estimation results for two datasets for humid and arid conditions using the isotope 26   transport model in HYDRUS-1D, in which we either did or did not consider fractionation. The global 27   sensitivity analysis using the Morris and Sobol' methods and the parameter estimation using the Particle 28   Swarm Optimization algorithm highlight the significant impact of considering evaporation fractionation on 29   inverse modeling. The Kling-Gupta efficiency (KGE) index for isotope data can increase by 0.09 and 1.49 30   for the humid and arid datasets, respectively, when selecting suitable fractionation scenarios. Differences in 31   estimated parameters propagate into the results of two practical applications of stable isotope tracing: i) the 32   assessment of root water uptake (RWU) and drainage travel times (i.e., the time elapsed between water 33   entering the soil profile as precipitation and leaving it as transpiration or drainage) in the lysimeter (humid 34   conditions) and ii) evaporation estimation in a controlled experimental soil column (arid conditions). The 35   peak displacement method with optimized longitudinal dispersivity provides much lower travel times than 36   those obtained using the particle tracking algorithm in HYDRUS-1D. Considering evaporation fractionation 37   using the Craig-Gordon (CG) and Gonfiantini models is likely to result in estimates of older water ages for 38   RWU than the no fractionation scenario. The isotope mass balance method that uses the isotopic 39   composition profile simulated by HYDRUS-1D while considering fractionation using the CG and 40   Gonfiantini models, or the measured evaporation isotope flux, provides comparable results in evaporation 41   estimation as the HYDRUS-1D water mass balance method and direct laboratory measurements. 49   1 Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

50           Reliable water balance simulations in the vadose zone are important to understand and forecast the [SOURCE: Page_2]

51   impact of anthropogenic disturbances such as global warming and land-use change on soil water storage, [SOURCE: Page_2]

52   groundwater recharge, and evapotranspiration. A detailed mechanistic understanding of water ﬂuxes in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

53   vadose zone could support optimal and efficient management strategies for promoting the long-term [SOURCE: Page_2]

54   sustainability of water resources and associated ecosystem functions (Penna et al., 2018). For example, the [SOURCE: Page_2]

55   exact quantification of evaporation affects water availability for plants (Nelson et al., 2020) and constrains [SOURCE: Page_2]

56   groundwater recharge (Condon et al., 2020). However, the conventional methods (e.g., pan experiments) [SOURCE: Page_2]

57   for estimating evaporation fluxes often require extensive field monitoring of water flow, which is often time- [SOURCE: Page_2]

58   consuming, expensive, labor-demanding, and affected by considerable uncertainty (Skrzypek et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_2]

59           Stable isotopes of hydrogen (2H) and oxygen (18O) are widely used to trace water fluxes across the [SOURCE: Page_2]

60   critical zone and can be expressed as isotopic ratios, 2H/1H and 18O/16O by using the δ notation (i.e., δ2H and [SOURCE: Page_2]

61   δ18O). The isotopic composition of shallow soil water provides insights into evaporation fractionation [SOURCE: Page_2]

62   characteristics. This information can be easily used to calculate corresponding evaporation fluxes. For [SOURCE: Page_2]

63   example, Skrzypek et al. (2015) combined the equations for evaporation estimation based on the revised [SOURCE: Page_2]

64   Craig-Gordon model (Craig and Gordon, 1965) and developed a software Hydrocalculator. Using this [SOURCE: Page_2]

65   software, they estimated evaporation losses and validated its results using pan measurements. This method [SOURCE: Page_2]

66   has been extended to soil evaporation estimation. For example, Sprenger et al. (2017) estimated that [SOURCE: Page_2]

67   evaporation was about 5 and 10% of infiltrating water in the heath and Scots pine soils, respectively. [SOURCE: Page_2]

86   hydrological models with appropriate soil and vegetation parameters and known boundary and initial [SOURCE: Page_3]

87   conditions. However, direct measurements of soil hydraulic and transport parameters required by such [SOURCE: Page_3]

88   models are time-consuming and labor-demanding. Therefore, such parameters are commonly obtained using [SOURCE: Page_3]

89   inverse modeling by minimizing the errors between easily-measured state variables and fluxes (e.g., soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

90   water contents and pressure heads at different soil depths or leachate water volumes) and corresponding [SOURCE: Page_3]

102   Levenberg-Marquardt Optimization (LMO) proved to be very efficient and was, therefore, implemented in [SOURCE: Page_4]

103   HYDRUS (Šimůnek et al., 2008). However, the LMO is sensitive to the initial parameter values provided [SOURCE: Page_4]

105   optimization algorithms, such as Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), have become more widespread over [SOURCE: Page_4]

257           Five soil hydraulic parameters (i.e., 𝜃𝑟 , 𝜃𝑠 , 𝑛, 𝛼, and 𝐾𝑠 ) need to be optimized for each layer of the [SOURCE: Page_13]

258   soil profile to simulate water flow using the HYDRUS-1D model. The residual water content 𝜃𝑟 was set to [SOURCE: Page_13]

259   zero to reduce the number of fitting parameters. To simulate isotope transport in the soil, the longitudinal [SOURCE: Page_13]

260   dispersivity 𝜆 also needs to be optimized. Since only the isotopic composition of the lysimeter discharge [SOURCE: Page_13]

261   was measured in the Stumpp et al. (2012) dataset, the dispersivity of three individual soil layers cannot be [SOURCE: Page_13]

262   estimated. Therefore, only one longitudinal dispersivity for the entire lysimeter was estimated. Therefore, [SOURCE: Page_13]

263   the total number of parameters p was 13 and 5 for the Stumpp et al. (2012) and Braud et al. (2009a) datasets, [SOURCE: Page_13]

264   respectively. The global sensitivity analysis (GSA) using both Morris and Sobol' methods was conducted in [SOURCE: Page_13]

265   this study to determine the most influential parameters and their interactions. The detailed description of [SOURCE: Page_13]

266   these two methods is shown in Method S$_2$ in the Supplementary Material. [SOURCE: Page_13]

267           The sensitivity analysis was conducted using Python's Sensitivity Analysis Library (SALib) [SOURCE: Page_13]

268   (Herman and Usher, 2017). The script produces the input parameter space, overwrites the input parameters [SOURCE: Page_13]

269   file, and runs the executable module of HYDRUS-1D. For each simulation of the Stumpp et al. (2012) [SOURCE: Page_13]

270   dataset, five Kling-Gupta efficiency (KGE) indices for different evaluation indicators were calculated, [SOURCE: Page_13]

271   including for the time series of the bottom water flux (KGE_bf), the soil water content at different depths [SOURCE: Page_13]

272   (KGE_wc), the bottom water isotopic composition (KGE_wi), the water retention curves (KGE_rc), and the [SOURCE: Page_13]

273   average of the four KGE values (KGE_avg). For each simulation of the Braud et al. (2009a) dataset, three [SOURCE: Page_13]

274   Kling-Gupta efficiency (KGE) indices for different evaluation indicators were calculated, including the final [SOURCE: Page_13]

275   soil water content profile (KGE_wc), the final water isotopic composition profile (KGE_wi), and the [SOURCE: Page_13]

276   average of the two KGE values (KGE_avg). The KGE index compares the correlation coefficient (r), the [SOURCE: Page_13]

277   ratio of mean values (β), and the ratio of variances (γ) between simulated and observed data. The value of [SOURCE: Page_13]

278   the KGE index is always smaller or equal to 1. The higher the KGE value, the better fit between the simulated [SOURCE: Page_13]

279   and observed values. The positive and negative KGE values are often considered "good" and "bad" solutions [SOURCE: Page_13]

280   (Knoben et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_13]

286           Non-convergent      runs    in   GSA     are   a    frequent   problem     when     using    nonlinear [SOURCE: Page_14]

287   environmental/hydrological models, and there are no clear indications on how to handle these "unfeasible" [SOURCE: Page_14]

289   biased conclusions, especially if non-convergent runs lie in informative regions of the parameter space. [SOURCE: Page_14]

290   Recently, Sheikholeslami et al. (2019) compared strategies such as median substitution, single nearest- [SOURCE: Page_14]

291   neighbor, or response surface modeling (Brunetti et al., 2017) to fill in for model crashes. Their results show [SOURCE: Page_14]

293   to reliable results and outperforms other strategies. We implemented a similar approach in the present work [SOURCE: Page_14]

294   but with important differences. In particular: [SOURCE: Page_14]

295       1. For each non-convergent point, we calculated its Euclidean distance from all other convergent [SOURCE: Page_14]

296           points in the GSA sample. [SOURCE: Page_14]

297       2. Convergent points were ordered in ascending order (i.e., from the closest to the farthest). [SOURCE: Page_14]

298       3. The 100 closest convergent points were used to train a response surface surrogate based on the [SOURCE: Page_14]

299           Kriging Partial Least Squares method (KPLS) (Bouhlel et al., 2016), which outperforms traditional [SOURCE: Page_14]

300           kriging on high-dimensional problems. [SOURCE: Page_14]

301       4. The trained KPLS surrogate was finally used to interpolate non-convergent runs in the original GSA [SOURCE: Page_14]

302           sample. [SOURCE: Page_14]

303   The use of multiple localized surrogates allowed for better reconstruction of the topological features of the [SOURCE: Page_14]

304   response surface in the vicinity of the non-convergent points. [SOURCE: Page_14]

316   2.4 Parameter optimization [SOURCE: Page_15]

317           The Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) algorithm was used in this study for parameter [SOURCE: Page_15]

318   optimization. In the PSO, a swarm of candidate solutions is moved around in the search space according to [SOURCE: Page_15]

319   a few equations. The movement of the particles is guided by the optimal position of themselves and the [SOURCE: Page_15]

320   whole swarm. Once improved positions are discovered, they are used to guide the swarm's movement. This [SOURCE: Page_15]

321   process is repeated until the global optimal position that all particles tend to follow is found (Shi and [SOURCE: Page_15]

322   Eberhart, 1998). [SOURCE: Page_15]

324   𝑤=-0.444) from Brunetti et al. (2016) were used in this study. The number of particle swarm and iterations [SOURCE: Page_15]

325   are 40 and 200, respectively. [SOURCE: Page_15]

326           The PySwarm Library in Python was used for the PSO. The process was similar to the GSA, except [SOURCE: Page_15]

327   that reduced ranges of parameters were used. In this way, the number of potential local minima is reduced, [SOURCE: Page_15]

328   and the convergence improves. Only the set of parameters leading to the maximum KGE_avg (i.e., minimum [SOURCE: Page_15]

576   to the initial value) distribution of soil water throughout the profile in the Non_Frac scenario (Fig. 9). In [SOURCE: Page_29]

577   fractionation scenarios, the peak value of the isotopic composition profile was inversely proportional to the [SOURCE: Page_29]

578   dispersivity value (Fig. 9 and Table 3), which is consistent with the conclusions from Braud et al. (2009b). [SOURCE: Page_29]

579           The isotopic composition profiles and the KGE_wi values differed dramatically (reached 1.48) [SOURCE: Page_29]

580   between different fractionation scenarios (Fig. 9 and Table 3). This implies that considering evaporation [SOURCE: Page_29]

29 [SOURCE: Page_29]

581   fractionation significantly impacts the isotopic composition profile in this example (arid conditions). The [SOURCE: Page_30]

582   Meas_Frac scenario had the highest KGE_wi (i.e., for the water isotopic composition), followed by the [SOURCE: Page_30]

583   Gon_Frac, and then CG_Frac, while the Non_Frac scenario performed the worst. This is understandable [SOURCE: Page_30]

584   since evaporation fractionation could not be neglected, and the measured evaporation isotope flux is the [SOURCE: Page_30]

585   most accurate for this example (Braud et al. 2009b). [SOURCE: Page_30]

586   4.2 Impacts of evaporation fractionation on practical applications [SOURCE: Page_30]

587   4.2.1 Estimation of drainage and RWU travel times [SOURCE: Page_30]

588           Differences in water travel times were not evident among different fractionation scenarios (Table [SOURCE: Page_30]

589   4), since the numerator in Eq. 2 is much larger than the denominator in the peak displacement method. As a [SOURCE: Page_30]

590   result, water travel times were similar for different fractionation scenarios despite a very different [SOURCE: Page_30]

591   dispersivity. However, for the particle tracking method based on water flow calculations, differences in [SOURCE: Page_30]

592   water travel times were evident among different fractionation scenarios (Table 2), despite their similar KGE [SOURCE: Page_30]

593   values (Table 1). In addition, differences in estimated soil hydraulic parameters may also cause [SOURCE: Page_30]

594   discrepancies in TTs of individual precipitation events and the temporal origin of water for RWU (Figs. S$_8$ [SOURCE: Page_30]

595   and 7~8). [SOURCE: Page_30]

596           Overall, the particle tracking method gave much higher travel times than the peak displacement [SOURCE: Page_30]

597   method (Table 2). Different results by these two methods may be associated with different rainfall events [SOURCE: Page_30]

598   selected for these calculations. The peak-displacement method calculates the travel times during frequent [SOURCE: Page_30]

599   and heavy precipitation events (precipitation events from 2005~2006), while particle tracking assesses the [SOURCE: Page_30]

600   travel times over longer periods (Zhou et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_30]

601           Notably, water travel times in the Non_Frac scenario obtained by the particle tracking method are [SOURCE: Page_30]

602   most consistent with the approximate estimate of 41weeks provided by previous studies with similar crops [SOURCE: Page_30]

603   and areas (Stumpp et al., 2009). It is worth mentioning that Asadollahi et al. (2020) pointed out that the SAS [SOURCE: Page_30]

604   approach was a good alternative for estimating water travel times when the system was too complicated to [SOURCE: Page_30]

605   be fully described by the HYDRUS-1D model. Our study demonstrates that the water-flow-based particle 30 [SOURCE: Page_30]

606   tracking module in HYDRUS-1D is another promising way of constraining estimation errors in water travel [SOURCE: Page_31]

607   times, especially when there is not enough isotope data to calibrate the lumped or physically based isotope [SOURCE: Page_31]

608   transport models. [SOURCE: Page_31]

609           In contrast, considering fractionation using either the CG or Gonfiantini models will likely led to [SOURCE: Page_31]

610   larger water travel time estimates than in the Non_Frac scenario (Table 2). This is because fractionation [SOURCE: Page_31]

611   scenarios result in a larger dispersivity (to increase the dispersion of isotopes) or lower hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_31]

612   conductivities (to decrease convection of isotopes), as discussed in Section 4.1. [SOURCE: Page_31]

613   4.2.2 Estimation of the evaporation flux [SOURCE: Page_31]

614           For evaporation estimation, the isotope-transport-based methods for different fractionation [SOURCE: Page_31]

615   (CG_Frac, Gon_Frac, and Meas_Frac) scenarios can give comparable results to the water-flow-based [SOURCE: Page_31]

616   methods, including laboratory measurements and the HYDRUS-1D water balance. In contrast, the [SOURCE: Page_31]

617   Non_Frac scenario can produce similar results only when using the water-flow-based method (HYDRUS- [SOURCE: Page_31]

618   1D water balance). However, since the measured evaporation flux was used as the upper boundary condition [SOURCE: Page_31]

619   in this (arid conditions) example, it is not clear whether the similarity between estimated evaporation [SOURCE: Page_31]

620   amounts using the HYDRUS-1D water balance method in the Non_Frac and fractionation (CG_Frac, [SOURCE: Page_31]

621   Gon_Frac, Meas_Frac) scenarios was due to this boundary condition, or because actual soil hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_31]

622   conductivities and water contents were continuously adjusted to actual soil fluxes without ever reaching full [SOURCE: Page_31]

623   saturation. However, it is clear that evaporation fractionation has a significant impact on the isotope transport [SOURCE: Page_31]

624   and isotopic compositions in arid conditions, as shown in Fig. 9. Therefore, the direct use of simulated [SOURCE: Page_31]

625   isotopic compositions in the Non_Frac scenario may result in large biases in practical applications in arid [SOURCE: Page_31]

626   conditions, as seen from the evaporation estimation results in Table 4. [SOURCE: Page_31]

627   4.3 Comparison of different climate conditions and implications for future studies [SOURCE: Page_31]

628           The soil saturated hydraulic conductivities (𝐾𝑠 ), and the retention curve shape parameter (𝛼) were [SOURCE: Page_31]

629   the parameters most affected by the consideration of evaporation fractionation for the humid condition [SOURCE: Page_31]

630   dataset (Table 1). For the arid condition dataset, these were the dispersivity (𝜆) and the retention curve shape 31 [SOURCE: Page_31]

631   parameter (𝛼) (Table 3). This is likely associated with the effects of soil texture on retention curves and soil [SOURCE: Page_32]

632   moisture conditions in different climate zones (Radcliffe and Šimůnek, 2018). Overall, soil water retention [SOURCE: Page_32]

633   and hydraulic conductivity curves (Fig. S12) in different fractionation scenarios were more similar for the [SOURCE: Page_32]

634   Braud et al. (2009a) dataset than the Stumpp et al. (2012) dataset (Fig. S$_8$). One reason is that the measured [SOURCE: Page_32]

635   evaporation flux was used as the upper BC in the former, which constrains the model flexibility. Another [SOURCE: Page_32]

636   reason is that there was only one soil layer in the Braud et al. (2009a) dataset, while there were three soil [SOURCE: Page_32]

637   layers in the Stumpp et al. (2012) dataset. There is likely a compensation effect between the parameters of [SOURCE: Page_32]

638   different layers, and thus the parameter values can vary more in the Stumpp et al. (2012) dataset. [SOURCE: Page_32]

639           While evaporation fractionation plays an essential role in parameter estimation in both cases, its [SOURCE: Page_32]

640   impact on model performance is relatively small in the example for humid conditions but more significant [SOURCE: Page_32]

641   in the example for arid conditions, as discussed in Sections 4.1 and 4.2. This is expected since evaporation [SOURCE: Page_32]

642   plays a more important role in the water balance of the arid dataset (Table 4) than in the humid dataset (Fig. [SOURCE: Page_32]

643   S13). These conclusions also indirectly validate the common assumption that evaporation fractionation may [SOURCE: Page_32]

644   be neglected in some humid regions but not in arid areas (Sprenger et al., 2016a). [SOURCE: Page_32]

645           However, parameter sensitivities and optimization results reflect complex combined effects of [SOURCE: Page_32]

646   climate, soil, and vegetation characteristics. The isotopic composition of soil water is not only affected by [SOURCE: Page_32]

647   evaporation fractionation, but also by the mixing of rainfall with soil water and different flow paths in the [SOURCE: Page_32]

648   soil, leading to its variations with depths and time. The insufficient knowledge of the spatiotemporal isotope [SOURCE: Page_32]

649   distribution (e.g., in shallow and deep depths or during different stages of evaporation) and the lack of such [SOURCE: Page_32]

650   information in the objective function may bias the parameter estimation results. For example, not including [SOURCE: Page_32]

651   isotopes from different soil depths within the soil profile might lead to an underestimation of evaporation [SOURCE: Page_32]

652   fractionation in general, biased estimation of water mixing within the profile, and a similar isotopic signal [SOURCE: Page_32]

653   in the discharge. In this study, we considered either the time series of the isotopic composition of the bottom [SOURCE: Page_32]

654   flux in the Stumpp et al. (2012) dataset or the final isotopic composition profile in the Braud et al. (2009a) [SOURCE: Page_32]

655   dataset. In addition, observation data types and spatiotemporal distributions are different for these two [SOURCE: Page_32]

32 [SOURCE: Page_32]

656   datasets, and this difference may affect the comparison of parameter estimation results between different [SOURCE: Page_33]

657   climate conditions. [SOURCE: Page_33]

658           The GSA was carried out for the Non_Frac scenario for the Stumpp et al. (2012) dataset and the [SOURCE: Page_33]

659   Meas_Frac scenario for the Braud et al. (2009a) dataset because they were closest to the experimental [SOURCE: Page_33]

660   conditions. This implicitly assumes that sensitivity remains the same for different model structures. [SOURCE: Page_33]

661   However, different model structures may affect GSA and PSO results, which should be further explored. [SOURCE: Page_33]

662   Last but not least, the impacts of possible transpiration fractionation, as observed in multiple studies, should [SOURCE: Page_33]

663   also be included in future analyses (e.g., Barbeta et al., 2019). Therefore, it is difficult to generalize the [SOURCE: Page_33]

664   results of this study or apply them to other specific conditions. [SOURCE: Page_33]

665   5 Summary and Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_33]

666           In this study, we analyzed parameter estimation results for two datasets collected under humid and [SOURCE: Page_33]

667   arid climate conditions using the isotope transport model, in which we either did or did not consider [SOURCE: Page_33]

668   evaporation fractionation. The global sensitivity analysis using the Morris and Sobol' methods and the [SOURCE: Page_33]

669   parameter estimation using the Particle Swarm Optimization algorithm highlight the significant impacts of [SOURCE: Page_33]

670   considering evaporation fractionation on parameter estimation and model performance. The KGE index for [SOURCE: Page_33]

671   isotope data can increase by 0.09 and 1.49 for the humid and arid datasets, respectively, when selecting [SOURCE: Page_33]

672   suitable fractionation scenarios. [SOURCE: Page_33]

673           The impact of different parameter values estimated when considering (or not) evaporation [SOURCE: Page_33]

674   fractionation propagates into practical applications of isotope transport modeling. The isotope-transport- [SOURCE: Page_33]

675   based method (peak displacement) gave much lower water travel times than the water-flow-based method [SOURCE: Page_33]

676   (particle tracking) for humid conditions. Considering fractionation using the CG and Gonfiantini models [SOURCE: Page_33]

677   will likely lead to larger water travel time estimates and ages for RWU. For arid conditions example, the [SOURCE: Page_33]

678   isotope-transport-based method (isotope mass balance) can provide comparable evaporation estimates for [SOURCE: Page_33]

679   different fractionation (CG_Frac, Gon_Frac, Meas_Frac) scenarios as the water-flow-based methods [SOURCE: Page_33]

33 [SOURCE: Page_33]

680   (HYDRUS-1D water balance and laboratory measurements). In contrast, the Non_Frac scenario can produce [SOURCE: Page_34]

681   reasonable evaporation estimation only when using the water-flow-based method. [SOURCE: Page_34]

682           The direct use of simulated isotopic compositions in the no fractionation scenario may result in large [SOURCE: Page_34]

683   biases in practical applications in arid regions where evaporation fractionation is more extensive than in [SOURCE: Page_34]

684   humid areas. Integrated use of water-flow and isotope-transport-based methods may provide mutual [SOURCE: Page_34]

685   validation and be an important way to avoid this problem. This research may shed some light on future [SOURCE: Page_34]

686   laboratory and field experimental designs regarding the practical applications of the isotope-transport [SOURCE: Page_34]

687   modeling in different climate zones. [SOURCE: Page_34]
