### 5.   Nathalie Bréda : Université de Lorraine, AgroParisTech, INRAE, SILVA, F-54000 Nancy, France

Abstract: [SOURCE: Page_2]

Since the 1980-90’s episodes of decline in Central European Forests, forest condition has been surveyed thanks to the trans-national network the International Co-operative Programme on Assessment and Monitoring of Air Pollution Effects on Forests (ICP Forests). It has been traditionally accepted that leaf loss is directly related to impairment of physiological condition of the tree. A few studies tried to correlate crown condition and growth trends while others concentrated on linking annual growth with crown observation at one date clustered into fertility classes. However, none focussed on the high frequency synchronism between leaf loss from annual network observations and annual radial growth issued from dendrochronology. Therefore, we jointly studied annual leaf loss observations and tree-ring width measurements on 715 common beech (Fagus sylvatica L.) trees distributed in the French part of the ICP monitoring network. Detrended inter-annual variations of leaf loss and tree-ring width index were used as response variables in the machine-learning algorithm Random Forest to investigate a common response to abiotic (current and lagged) and biotic hazards, to test the extent to which leaf loss helped to predict inter-annual variations in radial growth. Using Random Forest was effective to identify a common sensitivity to soil water deficit at different time lags. Previous-year climatic variables tended to control leaf loss while radial growth was more sensitive to current-year soil water deficit. Late frost damages were observed on crown condition in mountainous regions but no impact was detected on radial growth. Few significant biotic damages were observed on growth or leaf loss. Leaf loss series did not show a clear common signal among trees from a plot as did radial growth and captured fewer pointer years. Radial growth index did not fall below normal until a 20% leaf loss was reached. However, this threshold is driven by a few extreme leaf loss events. As shown by our joint analysis of leaf loss and radial growth pointer years, no relationship occurred in cases of slight or moderate defoliation. Crown condition is a poorer descriptor of tree vitality than radial growth. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Keywords: Dendrochronology; climate; drought; leaf loss; Random Forest; Fagus sylvatica; forest health monitoring; water balance. [SOURCE: Page_2]

1    1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

2    Forests have to cope with a variety of disturbances including acute stressors (climatic 3    events, insect outbreaks or fungal leaf diseases) and chronic pressures (atmospheric 4    pollution, root rots, adverse trends in temperatures) (Seidl et al., 2017). Since the 1980s, 5    forest decline in Europe has triggered a need for robust indicators to help to monitor forest 6    condition. [SOURCE: Page_3]

7    Diagnosing forest health has mainly been based on descriptive criteria related to primary 8    growth, summarized under the term "crown condition". These descriptors include crown 9    morphology, abnormal leaf discolouration and estimates of leaf deficit relative to a so-called 10   reference tree, later referred to as "leaf loss" (Nageleisen, 2005; Roloff, 1985). [SOURCE: Page_3]

11   Since the end of the 1970s, and the impressive episodes of decline in Central European 12   forests, several forest health monitoring networks have been set up. The first in France was 13   set up on a 16x^1 km grid, only in the mountains all along the eastern border, where foresters 14   were concerned by a severe health decline in fir.Observations were performed from 1983 to 15   1993 to observe the recovery of coniferous tree health (Barthod, 1994). In 1985, a trans- 16   national network, the International Co-operative Programme on Assessment and Monitoring 17   of Air Pollution Effects on Forests (ICP Forests), was created under the United Nations 18   Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE) (Mueller-Edzards et al., 1997). Forest condition 19   has therefore been monitored since 1986/87 on a large scale, though at a low intensity 20   (Level I), thanks to a systematic network of plots located at the nodes of a 16x^16 km grid in 21   all European forests. Annual visual observations of crown condition are carried out for 20 22   trees on these plots according to two criteria: leaf loss compared to a full folied “reference 23   tree” , and abnormal leaf colouration (Eichhorn et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   Visual assessment of crown condition from the ground has historically raised the 25   methodological problem of the experts’ objectivity (Innes et al., 1993). Despite this, many 26   studies have based their analyses on such data and used them to interpret inter-annual 27   variations and trends in crown condition as a function of environmental constraints. Through 28   a cross-sectional approach, van Leuween et al. (2000) proved the limited effect of air 29   pollution on crown condition compared to the effect of tree age and climate. Other studies, 30   based on cross-sectional mean-term and inter-annual resolution approaches, showed the 31   negative impact of drought events on tree crown condition (de Vries et al., 2014; Ferretti et 32   al., 2014; Popa et al., 2017; Seidling, 2007; Solberg, 2004). [SOURCE: Page_3]

ICP technical 37   and executive reports express damage results as the percentage of trees with more than 38   25% defoliation. However, to our knowledge, it has never been properly demonstrated that 39   this threshold is an appropriate breakpoint for deciding whether a tree is in good or poor 40   condition. Assessing the validity of this threshold was one goal of this study. [SOURCE: Page_4]

41   Radial growth, is also a well-established and widely-used indicator of tree health and 42   physiological status (Dobbertin, 2005). Research on the relationship between radial growth 43   and crown condition has shown no clear pattern. Authors discriminate tree populations 44   according to their growth performance and then retrieve characteristic leaf loss thresholds 45   that have a significant effect on radial growth a posteriori. However, the thresholds for growth 46   decline are species- and site-dependent (Becker, 1987; Bert et al., 1990). [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   Very few studies have investigated the connection between inter-annual variations in crown 48   condition and radial growth. Dittmar and Elling (2007) showed a decrease in increment with 49   leaf loss but their findings cannot be generalized because their study was restricted to only 50   12 trees. Seidling (2012) found a low correlation between inter-annual beech growth and leaf 51   loss and questioned using leaf loss to explain functional relationships between the two 52   indicators at the plot level. Solberg (1999) also found a weak correlation between tree- 53   specific long-term means for crown condition and growth. [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   This study focuses on common beech, one of the most important and widespread 55   broadleaved trees in France and Europe. In beech, each year, a new shoot grows from a 56   dormant bud at the end of the previous year's shoot. There are two types of shoots: 57   exploratory (long) and exploitation (short) shoots. The long, fast-growing exploratory shoots 58   extend into the space around the tree and are therefore located in the upper periphery of the 59   crown (Teissier Du Cros et al., 1981), where leaf loss is usually assessed. Therefore, leaf 60   loss as compared to a reference tree and assessed on the upper third of the crown, results 61   from abnormal primary development (short elongation, impaired crown development, bud 62   burst deficit), while cambial activity generates the secondary growth responsible for radial 63   increment. [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   There is solid knowledge of radial growth and crown development responses in beech to 65   climate. On one hand, water deficits impair twig elongation (Power, 1994; Stribley and 66   Ashmore, 2002) and reduce the number, rate of expansion and final size of the leaves 67   (Zahner, 1968). 260       2.6. Statistics [SOURCE: Page_13]

261   To provide deeper understanding of the relationship between leaf loss and tree-ring width, [SOURCE: Page_13]

262   pointer years were detected on raw tree-ring and leaf-loss series. Analysing pointer years is [SOURCE: Page_13]

263   typical in dendrochronological studies (Becker et al., 1994; Schweingruber et al., 1990). [SOURCE: Page_13]

264   Pointer years represent a growth reaction to abrupt changes in environmental conditions and [SOURCE: Page_14]

265   are usually expressed as a relative change in growth, in percentage. Usually, pointer years [SOURCE: Page_14]

266   correspond to a calendar year when, at least, 75% of the trees have the same sign of change [SOURCE: Page_14]

267   with rings at least 10% narrower, or wider, than the previous year (Becker et al., 1994). By [SOURCE: Page_14]

268   testing different thresholds, it is possible to distinguish very characteristic years from less [SOURCE: Page_14]

269   characteristic years. We transposed this technique to our leaf-loss series. However, because [SOURCE: Page_14]

270   no pointer years were detected with the above-mentioned thresholds, we defined pointer [SOURCE: Page_14]

271   years for radial growth and leaf loss as years when at least 60% of the trees shared a [SOURCE: Page_14]

272   variation of at least 10% with the previous year (Becker et al., 1994; Mérian and [SOURCE: Page_14]

273   Lebourgeois, 2011). [SOURCE: Page_14]

274   We calculated the Gini coefficient (GINI) of the de-trended leaf loss (LLd$_1$) and growth index [SOURCE: Page_14]

275   (rwi) series to compare growth sensitivity to climate. GINI is a relative index, quantifying [SOURCE: Page_14]

276   sensitivity scaled by mean and sample size (Biondi and Qeadan, 2008). We calculated GINI [SOURCE: Page_14]

277   for each tree and then averaged the GINI values in a second step. We used the [SOURCE: Page_14]

278   Gleichläufigkeit index (GLK) (Schweingruber, 1987) to check for a similarity in signal [SOURCE: Page_14]

279   between the series, in other words, their synchronization. [SOURCE: Page_14]

280   We chose to apply the machine-learning algorithm Random Forest (RF) (Breiman, 2001), a [SOURCE: Page_14]

281   nonparametric technique for classification or regression. RF is based on the Classification [SOURCE: Page_14]

282   And Regression Tree (CART) algorithm (Breiman et al., 1984). CART builds a decision tree [SOURCE: Page_14]

283   by finding the dichotomy at each iteration that minimizes a goodness-of-fit criterion among all [SOURCE: Page_14]

284   predictors. CART is known to be very sensitive to small changes in the data set (Genuer and [SOURCE: Page_14]

285   Poggi, 2019). RF is a tree-based ensemble method, which overcomes these limitations by [SOURCE: Page_14]

286   averaging regression over a great number of randomized variants of the decision trees [SOURCE: Page_14]

287   obtained with CART. The number of trees, ntree, is usually set between 500 and 2000. [SOURCE: Page_14]

288   Variants are obtained from bootstrap samples of the learning set (the bagging) and random [SOURCE: Page_14]

289   subsets of variables. Breiman (2001) showed, both theoretically and empirically, that these [SOURCE: Page_14]

290   averaged trees have fewer expected errors than do single trees. Because random subsets of [SOURCE: Page_14]

291   variables are selected at each node (control parameter mtry), the collinearity of the predictors [SOURCE: Page_15]

292   is reduced. [SOURCE: Page_15]

293   RF therefore has two interesting features: it is able to model nonlinear relationships and it [SOURCE: Page_15]

294   can handle redundancy in the variables. RF has proven to be a very efficient method, usually [SOURCE: Page_15]

295   ranked among the most efficient machine-learning methods (Genuer et al., 2015), both for [SOURCE: Page_15]

296   classification and for regression. [SOURCE: Page_15]

297   In addition to these advantages over regressions, RF makes it possible to rank the [SOURCE: Page_15]

298   importance of the predictor variables and to evaluate their marginal effect on the response [SOURCE: Page_15]

299   variable, without any a priori assumptions. The bagging process makes it possible to predict [SOURCE: Page_15]

300   the “out-of-bag” (OOB) samples from the “in-the-bag” ones. The importance of the variables [SOURCE: Page_15]

301   is then measured as the averaged increase of squared OOB residuals when the predictor is [SOURCE: Page_15]

302   permuted (Genuer et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_15]

303   Variable importance provides a measure to compare predictors within a dataset but does not [SOURCE: Page_15]

304   provide a threshold to select the most relevant ones. The R package “VSURF” (Genuer et [SOURCE: Page_15]

305   al., 2015) selects variables in two ways: for interpretative purposes, it can select all the [SOURCE: Page_15]

306   relevant predictors related to the variable under study (there can be redundancies); and, for [SOURCE: Page_15]

307   predictive purposes, it can select the smallest set of predictors that explains most of the [SOURCE: Page_15]

308   variability for the variable. Since we wanted to select predictors for functional interpretation [SOURCE: Page_15]

309   and not for prediction, we will only discuss the first type of selection in this paper. As a first [SOURCE: Page_15]

310   step, VSURF eliminated certain predictors based on the standard deviation of the variable’s [SOURCE: Page_15]

311   importance (averaged over 50 runs of RF). The remaining variables were used to construct [SOURCE: Page_15]

312   nested RFs starting with the single most important variable and finishing with all the [SOURCE: Page_15]

313   important predictors. The predictors leading to the smallest OOB error were kept. After some [SOURCE: Page_15]

314   exploratory analysis (not reported here), VSURF was implemented with ntree= 2000 and [SOURCE: Page_15]

315   mtry=p/3 (p, being the number of variables involved in the model). Most of the time, the [SOURCE: Page_15]

316   cross-validated optimised parameters were very close to these values; and even when they [SOURCE: Page_15]

317   were not, predictions were not significantly different from those obtained with the above [SOURCE: Page_15]

318   setting. We randomly sampled 70% of the data set to train the RF and kept the remaining [SOURCE: Page_16]

319   30% to test prediction-error. For each model, the pseudo-R$_2$ predictive accuracy metric was [SOURCE: Page_16]

320   computed on the test set as calculated in the “randomForest” package (Liaw and Wiener, [SOURCE: Page_16]

321   2002). This metric is defined as the proportion of total variation in the outcome explained by [SOURCE: Page_16]

322   the model (or forest); values range between 0 and 1. [SOURCE: Page_16]

323   Strobl et al. (2008) report that variable importance metrics can be biased towards correlated [SOURCE: Page_16]

324   variables in some situations and propose an improved method in order to take this into [SOURCE: Page_16]

325   account. The method requires implementing a conditional permutation scheme within a [SOURCE: Page_16]

326   group that can potentially contain large sets of covariables. Genuer et al. (2010) specifically [SOURCE: Page_16]

327   studied RF variable importance in the presence of groups of highly correlated explanatory [SOURCE: Page_16]

328   variables. They did not diagnose any such bias for variable selection. In our case, we [SOURCE: Page_16]

329   performed preliminary exploratory analyses with the “party” package (Strobl et al., 2009). We [SOURCE: Page_16]

330   consistently obtained extremely low importance scores for all variables (results not reported [SOURCE: Page_16]

331   here), making it completely impossible to come up with any interpretation. Strobl et al. (2008) [SOURCE: Page_16]

332   and Genuer et al. (2010) agree on the fact that, despite possible overestimation of the [SOURCE: Page_16]

333   importance score, the order of the variables is usually preserved. [SOURCE: Page_16]

334   We used the “PDP” package (Greenwell, 2017) to describe the relationships between the [SOURCE: Page_16]

335   selected predictors and the variables of interest and illustrated the results with partial [SOURCE: Page_16]

336   dependence plots. A partial dependence plot (PDP) shows if the relationship between the [SOURCE: Page_16]

337   response and the predictor is linear, monotonous or more complex. For a given predictor [SOURCE: Page_16]

338   value, the PDP tells what the average marginal effect on the prediction is. We used the R [SOURCE: Page_16]

339   3.3.2 statistical software for all of our statistical analyses. [SOURCE: Page_16]

496      4.2. Are inter-annual variations in leaf-loss and radial growth controlled by the same [SOURCE: Page_25]

497           climatic variables? [SOURCE: Page_25]

498   The systematic forest health monitoring network allowed us to work on a set of plots [SOURCE: Page_25]

499   representative of the species in the regions or on the whole territory. The various growing [SOURCE: Page_25]

500   conditions and ecological sites were taken into account through the calculation of specific [SOURCE: Page_25]

501   bio- and pedo-climatic variables for each plot. Our approach resulted in a robust growth and [SOURCE: Page_25]

502   leaf loss signal for each region. [SOURCE: Page_25]

503   Over the period 2001-2014, we observed that several variables had an effect on both radial [SOURCE: Page_25]

504   growth and crown condition. Most of these variables likely reflect ecophysiological [SOURCE: Page_25]

505   processes. Previous year’s (n-1) climatic variables mainly control LL inter-annual variations, [SOURCE: Page_25]

506   whereas radial growth is more sensitive to the climatic parameters of the current year (n). [SOURCE: Page_25]

507   This observation is in line with previous studies on crown development, radial growth and [SOURCE: Page_25]

508   their relationships with climate (Power, 1994; Thabeet et al., 2009) and confirm our [SOURCE: Page_25]

509   hypothesis that climatic factors during the previous year control inter-annual variations in leaf [SOURCE: Page_25]

510   loss. [SOURCE: Page_25]

511   Whether the effect of soil water deficit occurs on primary or secondary growth depends [SOURCE: Page_25]

512   mostly on when the stress occurs (Power, 1994). [SOURCE: Page_25]

513   Soil water deficit in the previous year increases leaf loss. This a consequence of the climatic [SOURCE: Page_25]

514   constraints on leaf preformation during the previous year. Cochard et al. (2005) suggest a [SOURCE: Page_25]

515   correlation between primary growth and secondary growth through xylem hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_25]

516   conductance. The shoots developing in year n depend on the number of leaf primordia in the [SOURCE: Page_25]

517   bud, which themselves depend on the outermost tree-ring of year n-1. Finally, the soil water [SOURCE: Page_25]

518   deficit of the previous year may increase the number of short shoots. In England (Stribley [SOURCE: Page_26]

519   and Ashmore, 2002), the annual growth of both the leader, or primary, shoots and the lateral, [SOURCE: Page_26]

520   or secondary, shoots were suppressed in drought years. A one-year delayed response of [SOURCE: Page_26]

521   crown condition to soil water deficit is a common observation throughout Europe (Bréda and [SOURCE: Page_26]

522   Badeau, 2008; Power, 1994; Seidling, 2007; Seidling et al., 2012; Wilson et al., 2008; Zierl, [SOURCE: Page_26]

523   2004). [SOURCE: Page_26]

524   Previous- and current-year soil water deficit controls tree-ring width. [SOURCE: Page_26]

525   In our study, tree-ring width decreased with increasing current summer (June-July) soil [SOURCE: Page_26]

526   water deficit (SWD). High evaporative demand and low precipitation during summer increase [SOURCE: Page_26]

527   soil water deficit, reducing canopy conductance (Lemoine et al., 2002) and gross primary [SOURCE: Page_26]

528   production (GPP) in beech stands (Granier et al., 2008, 2007). Ultimately, this can affect [SOURCE: Page_26]

529   carbon storage and lead to dieback (Bréda et al., 2006). GPP depends on 1/ the duration [SOURCE: Page_26]

530   and intensity of the water deficit cumulated over the growing season, and 2/ the growing [SOURCE: Page_26]

531   season length, i.e. the period of carbon uptake by the stand (Granier et al., 2008). [SOURCE: Page_26]

532   The negative effect of the previous year’s water deficit can be explained by a dysfunction in [SOURCE: Page_26]

533   carbon storage. Skomarkova et al. (2006) showed that 20% of the ring-width in corresponds [SOURCE: Page_26]

534   to carbon stored during the previous year. The previous year's level of growth may therefore [SOURCE: Page_26]

535   explain part of the ongoing year's growth. However, in addition to a delayed effect of the non- [SOURCE: Page_26]

536   structural carbohydrate amount on radial growth, beech trees may sacrifice growth to sustain [SOURCE: Page_26]

537   allocation to carbon storage. In young beech trees that had been submitted to an extremely [SOURCE: Page_26]

538   long and intense drought period, Chuste et al. (2019) observed that carbon was allocated in [SOURCE: Page_26]

539   storage at the expense of primary and secondary growth, that were severely reduced. [SOURCE: Page_26]

540 [SOURCE: Page_26]

541   The sensitivity of both LL and rwi to soil water deficit and mean April-to-August temperature [SOURCE: Page_26]

542   could be a result of carbon partitioning among secondary and primary growth and [SOURCE: Page_26]

543   reproduction (Drobyshev et al., 2010; Innes, 1994; Mund et al., 2010; Seidling, 2007). [SOURCE: Page_26]

544   Leaf and flower production depend on carbon concentrations during the previous year [SOURCE: Page_27]

545   (Cochard et al., 2005; Hoch et al., 2013; Miyazaki, 2013). Several authors show that after a [SOURCE: Page_27]

546   hot dry summer, beeches can sometimes produce a large number of flowers (Etzold et al., [SOURCE: Page_27]

547   2016; Hacket-Pain et al., 2017; Nussbaumer et al., 2018; Piovesan and Adams, 2002; [SOURCE: Page_27]

548   Vacchiano et al., 2018). This may lead to a trade-off between leaf production and [SOURCE: Page_27]

549   reproduction. In addition, heavy fruit production can also decrease the number of leaves per [SOURCE: Page_27]

550   ground area because primordial leaf buds are replaced by flower buds (Müller-Haubold et al., [SOURCE: Page_27]

551   2015). Microphylly can also lead to an abnormally high assessment of leaf loss. The [SOURCE: Page_27]

552   observer may confuse leaf loss and a more transparent crown with smaller leaves. [SOURCE: Page_27]

553   Fruit development depends on newly assimilated carbon and this can lead to a competition [SOURCE: Page_27]

554   for carbon between growth and reproduction (Hacket-Pain et al., 2017; Hoch, 2005; Hoch et [SOURCE: Page_27]

555   al., 2013; Miyazaki, 2013). In our study, however, when we added the presence or absence [SOURCE: Page_27]

556   of fructification, we found no relationship between reproduction and radial growth. However, [SOURCE: Page_27]

557   fructification, the product of flowering, did significantly increase leaf loss (i.e. primary growth [SOURCE: Page_27]

558   and leaf mass) in two ecological regions (Northern and Eastern lowlands). However, the fact [SOURCE: Page_27]

559   that fructification observations were not mandatory or systematic for the ICP Network data [SOURCE: Page_27]

560   we used may have caused an underestimation of the effect of fructification on growth and/or [SOURCE: Page_27]

561   leaf loss in our study. [SOURCE: Page_27]

562   Both rwi and LL responded to current- and previous-year bud burst date (estimated through [SOURCE: Page_27]

563   modelling). While an earlier bud burst date induces cambium activation and lengthens the [SOURCE: Page_27]

564   growing season (Davi et al., 2006), it also exposes the tree to a higher risk of late frost [SOURCE: Page_27]

565   damage in April. In our study, an early leaf unfolding in the previous year appeared to be an [SOURCE: Page_27]

566   advantage for both secondary and primary growth. Granier et al. (2008) observed that [SOURCE: Page_27]

567   canopy photosynthesis (GPP) depended on growing season length, i.e. the period of carbon [SOURCE: Page_27]

568   uptake. A high assimilation rate increases C storage, allows wider tree rings in twigs, and [SOURCE: Page_27]

569   enables buds to preformat a large number of leaf primordia. Indeed, the width of the [SOURCE: Page_27]

570   outermost ring is positively correlated to xylem conductance to the buds (Cochard et al., [SOURCE: Page_27]

571   2005). For beech, radial growth initiation is synchronous with leaf unfolding (Barbaroux and [SOURCE: Page_28]

572   Bréda, 2002) and is closely dependent on leaf photosynthesis (Granier et al., 2008, Michelot [SOURCE: Page_28]

573   et al., 2012). If previous-year leaf unfolding occurs early and there is no late frost damage, [SOURCE: Page_28]

574   the growing season is longer and there is therefore more assimilated carbon available for the [SOURCE: Page_28]

575   preformation of next year’s leaves and tree growth. For the current year, an early bud burst [SOURCE: Page_28]

576   reduces radial growth without any deleterious effects to crown condition. However, we [SOURCE: Page_28]

577   observed an effect of late frost on crown condition in mountainous regions. This agrees with [SOURCE: Page_28]

578   other studies of frost damage on current growth and leaf loss (Dittmar et al., 2006; Menzel et [SOURCE: Page_28]

579   al., 2015; Nolè et al., 2018; Príncipe et al., 2017); the intensity and frequency of late frosts [SOURCE: Page_28]

580   increase with altitude (Cailleret and Davi, 2011). The newly formed leaves are damaged and [SOURCE: Page_28]

581   the tree needs to mobilize more stored carbohydrates to create new foliation. The second [SOURCE: Page_28]

582   foliation is nitrogen-depleted and therefore less efficient; furthermore, there is less carbon [SOURCE: Page_28]

583   available to preform next year’s leaves (Augspurger, 2009; Awaya et al., 2009; Bascietto et [SOURCE: Page_28]

584   al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_28]

585   As opposed to previous findings, we observed that the accumulation of negative daily [SOURCE: Page_28]

586   maximum temperatures during winter impaired radial growth. As mentioned earlier, a [SOURCE: Page_28]

587   reduction in growth is usually interpreted as the result of damage to buds or young leaves [SOURCE: Page_28]

588   after a late frost. Oddly, the negative influence we observed on beech occurred for cold [SOURCE: Page_28]

589   temperatures accumulated during the dormancy period. Indeed, the reduction in growth with [SOURCE: Page_28]

590   winter temperatures cannot be attributed to bud damage. Winter buds have a high tolerance [SOURCE: Page_28]

591   to cold temperatures, though this tolerance decreases as budburst approaches (Gömöry and [SOURCE: Page_28]

592   Paule, 2011; Menzel et al., 2015). Lenz et al. (2016) demonstrated high frost resistance in [SOURCE: Page_28]

593   the upper parts of the tree, with temperatures of -40°C damaging only 50% of the buds. [SOURCE: Page_28]

594   However, as in previous, more local dendrochronological studies (Di Filippo et al., 2007; [SOURCE: Page_28]

595   Dittmar et al., 2003; Weigel et al., 2018), we observed a relationship between winter [SOURCE: Page_28]

596   temperatures and growth at national and regional scales. Power (1994) reported a significant [SOURCE: Page_28]

597   impact of winter temperatures from December to February on apical growth, i.e. primary [SOURCE: Page_28]

598   growth, on twigs of declining beech trees. This negative impact could lead to increased leaf [SOURCE: Page_29]

599   loss the following summer. [SOURCE: Page_29]

600   A deficiency in the frost hardening process is one possible explanation for beech growth [SOURCE: Page_29]

601   sensitivity to negative temperatures during winter. Converting starch to soluble sugars is [SOURCE: Page_29]

602   essential for hardening; this is observed through a decrease in starch and an increase in [SOURCE: Page_29]

603   soluble sugar content during winter (Barbaroux and Bréda, 2002; Charra-Vaskou et al., [SOURCE: Page_29]

604   2012). If a harsh winter is preceded by a summer drought or any event that could impair [SOURCE: Page_29]

605   carbon storage, reserves may not be fully restored by the time hardening begins, thus [SOURCE: Page_29]

606   leading to less efficient protection against freezing temperatures. These observations [SOURCE: Page_29]

607   encourage us to deepen our understanding of the processes responsible for this result at [SOURCE: Page_29]

608   different compartmental levels (crown, trunk, roots). [SOURCE: Page_29]

609   Thanks to data provided by the French Forest Health Department (DSF) on the causes of [SOURCE: Page_29]

610   observed leaf symptoms, we were able to explain most of the significant symptoms we found [SOURCE: Page_29]

611   on beech leaves in our study. In the Northern region, leaf loss increased with leaf coloration [SOURCE: Page_29]

612   (yellow, red), and a high proportion of abnormal leaf coloration occurred in the years [SOURCE: Page_29]

613   following the exceptionally early drought in 2011. Constantly increasing leaf consumption by [SOURCE: Page_29]

614   phyllophagous insects in the Alps has been observed since 2005 (data not shown here), thus [SOURCE: Page_29]

615   explaining the increase in leaf loss. Unfortunately, the insects causing the consumption have [SOURCE: Page_29]

616   not been clearly identified. Beech weevils have been recorded since 2011; however, their [SOURCE: Page_29]

617   impact remains unknown. Finally, the leaf necrosis we observed was the result of a frost in [SOURCE: Page_29]

618   2012. [SOURCE: Page_29]

619      4.3. Assessment of the relationship between rwi and LL [SOURCE: Page_29]

620           4.3.1. Leaf loss is a poor predictor of radial growth inter-annual variability [SOURCE: Page_29]

621   At the national scale and in the different ecological regions, there was a slight though [SOURCE: Page_29]

622   continuous decrease in radial growth with leaf loss. The typical vertical canopy profile and [SOURCE: Page_29]

623   differentiated leaves of the beech tree can explain our result. Leaf area is mostly distributed [SOURCE: Page_29]

624   in the upper and middle part of the crown (Holdaway et al., 2008) with small, thick sun- [SOURCE: Page_30]

625   exposed leaves at the top, and thinner larger leaves in the middle and at the bottom of the [SOURCE: Page_30]

626   canopy (Hagemeier and Leuschner, 2019). The sun-exposed leaves have higher [SOURCE: Page_30]

627   photosynthetic rates (higher maximum CO$_2$ assimilation rates, maximum stomatal [SOURCE: Page_30]

628   conductance) than do the shaded leaves (Scartazza et al., 2016; Urban et al., 2007). Only [SOURCE: Page_30]

629   the upper third of the crown is assessed (Eichhorn et al., 2016) where the photosynthesis [SOURCE: Page_30]

630   rate is maximum. Therefore, annual recorded leaf loss corresponds to a potential decrease in [SOURCE: Page_30]

631   carbon assimilation rate. However, leaf loss in the sun-exposed upper crown also induces a [SOURCE: Page_30]

632   reduction in transpiration. Thanks to a trade-off between transpiration and assimilation, leaf [SOURCE: Page_30]

633   loss should therefore have a limited impact on radial growth thanks to a more limited soil [SOURCE: Page_30]

634   water shortage. [SOURCE: Page_30]

635   However, even though we observed slowed radial growth starting from 5% leaf loss, values [SOURCE: Page_30]

636   did not fall below normal (rwi=1) until a 20% leaf loss was reached. This threshold must be [SOURCE: Page_30]

637   interpreted with precaution. While we did find a relationship between LL and rwi, LL ranked [SOURCE: Page_30]

638   low. This is because the correlation between growth and leaf loss only occurs in case of [SOURCE: Page_30]

639   extreme events, as shown by our joint analysis of leaf loss and radial growth pointer years; [SOURCE: Page_30]

640   no relationship occurs in cases of slight or moderate defoliation. Almost all of the pointer [SOURCE: Page_30]

641   years were due to climatic factors accounting for both the small radial increment and high [SOURCE: Page_30]

642   leaf loss (Table A.2). Often, severe soil water deficits were involved (lag effects form 2003’s [SOURCE: Page_30]

643   extreme summer drought and heat wave, a direct impact of the early drought event in spring [SOURCE: Page_30]

644   of 2011). In fact, in the regions where LL appeared to predict radial growth, removing the [SOURCE: Page_30]

645   common pointer years for the two indicators decreased the ability of LL to explain the [SOURCE: Page_30]

646   variation in radial growth (rwi above 1). Drobyshev et al.’s (2007) and Sánchez-Salguero et [SOURCE: Page_30]

647   al.’s (2013) results are in complete accordance with our findings. In their study, more [SOURCE: Page_30]

648   significant differences in increment were observed between healthy crowns (class 1, < 25 %) [SOURCE: Page_30]

649   and heavily declining crowns (class 3, > 60 %) than between other crown classes. Therefore, [SOURCE: Page_30]

650   leaf loss is a good indicator of growth potential only in case of extreme events and when leaf [SOURCE: Page_30]

651   loss is high. As supported by Seidling et al. (2012), leaf loss and radial growth are only [SOURCE: Page_31]

652   weakly correlated. [SOURCE: Page_31]

653          4.3.2. Crown condition, a poorer descriptor of tree vitality than radial growth [SOURCE: Page_31]

654   Leaf loss showed fewer significant relationships with the selected climate variables than did [SOURCE: Page_31]

655   radial growth. The LL series of common beech did not show as clear a common signal as did [SOURCE: Page_31]

656   rwi, as indicated by high between-tree variability in the leaf loss series and by a smaller [SOURCE: Page_31]

657   “Gleichläufigkeit” coefficient (year-to-year agreement between the series). In accordance with [SOURCE: Page_31]

658   earlier studies, we confirm that tree-ring series reflect exogenous signals more clearly for [SOURCE: Page_31]

659   common beech (Dittmar et al., 2003). [SOURCE: Page_31]

660   Describing crown condition and morphology enables forest managers to detect severe tree [SOURCE: Page_31]

661   dysfunction through discoloration, abnormal leaf loss or branch mortality (Innes, 1998; Ling [SOURCE: Page_31]

662   et al., 1993; Power, 1994). However, the multiple factors influencing inter-annual variations in [SOURCE: Page_31]

663   leaf deficiency are challenging to disentangle (Innes et al., 1993). Our surprisingly strong [SOURCE: Page_31]

664   GINI results for leaf loss, indicating a higher year-to-year variability than for growth, first led [SOURCE: Page_31]

665   us to question the reliability of the observations made by the pairs of operators. We [SOURCE: Page_31]

666   investigated this issue but found no clear relationship imputable to operator inconsistency. [SOURCE: Page_31]

667   Nevertheless, the GINI coefficient clearly increased when more than one pair of operators [SOURCE: Page_31]

668   assessed a given plot. Even though overall assessment quality has recently improved [SOURCE: Page_31]

669   (Ferretti et al., 2014; Meining et al., 2016), it is important to keep in mind that one of the main [SOURCE: Page_31]

670   weaknesses in the ICP monitoring network is operator subjectivity, which can lead to [SOURCE: Page_31]

671   inconsistency among assessments related to different sky conditions, observation years and [SOURCE: Page_31]

672   countries. [SOURCE: Page_31]

673   5. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_31]

674   Using the machine-learning algorithm Random Forest to identify the response of inter-annual [SOURCE: Page_31]

675   leaf loss or radial growth to biotic and abiotic constraints proved to be effective. The climatic [SOURCE: Page_31]

676   drivers of radial growth we found through this approach are in accordance with previous [SOURCE: Page_31]

677   studies. Several have an effect on both radial growth and crown condition, though previous- [SOURCE: Page_32]

678   year climatic variables tend to control leaf loss while radial growth is more sensitive to [SOURCE: Page_32]

679   current-year climate. There is a common sensitivity to soil water deficit but with different time [SOURCE: Page_32]

680   lags. Primary and secondary growth are related to each other through hydraulic structure or [SOURCE: Page_32]

681   resource partitioning. With regard to its use as an indicator of tree health, leaf loss captured [SOURCE: Page_32]

682   fewer pointer years than did radial growth. Leaf loss only explained a reduction in radial [SOURCE: Page_32]

683   growth in the case of extreme leaf loss events. Furthermore, radial growth, unlike leaf loss, [SOURCE: Page_32]

684   presented a strong common signal among trees, allowing us to study tree response to abiotic [SOURCE: Page_32]

685   factors with more assurance. Radial growth also allowed us to carry out a retrospective [SOURCE: Page_32]

686   analysis of living conditions thanks to more reliable measurements. A rigorous [SOURCE: Page_32]

687   dendrochronological approach including coring to the pith can help to accurately measure the [SOURCE: Page_32]

688   effect of age and is possibly more cost-effective than estimating a poor quality indicator like [SOURCE: Page_32]

689   leaf loss every year. A study comparing our results on beech with other species, including [SOURCE: Page_32]

690   coniferous trees, is underway in order to conclusively assess the performance of annual [SOURCE: Page_32]

691   visual estimations of leaf loss. [SOURCE: Page_32]
