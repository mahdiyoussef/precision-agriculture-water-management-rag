23    other with a low intensity) separated by a two-day flow interruption was applied three days [SOURCE: Page_1]

24    after the pesticides and bromide application. Batch sorption coefficients, Kd, were also [SOURCE: Page_1]

25    measured. While the Pesticides Properties Data Base (2020) indicated a decrease of sorption [SOURCE: Page_1]

42      1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

43 [SOURCE: Page_2]

44      Due to their degraded soil structure, soils cultivated under conventional tillage practices, [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   such as mouldboard ploughing, are generally more sensitive to surface runoff and soil loss by [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   water and wind erosion (Holland, 2004). In this context, the contamination of neighbouring [SOURCE: Page_2]

47   surface waters by agrochemicals is often increased (Alletto et al., 2010). In order to enhance [SOURCE: Page_2]

48   the sustainability of agricultural systems and reduce the drawbacks of conventional [SOURCE: Page_2]

49   agriculture, alternative cultivation practices such as conservation agriculture have been [SOURCE: Page_2]

50   developed. The main goals of conservation agriculture are to reduce soil degradation through [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

51   erosion but also the contamination of surface water by agrochemicals due to runoff or transfer [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   of sorbed solutes on eroded soil particles (Holland, 2004; Hobbs et al., 2008). Conservation [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   agriculture is defined as a combination of three main interlinked soil conservation techniques: [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   (i) minimal or no soil disturbance, (ii) permanent soil cover by crop residues and/or cover [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   crops and (iii) diversification of plant species in the crop rotation (FAO, 2016). The [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   interaction between these three principles leads to complex and interlinked changes in soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   physical, chemical and biological properties that remain poorly characterized. Taking into [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   account these changes is crucial to properly assess the performance of such agricultural [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   systems. However, contradictory published results especially on soil hydraulic processes and [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   pesticide transfers are reported (Green et al., 2003; Strudley et al., 2008; Alletto et al., 2010; [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   Verhulst et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_3]

62      Tillage decreases soil compaction in the tilled layer but in the same time it disrupts pore [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   connectivity between the tilled layer and deeper soil horizons thus limiting deep water [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   movements (Cameira et al., 2003; Fuentes et al., 2004). In addition, the destruction of low [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   stability aggregates by raindrop impacts may lead to the sealing of soil surface thus reducing [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   soil infiltration and enhancing the risk of erosion (Holland, 2004). On the contrary, in no- [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   tilled soils, because of less disturbance of the topsoil, total porosity is, in most cases, reduced [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   while, in the same time, a greater continuity of vertically oriented macropores is generally [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   observed leading to higher hydraulic conductivity in untilled than in tilled soils (Wahl et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   2004; Soto-Gómez et al., 2019). In addition, in conservation agriculture, the faunal activity is [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   enhanced, especially earthworms, resulting in a dense biomacropore network (Shipitalo et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   2000). Introducing cover crops can also improve water conductivity in no-tillage systems, by [SOURCE: Page_3]

73   creating stable biomacropore network through root development during crop growing season [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   (Abdollahi and Munkholm, 2014; Williams and Weil, 2004). Maintaining crop residues at the [SOURCE: Page_3]

75   soil surface progressively leads to an accumulation of soil organic matter in topsoil layers [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

76   (Kay and VandenBygaart, 2002) and improves aggregate stability (Devine et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   Moreover, the dead residues form a mulch that physically protects soil surface from crusting [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   (Baumhardt and Lascano, 1999). [SOURCE: Page_4]

79       Due to this higher proportion of (bio)macropores, preferential transport of water and [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   solutes is expected to be enhanced in conservation agriculture, thus increasing the risk of [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   contamination of groundwater (Isensee and Sadeghi, 1997). However, field studies reported [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   either higher (Elliott et al., 2000), lower (Gish et al., 1995) or no differences (Fortin et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   2002) in pesticide losses between no tillage and tillage practices. Laboratory leaching studies [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   on undisturbed soil columns also brought mixed results with higher (Levanon et al., 1993), [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   lower (Sigua et al., 1995) or equivalent (Porfiri et al., 2015; Okada et al., 2016) leaching in [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   tilled compared to untilled soils. [SOURCE: Page_4]

87       Field and laboratory studies have permitted to identify several factors influencing leaching [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   (Alletto et al., 2010). Rainfall parameters such as arrival time of the first rain after application, [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   the duration and intensity of the rain event were indeed reported as crucial to determine the [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   fate of pesticides in soils (Sigua et al., 1993; Isensee and Sadeghi, 1997). In the meantime, [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   pesticide properties also determine the fate of the compounds into the soil. When comparing [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   the movement of two different pesticides, Fermanich and Daniel (1991) attributed leaching [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   differences to the chemical characteristics (soil adsorption and water solubility) of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

94   compounds. However, despite these advances in knowledge, understanding of the effects of [SOURCE: Page_4]

95   agricultural practices on the fate of pesticides remains unsatisfactory, limiting our ability to [SOURCE: Page_4]

96   assess and predict the environmental impacts of cropping systems (Alletto et al., 2010; Marín- [SOURCE: Page_4]

97   Benito et al., 2018). A possible origin of these contradictory effects observed in the literature [SOURCE: Page_4]

98   may be that most of the studies are based on analytical approach and has thus attempted to [SOURCE: Page_4]

99   isolate the effects of the different agronomic levers mobilized in cropping systems [SOURCE: Page_4]

100   management (e.g. most of studies have focused on tillage effects, or on cover crop effects, [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

101   …), whereas a systemic approach (i.e. assessing the effects of interactions between levers) [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   would be more appropriate to evaluate pesticide environmental behaviour. In this study, we [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   therefore chose to characterize the behaviour of water and pesticides in two soils managed [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   under two different agricultural systems evaluated as a whole (i.e. including a combination of [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   management practices), one in conventional agriculture (tillage, bare soil, monoculture), the [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   other in conservation agriculture using the three levers (no tillage, cover crops and crop [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   rotations) for more than 10 years. We assume that a better understanding of the interactions [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   between the various components of a cropping system is needed to reveal its advantages and [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   disadvantages, and that the study of long-differentiated cropping systems can assist in the [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   decision-making process. [SOURCE: Page_5]

111      Three pesticides, widely used by farmers for maize production, and for which little [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   information is available in the literature, were chosen for the laboratory percolation [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   experiment on undisturbed soil columns. Metaldehyde is a molluscicide generally spread in [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   autumn and winter as baits pellets to protect all types of crops. In a recent review, Castle et al. [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   (2017) reported that metaldehyde is highly mobile in soil and can hence contaminate water [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   resources. Nicosulfuron is a post-emergence herbicide used to control grass species in maize. [SOURCE: Page_5]

117   Because of their anionic character, sulfonylurea herbicides are highly mobile in soil and could [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   contaminate ground waters (Gonzalez and Ukrainczyk, 1996). Regarding the effect of [SOURCE: Page_5]

119   cropping practices on the fate of nicosulfuron, Afyuni et al. (1997) found that conventional [SOURCE: Page_5]

120   tillage generally resulted in more runoff but lower herbicide losses by runoff than no-tillage. [SOURCE: Page_5]

121   Mesotrione is an herbicide that provides pre-emergence and post-emergence control of all the [SOURCE: Page_5]

122   important broad-leaved weeds in maize and some of the annual grass weeds. Very little has [SOURCE: Page_5]

123   been published on mesotrione transfer in soils. Rouchaud et al. (2001) found no movement of [SOURCE: Page_5]

124   mesotrione lower than 20 cm in soil that was attributed to a combination of its low mobility [SOURCE: Page_5]

125   and degradation rate. [SOURCE: Page_5]

133         2.1 Sites and agricultural managements [SOURCE: Page_6]

134 [SOURCE: Page_6]

135         The two sites of this study are located in the South West of France, in the Pyrénées- [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   Atlantiques and in the Gers French departments. According to the Köppen climate [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   classification, both sites have an altered oceanic climate. [SOURCE: Page_6]

138         The soil on the Pyrénées-Atlantiques site is a thick humic acid soil developed from [SOURCE: Page_6]

139   Quaternary silty alluvial deposits, classified, according to the World Reference Base for Soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   Resources (IUSS Working Group WRB, 2007) as a Vermic Umbrisol, and according to the [SOURCE: Page_6]

141   French Soil Classification (AFES, 2008), as a veracrisol, locally called “Touyas”. It has a [SOURCE: Page_6]

142   rich, deep (from 50 to 80 cm-depth) well-structured organic horizon (Table 1). Soil texture is [SOURCE: Page_6]

143   mainly formed by fine silt (> 450-500 g.kg-1) with a low proportion of sand (< 100-120 g.kg- 1 144       ). This soil type has a high agronomic potential, especially for maize (average yield ≈ 13-15 [SOURCE: Page_6]

145   t.ha-1) and soybean (average yield ≈ 3.7-4.2 t.ha-1) productions (mainly conducted without [SOURCE: Page_6]

146   irrigation). This site will be referred to as VER (veracrisol) site in the rest of the paper. [SOURCE: Page_6]

147         The soil on the Gers site is classified as a Stagnic Luvisol according to the World [SOURCE: Page_6]

148   Reference Base for Soil Resources (IUSS Working Group WRB, 2007), corresponding to a [SOURCE: Page_6]

149   luvisol redoxisol in the French Soil Classification (AFES, 2008), locally called “Boulbènes”. [SOURCE: Page_6]

150   It has a loamy surface layer (fine + coarse silt ≈ 450-500 g.kg-1) and an illuvial clay horizon [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

151   appearing between 45 and 55 cm. The substratum is a low-permeability alluvial pebbly layer [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   appearing at around 60-80 cm. With low organic carbon contents in the soil surface horizon [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   (in most cases < 7-8 g.kg-1) and high silt contents, these soils are strongly sensitive to [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   crusting. Due to these properties (especially hydromorphic conditions at low depth), this soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   type has a moderate agronomic potential with mainly irrigated crops (average maize yields ≈ [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   10-12 t.ha-1). This site will be referred to as LUV (luvisol) site in the rest of the paper. [SOURCE: Page_7]

157       Each site consists of two adjacent plots managed under conventional (TILL) and [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   conservation (CONS) practices. The conventional plots (TILL) are cultivated under maize [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   monoculture (for more than 40 years) and soil is left bare in winter. Tillage depth with a [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   mouldboard plough is about 22-23 cm on VER site and 30 cm on LUV site. In both [SOURCE: Page_7]

161   conservation plots (VER and LUV), agricultural management consists of (i) a diversified crop [SOURCE: Page_7]

162   rotation (including maize, soybean, cereals and sometimes intercrops of cereals and legumes), [SOURCE: Page_7]

163   (ii) a permanent soil surface cover by mulch and cover crops sown during the fallow periods [SOURCE: Page_7]

164   (on VER site, cover crop is composed by a mix of phacelia and faba bean, while on LUV site, [SOURCE: Page_7]

165   it is composed by a mix of two cereals and faba bean), and (iii) no-tillage practices. Such [SOURCE: Page_7]

166   conservation agriculture practices are performed since 2006 and 2000 respectively for VER [SOURCE: Page_7]

167   and LUV sites. [SOURCE: Page_7]

215      2.4 Leaching experiments [SOURCE: Page_9]

216 [SOURCE: Page_9]

217      The percolation experiments were conducted under unsaturated water flow in laboratory [SOURCE: Page_9]

218   conditions. A CaCl$_2$ solution (1.8 10-4 M CaCl$_2$) was applied at the soil surface by a rainfall [SOURCE: Page_9]

219   simulator. The rainfall simulator was placed a few centimetres above the soil surface and [SOURCE: Page_9]

220   consisted of 45 hypodermic needles (0.33 mm diameter) at 12.5 mm spacing and connected to [SOURCE: Page_9]

221   a membrane pump controlling the flow. To maintain unsaturated conditions, a negative [SOURCE: Page_9]

222   pressure of −80 cm was applied at the soil column base by placing a stainless steel mesh (20 [SOURCE: Page_9]

223   μm pore size) and a 0.5 cm layer of glass beads (diameter < 0.45 µm) at the soil bottom and [SOURCE: Page_9]

224   connecting the column base to a vacuum pump applying constant pressure. The layer of glass [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

225   beads provided a homogeneous contact between the soil and the mesh. To monitor water [SOURCE: Page_10]

226   content and water pressure, the columns were equipped with two TDR probes and three [SOURCE: Page_10]

227   tensiometers. [SOURCE: Page_10]

228      The experiments were designed to study the mobility of three pesticides under two rainfall [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   regimes. Before the pesticides application, the soil columns were equilibrated to similar [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   hydration conditions by receiving a constant rainfall of 2 mm.h-1 for a duration of 1 day until [SOURCE: Page_10]

231   steady state was reached. We checked that none of the three pesticides were detected in the [SOURCE: Page_10]

232   column effluents during this equilibration period. For only two columns of the LUV site, we [SOURCE: Page_10]

233   detected residual concentrations (9 to 51 times lower for nicosulfuron and 24 to 69 times [SOURCE: Page_10]

234   lower for mesotrione than the concentrations in the first sample collected after the pesticide [SOURCE: Page_10]

235   application). Then a 7.7-mL volume pulse containing the three pesticides and bromide, an [SOURCE: Page_10]

236   anionic water tracer, was manually applied at the mulch (CONS plots) or soil (TILL plots) [SOURCE: Page_10]

237   surface with a pipette. Little drops were applied on the whole column surface in order to [SOURCE: Page_10]

238   obtain a repartition as homogenous as possible. The concentrations of each pesticide in the [SOURCE: Page_10]

239   mixture reflects realist application dose on the field (Table 2). A series of two rain events [SOURCE: Page_10]

240   separated by a two-day flow interruption was applied three days after the pesticides and [SOURCE: Page_10]

241   bromide application to approximate field conditions. The first rain event lasted 4 h with a high [SOURCE: Page_10]

242   flow intensity of 10 mm.h-1 to evaluate the behaviour of pesticides under a high risk of [SOURCE: Page_10]

243   preferential flow. The second rain event lasted 10 to 15 days depending of the experimental [SOURCE: Page_10]

244   series with a low flow intensity of 2 mm.h-1 maintained until most of the pesticides were [SOURCE: Page_10]

245   eluted. Effluents were collected at regular time steps (every 12 min during the first rain; every [SOURCE: Page_10]

246   60 min for about 36 h at the beginning of the second rain and then every 90 min) and stored at [SOURCE: Page_10]

247   4 °C in darkness, and analysed within the next four days to avoid pesticide degradation. [SOURCE: Page_10]

248      During the pre-saturation phase of the columns, the presence of a very low conductive [SOURCE: Page_10]

249   plow pan (not higher than 0.6 mm.h-1 of hydraulic conductivity) in the TILL plot of LUV site [SOURCE: Page_10]

10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

250   prevented the establishment of a steady state. According to field observations of soil structure, [SOURCE: Page_11]

251   its thickness was about 7-8 cm, we therefore removed the 10-cm bottom layer of the three [SOURCE: Page_11]

252   replicates and applied the same procedure. This experimental issue limited the comparison [SOURCE: Page_11]

253   between sites and treatments and no statistical analysis were performed but provided useful [SOURCE: Page_11]

254   information to interpret water movement in this soil type under a conventional soil tillage [SOURCE: Page_11]

255   management. [SOURCE: Page_11]

256      At the end of the leaching experiment, columns were left to drainage during two days [SOURCE: Page_11]

257   before disassembly of the system. Four soil layers (0-3, 3-5, 5-10 and 10-20/30 cm) were [SOURCE: Page_11]

258   sliced to extract the remaining pesticides and bromide in soil. For each layer, soil was [SOURCE: Page_11]

259   homogenised and a single composite sample was collected and analysed. Mulches were also [SOURCE: Page_11]

260   collected for CONS plots. Samples were stored at -20°C before analysis. [SOURCE: Page_11]

449 [SOURCE: Page_19]

450      4.1 Influence of pesticide properties on pesticide leaching [SOURCE: Page_19]

451 [SOURCE: Page_19]

452      The molluscicide metaldehyde was found to be the most rapidly transferred pesticide [SOURCE: Page_19]

453   through the soil columns among the three studied pesticides, with a similar behaviour to the [SOURCE: Page_19]

454   bromide anionic tracer used to mimic water movement. Such differences in leaching [SOURCE: Page_19]

455   dynamics are not consistent with their pesticide properties found in the Pesticides Properties [SOURCE: Page_19]

456   Data Base (PPDB, 2020). According to solubility in water and sorption coefficient mentioned [SOURCE: Page_19]

457   in this base for these three molecules (Table 2), nicosulfuron should indeed be the more [SOURCE: Page_19]

458   mobile pesticide among the three studied, and, according to its low water solubility and its [SOURCE: Page_19]

459   affinity for organic carbon, metaldehyde should be the least mobile. However, leaching results [SOURCE: Page_19]

460   are consistent with the sorption coefficient values measured in the soil studied. Metaldehyde [SOURCE: Page_19]

461   was indeed found to be not retained in the batch experiments, even with the most organic soil [SOURCE: Page_19]

462   studied (VER site). To our current knowledge, very few data dealing with metaldehyde [SOURCE: Page_19]

463   retention in soils are available in the literature. However, our results indicate that this [SOURCE: Page_19]

464   molecule can be subject to very rapid transfer and therefore contamination of water resources, [SOURCE: Page_19]

465   partly due to very weak retention in soil. Finally, even though co-transport to dissolved or [SOURCE: Page_19]

466   colloidal organic matter was not quantified, and dissolved organic carbon was found [SOURCE: Page_19]

467   throughout the soil column experiments (data not shown), we suspect that these processes [SOURCE: Page_19]

468   may be relatively minor regarding the low adsorption affinity of the three molecules observed [SOURCE: Page_19]

469   in the batch sorption experiment study. [SOURCE: Page_19]

470      Cumulated nicosulfuron losses by leaching were the highest among the three molecules, [SOURCE: Page_19]

471   ranging from 47.5 to 78.8 % according to soil type and agricultural system. Retention of [SOURCE: Page_19]

472   nicosulfuron is weak (Olivera et al., 2001; PPDB, 2020), as found in our soil samples. It [SOURCE: Page_19]

19 [SOURCE: Page_19]

473   seems not to be influenced by organic carbon (Gonzalez and Ukrainczyk, 1996), but rather by [SOURCE: Page_20]

474   clay minerals content, especially smectites on which it is rapidly and strongly sorbed [SOURCE: Page_20]

475   (Ukrainczyk and Rashid, 1995). For some authors, such a rapid sorption on clay minerals [SOURCE: Page_20]

476   would strongly limit nicosulfuron leaching to groundwater (Gonzalez and Ukrainczyk, 1999). [SOURCE: Page_20]

477   Our study highlights the high potential risks of leaching of this molecule on alluvial soils with [SOURCE: Page_20]

478   low clay content (< 150 g.kg-1) that could lead to groundwater contamination. [SOURCE: Page_20]

479      Mesotrione leaching was found to be the slowest in coherence with the higher sorption [SOURCE: Page_20]

480   coefficient values (compared to the two other studied pesticides) measured in the studied [SOURCE: Page_20]

481   soils, reaching a maximum of 6.4 L.kg-1 (replicate area c of the CONS plot on VER site). [SOURCE: Page_20]

482   Such values were among the highest found in the literature for this compound (Mendes et al., [SOURCE: Page_20]

483   2016), and its sorption behaviour was in agreement with others works: positively correlated to [SOURCE: Page_20]

484   clay content and negatively correlated to pH (Dyson et al., 2002; Alekseeva et al., 2014; [SOURCE: Page_20]

485   Mendes et al., 2016; Carles et al., 2017). Despite no correlation was found with soil organic [SOURCE: Page_20]

486   carbon content, these higher sorption values for mesotrione could be explained by its affinity [SOURCE: Page_20]

487   to organic constituents, especially fulvic acids (Dyson et al., 2002). In a recent study Mendes [SOURCE: Page_20]

488   et al. (2018), showed that mesotrione leaching could reach 80 % of initial applied mass in [SOURCE: Page_20]

489   some soils. In our study, cumulated loss of mesotrione by leaching ranged from 10.1 to 50.8 [SOURCE: Page_20]

490   % of initial applied mass. Considering an applied agronomic dose of 150 g.ha-1, the presence [SOURCE: Page_20]

491   of mesotrione in groundwater with concentration above the fixed limit by the European Water [SOURCE: Page_20]

492   Framework directive is likely to occur. [SOURCE: Page_20]

493      As mentioned in the results section, all pesticides studied had a default in mass balance [SOURCE: Page_20]

494   ranging from 17 to up to 88 % that could be due to their (relatively rapid) degradation in soils. [SOURCE: Page_20]

495   For metaldehyde, with the highest mass balance default, it is likely that part of it was [SOURCE: Page_20]

496   degraded before the first rainfall was applied. Its degradation half-life in soils is indeed short, [SOURCE: Page_20]

497   from less than 1 day (Zhang et al., 2011) to a few days (PPDB, 2020), and metaldehyde is [SOURCE: Page_20]

20 [SOURCE: Page_20]

498   also supposed to be highly volatile. Both dissipation processes may explain the low recovery [SOURCE: Page_21]

499   rate of metaldehyde in our column experiments. Furthermore, during leaching, its rapid [SOURCE: Page_21]

500   degradation rate could explain why metaldehyde Tp were always lower that bromide Tp. [SOURCE: Page_21]

501   Indeed, rapid degradation rates can reduce the effluent concentrations leading to a truncated [SOURCE: Page_21]

502   peak and so an apparent early peak (van Genuchten and Wagenet, 1989; Brusseau, 1992). For [SOURCE: Page_21]

503   nicosulfuron and mesotrione, default in mass balance may not be explained by volatilisation, [SOURCE: Page_21]

504   both of these herbicide families (sulfonylurea and triketone) being considered as non-volatile [SOURCE: Page_21]

505   (Russell et al., 2002; Dumas et al., 2017). For nicosulfuron, bound residue formation could [SOURCE: Page_21]

506   explain the difference in mass balance. In a degradation study performed with 14C- [SOURCE: Page_21]

507   nicosulfuron on the same soils (data not shown), bound residues were found to represent from [SOURCE: Page_21]

508   25 to 40 % of applied herbicide after only 7 days of incubation. For mesotrione however, it [SOURCE: Page_21]

509   has been suggested that due to their triketonate function, triketones have a pronounced ability [SOURCE: Page_21]

510   to form extremely stable complex with transition metals (Dumas et al., 2017). On another [SOURCE: Page_21]

511   side, Cherrier et al. (2005) reported that sulcotrione (identical to mesotrione except for one [SOURCE: Page_21]

512   group on the benzene cycle) undergoes a fast transformation that results in a small amount of [SOURCE: Page_21]

513   non-extractable residues of around 12.5% after 65 days. [SOURCE: Page_21]

539      4.3 Influence of agricultural systems on pesticide leaching [SOURCE: Page_22]

540 [SOURCE: Page_22]

542   conventionally-tilled soil due to an increase in organic carbon content at the soil surface [SOURCE: Page_22]

544   mulch, that could intercept and retain pesticides. In our study, we have chosen soils that have [SOURCE: Page_22]

545   experienced conservation agriculture for many years (since 2006 for VER site and 2000 for [SOURCE: Page_22]

546   LUV site). This time duration should promote a high contrast in the physical, chemical and [SOURCE: Page_22]

547   biological properties of the soils compared to the same soil managed under tillage. However, [SOURCE: Page_22]

22 [SOURCE: Page_22]

548   no clear trend in pesticide retention between the two agricultural systems has been observed, [SOURCE: Page_23]

549   probably due to the poor affinity of the studied pesticides with organic carbon. Differences in [SOURCE: Page_23]

550   pesticide retention in the mulches from VER and LUV sites measured at the end of the [SOURCE: Page_23]

551   percolation experiments have nevertheless been observed, with a            greater amounts of [SOURCE: Page_23]

552   pesticides retained in the mulch of the LUV site (Fig. 2). This could be due to differences in [SOURCE: Page_23]

553   the cover crop composition between the two conservation agriculture systems (Cassigneul et [SOURCE: Page_23]

554   al., 2015, 2016). In a complementary study, biodegradation of nicosulfuron has been studied [SOURCE: Page_23]

555   for these two agricultural managements on both sites and no significant difference has been [SOURCE: Page_23]

556   found in the topsoil layers despite a greater microbiological activity in conservation [SOURCE: Page_23]

557   agriculture plots (data not shown). Consequently, it is therefore unlikely that, for these types [SOURCE: Page_23]

558   of soil, there will be a significant reduction in the amount of pesticides available for leaching [SOURCE: Page_23]

559   by a significant improvement of the retention and/or degradation of molecules in conservation [SOURCE: Page_23]

560   agriculture systems. [SOURCE: Page_23]

561      Regarding the water dynamics, on VER site, the natural structure of this soil, in relation to [SOURCE: Page_23]

562   its high organic carbon content, did not lead to a significant differentiation of infiltration [SOURCE: Page_23]

563   capacities of the surface horizon between the annually ploughed plot and the conservation [SOURCE: Page_23]

564   agriculture plot. These lab results are confirmed by field hydraulic conductivity measurements [SOURCE: Page_23]

565   between 0-25 cm-depth giving saturated conductivity values ranging from 46 to 97 mm.h-1 [SOURCE: Page_23]

566   without any difference between tilled and untilled plot (data not shown). On LUV site, [SOURCE: Page_23]

567   however, ploughing resulted in the formation of an impermeable layer (with a hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_23]

568   conductivity < 0.6 mm.h-1), which did not allow water to percolate sufficiently rapidly to [SOURCE: Page_23]

569   maintain the targeted water pressure (- 80 cm) at the bottom of the soil column and resulted in [SOURCE: Page_23]

570   a progressive saturation of the soil column. Such a low conductivity of the plough pan has [SOURCE: Page_23]

571   previously been described on a similar soil type and agricultural management (Alletto et al., [SOURCE: Page_23]

572   2010), and could therefore be considered a representative physical property for this cropping [SOURCE: Page_23]

23 [SOURCE: Page_23]

573   system (ie soil type x agricultural management). The removal of this compact zone made [SOURCE: Page_24]

574   these percolations feasible. This result provided us with important information on the water [SOURCE: Page_24]

575   regime of this plot. As a consequence on the field, during rainfall or irrigation on this soil type [SOURCE: Page_24]

576   managed with regular deep soil tillage, the ploughed horizon is very rapidly saturated due to a [SOURCE: Page_24]

577   too low infiltration rate below 20 cm, the water then runs off by lateral subsurface flow (and [SOURCE: Page_24]

578   probably also by surface runoff) dragging the compounds present into solution such as [SOURCE: Page_24]

579   pesticides or nitrates. When the combination of the three agronomical levers of conservation [SOURCE: Page_24]

580   agriculture are applied, significant changes in the physical properties of LUV soil occur and [SOURCE: Page_24]

581   lead to an improvement of water infiltration by a greater connectivity of mesopores and [SOURCE: Page_24]

582   macropores. This soil functioning, which becomes close to that of soils with high agronomic [SOURCE: Page_24]

583   potential such as those on VER site, has the advantage of no longer generating subsurface [SOURCE: Page_24]

584   runoff, but can lead to high degree of preferential flow (Shipitalo et al., 2000; Cameira et al., [SOURCE: Page_24]

585   2003; Jarvis, 2007). [SOURCE: Page_24]

587        5. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_24]

588 [SOURCE: Page_24]

589        We evaluated pesticide leaching in agricultural systems combining the three levers of [SOURCE: Page_24]

590   conservation agriculture (no tillage, cover crops and crop rotation) and in conventional [SOURCE: Page_24]

591   agriculture systems (mouldboard ploughing). Pesticide percolation on undisturbed soil [SOURCE: Page_24]

592   columns sampled in two adjacent plots cultivated under conservation and conventional [SOURCE: Page_24]

593   agriculture were performed in two different sites. The study revealed the influence of [SOURCE: Page_24]

594   pesticide properties, soil type and agricultural systems on pesticide leaching. The main results [SOURCE: Page_24]

595   are the following: [SOURCE: Page_24]

596   i.      Pesticide properties partly determined their leaching. Pesticide properties are known to [SOURCE: Page_24]

597   play a major role in their environmental fate. Databases exist to inform them, but we have [SOURCE: Page_24]

24 [SOURCE: Page_24]

598   seen through the use of information from the PPDB (2020) that caution should be exercised in [SOURCE: Page_25]

599   their use. Ideally, when possible, it is preferable to carry out measurements directly on the [SOURCE: Page_25]

600   soils studied to get more accurate values of their properties. In this study, the measured Kd [SOURCE: Page_25]

601   were in better agreement with the delay of pesticides in undisturbed soil columns experiments [SOURCE: Page_25]

602   than PPDB (2020) values which were less reliable in view of our leaching results. However, [SOURCE: Page_25]

603   in the presence of high degree of preferential flow, potential retention of pesticides can be [SOURCE: Page_25]

604   masked, as illustrated by the differences between ‘expected’ retardation factors calculated [SOURCE: Page_25]

605   from the batch sorption coefficients Kd and the delays in pesticide leaching in the undisturbed [SOURCE: Page_25]

606   soil columns. [SOURCE: Page_25]

607   ii.     Soil type strongly influenced pesticide leaching by conditioning the transport [SOURCE: Page_25]

608   mechanisms. While the two soils studied had an alluvial origin and a low clay content, their [SOURCE: Page_25]

609   own structure generates a different degree of preferential flow that translated into 9 to 19% of [SOURCE: Page_25]

610   the pesticide mass leached during the first intensive rain event in the LUV site of this study, [SOURCE: Page_25]

611   whereas less than 3% of the pesticide mass were leached in the VER site of this study during [SOURCE: Page_25]

612   the first rain event. [SOURCE: Page_25]

613   iii.    The agricultural practices diversely influenced pesticide leaching on the two sites. In [SOURCE: Page_25]

614   the case of the Stagnic Luvisol, the role of agricultural systems on the soil water dynamics [SOURCE: Page_25]

615   and, consequently, on the fate of pesticides become more important. As observed in this [SOURCE: Page_25]

616   study, in ploughed soil of alluvial valley (LUV site), transfers by drainage are strongly limited [SOURCE: Page_25]

617   by a hydraulic discontinuity due to tillage operations. This severe limitation of water drainage [SOURCE: Page_25]

618   could trigger lateral subsurface flows in the field. Conservation agriculture (for several years), [SOURCE: Page_25]

619   improved the pore network connectivity and the water flow became predominantly vertical. In [SOURCE: Page_25]

620   such a situation, the main risk is that preferential flows in the macroporosity occur and [SOURCE: Page_25]

621   generate pesticide transfers. [SOURCE: Page_25]
