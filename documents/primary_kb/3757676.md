31   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   An increasing interest exists in improving the soil quality following the utilization of organic wastes [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   (OW) within the environmentally friendly bio-refinery approach (Alburquerque et al., 2012; Gissén et [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   al., 2014). In this respect, anaerobic digestion is a major building block in the circular bioeconomy. [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   Furthermore, both energy (ie. biomethane) and organic fertilizers (ie. digestate) replacing the chemical [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   fertilizers provided to crops can be recovered from anaerobic digestion of OW. From an agronomic [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   and economic point of view, digestate use as a fertilizer can be considered not only as a supplement [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   for traditional organic fertilizers (i.e. slurry) but also as an alternative fertilizer, which under certain [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   soil conditions, is more effective than using NPK fertilizer (Barlog et al., 2019). Indeed, anaerobic [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   digestion is reported as a suitable treatment to easily recover P and N fertilizers from digestate [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   (Mazzini et al., 2020). This is due to organic matter and free organic nutrients biodegraded into [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   mineral forms. However, agricultural reuse of treated OW as soil fertilizers is limited by [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   environmental constraints related to the quality of organic matter, nutrient availability and safety [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   issues. Furthermore, the chemical composition of digestate depends on the nature of feedstock and [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   digestion process (Alburquerque et al., 2012, Guilayn et al., 2019, Barlog et al., 2019). Indeed, a [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   statistical classification of 91 digestates based on their agronomic quality (i.e. C, N, P, total solids, [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   organic matter) was applied on a large range of raw digestates by (Guilayn et al., 2019) and revealed 6 [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   groups of different digestate typology. By analyzing the obtained digestates groups, two criteria [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   impacted the classification: the TS concentration of the digestates associated with dry or wet anaerobic [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   digestion process and the feedstock type, as following: liquid fibrous feedstock (crop residues silage, [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   cattle slurry), liquid sewage sludge, liquid pig slurry, slurries co-digested with silage and green wastes [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   (dry anaerobic digestion), municipal solid wastes (dry anaerobic digestion) and fibrous feedstock (dry [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   anaerobic digestion of cattle manure and green waste). In this framework, mineral N can be readily [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   available for crops but can be temporarily immobilized by microbial biomass during OW [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   mineralization in soil (Lashermes et al., 2010). Therefore, it is important to know the OW [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   mineralization N kinetics to optimize the N supply synchronisation with plant requirement in [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   agricultural systems. [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   In this context, to better control and manage the quality of digestates, a better knowledge of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   accessibility and availability of nutrients and organic matter would improve the fertilizing potential of [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   these products. According to Möller et al. (2012), an accurate characterization of digestate nutrient [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   content and organic matter (OM) composition, combined with experiments to assess the N [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   mineralization and N immobilization processes after field spreading, would be essential for a better [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   characterization of the driving factors driving N turnover in the soil. [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   Total content of nutrients in OW harldy predicts their fertilizer potential. Ahmad et al. (2018) reported [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   more than 80% of applied P is rapidly immobilized being unavailable for plant following [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   adsorption/precipitation processes or conversion into organic form. To gain a better insight on this [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   issue, many authors proposed to characterize the available P via P fractionation. He et al. (2010) [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   reported that bioavailability of applied P depends on the presence of specific P forms and that labile P [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   includes the sum of inorganic and organic P from H2O and NaHCO$_3$ extracted fractions. In this regard, [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   Grigatti et al. (2015; 2017; 2019), performed a dedicated SCE on digestates and composts in order to [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   measure the phosphorous potentially available for plants. The authors found good correlations [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   between the water- and the sodium bicarbonate-extractable P from composts with short- and medium- [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   term plant P uptake. [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   Lashermes et al. (2010) proposed an OW classification based on their chemical characteristics to [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   predict N availability. However, only 2% of the 273 OW studied came from similar typology of [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   digestates coming from fibrous feedtstock anaerobic digestion with low N availability (i.e. digestates [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   was classified in a group with initial N concentration under 65 g kg-1 and C: N ratio around 15). [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   However, according to Guilayn et al. (2019), the N content from digestates varies from 20 g kg-1 to [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   175 g kg-1 and C: N ratio varies from 2 to more than 30. It would be interesting to complete the [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   Lashermes study with different typologies of digestate to classify their fertilizer potential. To do that, [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   the first step would be to evaluate the impact of digestate typology on plant growth. Recently, a [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   methodology proposed for organic matter characterization has been applied to a large range of OW in [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   order to predict both bio-avalability and complexity/biodegradability of the organic matter (Jimenez et [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   al., 2015). This technique has been successfully used for OM biodegradation kinetics modelling [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   (anaerobic digestion, compost, soil) (Jimenez et al., 2017) and for organic N dynamics in anaerobic [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   digestion (Bareha et al., 2018; 2019). The basic idea was to find a consistent characterization method [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   in order to describe the bioprocess kinetics and to model the whole treatment chain in terms of organic [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   carbon fate, e.g. anaerobic digestion, compost and organic matter fate in soil. The methodology used is [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   based on SCE to simulate organic matter bioaccessibility for microorganisms combined with three- [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   dimensional fluorescence spectroscopy to analyze the organic matter complexity. The challenge is [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   now to transfer this technique to the characterization of nitrogen accessibility in order to predict its [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   fate after land-spreading and its availability for plant growth. [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   Apart from the previously mentioned studies, there is no literature information on the development of [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   indicators for both N and P plant availability from digestates. This is the reason this study aimed to [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   propose indicators based on chemical extractions and characterization for N and P. [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   In this light, the objectives of this work were to determine the plant-available N and P fractions based [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   on characterization of the accessibility study in order to: (i) assess the impact of typology of OW on [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   the nutrient availability, and (ii) use these indicators to predict plant’s nutrients recovery. This study, [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   focussed on a wide range of anaerobic digestates from many types of OW. The results could be used [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   for rapid diagnostic of the (N, P) fertilization potential of a digestate. [SOURCE: Page_5]
