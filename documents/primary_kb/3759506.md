### 1. Introduction

From the 1930s on, soil degradation and erosion became pressing issues worldwide. These [SOURCE: Page_3]

problems were mainly tackled through anti-erosion initiatives, as shown in the works of Bennet [SOURCE: Page_3]

(1939), the so-called “father of soil conservation” (Roose et al., 2010). Hereafter, rural [SOURCE: Page_3]

development programs often included a “water and soil conservation” component. Such [SOURCE: Page_3]

programs rely on different types of hydraulic structures designed to limit erosion, gullying and [SOURCE: Page_3]

silting up of downstream dams. Some are designed to reduce and slow down runoff, while [SOURCE: Page_3]

retaining the soil by creating benches or ridges made of stone and vegetation. Others aim to [SOURCE: Page_3]

“trap” the sediment in structures positioned around the catchment, which is the case of hillside [SOURCE: Page_3]

reservoirs. Huge spaces of this type have been created around the world, particularly in [SOURCE: Page_3]

Mediterranean regions, as these are considered particularly vulnerable to land degradation. [SOURCE: Page_3]

As water and society are both deeply rooted in mutually shaped relationships (Casciarri and [SOURCE: Page_3]

Van Aken, 2013), introducing hydraulic infrastructures in a landscape will inevitably affect [SOURCE: Page_3]

both ecology, the organization of social groups and water/society relationships. The abundant [SOURCE: Page_3]

scientific literature on hillside reservoirs in both social and physical sciences, testifies to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

difficulty of grasping their implications in a given area. This literature is highly segmented, and [SOURCE: Page_4]

separates physical (hydrology and soil sciences) and social approaches in two very distinct [SOURCE: Page_4]

worlds of thought, with little communication between them, as is often the case for water and [SOURCE: Page_4]

more generally for “natural resources” (Mosse, 1997; Baviskar, 2003; Bakker and Bridge, [SOURCE: Page_4]

2006). On the one hand, hydrologists and soil scientists focus their attention on how physical [SOURCE: Page_4]

processes such as erosion or runoff are affected by the construction of benches or reservoirs: [SOURCE: Page_4]

their analyses focus on how to better control the natural flows of sediments and water. Their [SOURCE: Page_4]

research questions therefore focus on the "how": the positionning and dimensioning the works, [SOURCE: Page_4]

sediment flows, runoff and water infiltration is influenced (i.e. Baccari et al., 2008; Zucca et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al., 2015). This research can be viewed as the scientific counterpart of hydraulic development [SOURCE: Page_4]

policies, in which water and soil are considered as “resources” to be developed in the most [SOURCE: Page_4]

efficient way. On the other hand, social scientists focus on the economic, political and social [SOURCE: Page_4]

aspects of water and soil conservation. The vast majority of their studies focus on hillside [SOURCE: Page_4]

reservoirs, also known as small dams, which are the most visible developments, and whose [SOURCE: Page_4]

particularity is to be able to store water, and therefore to enable hydro-agricultural activities. [SOURCE: Page_4]

These studies focus on three main aspects. First, the economic potential of the reservoirs for [SOURCE: Page_4]

productive activities including agriculture, fish farming, watering livestock, and leisure [SOURCE: Page_4]

activities (i.e. Cecchi, 2007). The main entry is, however, agricultural water management, in [SOURCE: Page_4]

line with the productivity function of hydraulic development planning (i.e. Mugabe et al., 2003; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Ashraf et al., 2007). The second way of considering the social dimensions of reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_4]

concerns how people organize activities and distribute water and/or land rights (Mosse, 1997; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Sally and Levite, 2011).. The third approach focuses on water policy analysis. Social scientists [SOURCE: Page_4]

highlight the logics behind development choices and their social and political consequences, [SOURCE: Page_4]

particularly problems linked with corruption (Venot et al., 2011; Venot and Hirvonen, 2013). [SOURCE: Page_4]

Many authors have also focused on the difficulties and conflicts associated with the creation of [SOURCE: Page_5]

water users associations, which is the institutional component of watershed development [SOURCE: Page_5]

programs (Mosse, 1997; Aubriot and Prabakhar, 2011; de Fraiture et al., 2014). Finally, a very [SOURCE: Page_5]

small corpus of research literature highlights the way in which hydraulic infrastructures are set [SOURCE: Page_5]

up as “symbolic resources” (Mosse, 1997). [SOURCE: Page_5]

This last approach, focused on the resource making process, is particularly enlightening for [SOURCE: Page_5]

several reasons that guided our analytical choices during this interdisciplinary study of Tunisian [SOURCE: Page_5]

hillside reservoirs. The starting point was that dialog between hydrologists and social scientists [SOURCE: Page_5]

is difficult, if not impossible, due to their epistemologically and axiologically incompatible [SOURCE: Page_5]

orientations concerning hillside reservoirs, and more generally water (Wesselink et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_5]

However, scientists must overcome both the “dangers in an over-socialized view of natural [SOURCE: Page_5]

resources” (Mosse, 1997, p.473) and the “presumptions contained in the term ‘natural [SOURCE: Page_5]

resource’ [… where] ‘natural’ suggests an existence outside culture” (Bavsikar, 2003, p.5053). [SOURCE: Page_5]

To tackle this epistemological challenge, Valadaud and Aubriot (2019) underlined the [SOURCE: Page_5]

importance for social scientists to consider the materiality of water, by adopting a sociotechnical [SOURCE: Page_5]

approach to water/society relationships. Thanks to the physical structure of technical objects [SOURCE: Page_5]

and the way they operate, this focus makes it possible to observe the many different functions [SOURCE: Page_5]

the objects fulfil, irrespective of whether the functions are material or symbolic, physical or [SOURCE: Page_5]

social (Sigaut, 2010). Here, we argue that the double entry, i.e. via the materiality of water and [SOURCE: Page_5]

via the resource making process, is also a powerful way of reconciling social and hydrological [SOURCE: Page_5]

analysis of water/society relationships around hillside reservoirs. [SOURCE: Page_5]

This article reports on an interdisciplinary field study in the Merguellil wadi catchment, in [SOURCE: Page_5]

Central Tunisia, between 2011 and 2013, conducted by an anthropologist, a hydrologist and [SOURCE: Page_5]

and a technician specialized in quantitative and qualitative field data collection. This study [SOURCE: Page_5]

represents a “sociohydrological” way of considering the relationships between water and [SOURCE: Page_5]

society (Riaux and Massuel, 2014; Riaux, 2019), between the socio-hydrology approach used [SOURCE: Page_6]

by hydrologists (Sivapalan et al., 2012) and the hydrosocial approach used by critical [SOURCE: Page_6]

geographers (Linton and Budds, 2014). Such an interdisciplinary dialog between anthropology [SOURCE: Page_6]

and hydrology involves a continuous “negotiation” (Massuel et al., 2018), which in the present [SOURCE: Page_6]

study, comprised four steps and is detailed below. [SOURCE: Page_6]

To trace the history of watershed development policies implemented in Central Tunisia, we first [SOURCE: Page_6]

analyze the multiple embedded logics underlying Tunisian hillside reservoir planning. In the [SOURCE: Page_6]

second part of the text, we present the results of our original survey of water use and [SOURCE: Page_6]

management practices surrounding the hillside reservoirs in the Merguellil catchment. These [SOURCE: Page_6]

initial observations underpinned the process of negotiating an interdisciplinary framework to [SOURCE: Page_6]

analyze the social, physical and technical aspects of hillside reservoirs. We then present the [SOURCE: Page_6]

“sociohydrological narratives” of four hillside reservoirs that combine the inhabitants’ accounts [SOURCE: Page_6]

of their reservoir history. In the third section, we analyze the ethnographical material through [SOURCE: Page_6]

the lense of resource making processes. Considering the way in which hydraulic objects operate [SOURCE: Page_6]

in a both socio-political and hydrological landscape enables us to analyze different aspects of [SOURCE: Page_6]

the resource making process, which, in turn, allowed us to analyze how a hydraulic technique [SOURCE: Page_6]

is interpreted differently by different actors, leading to contradictory situations, for example, [SOURCE: Page_6]

the inhabitants’ concerns about how to ensure a resource that is often considered unreliable, can [SOURCE: Page_6]

be secure in the long term. The explanation of this contradiction can be found in the symbolic [SOURCE: Page_6]

functions fulfilled by hillside reservoirs. [SOURCE: Page_6]

### 2.1. Historical overview of watershed development policies in Tunisia

Water and soil conservation works in Tunisia began in the 1950s, first implemented by the [SOURCE: Page_7]

hydraulic services of the colonial Public Works, then after Independence (1956) by the Forest [SOURCE: Page_7]

Direction of the Ministry of Agriculture, until a dedicated Water and Soil Conservation [SOURCE: Page_7]

Department was created in 1983 (Roose, 2002). In 2001, the perimeter of the Water and Soil [SOURCE: Page_8]

Conservation Department was redefined, with a focus on agricultural development in rural [SOURCE: Page_8]

areas, as reflected in its name: General Direction for the Development and Conservation of [SOURCE: Page_8]

Agricultural Land (French acronym DG-ACTA). These developments marked a change in the [SOURCE: Page_8]

way of conceiving State interventions in rural areas that is nicely illustrated by the history of [SOURCE: Page_8]

Initially, the aim behind these developments was twofold. The first was to control erosion [SOURCE: Page_8]

caused by runoff to prevent soil degradation. It first involved a combination of benches and [SOURCE: Page_8]

anti-erosion plantations that were largely inspired by the so-called “traditional hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_8]

techniques”: meskat and tabia, used in other parts of the country (El Amami, 1977; Hill and [SOURCE: Page_8]

characteristics, which meant they can be built locally, which was the second objective of these [SOURCE: Page_8]

developments. At Independence, above all, this hydraulic development policy had social [SOURCE: Page_8]

Nations World Food Program, this policy has led to substantial soil conservation works. The [SOURCE: Page_8]

developed, with priority given to social and political issues, and the technical robustness of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

complementary tool to reduce erosion and protect downstream land, leading to a Tunisian- [SOURCE: Page_8]

From the 1980s on, a new objective emerged in development policies: that of preventing the [SOURCE: Page_8]

downstream dams. This led to the idea of water conservation in addition to the original idea of [SOURCE: Page_9]

soil conservation. However, it was in the framework of the national strategy for Water and Soil [SOURCE: Page_9]

Conservation (CES I, 1990-2000) that its full scope was revealed with 1,000 hillside reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_9]

800 of the planned hillside reservoirs have already been built, including 55 in the upstream [SOURCE: Page_9]

Merguellil basin, justified by protecting the El Haouareb dam built in 1989. These [SOURCE: Page_9]

developments have mainly technical objectives, but local development is still used to justify [SOURCE: Page_9]

objectives regained importance. Thus, the Ministry of Agriculture’s “Kairouan Hills” [SOURCE: Page_9]

reservoirs. The only function of some hillside reservoirs today is to recharge groundwater [SOURCE: Page_9]

through infiltration of their stored water. In this case, local inhabitants are forbidden to use the [SOURCE: Page_9]

### 2.2. The confusing and conflicting functions assigned to hillside reservoirs

Over the past 70 years, hillside reservoirs have been the subject of several policies, tackling [SOURCE: Page_9]

problems as diverse as poverty, rural exodus, land degradation and water management. Their [SOURCE: Page_9]

history can be divided into four main periods associated with five major development goals that [SOURCE: Page_10]

do not replace but rather complement one another (fig 2). [SOURCE: Page_10]

Figure 2. Overlapping functions assigned to hillside reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_10]

The overlapping of functions attributed to hillside reservoirs illustrates their role as “boundary [SOURCE: Page_10]

objects”, as pointed out by Venot and Hirvonen (2013) following Star and Grisemer (1989) and [SOURCE: Page_10]

explaining the success of these hydraulic infrastructures in which each actor saw the [SOURCE: Page_10]

embodiment of their own objectives. [SOURCE: Page_10]

However, this analysis explains but does not get round the fact that contradictory functions are [SOURCE: Page_10]

attributed to hillside reservoirs. The most visible contradiction lies in the way of defining the [SOURCE: Page_10]

location at which the water should be valued. Rural development policies promote local use of [SOURCE: Page_10]

water, particularly for agricultural development. The hillside reservoirs are then explicitly [SOURCE: Page_10]

considered to be a tool for local development, targeting the people who live near the reservoir. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Conversely, watershed development aims to control hydraulic flows so that water can be used [SOURCE: Page_10]

elsewhere. Thus, limiting the silting up of dams is the result of a policy oriented towards [SOURCE: Page_10]

regional hydro-agricultural development. In the same way, upstream groundwater recharge [SOURCE: Page_10]

plays a role in using upstream water to supply downstream urban areas with drinking water. In [SOURCE: Page_10]

this case, the role of hillside reservoirs is considered at the regional level and no longer at the [SOURCE: Page_10]

local level. [SOURCE: Page_10]

These contradictions are embodied in the mode of operation of hillside reservoirs: several [SOURCE: Page_11]

different hydrological operating modes are expected of the same type of infrastructure. [SOURCE: Page_11]

Reservoirs intended for groundwater recharge, for example, should facilitate infiltration of [SOURCE: Page_11]

runoff water, and consequently empty rapidly. Conversely, reservoirs intended for local [SOURCE: Page_11]

irrigation should collect as much water as possible and store it for use in periods when crops [SOURCE: Page_11]

need water. Reservoirs designed to limit silting up of dams must “trap” as much sediment as [SOURCE: Page_11]

possible, consequently they silt up rapidly, thereby reducing their water storage capacity and [SOURCE: Page_11]

their lifespan. [SOURCE: Page_11]

Theoretically, the location, design and dimensioning of the technical objects serves to meet [SOURCE: Page_11]

these diverse objectives. Despite robust technical expertise (Zucca et al., 2015), the operating [SOURCE: Page_11]

process of hillside reservoirs can make it difficult to match their expected functions. The [SOURCE: Page_11]

problem of trying to match functions and modes of operation appears to be partly resolved [SOURCE: Page_11]

through a shift in the discourse by the administrations in charge of these developments. As we [SOURCE: Page_11]

observed in the discourse recorded during several meetings of the regional administrations in [SOURCE: Page_11]

charge of water management and agriculture, in Kairouan, the flaunted functions of a reservoir [SOURCE: Page_11]

may evolve with its actual operation. For example, the recent argument in favor of groundwater [SOURCE: Page_11]

recharge appears to be the result of a change in the discourse concerning reservoirs that have [SOURCE: Page_11]

failed to hold water for irrigation. The implementation of a new development program may also [SOURCE: Page_11]

alter the role officially attributed to a reservoir. For example, some reservoirs designed to limit [SOURCE: Page_11]

silting up of downstream dams but that have satisfactory filling characteristics have been [SOURCE: Page_11]

included in the irrigation component of the "Kairouan Hills" rural development program [SOURCE: Page_11]

(Cheikh-Rouhou, 2014). This could be one illustration of the pragmatism deployed by the [SOURCE: Page_11]

officers in charge of managing the contradictions that underlie public policies. But this does not [SOURCE: Page_11]

solve the concrete effects of these contradictions when these reservoirs are constructed on [SOURCE: Page_11]

territories that have already been appropriated, organized and valued by their inhabitants. [SOURCE: Page_11]

### 3.1. A negotiated ethnography of hillside reservoirs

The Merguellil wadi catchment is located in the mountainous hinterland of the Kairouan region [SOURCE: Page_12]

in central Tunisia, it is known as a marginalized area and is somewhat neglected by public [SOURCE: Page_12]

actions (Attia, 1984; Gana, 2012; Fautras, 2015). Until the middle of the 20th century, the great [SOURCE: Page_12]

based on the complementary use of local resources and mobility. In the last century, rural and [SOURCE: Page_12]

agricultural development policies have encouraged a shift to sedentary and more intensive [SOURCE: Page_12]

farming practices. Irrigation started to play a central role in the production systems of the vast [SOURCE: Page_12]

plain located downstream. Upstream, water and soil conservation programs sought to reduce [SOURCE: Page_12]

soil erosion and soil loss as well as prevent the silting up of the large downstream El Haouareb [SOURCE: Page_12]

dam. Most hillside reservoirs were thus not specifically designed to develop local irrigation [SOURCE: Page_12]

(Ogilvie et al. 2019). However, hillside reservoirs “create” a potential water supply and [SOURCE: Page_12]

populations see their semi-arid territory becoming the site of potential hydro-agricultural [SOURCE: Page_12]

development. It is easy to imagine both the expectations that these developments generate, and [SOURCE: Page_12]

the stakes they represent locally. [SOURCE: Page_12]

However, despite the abundant literature on hillside reservoirs, very little has been published [SOURCE: Page_12]

about how local “beneficiaries” really perceive and welcome the reservoirs. As we have seen, [SOURCE: Page_12]

most research focuses on their hydrological functioning, while only a few authors have studied [SOURCE: Page_12]

the “uses” and associated economic benefits of the reservoirs (e.g. Selmi, 1996; Selmi and [SOURCE: Page_12]

Talineau, 1997; Fleskens et al., 2005). These works conclude (negatively) that reservoirs are [SOURCE: Page_12]

“underused” (Selmi, 1996, p. 256), or “manifestly underexploited” (Selmi and Talineau, 1997, [SOURCE: Page_12]

p. 462). However, the reservoirs were evaluated without considering the original function [SOURCE: Page_13]

attributed to each reservoir. Can this explain the observed “under-exploitation” of hillside [SOURCE: Page_13]

reservoirs? [SOURCE: Page_13]

The decision to survey people living in the vicinity of the reservoirs was made rapidly. [SOURCE: Page_13]

reservoirs was therefore drawn up for the entire catchment that identified a wide variety of [SOURCE: Page_13]

novel classification of the diversity of hydro-sociosystems (Ogilvie, 2015; Ogilvie et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_13]

### 4.2. Hillside reservoirs’ materiality and the process of symbolic resource making

The second way of looking at the functions performed by hillside reservoirs in the process of [SOURCE: Page_29]

resource making also focuses on their physical properties. But this time, it refers to their [SOURCE: Page_29]

“materiality” as hydraulic objects, visibly anchored in the physical and social space (Aubriot, [SOURCE: Page_29]

reservoirs and give it different roles in their world of meaning. [SOURCE: Page_29]

Through the sociohydrological narratives, our intervieees told the story of both the construction [SOURCE: Page_29]

of a reservoir and that of a "new" social group. The members of this group are linked by [SOURCE: Page_29]

accounts of the construction of these reservoirs then represent a kind of ontological narrative, [SOURCE: Page_29]

Mosse (1997, p. 498) transposes to the Indian water tanks: “They are part of the socialization [SOURCE: Page_29]

of space and the 'production of locality', both in itself and in opposition to other localities as [SOURCE: Page_29]

social and ecological places”. The production of the locality around hydraulic objects is, in the [SOURCE: Page_29]

Tunisian case, externally driven and very recent. In this case, the “hydraulic locality” is built [SOURCE: Page_29]

on land whose appropriation and use was previously organized around lineage and extensive [SOURCE: Page_29]

livestock breeding. The insistence of our interviewees on the changes in productive activities [SOURCE: Page_29]

towards sedentary arboriculture reveals the depth of the changes operated by the inhabitants in [SOURCE: Page_29]

recent decades and in some way ratified by the ‘arrival’ of a reservoir. The place of the land [SOURCE: Page_29]

hierarchical pre-existing organization, despite the socio-spatial reconfigurations occurring [SOURCE: Page_29]

around the reservoir. It can also be seen as a critique of the place that wardens occupy in the [SOURCE: Page_29]

social order of the new “hydraulic locality”. We indeed observed that the reservoir wardens [SOURCE: Page_30]

often have priority when it comes to accessing the reservoir, particularly by appropriating the [SOURCE: Page_30]

pumps allocated to the water users associations. When the interviews probed deeper into the [SOURCE: Page_30]

issue, they quickly turned into a narrative of grievance about wardens’ behavior, whether [SOURCE: Page_30]

questioning their legitimacy (because they come from outside the local community, because [SOURCE: Page_30]

their families did not originally own any land around the lake, etc.) or criticizing their behavior [SOURCE: Page_30]

(abuse of power, abuse of collective property). The reservoir also gave rise to discourse on [SOURCE: Page_30]

inequalities and local hierarchies: in other words, a political discourse. The more in-depth the [SOURCE: Page_30]

interviews became, the stronger the criticism of the past political regime and hierarchies of local [SOURCE: Page_30]

influence. [SOURCE: Page_30]

Another dimension of the accounts concerns the connection that the reservoir creates between [SOURCE: Page_30]

the local group and the outside. Populations of the Merguellil area, and more generally the [SOURCE: Page_30]

inhabitants of the country’s rural areas, share a feeling of exclusion compared to the [SOURCE: Page_30]

economically and politically more favoured urban and coastal areas^4. Yet, receiving a hillside [SOURCE: Page_30]

reservoir on one’s territory involves regular visits from State officers, who assess the state of [SOURCE: Page_30]

the hydraulic infrastructures. The presence of these reservoirs also attracts researchers, as is the [SOURCE: Page_30]

case of one of the reservoirs studied here, which welcomes generations of research programs. [SOURCE: Page_30]

the outside. The reservoir then presents itself as a “relational resource”. But the relation is [SOURCE: Page_30]

created, directed and maintained by the State in an almost unilateral way. Moreover, the State's [SOURCE: Page_30]

interest in “the reservoirs” as a whole and not in “this particular reservoir” clearly unbalances [SOURCE: Page_30]

the relationship: the interests of the members of the hydraulic locality do not necessarily match [SOURCE: Page_30]

those of the authorities. Moreover, the appropriation of the reservoir by the inhabitants is not [SOURCE: Page_30]

### 5. Conclusions

Tunisian hillside reservoirs are embedded in a multiform process of resource making, where [SOURCE: Page_32]

the created resources are both productive and symbolic. Each actor appropriates this technical [SOURCE: Page_32]

object and makes it embody their own objectives, play different roles, in turn contradictory and [SOURCE: Page_32]

complementary. [SOURCE: Page_32]

Hillside reservoirs therefore not only fulfil a productive function by supplying water for several [SOURCE: Page_32]

uses. They also generate relational resources. On the one hand, the reservoirs are at the heart of [SOURCE: Page_32]

the production of a "hydraulic locality" around which rural populations are recomposing a [SOURCE: Page_33]

socio-political order in a deeply modified productive landscape. On the other hand, they [SOURCE: Page_33]

materialize the authority of the State even to the ends of the nation. As a result, the limits that [SOURCE: Page_33]

certain reservoirs present in terms of local productive resources are used to produce relational [SOURCE: Page_33]

resources. In this process, the reservoir warden plays a central role as a “resource broker”, by [SOURCE: Page_33]

mediating the relationships between the locality and the public authority, between the reservoir [SOURCE: Page_33]

and the resource making processes used by the different stakeholders. [SOURCE: Page_33]

This analysis emphasizes the value of moving beyond the essentialization of water as a resource [SOURCE: Page_33]

to go further in the social and political process underlying the production of the various [SOURCE: Page_33]

resources that can be produced from a reservoir: local or regional water resources as much as [SOURCE: Page_33]

social and political resources. This also reveals the different logics at work around the reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_33]

and the way these logics interfere with each other, sometimes leading to contradictions and [SOURCE: Page_33]

misunderstandings. However, it is also essential to account for the strong influence of [SOURCE: Page_33]

biophysical processes on the social and political dynamics involved in the resource making [SOURCE: Page_33]

process. Biophysical contingencies constrain water resource making, thus revealing other kind [SOURCE: Page_33]

of resources that hydraulic objects could provide. [SOURCE: Page_33]

The analytical entry associating the technical object, its materiality and its action on biophysical [SOURCE: Page_33]

processes is particularly enlightening for the analysis of resource making processes. It allows [SOURCE: Page_33]

the observation that, in the case of Tunisian hillside reservoirs, the shape and operating of the [SOURCE: Page_33]

technical object are not determined, upstream, by the functions attributed to the reservoirs. [SOURCE: Page_33]

Quite the reverse, it is because their most obvious and productive functions (storing water) are [SOURCE: Page_33]

failing that the actors seize on other possible functions of the reservoir to try to turn them into [SOURCE: Page_33]

resources. This transposition work is carried out with equal efficiency by the officers of the [SOURCE: Page_33]

State and by the members of the hydraulic locality, particularly the reservoir warden. But the [SOURCE: Page_33]

resulting resources remain unequally distributed among the different actors, forcing the less [SOURCE: Page_34]

advantaged to leave the locality. [SOURCE: Page_34]

This last observation links physical, political and social processes, thereby responding to one [SOURCE: Page_34]

of the challenges of sociohydrological approaches (Wesselink et al., 2016; Massuel et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_34]

Consequently, this sociohydrological research on Tunisian hillside reservoirs allows us to [SOURCE: Page_34]

affirm that the interdisciplinary negociated approach, associating observation of technical [SOURCE: Page_34]

objects and analysis of the process of resource making, is a powerful way to go beyond the [SOURCE: Page_34]

scissions between physical and social sciences, to reconcile the different approaches to water- [SOURCE: Page_34]

society relationships. [SOURCE: Page_34]
