Introduction 1 2 3   39   Mediterranean agriculture is an important sector from economic, social and environmental per- 4 5   40   spectives, especially for the southern and eastern Mediterranean countries. It is a significant 6 7 8   41   contributor to gross domestic product (GDP) in these countries; it ensures food security, and it 9 10   42   helps reduce rural migration by providing local jobs (Tanyeri-Abur, 2015). Rainfed agriculture 11 12 13   43   covers 80% and 75% of cultivated lands in the world and North Africa, respectively 14 15   44   (Bhattacharya, 2019; Wani et al., 2009). It is generally based on family systems, and it has 16 17 18   45   significant room for improvement in water productivity (Ruben and Pender, 2004). In hilly 19 20   46   areas, the productivity of rainfed agriculture depends not only on the rainfall regime but also 21 22 47   on the spatiotemporal distribution of surface water and sediment flows (Norouzi et al., 2010). 23 24 25   48   Until now, Mediterranean public policies within hilly areas have mainly focused on mobilizing 26 27   49   blue water resources for irrigated agriculture through the planning of hydroagricultural infra- 28 29 30   50   structures (e.g., dams, reservoirs) and have focused less on optimizing the use of green water 31 32   51   for rainfed agriculture (Nouri et al., 2020). 33 34 35   52   Despite of numerous benefits for Mediterranean hilly areas, rainfed agriculture undergoes sev- 36 37 38   53   eral pressures (Brun et al., 2016), either climate-driven (floods and erosion, heat waves, rainfall 39 40   54   shortages) or anthropogenic-driven (population growth, increasing agricultural activities and 41 42 43 55   hydroagricultural infrastructures). From a sustainability perspective, it is important to quanti- 44 45   56   tatively manage agricultural water, where some of the numerous solutions to be explored in- 46 47   57   volve the spatiotemporal modulation of anthropogenic actions, individually or in combination 48 49 50   58   (IAASTD, 2008). These solutions imply processes (fluxes, storages and transformations) and 51 52   59   several components (e.g., root zone, aquifer, vegetation, hydroagricultural infrastructures, ag- 53 54 55   60   ricultural practices) within agrosystems. The exploration of these solutions must account for 56 57   61   two key points. First, in situ scientific experiments are not suitable for both large agricultural 58 59 60 62   areas and forecasts in a climate change context, which makes necessary the use of process 61 62 63                                                                                                      3/52 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   modelling for numerical simulations (Jones et al., 2017). Second, the water cycle and crop 1 2 64   dynamics are strongly linked. Vegetation cover drives the rainfall repartition between runoff 3 4 5   65   and infiltration in relation to fluxes within hydrographic networks towards lakes and reservoirs, 6 7   66   to soil water storage and to aquifer refill. The water cycle drives root zone water content and 8 9 10   67   crop water consumption, with consequences on agricultural yields. It is therefore important to 11 12   68   properly characterize the interactions between the water cycle and crop functioning within ag- 13 14 15   69   rosystems (Kanda et al., 2018). 16 17 18   70   Integrated process modelling should be able to simulate, within hilly catchments, crop func- 19 20   71   tioning and the water cycle along with their interactions. This requires parsimonious crop mod- 21 22 72   els that (1) minimize the number of parameters for realistic simulations with spatialization pur- 23 24 25   73   poses and (2) simulate crop functioning in relation to water dynamics within the root zone layer 26 27   74   and underlying shallow aquifer. The literature provides a large number of crop models that 28 29 30   75   describe plant functioning and growth along with crop yield (Weiss et al., 2020). Well known 31 32   76   examples are APSIM (Keating et al., 2003), DSSAT (Jones et al., 2003), EPIC (Williams et 33 34 35   77   al., 1984), STICS (Brisson et al., 2003), WOFOST (Todorovic et al., 2009; de Wit et al., 2019), 36 37   78   AquaCrop (Raes et al., 2009; Steduto et al., 2009), CropSyst (Stöckle et al., 2003), or AqYield 38 39 40 79   (Constantin et al., 2015; Tribouillois et al., 2018). Based on the primary factors that describe 41 42   80   crop functioning, Todorovic et al. (2009) classified crop models into (1) carbon-driven models 43 44   81   such as WOFOST, CROPGRO, and DSSAT, (2) solar radiation-driven models such as 45 46 47   82   CERES, EPIC, STICS, and APSIM, and (3) water-driven models such as AquaCrop and 48 49   83   CropSyst in which biomass production is proportional to the amount of transpired water. 50 51 52   84   CropSyst is a model based on water and solar radiation. When the vapour pressure deficit 53 54   85   (VPD) is very low, the transpiration-biomass relationship is replaced by a radiative approach 55 56 57   86   in which biomass is determined on the basis of intercepted photosynthetically active radiation 58 59 60 61 62 63                                                                                                    4/52 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_4]

87    (IPAR, Stöckle et al., 2003; Kanda et al., 2018). Among the aforementioned crop models, Aq- 1 2 88   uaCrop is therefore the unique water-driven model (Kanda et al., 2018). It is thus an interesting 3 4 5 89    model for addressing the coupling of crop functioning and the water cycle within Mediterra- 6 7 90    nean hilly catchments typified by rainfed agriculture. 112    (SWC) (Nyakudya and Stroosnijder, 2014; Sghaier et al., 2014). Similar to most crop models, 1 2 113   AquaCrop was designed and evaluated for local applications at the plot level over flat terrains. 3 4 5 114   More recently, the model was evaluated on hilly terrains, either in a multilocal way that disre- 6 7 115   garded interplot water exchanges (Alaya et al., 2019; Han et al., 2019) or in a distributed way 8 9 10 116   that accounted for interplot water exchanges (Van Loo and Verstraeten, 2021). However, more 11 12 117   research is needed to address the diversity of situations induced by Mediterranean rainfed hilly 13 14 15 118   agrosystems in relation to cropping, soil and topographic conditions. In addition, AquaCrop 16 17 119   was evaluated on only a few variables simultaneously, whereas any multicriteria evaluation is 18 19 20 120   likely to provide a better assessment of model capacities. 21 22 121   The objective of this study is to deepen the evaluation of the capabilities of the AquaCrop 23 24 25 122   model for rainfed crops within hilly Mediterranean catchments. We propose (1) to consider 26 27 123   little-studied crops (e.g., faba bean and oats) under subhumid to semiarid climates, (2) to con- 28 29 30 124   sider combinations of crops (faba bean, oats, wheat, barley) and soils (Vertisols, Cambisols 31 32 125   and Luvisols) for contrasted hydroclimatic years, and (3) to conduct a more substantial mul- 33 34 35 126   ticriteria analysis that includes simultaneously vegetation canopy (CC, AGB), soil water con- 36 37 127   tent integrated over topsoil and root zones, and water fluxes (ETa, runoff as infiltration excess). 38 39 40 128   We focus here on the evaluation of AquaCrop without addressing calibration issues. The cur- 41 42 129   rent paper is structured as follows. We briefly present the AquaCrop model. We introduce the 43 44 130   study area and the datasets used, as well as the strategy for evaluating the model. Thereafter, 45 46 47 131   we analyse the comparison of the simulations against the observations by exploring the possible 48 49 132   influences of soil and crop type. We finally discuss these results in light of former studies, and 50 51 52 133   we conclude with our contribution to the assessment of AquaCrop performances, along with 53 54 134   further perspectives. 55 56 57 58 59 60 61 62 63                                                                                                     6/52 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_6]

135    2. Presentation of the AquaCrop model 1 2 3 136   Detailed presentations of AquaCrop (https://www.fao.org/aquacrop/en/) are given by Steduto 4 5 137   et al., (2009), Raes et al. (2009) and Salman et al. (2021). We detail here the specificities related 6 7 8 138   to the methodological choices on which the current study relies. Developed by FAO, AquaCrop 9 10 139   is a parsimonious (reduced number of parameters) crop model that aims to simulate crop bio- 11 12 13 140   mass and yield by considering water as the main driver of crop functioning (Kanda et al., 2018). 14 15 141   Operating at a daily time step, AquaCrop simulates the vertical exchanges between the different 16 17 18 142   components of the soil–plant-atmosphere continuum. 19 20 21 143   AquaCrop describes the soil as a reservoir split into several horizons (5 maximum). Each hori- 22 23 144   zon is characterized by texture or related hydrodynamic properties: soil moisture at field ca- 24 25 145   pacity (HFC), soil moisture at wilting point (HWP), soil moisture at saturation (HSAT), satu- 26 27 28 146   rated hydraulic conductivity (KSAT) and drainage coefficient (τ). 29 30 31 147   The model calculates soil evaporation (Es) and crop transpiration (Tr) separately, which per- 32 33 148   mits the quantification of the amount of water unused by vegetation (Steduto et al., 2009). 34 35 36 149   Another feature of the model is the description of canopy growth by using canopy cover (CC) 37 38 150   instead of leaf area index (LAI). The model calculates Tr as a function of CC, and biomass is 39 40 41 151   determined as a function of both Tr and normalized water productivity (WP*). The yield is 42 43 152   finally calculated by multiplying biomass by harvest index (HI). Water productivity (WP*) 44 45 153   accounts for atmospheric concentration [CO2] and therefore permit to apply AquaCrop in pro- 46 47 48 154   spective climate contexts related to precipitation, air temperature, evaporative demand and 49 50 155   [CO2]. 51 52 53 156   The soil water content at each time step results from the balance of drainage, infiltration from 54 55 56 157   rainfall/irrigation, soil evaporation and crop transpiration. AquaCrop accounts for four types 57 58 158   of stress that affect crop growth: water stress, heat stress, fertilization stress and salinity stress. 59 60 61 62 63                                                                                                         7/52 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_7]

159    Depending on the type of stress, the target parameters of the model change. For example, water 1 2 160   stress affects leaf and CC expansion, root zone expansion, transpiration and the harvest index. 3 4 5 161   The main variables simulated by the model relate to crop productivity (canopy cover, dry 6 7 8 162   aboveground biomass and yield) and water balance (soil water content, runoff, infiltration, 9 10 163   drainage, capillary rise, soil evaporation and vegetation transpiration). The model parameters 11 12 13 164   are related to the crop (conservative parameters, fixed for a given species and nonconservative 14 15 165   parameters, varying according to the varieties), the soil (horizon number, texture or hydrody- 16 17 18 166   namic parameters), and the groundwater table (depth). The forcing variables of the model are 19 20 167   the climatic variables: reference evapotranspiration (ET0), air temperature, rainfall and mean 21 22 168   annual [CO2]. Agricultural practices include sowing date, fertilization and irrigation. Finally, 23 24 25 169   the initial conditions include the initial soil water and salinity content. 26 27 28 170   3. Materials and methods 29 30 31 171   3.1. Study site 32 33 34 172   The study was conducted within the Kamech catchment, which is the southern site of the Med- 35 36 173   iterranean Observatory of Rural Environment and Water (French acronym OMERE, Molénat 37 38 39 174   et al., 2018) that has collected multiple observations over the last 30 years. Kamech is a small 40 41 175   hilly catchment area (2.6 km² size) located within the Cap-Bon Peninsula, northeastern Tunisia 42 43 44 176   (10°52'-10°53'E and 36°52'-36°53' N, 108 m above sea level - asl.). It is representative of the 45 46 177   climatic and cropping conditions of the region. The climate is Mediterranean subhumid/semi- 47 48 178   arid. The rainy season spans from October to March (Mekki, 2003) with a cumulative rainfall 49 50 51 179   of 635 mm (annual average over the [1994-2020] period) and an annual reference evapotran- 52 53 180   spiration of 1366 mm (Molénat et al., 2018). The area is 70% agricultural, combining crops 54 55 56 181   and livestock, with significant spatiotemporal variability in land cover and spatial heterogene- 57 58 182   ity in soil types (Mekki, 2003; Mekki et al., 2006). 59 60 61 62 63                                                                                                  8/52 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_8]

183    The Kamech catchment is typified by a diversified land cover that encompasses rainfed and 1 2 184   fodder crops (Mekki, 2003; Zitouna-Chebbi et al., 2018). The dominant crops are winter cereals 3 4 5 185   (durum wheat, barley, oats and triticale) and legumes (chickpeas, faba bean and peas). Altitudes 6 7 186   range from 80 m to 200 m, and terrain slopes vary between 0 and 30%. The geological substra- 8 9 10 187   tum is from the Miocene epoch, and it is mainly made of marl and clay (Mekki, 2003; Molénat 11 12 188   et al., 2018). The four dominant soils are Cambisols, Luvisols, Vertisols and Regosols. They 13 14 15 189   cover 46%, 26%, 10% and 18% of the catchment area, respectively (Mekki et al., 2018b). The 16 17 190   soil depth varies from a few millimetres to 2 m. 18 19 20 191   Kamech is also typified by a large occurrence of swelling clay soils with shrinkage cracks that 21 22 192   occur from March to December (Mekki, 2003; Inoubli, 2016). The closing of cracks heavily 23 24 25 193   depends on rainfall at the beginning of the wet season and completely ends after cumulative 26 27 194   rainfalls of approximately 200 mm ± 50 mm (Mekki, 2003). At their maximum opening, they 28 29 30 195   have a water storage capacity of approximately 70 mm. Runoff occurs from December to 31 32 196   March, when cracks are closed (Mekki, 2003; Inoubli, 2016). 33 34 35 197   3.2. Discussion 21 22 23 599   5.1. Canopy cover (CC) 24 25 26 600   The large RMSE and NRMSE values on CC were ascribed to an overestimation of CC obser- 27 28 601   vations by AquaCrop simulations throughout the senescence phase. This overestimation could 29 30 602   result from the fact that the model disregarded the effect of high temperatures on crop func- 31 32 33 603   tioning during the senescence phase (Andarzian et al., 2011). First, AquaCrop accounted for 34 35 604   the effect of heat stress (low and high temperatures) on the pollination and harvest index only. 36 37 38 605   Second, the early senescence we observed was not due to water stress: the seasonal courses of 39 40 606   ETa and ET0 observed for wheat in 2013 in plot A (Fig. SP4) started to diverge as of DAS 140 41 42 43 607   (19 Apr 2013), while senescence began at DAS 120 (30 Mar 2013). According to local farmers, 44 45 608   early or sudden senescence of vegetation after heat waves has been observed in Kamech. 46 47 48 609   Other studies reported overestimations of field observations by AquaCrop for CC at the end of 49 50 610   the crop cycle for wheat in arid / semiarid climates, with lower magnitudes (Andarzian et al., 51 52 53 611   2011; Sghaier et al., 2014; Toumi et al., 2016). Beyond such differences during the senescence, 54 55 612   the method used to convert LAI to CC for cereals might be an additional source of uncertainty, 56 57 58 613   since the conversion was calibrated on hemispherical photos and applied on planimetric meas- 59 60 614   urements, both observation types leading to physical differences (Jonckheere et al (2005). 61 62 63                                                                                                      33/52 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_33]

615    5.2. Aboveground Biomass (AGB) 1 2 3 616   AquaCrop simulated AGB well, with an overestimation trend at the end of the crop cycle (e.g., 4 5 617   faba bean, Fig. SP3). This could be ascribed to the overestimation of CC observations by Aq- 6 7 8 618   uaCrop simulations during the senescence phase in relation to a possible delay in senescence 9 10 619   by model simulations. For faba bean plot P5 in 2001, this could not be shown due to the lack 11 12 13 620   of CC measurements at the end of the crop cycle. However, Fig. SP2 indicates a recurrent 14 15 621   overestimation of CC observations by AquaCrop simulations at the end of the crop cycle for 16 17 18 622   different crop types and soil classes. Other studies have reported an overestimation of AGB 19 20 623   observations by AquaCrop simulations, either at the end of the crop cycle (Katerji et al., 2013; 21 22 624   Ahmadi et al., 2015; Sandhu and Irmak, 2019) or during the growth phase (Sghaier et al., 2014). 23 24 25 625   5.3. Actual evapotranspiration (ETa) 26 27 28 626   AquaCrop showed acceptable performance in simulating ETa for wheat and oats, with a trend 29 30 31 627   to slightly overestimate observations. The overestimation of ETa from DAS 130 (9 April 2013) 32 33 628   for wheat and DAS 140 (3 May 2005) for oats could be related to the overestimation of CC 34 35 36 629   observations during the senescence. Masasi et al. (2019) reported a similar trend for sorghum 37 38 630   in a semiarid climate, and they suspected large atmospheric evaporative demand and poor char- 39 40 41 631   acterizations of soil hydrodynamic parameters. Despite this overestimation in ETa, AquaCrop 42 43 632   well reproduced the divergence between ETa and ET0 courses at the end of the crop cycle. 44 45 46 633   The differences between simulated and observed ETa values could also be due to (1) the eddy 47 48 634   covariance measurements that tend to underestimate ETa (Boudhina et al., 2019; Leuning et 49 50 51 635   al., 2012), and (2) the reconstruction of missing ETa data that induces uncertainties (Boudhina 52 53 636   et al., 2018; Zitouna-Chebbi et al., 2018). Besides, Katerji et al. (2013) recalled that the ETa 54 55 56 637   calculation method in AquaCrop has been subject to several criticisms, especially when applied 57 58 638   in semiarid Mediterranean regions (Katerji and Rana, 2006; Lovelli et al., 2007). A first source 59 60 61 62 63                                                                                                 34/52 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_34]

639    of error is assuming a constant surface resistance, since several studies in semiarid and arid 1 2 640   regions have shown that it leads to underestimating ET0 compared to lysimeter measurements 3 4 5 641   (Katerji and Rana 2006). A second source of error is using the default value proposed by Allen 6 7 642   et al. (1998) for cultural coefficient Kc, since several works reported differences up to 40% 8 9 10 643   between this value and those observed in situ (Katerji and Rana 2006). 11 12 13 644   In contrast to the present study, previous works reported an underestimation for ETa by Aqua- 14 15 645   Crop for maize and tomato (Katerji et al., 2013) and for wheat (Toumi et al., 2016) in a Medi- 16 17 18 646   terranean climate. No explanation could be found for this contradiction. For two calibration 19 20 647   plots, Toumi et al. (2016) reported RMSE values of 0.47 mm day-1 and 0.69 mm day-1, which 21 22 648   were similar to the RMSE value found in the current study for wheat in 2013 in plot A. 23 24 25 649   5.4. Runoff (R) 26 27 28 650   For wheat in plot P7 in 2001, wheat in plot P9 in 2002, and oat in plot P6 in 2002, AquaCrop 29 30 31 651   simulated significant runoff values on DAS 19 (3 Dec 2000), DAS 2 (13 Dec 2001), and DAS 32 33 652   12 (11 Dec 2001), respectively. However, we could not evaluate AquaCrop simulations before 34 35 36 653   the second half of December, due to the lack of in situ measurements. However, it was possible 37 38 654   to verify the potential occurrence of runoff. According to Mekki (2003), rainfall greater than 39 40 41 655   20 mm day-1 is likely to generate runoff, regardless of surface conditions. Thus, the rainfall 42 43 656   recorded on DAS 19 for plot P7 in 2001, DAS 2 for plot P9 in 2002, and DAS 12 for plot P6 44 45 657   in 2002 were equal to 40 mm, 20 mm, and 28 mm, respectively (data not shown), which could 46 47 48 658   have produced runoff. This was also consistent with the slight increase of outlet lake level in 49 50 659   early December (compared to mid-November, data not shown). Overall, the runoff simulations 51 52 53 660   by AquaCrop early in the crop cycle were consistent with observations and expert knowledge. 54 55 56 661   For wheat in plot P9 in 2002 (DAS 10, 21 Dec 2001), wheat in plot A in 2013 (DAS 9, 09 Dec 57 58 662   2012), and oats in plot P6 in 2002 (DAS 20, 19 Dec 2001), we noted large values of model- 59 60 61 62 63                                                                                                35/52 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_35]

663    simulated runoff early in the crop cycle, but we did not observe coincident runoff events from 1 2 664   field observations. We observed the same difference between simulated and unobserved peak 3 4 5 665   runoff at the end of the crop cycle, for wheat in 2013 in plot A and for oats in 2002 in plot P6. 6 7 666   This could be explained by the presence of shrinkage cracks, which are known to generate 8 9 10 667   preferential infiltration at the expense of runoff (Inoubli et al., 2017). 11 12 13 668   Wolka et al (2021) reported one of the few assessments on the ability of AquaCrop to simulate 14 15 669   runoff. However, it was difficult to compare these results with ours, because of larger 19 20 671   rainfall and runoff accumulations for Wolka et al. (2021). 21 22 23 672   5.5. Soil water content (SWC) 24 25 26 673   AquaCrop showed good performance in simulating soil water content. For oats, the underesti- 27 28 674   mation of SWC observations was ascribed to the overestimations of ETa and runoff. The dif- 29 30 31 675   ferences between simulations and observations could be due to (1) inaccurate soil moisture 32 33 676   initialisations, (2) poor characterizations of soil hydrodynamic properties (HWP and HFC) and 34 35 36 677   (3) inadequate AquaCrop formalisms when simulating water fluxes (ETa, runoff, drainage). 37 38 678   Additionally, disregarding capillary rise was not critical because most plots were located at 39 40 41 679   slope tops and therefore relatively far from possible shallow aquifers. Overall, the errors in 42 43 680   SWC simulations were ascribed to the characterization of soil hydrodynamic properties (HWP 44 45 681   and HFC), given the accuracies of AquaCrop simulations for water fluxes and crop variables. 46 47 48 682   Previous studies reported overestimations of SWC observations by AquaCrop simulations, no- 49 50 51 683   tably for wheat (Andarzian et al., 2011), maize (Nyakudya and Stroosnijder, 2014) and barley 52 53 684   (El Mokh et al., 2017). These overestimations were often noticed during dry periods, which 54 55 56 685   can be explained by constraints on SWC, since the latter cannot drop below HWP. 57 58 59 686   5.6. Oat crops could be used as animal fodder, 3 4 5 689   which is a common practice within Kamech and the surrounding region, with subsequent graz- 6 7 690   ing and cutting operations throughout the crop cycle. However, AquaCrop does not account 8 9 10 691   for the dynamics of the crop canopy induced by such agricultural practices. Nyathi et al (2018) 11 12 692   tried to parameterize this practice for leafy vegetables, by performing independent simulations 13 14 15 693   for any crop and assuming that the initial canopy cover (CC0) was reset according to the re- 16 17 694   maining canopy cover (from 1 to 2%) after each harvest. 18 19 20 695   When dealing with AquaCrop performance according to soil class, the analysis of model sim- 21 22 696   ulations permitted us to draw partial conclusions only. For runoff and CC, the best results were observed with Vertisols and Cambisols, 28 29 30 699   respectively. For ETa, for which we had measurements on Vertisols only, no conclusion can be 31 32 700   drawn regarding AquaCrop performance according to soil class. 33 34 35 701   When dealing with linear regressions on validation for both crop types and soil classes, the 36 37 38 702   t test provided p values larger than 5% for most cases (e.g., AGB, CC, SWC), which indicated 39 40 703   that AquaCrop performances could be considered satisfactory. For some crop/soil combina- 41 42 43 704   tions, the t test provided p values lower than 5% (e.g., ETa, R, SWC), although the offset re- 44 45 705   mained relatively low (e.g., ETa and SWC offset for oats and Cambisols, respectively). 46 47 48 706   5.7. Main outcomes 49 50 51 707   To our knowledge, the present work is the first study using AquaCrop for faba bean and oats 52 53 708   in a semiarid Mediterranean climate. According to the results we obtained, AquaCrop can ac- 54 55 56 709   ceptably simulate the functioning of these two crops by using crop parameters available in the 57 58 59 60 61 62 63                                                                                                  37/52 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_37]

710    literature. Additionally, AquaCrop simulations are acceptable for various combinations of soils 1 2 711   and crops across contrasted hydroclimatic years. 3 4 5 712   Although there were gaps in database on which the current study relied, it was rich enough to 6 7 8 713   draw several lessons. According to the results we obtained, the model performance was closely 9 10 714   related to the formalism used for simulations. AquaCrop showed good performance in simu- 11 12 13 715   lating biomass and soil water content for all crops, on the basis of parameterizations and forcing 14 15 716   (1) that were as adequate as possible for the crops and soils to be studied, and (2) that were in 16 17 18 717   line with literature recommendations. The performance of the model was moderate for the sim- 19 20 718   ulation of CC, with a possible delay in senescence for most of the crops we addressed. The 21 22 719   model showed acceptable performance in simulating ETa, although it was delicate to conclude 23 24 25 720   according to the dataset size (2 years - plots). 26 27 28 721   Runoff was poorly simulated at both the beginning and end of the crop cycle because of shrink- 29 30 722   age cracks for clay soils. Soil cracking is a complex phenomenon that is very difficult to include 31 32 33 723   in numerical modelling, especially in simplified models. Runoff simulations were acceptable 34 35 724   for the other stages of the crop cycle when the cracks were closed. Despite this, the simulations 36 37 38 725   were acceptable to simulate the dynamics of soil water content and crop variables (AGB), as 39 40 726   shown in Figs. 4-5-9-10 and Tabs 5-6-10-11, which was ascribed to the moderate influence of 41 42 43 727   runoff on the soil water balance. However, in the perspective of agro-hydrological studies that 44 45 728   require the consideration of lateral fluxes between the different components of the cultivated 46 47 729   landscape (Van Loo and Verstraeten, 2021), the runoff modelling by AquaCrop was no longer 48 49 50 730   acceptable for our study site, and it will be necessary to consider a more realistic runoff-infil- 51 52 731   tration partitioning model. 53 54 55 732   6. 2   Different approaches are proposed in the literature to determine soil moisture at wilting point 3   (HWP) and at field capacity (HFC), including (1) pedotransfer functions (e.g., Saxton and 4   Rawls, 2006) based on the texture of different soil horizons, (2) direct laboratory measurements 5   from soil samples (Cassel and Nielsen, 1986) and (3) the agroclimatic method which determines 6   HFC and HWP from soil moisture time series throughout the crop growth cycle (Sreelash et al., 7   2017). [SOURCE: Page_53]

8   According to expert knowledge about the soil conditions within our study site (Revaillot et al., 9   2021), the pedotransfer functions are not suitable for the Kamech soils, since the latter are 10   typified by large instability due to poor silt structure. Therefore, we determine HWP and HFC 11   using the laboratory method and the agroclimatic method, and we compared the resulting 12   estimates in order to choose the most reliable ones. [SOURCE: Page_53]

13   For all plots, we had laboratory measurements of HFC and HWP carried out either on the plots 14   or on neighbouring plots, along with soil moisture data. For the agroclimatic method proposed 15   by Sreelash et al. (2017), HFC corresponds to the maximum soil moisture value without 16   considering measurements after rainfalls or irrigation events, and HWP corresponds to the 5th 17   percentile of the minimum soil moisture measured throughout the crop growth cycle. To 18   determine HFC and HWP by the agroclimatic method, we used all soil moisture data available 19   for each of the eight plots, beyond the datasets used for AquaCrop evaluation. This led to 20   include additional soil moisture data from 2002 on plot P$_5$ and P$_8$, from 2001 and plot P$_6$ and 21   P$_9$, and from 2006 on plot A. All the soil moisture measurements we considered were collected 22   using the same protocol described in the Section 3.3.6 of the article. For HFC, we assumed that 23   a rainfall accumulation lower than 10 mm does not have a large influence, and we therefore 24   excluded all measurements for which a rainfall accumulation larger than 10 mm was recorded 25   in the previous 48 hours. For HWP, we take the 5th percentile of the minimum value measured 26   throughout the crop growth cycle. [SOURCE: Page_53]

58   When dealing with growth cycle of cereals (wheat, barley, oats), we could use leaf area index 59   (LAI) measurements performed with planimeters. In order to validate the AquaCrop 60   simulations of canopy cover (CC), we used Equation SP$_2$ to convert planimetry-based LAI data 61   into CC, considering that this equation had been used in many studies (Araya et al., 2010; Abrha 62   et al., 2012; Yuan et al., 2013; Pereira et al., 2015; Zeleke, 2019): [SOURCE: Page_56]

63                            𝐶𝐶 = 1 − 𝑒 (−𝑘×𝐿𝐴𝐼)             (Equation SP$_2$) [SOURCE: Page_56]

64   The coefficient k is an extinction coefficient related to the interception of light by crop canopy 65   cover (Jeuffroy and Ney, 1997; Pereira et al., 2015). It varies according to crop and variety. 66   Different values of k have been proposed in literature for a given crop. For example, the 67   proposed values for barley are k = 0.5 (Pereira et al., 2015), k = 0.48 (Belhouchette et al., 2008) 68   and k = 0.65 (Abrha et al., 2012). For wheat, (Jin et al., 2014) proposed a k value of 0.65. [SOURCE: Page_56]

69   To determine a k value that was suitable to our conditions, we used hemispherical photos that 70   permitted to simultaneously estimate LAI and CC. These photos were collected between 2018 71   and 2020 thanks to a camera equipped with a fisheye objective. Within each plot, between 10 72   and 15 photos were collected in a random manner. Table SP$_2$ summarises the number of plots 73   and measurements available per crop. [SOURCE: Page_56]

74   Table SP$_2$. Number of plots and hemispherical photos available for each crop, to be used for 75                       determining the value of coefficient k in Equation SP$_2$. [SOURCE: Page_56]

Crop              Years              Plot number       Measurements number Wheat       2018 - 2019 - 2020             8                   42 Barley         2019 - 2020                 2                   10 Oats              2020                    1                    4 [SOURCE: Page_56]

76   The hemispherical photos were treated with the CAN-EYE software (Weiss et al., 2008) to 77   derive first gap fraction, and then LAI and CC. CAN-EYE permitted to distinguish two types 78   of LAI, namely (1) effective LAI that accounts for leaf aggregation, and (2) true LAI that 79   corresponds to actual leaf surface. The different LAI estimates proposed by CAN-EYE are 80   discussed in Weiss et al. 84      On the other hand, hemispherical photography is an indirect, non-destructive technique that 85       permits a significant spatiotemporal sampling of crop canopy within field and throughout 86       the crop growth cycle. It includes other plant green elements such as stems, which 87       corresponds to Plant Area Index (PAI) rather than LAI. 88      The goal here was to convert planimetric measurements of LAI into CC estimates by using 89       Equation SP$_2$. When estimating LAI from hemispherical photos, it was necessary to select 90       the CAN-EYE method that provided LAI estimates as close as possible to the planimetric 91       method. According to Weiss et al (2008), the true LAI calculated by CAN-EYE is the 92       closest to the planimetric LAI, although the relationship between true LAI measured by 93       CAN-EYE and planimetric LAI depends on crop and phenological stage (Demarez et al., 94       2008; Fang et al., 2018). Therefore, we choose true LAI for equation SP$_2$. [SOURCE: Page_57]

95   For the present study, we decided to set a single k value for cereals (wheat, barley and oats), 96   equal to 0.57 (R$_2$ = 0.95; RMSE = 0.05). Indeed, the coefficient of variation between the 97   different k values across cereal crops was about 15%, thus comparable to the measurement error 98   (Weiss et al., 2008). Moreover, the three cereal crops we considered were straw cereals with 99   similar leaf geometry that induces similar radiative transfer processes within the canopy. [SOURCE: Page_57]
