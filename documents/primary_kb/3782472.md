24   Abstract 25   During the last 10 years, several attempts to map soil attributes at the scale of mainland [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   France have been realised. We exemplify them by seven major outputs: maps of organic C [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   stocks, trace elements (TE), microbial density and diversity, soil thickness, available water [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   capacity (AWC), extractable P, and changes in soil pH. We first briefly describe the data and [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   the methods used to produce these maps and summarise their main results. We then focus [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   on their impacts on various categories of the public, i.e. the general public and citizens; [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   farmers; private companies; non-governmental organisations; agricultural development [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   organisations, stakeholders, and national agencies; French governmental bodies; and [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   international organisations. We also analyse the demands that came to the French National [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   Soil Information Centre from 2008 to 2018 and the impact that our activities had in various [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   media. Soil organic C had the largest impact in nearly all categories of end-users, which may [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   be linked to the recent ‘4 per 1000’ initiative launched by the French governmentduring the [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   COP21 and to the fact that farmers are interested in increasing the organic matter content of [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   their soil for increasing the fertility. TE obtained high scores, which may be related to [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   citizens’ care about health and to the fact that governmental bodies and national agencies [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   have a major interest in site contamination assessments. The soil P content, pH, and AWC [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   1. Introduction 58   The 1:250.000 soil mapping program of France is nearly complete. During this program, [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   mapping has mainly been conducted using conventional methods. However, there is a [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   discernible need to obtain more precise soil data using other methods, among which digital [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   soil mapping (DSM; McBratney et al., 2003) is attracting considerable attention (Richer-de- [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   Forges et al., 2019; Voltz et al., 2018). In their seminal paper, McBratney et al. (2003) [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   proposed a generic framework called the soil spatial prediction function with spatially auto- [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   correlated errors (scorpan-SSPFe) for producing digital soil maps (Minasny and McBratney, [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   2016). Since the early 1990s, a large number of French papers have dealt with DSM, [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   although they most often focused on local applications. These applications included various [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   methods, such as i) extrapolation of already mapped areas in various French regions (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   Lagacherie et al., 1995; Voltz et al., 1997; Lagacherie and Voltz, 2000; Grinand et al., 2008); [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   ii) the use of disaggregation approaches (Nauman et al., 2012) such as DSMART (Odgers and [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   Clifford, 2014) to disaggregate conventional soil maps for Brittany (Vincent et al., 2016); iii) [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   DSM of specific soil attributes of interest (e.g. Bourennane et al., 1996, 2003; Bourennane [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   and King, 2003; Arrouays et al., 1995; Chaplot et al., 2000) or even of some diagnostic [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   horizons (Richer-de-Forges et al., 2017). More recently, regional attempts to map soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   attributes according to GlobalSoiMap specifications (Arrouays et al., 2014) have been made [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   in Southern France (e.g. Vaysse and Lagacherie, 2015, 2017), and considerable effort has [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   been directed towards investigating the potential of new sensors to map soil properties at [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   the local or regional level (e.g. Gomez et al., 2015, 2019; Vaudour et al., 2014, 2016, 2019; [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   Zaouche et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   soil information available in the centralised national database of soil profiles ‘DoneSol’ and [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   products are detailed in the Materials and methods section of this paper. [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   various end-users, including citizens and the general public; farmers; private companies; [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   stakeholders and national agencies; policymakers; and international bodies, such as the [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   Global Soil Partnership (GSP) of the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organisation (UN- [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   FAO). We present selected examples of predictions of soil organic C (SOC) stocks, the [SOURCE: Page_5]

174   2.2.4. Data and methods used for main national DSM products [SOURCE: Page_9]

175   2.1.4.1. Soil P [SOURCE: Page_9]

176   In the database, a total of 850606 results from soil analyses contained available P content [SOURCE: Page_9]

177   values (in mg P2O$_5$ kg−1). These values were determined by one of the three standardised [SOURCE: Page_9]

178   methods (AFNOR, 1996) used by French soil analysis laboratories, i.e. the "Dyer" method [SOURCE: Page_9]

179   (NFX 31-10), which involves the measurement of soluble P in a citric acid monohydrate [SOURCE: Page_9]

180   solution; the "Joret-Hébert" method (NFX 31-161: determination of soluble P in an [SOURCE: Page_9]

181   ammonium oxalate solution); and the "Olsen" method (ISO 11263: determination of P in a [SOURCE: Page_9]

182   sodium hydrogen carbonate solution). The originality of the method used lies in quantitative [SOURCE: Page_9]

183   results originating from the French Soil Test Database and their evaluation using the RegiFert [SOURCE: Page_9]

184   software (Denoroy et al. 2004), which incorporates soil characteristics and crop sensitivity to [SOURCE: Page_9]

185   nutrient availability. Briefly, RegiFert introduces two threshold values (L$_1$ and L$_2$, with L$_1$ < [SOURCE: Page_9]

186   L$_2$) to interpret the P content in soils according to crop needs. These threshold values [SOURCE: Page_9]

187   depend on the sensitivity of plants to the P availability, on the analytical methods chosen for [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

188   P2O$_5$, as well as on the soil characteristics: the CaCO$_3$ content, pH, clay fraction, and SOC [SOURCE: Page_10]

189   content. This methodology constituted an attempt to gather all analytical results into a [SOURCE: Page_10]

190   common diagnostic framework on the national scale (for France), while taking into account [SOURCE: Page_10]

191   the local pedological context and crop production requirements. Data stored in BDAT over [SOURCE: Page_10]

192   the period of 1990–2004 were originally geographically located on a municipal scale but [SOURCE: Page_10]

193   were then aggregated on the "cantonal" scale for statistical and legal reasons. The French [SOURCE: Page_10]

194   "cantons" are administrative jurisdictions defined as a grouping of several municipalities [SOURCE: Page_10]

195   with a mean land area of 140 km^2. [SOURCE: Page_10]

294   3. Synthesis of main results [SOURCE: Page_14]

295   3.1. Soil P maps [SOURCE: Page_14]

296   Follain et al. (2009) assessed the P bioavailability in arable topsoils in France at a national [SOURCE: Page_14]

297   scale. Using this procedure, they concluded that 77% of cantons were on average below the [SOURCE: Page_14]

298   critical level regarding crop needs. In France, a canton is an administrative unit that [SOURCE: Page_14]

299   combines several municipalities. From an environmental standpoint, this situation could be [SOURCE: Page_14]

300   considered as generating the lowest water pollution risk. Nevertheless, 23% of the cantons [SOURCE: Page_14]

301   exhibited a soil P accumulation that is not necessary with regard to agricultural uses. In this [SOURCE: Page_14]

13 [SOURCE: Page_14]

302   case, no fertilisation is required, and the pollution risk is increased. For instance, focusing on [SOURCE: Page_15]

303   Brittany—a region with intensive livestock production—Lemercier et al. (2008) showed a [SOURCE: Page_15]

304   strong P accumulation in soils with an increased risk of P losses into water bodies and thus of [SOURCE: Page_15]

305   eutrophication. [SOURCE: Page_15]

306   3.2. Changes in soil pH [SOURCE: Page_15]

307   The authors showed that the soil pH increased in 36% of arable soils monitored from 1996 to [SOURCE: Page_15]

308   2010, indicating that soils became less acidic across the country. Conversely, reductions in [SOURCE: Page_15]

309   the pH were almost never detected. Their statistical framework smoothed the effects of [SOURCE: Page_15]

310   some limitations of the database, i.e. the variable number of samples collected during both [SOURCE: Page_15]

311   periods and sampling location uncertainties. For large parts of France, no trend was [SOURCE: Page_15]

312   confirmed, indicating that the program should either gather more samples or wait for a [SOURCE: Page_15]

313   longer time to detect such evolutions. [SOURCE: Page_15]

314   3.3. Trace elements [SOURCE: Page_15]

315   Detailed results are provided by Villanneau et al. (2008), Saby et al. (2011), and Marchant et [SOURCE: Page_15]

316   al. (2017). Villanneau et al. (2008) and Saby et al. (2011) reported that high concentrations of [SOURCE: Page_15]

317   Pb, Cr, Ni, and Zn occurred in soils on crystalline rocks, whereas the Cd concentrations were [SOURCE: Page_15]

318   high in soils developed on Jurassic rocks. Volcanic parent material led to high concentrations [SOURCE: Page_15]

319   of Cr, Cu, Ni, and Zn but low concentrations of Pb and Tl. The diffuse pollution of certain [SOURCE: Page_15]

320   elements (mainly Pb and to a lesser extent Zn) was evident in industrial regions in the north [SOURCE: Page_15]

321   and the northeast of France and close to Paris. The pattern of some outlying values was [SOURCE: Page_15]

322   indicative of more local anthropogenic processes, such as diffuse industrial pollution in the [SOURCE: Page_15]

323   north of France and close to Paris and application of Cu in vineyards, as well as of geological [SOURCE: Page_15]

324   anomalies, such as high concentrations of some TEs in the south of the Massif Central [SOURCE: Page_15]

14 [SOURCE: Page_15]

325   Mountains. Marchant et al. (2017) used geostatistical methods to map the expected [SOURCE: Page_16]

326   concentrations of As and Hg in the topsoil and determine the probabilities that legislative [SOURCE: Page_16]

327   thresholds are exceeded. They found that with the exception of some areas where the [SOURCE: Page_16]

328   geogenic concentrations and soil adsorption capacities are very low, the As concentrations [SOURCE: Page_16]

329   are often higher than the thresholds, indicating that further assessment of some areas is [SOURCE: Page_16]

330   required. There were regions with elevated Hg concentrations, which could be related to [SOURCE: Page_16]

331   volcanic material, natural mineralisation, and industrial contamination. These regions are [SOURCE: Page_16]

332   more diffuse than the hotspots of As, reflecting the greater volatility of Hg and therefore the [SOURCE: Page_16]

333   greater ease with which it can be transported and redeposited. [SOURCE: Page_16]

334   3.4. SOC maps and maps of potential of additional SOC storage [SOURCE: Page_16]

335   A detailed description of SOC maps was presented by Mulder et al. (2016). Briefly, the maps [SOURCE: Page_16]

336   showed large effects of climatic conditions (particularly in mountains), as well as major [SOURCE: Page_16]

337   effects of the land use, soil parent material, and soil clay content. The predicted SOC content [SOURCE: Page_16]

338   was unbiased and exhibited good agreement with the measured SOC, despite poor model [SOURCE: Page_16]

339   diagnostics and a performance reduction with increasing depth. Moreover, the national map [SOURCE: Page_16]

340   outperformed SoilGrids, which is a global map (Hengl et al., 2014). The total SOC for the [SOURCE: Page_16]

341   upper 0–30-cm soils of France was estimated to be approximately 3.6 Pg. By aggregating [SOURCE: Page_16]

342   these results to a coarser resolution (1 km), Martin et al. (2017) found similar geographic [SOURCE: Page_16]

343   distributions and total SOC stocks. [SOURCE: Page_16]

344   The SOC storage potential was modelled by Chen et al. (2019a). They defined different C- [SOURCE: Page_16]

345   landscape zones (CLZs) in France for modelling the soil C sequestration, thereby building [SOURCE: Page_16]

346   upon the research of Mulder et al. (2015). They then computed estimates of the highest [SOURCE: Page_16]

347   possible values using percentiles of 0.8, 0.85, 0.9, and 0.95 of the measured SOC stocks [SOURCE: Page_16]

15 [SOURCE: Page_16]

348   within these CLZs for arable soils. The SOC storage potential was calculated as the difference [SOURCE: Page_17]

349   between the maximum SOC stocks and the current SOC stocks. [SOURCE: Page_17]

350   3.5. Soil thickness [SOURCE: Page_17]

351   Lacoste et al. (2016) compared three spatially explicit modelling approaches for the ST for [SOURCE: Page_17]

352   mainland France (540 km^2): i) regression tree modelling (RTM), ii) gradient boosting [SOURCE: Page_17]

353   modelling (GBM), and iii) multi-resolution kriging (MrK) for large datasets. The RMQS dataset [SOURCE: Page_17]

354   was used for calibration; the IGCS dataset was used for validation. Exhaustive environmental [SOURCE: Page_17]

355   data were used to characterise the climate, the organisms, the topography, and the other [SOURCE: Page_17]

356   known soil forming factors, according to the Soil-Landscape paradigm (Jenny, 1941; [SOURCE: Page_17]

357   McBratney et al., 2003). The maps’ accuracy and difference were assessed by comparing the [SOURCE: Page_17]

358   ST predictions to observed data (RMQS and IGCS datasets) and to an available soil map [SOURCE: Page_17]

359   (1:1M). As expected, the three DSM approaches predicted the ST trend mainly from the [SOURCE: Page_17]

360   covariates derived from the digital elevation model. The three predictive maps exhibited [SOURCE: Page_17]

361   similar accuracy and were consistent with the 1:1M map. The four maps exhibited similar [SOURCE: Page_17]

362   spatial patterns at the country scale. The three maps had poor performance for estimating [SOURCE: Page_17]

363   the highest and lowest ST values. The mean soil depth for France was approximately 1 m. [SOURCE: Page_17]

364   Chen et al. (2019b) produced maps showing the probability of exceeding the thickness of [SOURCE: Page_17]

365   each GlobalSoilMap standard depth: 5, 15, 30, 60, 100, and 200 cm. Additionnally, a [SOURCE: Page_17]

366   bootstrapping approach was used to assess the 90% confidence intervals. The results [SOURCE: Page_17]

367   indicated that the RSF method was able to correct for right-censored data entries occurring [SOURCE: Page_17]

368   within a given dataset. The RSF was more reliable for thin (0.3 m) and thick soils (1–2 m), as [SOURCE: Page_17]

369   they performed better (overall accuracy ranging from 0.793 to 0.989) than soils with a [SOURCE: Page_17]

16 [SOURCE: Page_17]

370   thickness between 0.3 and 1 m. This study provided a new approach for modelling right- [SOURCE: Page_18]

371   censored soil information. [SOURCE: Page_18]

372   3.6. Available water capacity [SOURCE: Page_18]

373   Román Dobarco et al. (2019a) developed class-PTFs and continuous-PTFs with associated [SOURCE: Page_18]

374   uncertainties and assessed their domain of applicability across metropolitan France. Román [SOURCE: Page_18]

375   Dobarco et al. (2019b) predicted the AWC for mainland France according to the [SOURCE: Page_18]

376   GlobalSoilMap specifications for depth intervals and uncertainty, to quantify the uncertainty [SOURCE: Page_18]

377   of the AWC accounting for the uncertainty of the soil input variables and the PTF [SOURCE: Page_18]

378   coefficients. Spatial predictions for θFC and θPWP overestimated the soil moisture contents for [SOURCE: Page_18]

379   coarse textures and underestimated the soil moisture contents for fine and very fine texture [SOURCE: Page_18]

380   classes. Across the majority of mainland France, the main sources of uncertainty of the [SOURCE: Page_18]

381   elementary AWC were coarse elements and soil texture, but the contribution of the [SOURCE: Page_18]

382   uncertainty of the PTF coefficients increased in areas dominated by very sandy and clayey [SOURCE: Page_18]

383   textures. The modelling framework allows the continuous improvement of the AWC [SOURCE: Page_18]

384   estimates through the replacement of each component of the AWC calculation when more [SOURCE: Page_18]

385   accurate maps (e.g. next versions of GlobalSoilMap for clay, sand, and coarse elements) and [SOURCE: Page_18]

386   new PTFs are developed (see sections 5.1 and 5.3). [SOURCE: Page_18]

387   3.7. Soil microbial density and biodiversity [SOURCE: Page_18]

388   Ranjard et al. (2010, 2013) provided the first extensive maps of the soil microbial biomass and [SOURCE: Page_18]

389   bacterial biodiversity at the scale of mainland France. The maps revealed large [SOURCE: Page_18]

390   biogeographical patterns of microbial density and bacterial biodiversity, indicating an [SOURCE: Page_18]

391   heterogeneous and spatially structured distribution of these microbial parameters (Dequiedt [SOURCE: Page_18]

392   et al., 2011; Terrat et al., 2017). The main factors driving the microbial distribution on a wide [SOURCE: Page_18]

17 [SOURCE: Page_18]

393   scale are the physicochemical properties of soils (texture, pH, and organic C contents) and the [SOURCE: Page_19]

394   land use. Soils from land used for intensive agriculture—particularly cropland and vineyards— [SOURCE: Page_19]

395   exhibited the smallest biomass pools but the highest bacterial diversity. This observation [SOURCE: Page_19]

396   supported a positive relationship between bacterial diversity and soil disturbance due to [SOURCE: Page_19]

397   cropping intensity, which decreased in the following order: Vineyards-Orchards > Crops > [SOURCE: Page_19]

398   Grasslands > Forests. Using the bacterial diversity data, Karimi et al. (2018) characterised the [SOURCE: Page_19]

399   biogeography of each major and minor bacterial taxonomic group and deduced their [SOURCE: Page_19]

400   ecological attributes as well as their distribution patterns. [SOURCE: Page_19]

401   They also investigated the bacterial interactions (co-occurrence) network of the bacterial [SOURCE: Page_19]

402   community according to land use. In contrast to the bacterial richness, the network complexity [SOURCE: Page_19]

403   was found to decrease from vineyards to forests, with a loss of >80% of the interactions [SOURCE: Page_19]

404   (Karimi et al., 2019). Overall, they concluded that soil perturbation due to intensive cropping [SOURCE: Page_19]

405   significantly reduces the complexity of the bacterial network, although the richness is [SOURCE: Page_19]

406   increased, suggesting a potential negative impact on the soil bacterial functioning of the crop [SOURCE: Page_19]

407   system. In this book, the soil microbial habitat was designed on a wide scale, as for macro- [SOURCE: Page_19]

408   organisms. Sixteen distinct terrestrial microbial habitats were defined, emphasising the [SOURCE: Page_19]

409   importance of upgrading policies for biodiversity conservation by integrating microbial [SOURCE: Page_19]

410   habitats. [SOURCE: Page_19]
