Abstract: [SOURCE: Page_3]

Membrane diafiltration process was developed in order to fractionate the water soluble proteins [SOURCE: Page_3]

of the supernatant obtained after Spirulina bead milling disruption and centrifugation. The [SOURCE: Page_3]

obtained crude extract was filtered with 50 kDa, 150 kDa, 300 kDa or 0.2 µm membranes. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Phycocyanin purified extracts were produced after filtration on the 300 kDa or 0.2 µm [SOURCE: Page_3]

membranes. The filtration on 0.2 µm membrane allowed high recovery (more than 89 %) of [SOURCE: Page_3]

(depletion of C-PC into the retentate relatively to the A-PC; enrichment into the permeate). [SOURCE: Page_3]

fouling occurred with the 300 kDa membrane leading to low permeate fluxes (65 L.h-1.m-2). A [SOURCE: Page_3]

fractionation of proteins by the size was possible for the 50, 150 and 300 kDa diafiltrations. [SOURCE: Page_3]

### 1. Introduction

Microalgae are one of the most promising renewable feedstocks of healthy food ingredients and [SOURCE: Page_4]

functional food products. Due to their richness of highly valuable bioactive compounds, they [SOURCE: Page_4]

can be used to enhance the nutritional value of food and, thus, to favorably affect human health [SOURCE: Page_4]

by improving the well-being and quality of life, but also curtailing disease and illness risks [1]. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Among them, Arthrospira platensis (Spirulina), a blue-green alga, is a photosynthetic [SOURCE: Page_4]

filamentous cyanobacterium often considered for biomass production due to its high cell growth [SOURCE: Page_4]

rate, facility of harvesting and potential market as a human food supplement and animal feed. [SOURCE: Page_4]

The microalga A. platensis possesses high protein content, unique composition of fatty acids, [SOURCE: Page_4]

pigments (phycocyanin and β-carotene), and high carbohydrate content [2]. Besides, this [SOURCE: Page_4]

microalga contains 46-71% (w/w) (dry weight basis) of protein and has interesting nutritional [SOURCE: Page_4]

properties due to its content in essential amino acids, vitamins (particularly vitamin B12) as [SOURCE: Page_4]

well as minerals [3, 4, 5]. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Given its interesting characteristics, the cultivation of Spirulina could be a solution to improve [SOURCE: Page_4]

human health, especially nutrition in Third World countries and also to develop industrial crops. [SOURCE: Page_4]

This cyanobacterium currently seems one of the best solutions for the simple production of a [SOURCE: Page_4]

dietary supplement of high quality and, as a result, large companies have embarked on the [SOURCE: Page_4]

culture of this organism on an industrial scale. In addition to proven nutritional properties, [SOURCE: Page_4]

Spirulina is now enjoying a revival of interest from the international scientific community [SOURCE: Page_4]

because of its possible use as a source of pharmaceuticals, and nutraceuticals compounds. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Indeed, the potential of this microalga seems to be important and this mainly thanks to its two [SOURCE: Page_4]

phycobiliproteins, C-phycocyanin (C-PC) and allophycocyanin (A-PC), acting as a component [SOURCE: Page_4]

2 [SOURCE: Page_4]

of light-collecting complexes in the cyanobacteria [6, 7, 8]. C-PC is the major photosynthetic [SOURCE: Page_5]

pigment compared to A-PC [9]. C-PC has demonstrated a great interest for both nutraceutical [SOURCE: Page_5]

and health food industries (natural blue colorant), as well as in biomedical research and clinical [SOURCE: Page_5]

diagnostics (fluorescent probes) [10, 11, 12]. Furthermore, it has also been highlighted that this [SOURCE: Page_5]

pigment has positive effects on impaired physiological conditions such as antioxidation, anti- [SOURCE: Page_5]

inflammation, antitumor, and immunity enhancements [13]. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Thus, the whole Spirulina can be used but its refining, that is to say the extraction and [SOURCE: Page_5]

purification of molecules from this bio-resource bring real added value. Currently, microalgal [SOURCE: Page_5]

soluble proteins are recovered using chemical methods such as pH shifting [5, 14], three phases [SOURCE: Page_5]

partitioning [15] or aqueous two-phase extraction [16]. Unfortunately, these environmentally [SOURCE: Page_5]

unfriendly techniques use a large amount of solvents and chemicals and can lead to the [SOURCE: Page_5]

extraction of low-purity proteins with high salt content, denaturation and poor functional [SOURCE: Page_5]

properties, or to be incompatible with industrial-scale applications. In this context, membrane [SOURCE: Page_5]

filtration technologies, especially ultrafiltration, are presented as advantageous methods to [SOURCE: Page_5]

purify and concentrate components of microalgal biomass with mild operating and chemical- [SOURCE: Page_5]

free conditions [17, 18, 19]. The membrane filtration is notably useful for the concentration of [SOURCE: Page_5]

large volumes and can be easily automatized and scaled up to an industrial level [20]. Also, this [SOURCE: Page_5]

reduces the possible denaturation, deactivation and/or degradation of interest biological [SOURCE: Page_5]

molecules. To date, several works have employed this technique on the separation and [SOURCE: Page_5]

concentration of microalgal proteins, notably from Chlorella vulgaris [21], Haematococcus [SOURCE: Page_5]

pluvialis [14], Tetraselmis suecica [22] and Nannochloropsis gaditana [23, 24]. Concerning [SOURCE: Page_5]

Spirulina, a water-soluble protein-pigment complex such as phycocyanin can be recovered by [SOURCE: Page_5]

membrane filtration after cell disruption. Herrera et al. (1989) [25] used ultrafiltration [SOURCE: Page_5]

membrane with a molecular weight cut-off of 50 kDa to concentrate up to a volume reduction [SOURCE: Page_5]

factor of 1.9 a Spirulina maxima‘s extract. This methodology made it possible to obtain food [SOURCE: Page_5]

3 [SOURCE: Page_5]

grade phycocyanin. Jaouen et al. (1999) [26] obtained 96 % of phycocyanin retention during [SOURCE: Page_6]

the filtration on a 100 kDa tubular membrane of an extract resulting from ultrasonic breakage [SOURCE: Page_6]

of Spirulina platensis. More recently, Chaiklahan et al. (2011) [27] obtained 99 % of [SOURCE: Page_6]

phycocyanin retention during the filtration of a Spirulina sp. extract, on a membrane with a [SOURCE: Page_6]

molecular weight cut-off equal to 50 kDa. These works show that membrane filtration can be [SOURCE: Page_6]

used for an almost complete retention of phycocyanin. An original work would be to show that [SOURCE: Page_6]

it is possible to use membranes for fractionation/separation of proteins of Spirulina. In this last [SOURCE: Page_6]

case, a part of proteins should pass through the membranes while the other part should be [SOURCE: Page_6]

retained. The fractionation could in particular make it possible to produce different protein [SOURCE: Page_6]

extracts of different size classes: each class could have different bio-activities. Nevertheless, [SOURCE: Page_6]

severe membrane fouling can induce a non-selective separation and low permeate fluxes. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Proteins would be difficult to recover in case of membrane adsorption, protein-protein or [SOURCE: Page_6]

protein-polysaccharide interactions in the bulk solution and/or membrane [28]. Moreover, [SOURCE: Page_6]

filtration performance will depend on the membrane chosen (material, pore diameters), feed [SOURCE: Page_6]

protein concentration, physico-chemical characteristics of solutions to be filtered (pH, ionic [SOURCE: Page_6]

strength) as well as operating conditions of filtration (pressure, cross-flow velocity, [SOURCE: Page_6]

temperature, etc). Thus, only an experimental study can account for extending of membrane [SOURCE: Page_6]

fouling and the feasibility to fractionate proteins coming from crushed Spirulina. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Thus, the aim of this study was to evaluate the feasibility to use diafiltration for protein [SOURCE: Page_6]

fractionation of Spirulina (A. platensis). Ultrafiltration in diafiltration mode was used to [SOURCE: Page_6]

selectively let pass proteins through the membranes and produce different protein extracts more [SOURCE: Page_6]

or less purified, notably in salt and chlorophyll. Various molecular weight cut-offs of [SOURCE: Page_6]

membranes were used. Membrane fouling extend was evaluated in relationship with the [SOURCE: Page_6]

selectivity of operations as well as hydraulic performances. [SOURCE: Page_6]

### 2.1. Spirulina protein extraction by bead milling

Spirulina suspensions were prepared in Milli-Q® water and used as feed for the disruption [SOURCE: Page_7]

experiment. The suspensions contained 20 grams of dried Spirulina per liter of water. Cell [SOURCE: Page_7]

disruption was conducted in a Dyno®-mill multi-lab from Willy A Bachofen AG (Muttenz, [SOURCE: Page_7]

Switzerland) containing 0.5-0.75 mm glass beads at 80% filling percentage. Experiments were [SOURCE: Page_7]

run in continuous mode in 3 passes. Samples were pumped from an agitated feed tank to a 600 [SOURCE: Page_7]

mL grinding chamber containing the grinding medium at a flow rate of 150 mL/min and a [SOURCE: Page_7]

rotation speed of 10 m.s-1. The system was operated in batch recirculation mode, with a constant [SOURCE: Page_7]

agitation speed of 2986 rpm. At the end of the first passage the suspension was recovered in a [SOURCE: Page_7]

second tank and transferred to the first agitated tank to be pumped again. The temperature of [SOURCE: Page_7]

the suspension was maintained at ∼10 °C with a cold glycerol solution circulating in the cooling [SOURCE: Page_7]

jacket of the grinding chamber. After cell disruption the suspensions were centrifuged using a [SOURCE: Page_7]

Sorvall RC$_6$ Plus centrifuge (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc, Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France) at [SOURCE: Page_7]

12,000 x g for 30 min at 5 °C. The supernatant was stored at -20 °C before thawing at ambient [SOURCE: Page_7]

temperature for at least 12 h, just before the membrane filtration experiments. [SOURCE: Page_7]

### 2.2. Filtration experiments

A diafiltration process was conducted using an experimental set-up previously described by [SOURCE: Page_7]

Guilbaud et al. (2012) [29], equipped with a 0.032 m² tubular ceramic membranes (TAMI [SOURCE: Page_7]

5 [SOURCE: Page_7]

Industries, Inside CeRAM, Cell 60, 7 channels) with different molecular weight cut offs (50 [SOURCE: Page_8]

kDa, 150 kDa, 300 kDa) or pore size diameters (0.2 µm) at a fixed trans-membrane pressure [SOURCE: Page_8]

(TMP) of 4 bar and with a cross-flow velocity of 5 m.s-1. All filtrations were carried out at 20 [SOURCE: Page_8]

°C, in batch mode. The TMP and cross-flow velocity were kept constant during experiments. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The supernatant obtained after cell disruption then centrifugation (2 L) was diafiltrated with [SOURCE: Page_8]

MilliQ water having a pH and a conductivity respectively equal to 6.5 and 0.12 mS.cm-1. A [SOURCE: Page_8]

constant volume of the solution (2 L) was maintained within the feed tank during the [SOURCE: Page_8]

diafiltrations. Five diavolumes were added before stopping filtration (addition of 10 L of [SOURCE: Page_8]

MilliQ water). During this step, retentate and permeate samples were taken for future analysis, [SOURCE: Page_8]

at the beginning and at the end as well as in the course of the filtration. The permeate is weighted [SOURCE: Page_8]

during the filtration using an electronic balance with the accuracy of 0.01 g. Thus, the permeate [SOURCE: Page_8]

flux expressed in L.h−1.m−2 was evaluated from the permeate mass according to equation 1: [SOURCE: Page_8]

(1) J = Mpermeate / (ρ . t . A) [SOURCE: Page_8]

where Mpermeate (kg) is the permeate mass, ρ (kg.L-1) is the volume mass of the permeate, t (h) [SOURCE: Page_8]

is the permeation time and A (m^2) is the membrane area. [SOURCE: Page_8]

After each diafiltration experiment, the membrane was chemically cleaned according to the [SOURCE: Page_8]

manufacturer recommendations. Firstly, the membrane was cleaned with sodium hydroxide [SOURCE: Page_8]

solution (pH = 12 – Sigma-Aldrich S8045) at 60 °C for 30 minutes in closed loop (recirculation [SOURCE: Page_8]

of permeate and retentate into the feed tank). Then, the pilot plant was rinced with MilliQ water [SOURCE: Page_8]

until neutral pH at 20 °C in the open loop (discharge of the permeate). Afterward, the membrane [SOURCE: Page_8]

was cleaned using a nitric acid solution (pH = 1.2 – Sigma-Aldrich 695041) at 50 °C for 15 [SOURCE: Page_8]

minutes in closed loop. Finally, the membrane was rinced by MilliQ water until neutral pH. [SOURCE: Page_8]

This procedure was also used for the membrane deconditioning. More than 90 % of the water [SOURCE: Page_8]

fluxes were recovered after the cleaning. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The mean retention rate (R) of components was calculated as described in equation 2: [SOURCE: Page_8]

6 [SOURCE: Page_8]

R = (CR – CP) / CR x 100                                   (2) [SOURCE: Page_9]

CR and CP refer to the concentration within the retentate and permeate respectively. [SOURCE: Page_9]

The recovery rates within the retentate (YR) and the permeate (YP) were calculated following [SOURCE: Page_9]

equations 3 and 4, respectively. [SOURCE: Page_9]

YR = (CRend . VRend) / (CR$_0$ . VR$_0$) x 100                           (3) [SOURCE: Page_9]

YP = (CPend . VPend) / (CR$_0$ . VR$_0$) x 100                           (4) [SOURCE: Page_9]

CR$_0$ and VR$_0$ refer to the concentration and volume of the crude extract to be filtered [SOURCE: Page_9]

respectively. CRend and CPend refer to the concentrations within the retentate and the whole [SOURCE: Page_9]

permeate obtained at the end of diafiltrations. VRend and VPend refer to all the volumes of [SOURCE: Page_9]

retentate (2 L) and permeate recovered at the end of diafiltrations (10 L). Total recovery rate [SOURCE: Page_9]

(YT) corresponds to the retentate plus the permeate recovery rates. [SOURCE: Page_9]

After each diafiltration, 9.5 L of its permeate were introduced into a tank in order to be [SOURCE: Page_9]

concentrated on a 5 kDa tubular ceramic membrane (TAMI Industries, Inside CeRAM, Cell 60, [SOURCE: Page_9]

7 channels, 0.032 m²). All concentrations were carried out at 20 °C, in batch mode, at a TMP [SOURCE: Page_9]

and cross-flow velocity equal to respectively 6 bar and 5 m.s-1. [SOURCE: Page_9]

Thus, for the rest of the article, the “crude extract” will refer to the supernatant obtained after [SOURCE: Page_9]

the centrifugation of the microalga grinding solution. The crude extracts will be diafiltered. The [SOURCE: Page_9]

“raw permeate of diafiltration” will refer to the permeate of diafiltration without further [SOURCE: Page_9]

treatment. The “mean raw permeate” refer to the whole permeate recovered at the end of a [SOURCE: Page_9]

diafiltration process. The “concentrated permeate of diafiltration” will refer to the permeate [SOURCE: Page_9]

recovered after diafiltration and concentrated on a 5 kDa membrane. It corresponds to the final [SOURCE: Page_9]

retentate of a concentration experiment. [SOURCE: Page_9]
