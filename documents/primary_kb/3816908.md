1    Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

2    Water-level fluctuation (WLF) is a widespread management action in lakes and reservoirs whose [SOURCE: Page_2]

3    impacts on contaminant fate have seldom been investigated. We used near shore hourly measurements [SOURCE: Page_2]

4    (n = 2122) of turbidity (contaminant proxy) and water velocity (sediment resuspension proxy) to track [SOURCE: Page_2]

5    high-frequency contaminant dynamics during a 0.6 m change in water level observed in autumn 2017 [SOURCE: Page_2]

6    in a large French lake. Simultaneously, discrete trace metal measurements highlighted that trapped [SOURCE: Page_2]

7    sediment was more contaminated and finer than surficial sediment supporting that suspended particles [SOURCE: Page_2]

8    (measured by turbidity) were a preferential medium for contaminant mobility. General additive models [SOURCE: Page_2]

9    involving tensor products revealed the enhancement of wind-speed and river discharge effects on [SOURCE: Page_2]

10   turbidity with water draw down. The decrease of the explained deviances by the models over time-lags [SOURCE: Page_2]

11   indicated short time-scale response of turbidity to external forcing. Three of the four major turbid [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   events occurred at the lowest water-level and were concomitant of sediment resuspension as well as [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   precipitation events and / or river flood suggesting a complex interplay among in-lake and watershed [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   processes at controlling sediment mobility during the WLF. These results shed in light that WLF can [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   affect lake littoral hydrodynamic cascading up to the enhancement of contaminant mobility. Sediment [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   resuspension may be an overlooked feature of WLF increasing contamination risk and exposure for [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   littoral organisms with widespread ecological consequences due to the large number of water-level [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   regulated ecosystems. [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   The fate of contaminants in aquatic ecosystems depends on physical transport and mixing as well as [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   geochemical processes both acting at different time scales that remain difficult to capture in the field. [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   Discrete and passive sampling (e.g. Diffusive Gradient in thin film) provide time-average insights on [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   local contamination yet masking real-time contaminant behavior (Lécrivain et al. 2018, Bretier et al. [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   2019). Recent approaches such as electroanalytical methods progressively open the way for real-time [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   monitoring of contaminants while their implementation remain currently limited to short time-scales [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   (i.e. hours to days, Garnier et al. 2006) or necessitate stringent monitoring systems (Superville et al. [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   2015). Contaminant bioavailability modeling accounting for physical and chemical characteristics of [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   sediment, water and contaminant can make valuable predictions for an array of contaminants but [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   necessitate a high number of parameters at work (e.g. partitioning coefficients) and still a consequent [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   environmental monitoring (Di Toro et al. 2005; Paquin et al. 2002, Brix et al. 2017). [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   Alternatively, turbidity, measured as nephelometric turbidity units (NTU), is among the most basic [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   environmental integrative information that can be recorded at high-frequency. It quantifies the [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   cloudiness of water related to the amount of suspended particles that are a preferential medium for [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   adsorbed contaminant (Chiffre et al. 2015; Kalnejais et al. 2007). The ability of particles to adsorb [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   contaminant change with particle grain size due to changes in specific surface area (Martincic et al. [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   1990) (Sutherland 2003) and suspended particles tend to contain much higher contamination that the [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   bulk sediment they originate due to smaller grain size (Kalnejais et al. 2007). Turbidity can also [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   inform on the dynamics of dissolved contaminants as soluble contaminant from soils or sediment [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   generally co-occur with particle resuspension events (Cantwell et al. 2002; Fetters et al. 2016). For [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   instance, Bretier et al. (2019) recently reported a sharp increase of Mn, Ni, Co and As dissolved [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   concentrations concomitantly with high suspended particulate matter (i.e. high turbidity) triggered by [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   sediment release from a dam flushing event in the Upper Rhône River. Consequently, the [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   consideration of the size of suspended particles in complement to turbidity (i.e. the overall particle [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   concentration) could provide valuable indicators for tracking contaminant fate in freshwaters overtime. [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   Rather than estimating the actual contamination that can be obtained by relevant discrete sampling, [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   these indicators could enable the assessment of the relative changes in contaminant mobility and [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   ultimately biota exposure to contaminant overtime (Weltens et al. 2000). [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   We investigated high-frequency (hourly measurements) dynamics of littoral turbidity and water [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   velocity along with external forcing (i.e. precipitation, wind speed, river discharge) during a water- [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   level fluctuation event (WLF, ∆depth = 60 cm) that occurred in autumn 2017 in the largest natural [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   French lake (Lake Bourget). A sediment resuspension model quantifying the occurrence of grain size- [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   specific sediment resuspension events was derived from water velocity (Soulsby and Whitehouse [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   1997, Graham et al. 2016). Complementarily, discrete measurements of trace metals (TM) were [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   performed at three occasions (before-during-after WLF) among two different matrices (surficial and [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   trapped sediments) to assess the actual contamination of the littoral and its possible variation during [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   the WLF. [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   The overarching aim of this study was to evaluate the dynamic of suspended particles (i.e. turbidity) as [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   a proxy for TM dynamic during the WLF tackled through the following working hypotheses: [SOURCE: Page_4]

63       1) The low water-level during the WLF should foster wind-induced sediment resuspension [SOURCE: Page_4]

64           (Cózar et al. 2005; Hofmann et al. 2008), increasing turbidity and metal mobility. [SOURCE: Page_4]

65       2) The effects of external forcing on turbidity should modulate by the WLF and increased for low [SOURCE: Page_4]

66           water-level. [SOURCE: Page_4]

67       3) The occurrence of high turbidity events should be associated to sediment resuspension events [SOURCE: Page_4]

68           indicating that in-lake processes may be responsible for turbidity. Alternatively, watershed [SOURCE: Page_4]

69           processes (i.e. export of suspended particles) should be involved. [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   Overall, these investigations were expected to provide insights regarding the influence of WLF on [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   contaminant dynamics, an often overlooked feature of WLF within lake littorals. [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   2. Materials and methods [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   2.1. Lake Bourget and study sites [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   Lake Bourget is the largest French natural lake (44.5 km², 45.43’58.2”N 5°51’59.1”E) sheltered within [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   the limestone mountains of the Epine and Bauges. The catchment area of the lake is 560 km^2 and [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   covered by forests (45 %), agricultural areas (22 %), grazing fields (12 %), urban areas (13 %) and [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   water bodies (8 %) (CISALB 2009). The littoral sediment contained by a mixture of contaminants [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   exerting low but heterogeneous toxicity. Higher contaminant concentrations were reported in the [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   southeastern region of the lake mostly resulting from local watershed run-offs (Lécrivain et al. 2018; [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   Lécrivain et al. 2019). We selected this southeastern region for the study area. This area is [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   characterized by a ~ 5 % gentle slope close to the outlet of a small arm of the Tillet River, one of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   main tributary of the lake (45°40’00.6”N 5°53’39.0”E) (Appendix 1). Three study sites distant of 20 m [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   to each other were retained along a littoral transect (Appendix 1). The shallowest site (S$_0$.7) was [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   located ~ 10 m away from the shoreline at 0.7 m depth and the two other points (S$_1$.5 and S$_2$.5) at ~ 30 [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   m and ~ 50 m fromthe shoreline at depths of 1.5 m and 2.5 m respectively. The experimental design as [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   well as physico-chemical monitoring among the study sites are presented in Appendix 2. [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   2.2. Trace metal contamination [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   TM discrete measurements were performed at three temporal steps over the WLF. On the 2017/08/31, [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   step 1 provided information before the water-level dropped down. On 2017/10/19, step 2 provided [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   information at the lowest level. Finally, on 2018/04/24, step 3 provided information resulting from the [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   water-level increase (step 3). [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   Triplicates of TM measurements were performed at each site among two different matrices: surficial [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   and trapped sediments. Sediment traps were deployed two weeks prior sampling sessions to ensure [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   sediment accumulation. After sampling, the surficial and trapped sediments were placed in pre-washed [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   polyethylene bottles, transported to the laboratory in coolers, sieved at 2 mm to remove macro-debris [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   and stored at 4°C during a maximum of 24 hours. [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   Prior to the measurements, bulk sediments were mineralized in a microwave oven (CEM/MARS [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   Xpress, 2 mL of ultra-pure HNO$_3$ + 6 mL of ultra-pure HCl, 180 °C for 30 min, filtration on a 0.45- [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   µm Whatman™ filter and dilution of the residue in 25 mL of ultra-pure water). The sediment grain [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   size spectra were quantified using a Malvern Mastersizer 2000G. TM concentrations were then [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   estimated by atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS). The total sediment contents of Cd, Cr, Cu, Ni [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   and Pb were measured in a graphite furnace at 1500 °C, and the total sediment content of Zn was [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   measured with an air-acetylene flame (Perkin Elmer, model PinAAcle 900T). The detection limits [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   were as follows: Cd = 2.5 µg kg-1 DM, Cr = 10 µg kg-1 DM, Cu = 3.5 µg kg-1 DM, Ni = 17.5 µg kg-1 [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   DM, Pb = 12.5 µg kg-1 DM, Zn = 375 µg kg-1 DM. Certified reference material (River sediment LGC$_6$ [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   187) was used for standardization, and triplicate measurements were done to ensure that analytical [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   errors were under 2%. [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   2.3. Turbidity and external forcings [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   Water turbidity was measured at hourly frequency using an Aqua Troll 600 multiparameter probe [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   located at site S$_1$.5 to quantify the amount of particles present in the open water. We performed 2-point [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   calibration at 10 NTU and 100 NTU for turbidity measurements using In Situ’s turbidity standards and [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   a new calibration was performed at the beginning of each new step. Hourly measurements for wind [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   speed and precipitation were collected from the meteorological station of Météo-France Chambéry- [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   Aix-73329001 (Appendix 3). Hourly discharges of the Leysse River were extracted from the national [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   station of La Motte-Servolex-V1315020 (http://hydro.eaufrance.fr/, Appendix 3). The flood plume of [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   the Leysse River typically flows North-East toward the studied area (Vinçon-Leite et al. 1998) and the [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   discharges of both the Leysse River and the Tillet River (nearby the study area) exhibit similar [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   hydrological regimes and only information from the Leysse River were considered here. [SOURCE: Page_6]

149   2.5. Statistical analyses [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   Mann-Whitney tests were used to estimate the significance of the difference in TM concentrations [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   among surficial and trapped sediment. Generalized additive models (GAM, Hastie and Tibshirani [SOURCE: Page_8]

152   1990, Wood 2006) were selected to analyze the relationships between turbidity and the external [SOURCE: Page_8]

153   forcing. GAMs are flexible non-parametric models that accommodate for nonlinear relationships [SOURCE: Page_8]

154   between variables and can support different distributions of the response variable. The extent of [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   nonlinear relationship among variables is quantified through the estimated degree of freedom (edf) [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   where edf = 1 indicate linear relationship and edf higher than 1 indicate progressively higher nonlinear [SOURCE: Page_8]

157   relationship. A first GAM was computed considering water pressure as single variable to identify the [SOURCE: Page_8]

158   general influence of WLF on turbidity. A second GAM that considered the three external forcing was [SOURCE: Page_8]

159   fitted to assess their respective influence on turbidity. A last GAM involving tensor product smooths [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   (Wood 2006) was finally fitted to address the possible modulation of WLF on the relationships [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   between turbidity and the external forcing. The tensor product smooths allow the smooth relationship [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   between turbidity and the external forcing to change nonlinearly with WLF. The tensor product [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   interaction terms in the GAM reflected how the relationship between turbidity and the external forcing [SOURCE: Page_8]

164   can be modulated by WLF. Tensor product interaction terms are suitable to model responses to [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   interactions of variables with different units, which is the case in this study, and have been shown to [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   provide fruitful insight among complex ecological interactions (e.g. Bacheler et al. 2009, Kvile et al. [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   2016). The possible time-lag response of turbidity to the external forcing was investigated by fitting [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   the last GAM with delayed external forcing from 0 to 48 hours (i.e. responses variables were modeled [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   against external forcing previously recorded from 0 hour (instantaneous) to 48 hours). We considered [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   four knots and cubic regression splines to construct the smoothing splines and fitting the GAM and [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   their performances were assessed by the proportion of deviance explained. Turbidity was log^10- [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   transformed prior the analyses and a Gamma distribution was considered for log^10-transformed [SOURCE: Page_8]

173   turbidity as it was a continuous variable bounded down 0 and clearly diverging from a normal [SOURCE: Page_8]

174   distribution due to its right-skewness. [SOURCE: Page_8]

175   All statistical analyses as well as graphical displays were performed using R 3.6.3 (R Development [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   Core Team 2020) and the packages mgcv (Wood and Augustin 2002, Wood 2006), ggplot^2 (Wickham [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   2009). [SOURCE: Page_8]

233   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_12]

234   4.1. Turbidity as a contaminant proxy [SOURCE: Page_12]

235   Turbidity was especially variable over the study period and three of the four major turbid events [SOURCE: Page_12]

236   recorded occurred during the lowest water-level period while the overall positive effect of water [SOURCE: Page_12]

237   drawdown on turbidity could be more precisely quantified using a GAM. These results enabled to [SOURCE: Page_12]

238   validate the hypothesis of an increasing turbidity as long as the water-level was lowered during the [SOURCE: Page_12]

239   WLF. Further, the higher TM concentrations measured in trapped sediment compared to those in [SOURCE: Page_12]

240   surficial sediment, likely related to their finer grain-size spectra (Martincic et al. 1990; Zhang et al. [SOURCE: Page_12]

241   2002, Sutherland 2003), supported the hypothesis that suspended particles were a preferential medium [SOURCE: Page_12]

242   for TM mobility. Turbidity could hence be a relevant proxy to inform of the amount of TM within the [SOURCE: Page_12]

243   open-water of lake littoral (Yao et al. 2016, Nasrabadi et al. 2016). As a consequence, WLF may foster [SOURCE: Page_12]

244   TM exposure of the littoral biota as along with particle-adsorbed TM, dissolved TM have been shown [SOURCE: Page_12]

245   to increase with turbidity (Brétier et al. 2019). The contamination risk of TM for the littoral biota [SOURCE: Page_12]

246   would be enhanced during the lowest water-levels of WLF representing an adverse effect on littoral [SOURCE: Page_12]

247   organisms of this management action. Complementary investigations such as TM sequential extraction [SOURCE: Page_12]

248   (Lécrivain et al. 2019) and / or quantifying TM dissolved concentrations may complement our [SOURCE: Page_12]

249   approach to better decipher TM exposure and their bioavailability for the littoral biota (Eggleton and [SOURCE: Page_12]

250   Thomas 2004, Atkinson et al. 2007; Fetters et al. 2016, Nasrabadi et al. 2018). [SOURCE: Page_12]

251   4.2. Modulation of turbidity by water-level fluctuation [SOURCE: Page_12]

252   Among the external forcings considered, wind speed and river discharge had significant positive [SOURCE: Page_12]

253   effects on turbidity yet their explanatory power (i.e. deviance explained) remained limited when [SOURCE: Page_12]

254   considered additively. Their positive influence on turbidity was expected as both wind speed and [SOURCE: Page_12]

255   discharge can either trigger sediment resuspension (Zheng et al. 2013) or support the input of [SOURCE: Page_12]

256   sediments from the watershed (Hofmann et al. 2008) and two main reasons may explain their limited [SOURCE: Page_12]

257   explanatory power. First, these measurements were performed at the closest long term monitoring [SOURCE: Page_12]

258   stations from the studied sites (i.e. a distance of few km) possibly limiting the accuracy of these [SOURCE: Page_12]

11 [SOURCE: Page_12]

259   variables actually acting at the study area. Second, other factors that were not accounted for such as [SOURCE: Page_13]

260   upwelling may also have been involved in littoral variation of turbidity. Accounting for the [SOURCE: Page_13]

261   interactions of wind speed and river discharge with WLF through the use of the tensor products in [SOURCE: Page_13]

262   GAM to model their effects on turbidity drastically increased the explanatory power of the GAM. This [SOURCE: Page_13]

263   result indicated that WLF would effectively modulate the effects of external forcings on turbidity. [SOURCE: Page_13]

264   More precisely the water drawdown increased the effects of the external forcings on turbidity which [SOURCE: Page_13]

265   amplifies the amount of suspended particles highlighting direct influence on WLF on contaminant [SOURCE: Page_13]

266   mobility. [SOURCE: Page_13]

267   The decrease of deviance explained by the GAM over successive time lags suggested short time-scale [SOURCE: Page_13]

268   (i.e. hours) response of turbidity to the external forcings. This result supports the relevance for [SOURCE: Page_13]

269   considering high-frequency measurements to address contaminant mobility in lake littoral. [SOURCE: Page_13]

270   Complementarily, the maintaining of rather high deviance explained at larger time-lags indicated that [SOURCE: Page_13]

271   the external forcings may still influence turbidity at longer time-scale (i.e. several days). This result is [SOURCE: Page_13]

272   in accordance with the sinking speed of sediment modal grain size that may be of several days [SOURCE: Page_13]

273   considering turbulent regime in the lake littoral (Appendix 5). In other words, the external forcings [SOURCE: Page_13]

274   would almost instantaneously affect particle and contaminant mobility that would maintain (i.e. [SOURCE: Page_13]

275   delayed effect) for several days which constitutes suitable conditions for biota exposure to [SOURCE: Page_13]

276   contaminant. [SOURCE: Page_13]

277   4.3. Linking turbidity to in-lake processes [SOURCE: Page_13]

278   The lake littoral supported high hydrodynamic constraints as shown by the bottom water velocity that [SOURCE: Page_13]

279   regularly exceeded 10 cm s-1 corresponding to shear stress higher than 10 N m² with maximal values [SOURCE: Page_13]

280   of ~ 20 N m² (Appendix 6). Such hydrodynamic constraint was of similar magnitude than those [SOURCE: Page_13]

281   reported by Kalnejais et al. (2007) in the Boston Harbor (USA) for which resuspension events due to [SOURCE: Page_13]

282   shear stress contributed up to 60% of the flux of resuspended metal in an average year. The [SOURCE: Page_13]

283   resuspension events increased exponentially as long a sediment grain size decreased with a major [SOURCE: Page_13]

284   inflexion at ~ 50 µm grain size in diameter. This result may be linked to the noisy variations of low [SOURCE: Page_13]

285   turbidity outside of the major turbid events and explain the grain size spectra of the littoral sediment [SOURCE: Page_13]

12 [SOURCE: Page_13]

286   (Appendix 4) whose fraction smaller than 100 µm in diameter drastically decreased. The lake littoral [SOURCE: Page_14]

287   hence primarily acts as a transport area for fine sediment to storage compartments in deeper zones [SOURCE: Page_14]

288   (Morales-Marin et al. 2018). This assertion is supported by the finer particle size of deep sediment [SOURCE: Page_14]

289   compared to littoral ones often associated to higher contaminant concentrations. [SOURCE: Page_14]

290   The major turbid events were mostly associated to sediment resuspension events when considering [SOURCE: Page_14]

291   mode values of trapped and surficial sediment indicating that in-lake processes involving bottom shear [SOURCE: Page_14]

292   stress may explain their onsets. However, most turbid events were also concomitant of high river [SOURCE: Page_14]

293   discharge or precipitation suggesting that watershed processes (i.e. particle export from the watershed) [SOURCE: Page_14]

294   would also be involved in the turbidity variations. For instance, the longest turbid event of several [SOURCE: Page_14]

295   weeks that started by mid-October was associated to a unique resuspension event for the mode of [SOURCE: Page_14]

296   trapped sediment and its duration may then not been only attributable only to in-lake process [SOURCE: Page_14]

297   supporting lake sediment remobilization. The complex interplay between in-lake and watershed [SOURCE: Page_14]

298   processes likely control the onset and duration of turbid events whose relative implication likely varied [SOURCE: Page_14]

299   among turbid events ultimately constraining contaminant mobility. This complexity was tentatively [SOURCE: Page_14]

300   addressed using the GAM considering wind speed (i.e. in-lake process) and river discharge (i.e. [SOURCE: Page_14]

301   watershed process) that may provide useful forecast for turbidity dynamic within the littoral of this [SOURCE: Page_14]

302   large natural French lake. [SOURCE: Page_14]

303   Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_14]

304   We showed that WLF may significantly modulate the influence of the external forcing that drove the [SOURCE: Page_14]

305   littoral turbidity. The enhancement of turbidity for the lowest water level associated to higher TM [SOURCE: Page_14]

306   contamination of resuspended sediment provided evidence of the influence of WLF on TM mobility. [SOURCE: Page_14]

307   Biota exposure may hence be amplified as ingestion of suspended contaminated particles is a common [SOURCE: Page_14]

308   contamination pathway for both benthic and pelagic consumers (Weltens et al. 2000, Li et al. 2017) [SOURCE: Page_14]

309   along with passive contamination via dissolved contaminant assimilation. Overall, turbidity may be a [SOURCE: Page_14]

310   suitable proxy for high-frequency monitoring in complement to discrete contaminant measurements in [SOURCE: Page_14]

311   highly dynamic aquatic ecosystems such as lake littoral. Due to the large number of water-level [SOURCE: Page_14]

312   regulated ecosystems affected by WLF and in the perspective of their increasing establishment [SOURCE: Page_14]

13 [SOURCE: Page_14]

313   (Farinotti et al. 2019), the effects of WLF on contaminant fate may have underestimated indirect and [SOURCE: Page_15]

314   delayed impacts on littoral biodiversity compared to direct and more commonly reported alterations [SOURCE: Page_15]

315   (Carmignani and Roy 2017; Evtimova and Donohue 2014). [SOURCE: Page_15]
