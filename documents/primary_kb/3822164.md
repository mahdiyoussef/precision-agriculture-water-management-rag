57   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   In Europe, the production and use of persistent organic pollutants (POPs) are [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   prohibited or severely restricted since 2004 through the Stockholm Convention [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   (http://www.pops.int/),          but     POPs   still    cause   environmental   concerns. [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and organochlorine pesticides (OCPs) remain in [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   the environment for decades because of their slow biodegradation and improper [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   handling of contaminated wastes.           Levels of POPs in animals increase with age [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   (Vives et al., 2005; Binnington & Wania, 2014) and trophic level (Goutte et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   Exposure to POPs causes a wide range of adverse effects, including neurotoxicity, [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   endocrine     disruption,     immune      dysfunction,    reproductive   impairment,   and [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   developmental abnormalities, which may ultimately compromise survival and [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   reproductive output and lead to population declines of wild vertebrates (Goutte et [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   al., 2014, 2015; Salice et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_5]

70          Pollution, and especially industrial and agricultural discharge, is one of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   major threats to freshwater ecosystems (Holt, 2000; Dudgeon et al., 2006). Wetlands [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   support an extremely rich biodiversity, but are among the most transformed and [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   threatened ecosystems of the world (Revenga et al., 2005), experiencing rates of [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   population declines and species extinctions far higher than those in forests, [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   grasslands, and coastal ecosystems (Dudgeon et al., 2006). Deltas are often exposed to [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   high water pollution from intensive agriculture (Kuenzer & Renaud, 2012) and from [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   discharge of contaminants in upstream waters. The Camargue, in the Rhône River [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   delta, is the largest wetland in France and is of international importance under the [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   Ramsar Convention (https://www.ramsar.org/), but it is heavily impacted by [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   human activities (Cheiron et al., 2018). The contamination of water bodies by [SOURCE: Page_6]

81   agricultural, industrial, and urban discharges has been well studied over the past [SOURCE: Page_6]

82   decades (Comoretto et al., 2007). In particular, levels of PCBs in sediments of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

83   Rhône River increase from upstream to downstream, reaching 417 μg/kg dw for the [SOURCE: Page_6]

84   sum of 7 PCB congeners (28, 52, 101, 118, 138, 153, and 180, Mourier et al. 2014). [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   Mean flux over the 2011–2016 period averaged 14 kg/year for PCB 180 in suspended [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   particulate matter at the outlet of the Rhône River basin          (Poulier et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   Previous studies conducted in the Camargue confirmed that birds (Berny et al., 2002) [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   and fish (Roche et al., 2002, 2003, 2009a) are exposed to OCPs and PCBs. [SOURCE: Page_6]

89          Freshwater turtles remain poorly studied in ecotoxicology although they can [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   provide useful information on local contamination because of their longevity [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   (Campbell & Campbell, 2002 ; EL Hassani et al., 2019 ; Gaus et al., 2019), high trophic- [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   level, and low dispersal capacity (Ming-ch’eng Adams et al., 2016). Moreover, as [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   ectothermic vertebrates, they have a lower ability to metabolize pollutants than [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   endothermic mammals and birds (de Solla, 2015). The European pond turtle, Emys [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   orbicularis, a long-lived (> 40-80 years) opportunistic predator (fish, amphibian, [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   crayfish) and scavenger (Ottonello et al. 2005; Ficetola & De Bernardi, 2006), is facing [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   significant population declines due to multiple environmental alterations, including [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   water pollution (Cheylan 1998). This species is listed as “near threatened” (NT) on [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   the UICN Red List of threatened species in France (REF). Pollution by trace metal [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   elements has been studied in E. orbicularis (Namroodi et al., 2017 ; Guillot et al., 2018 ; [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   Beau et al., 2019), but data are lacking on burdens of POPs. [SOURCE: Page_6]

102          We studied contamination levels in two populations of E. orbicularis at the [SOURCE: Page_7]

103   Natural Reserve of the Tour du Valat, in the Camargue, France. Several individuals [SOURCE: Page_7]

104   were of known-age due to       capture-mark-recapture (CMR) program, starting more [SOURCE: Page_7]

105   than 20 years ago (Olivier et al., 2010 ; Ficheux et al., 2014 ; Arsovski et al., 2018). The [SOURCE: Page_7]

106   hydraulic system combines irrigation canals originating from the Rhône river and [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   drainage canals, which are exposed to various environmental contaminants from the [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   Rhône river and agricultural plots, especially rice fields (Chauvelon, 1996). In the [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   present study, we assessed recent (2018) levels of OCPs and PCBs in plasma of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   European pond turtle (n = 174). We tested the effects of individual traits (mass, sex, [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   and age) on POP burdens. We expected that older and larger individuals should [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   locations in the agricultural hydraulic system (irrigation vs drainage). We expected [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   high levels of OCPs in turtles from the drainage site, as a possible consequence of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   remobilization of pesticides from agricultural soils, and high levels of PCBs in turtles [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   2. Material and methods [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   2.1. Sampling sites and capture [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   We conducted the study in the Natural Reserve of the Tour du Valat (43°30’ N, 4°40’ [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   E, Fig. 1) in France. We captured European pond turtles (n = 174) in canals and [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   marshes from 24 April to 26 July 2018 by hand or with funnel traps (Olivier et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   2010; Ficheux et al., 2014). We captured turtles at two sites: (1) irrigation canals and [SOURCE: Page_8]

126   their associated marshland (site of Esquineau, n = 126) and (2) drainage canals of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

127   Fumemorte basin and marshes filled with water by these canals (site of Faïsses, n = [SOURCE: Page_8]

128   48). The sex ratio was the same between the two sites (58.3% females at Faïsses and [SOURCE: Page_8]

129   60.3% females at Esquineau, Pearson's Chi-squared test, p = 0.95). [SOURCE: Page_8]

130         Individual turtles were identified as part of a long-term CMR program with [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   shallow notches on the marginal and nuchal scales in unique combinations (Olivier, [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   2002 ; Olivier et al., 2010). Individuals were weighed and sexed by visual observation [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   of the secondary sexual characters. The year of birth can be determined if the first [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   capture occurred within the first 5 years of life by counting the number of growth [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   streaks (Castanet, 1988). This long-term survey starts in 1997, although a few tens of [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   individuals were also marked as adults from 1976. In our data set, 66 of the 174 [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   individuals were of known-aged (5 to 26 year-old, corresponding to a first capture [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   between 1997 and 2018) and some were first captured as adults in 1976, thus being [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   considered as more than 44 year-old. When turtles were captured after their first 5 [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   years of life (N = 108 in the present study), we determined a stage of growth [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   according to growth streaks located on the plastron (Olivier, 2002). By combining [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   estimated age and stage of growth, we then attributed an age class to each turtle: 4 to [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   8 year-old (N = 20), 9 to 13 year-old (N = 46), 14 to 22 year-old (N = 54), and 23 to 44+ [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   year-old (N = 54). Turtles were released on the same day at their capture site. [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   2.2. Blood sampling [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   Plasma is a good matrix to determine individual burdens of POPs because blood [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   sampling is minimally invasive and because circulating levels of POPs in blood are 7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   significantly correlated to concentrations of POPs stored in fat (Keller et al., 2004 ; [SOURCE: Page_9]

153   Company® syringe (Somerset, USA) pre-impregnated with heparin to prevent blood [SOURCE: Page_9]

154   clotting during collection and equipped with a 25G needle (Franklin Lakes, USA). [SOURCE: Page_9]

155   We then centrifuged the samples to separate the plasma from the red blood cells. We [SOURCE: Page_9]

156   collected blood samples from 174 individuals (126 from Esquineau and 48 from [SOURCE: Page_9]

157   Faïsses), 66 individuals were of known age ranging from 5 to 31 years old (32 females [SOURCE: Page_9]

158   and 19 males from Esquineau; 9 females and 6 males from Faïsses). Samples were [SOURCE: Page_9]

159   stored at −18 °C until analysis in the UMR 7619 METIS of Sorbonne Université, [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   France. [SOURCE: Page_9]

228   4. Discussion 229 [SOURCE: Page_12]

230   The aim of this study was to characterize legacy levels of POPs (OCPs and PCBs) in [SOURCE: Page_12]

231   the plasma of European pond turtles in the Camargue, France, and to determine [SOURCE: Page_12]

232   whether these levels were a function of individual traits and habitat types. Plasma [SOURCE: Page_12]

233   levels of POPs were low and often below limits of quantification (from 0.2 to 3.1 [SOURCE: Page_12]

234   ng/mL). We found significant differences in burdens of POPs between turtles from [SOURCE: Page_12]

235   the two sampling sites and these differences could be attributed to the hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_12]

236   system (drainage/irrigation). The occurrence and levels of PCB-153 were higher in [SOURCE: Page_12]

237   older turtles. [SOURCE: Page_12]

238          The Rhône River has been historically contaminated, leading to an [SOURCE: Page_12]

239   accumulation of POPs in sediments downstream (Mourier et al. 2014). Previous [SOURCE: Page_12]

240   studies conducted on several animal taxa in the Camargue confirmed high exposure [SOURCE: Page_12]

241   during the last decades, with high concentrations of PCBs in muscles of eels (Anguilla [SOURCE: Page_12]

242   anguilla) fished between 1997 and 2000 (Roche et al., 2004), but also in eggs of little [SOURCE: Page_12]

11 [SOURCE: Page_12]

243   egrets (Egretta garzetta) collected in 1996 (Berny et al., 2002). We detected low levels of [SOURCE: Page_13]

244   POPs in the pond turtle in Camargue, which could indicate a decline in legacy POP [SOURCE: Page_13]

245   exposure for wild species in the Camargue. Although a longitudinal study of the [SOURCE: Page_13]

246   same species at the same locations would be required to test this hypothesis, [SOURCE: Page_13]

247   decreasing PCB concentrations have been observed in the Rhône River sediments [SOURCE: Page_13]

248   over the last decades (Liber et al., 2019) and water analyses conducted by the Nature [SOURCE: Page_13]

249   Protection National Society (SNPN) have not detected PCBs and OCPs in the [SOURCE: Page_13]

250   Fumemorte      canal   since   2011    (Cheiron,   2019).   Moreover,     organochlorine [SOURCE: Page_13]

251   contamination across food webs tended to diminish in the Vaccarès lagoon (Roche et [SOURCE: Page_13]

252   al., 2009a), the largest water body of the Camargue Biosphere Reserve. Exotic red [SOURCE: Page_13]

253   swamp crayfish (Procambarus clarkii), the main prey of European pond turtles in the [SOURCE: Page_13]

254   Camargue (Ottonello et al., 2005), were not contaminated by POPs in 2019 (i.e. < [SOURCE: Page_13]

255   LOQ, with LOQ ranging from 0.9. to 1.98 ng/g dw, unpublished data). [SOURCE: Page_13]

256          One of the first studies looking at the plasma concentration of POPs in turtles [SOURCE: Page_13]

257   documented high PCBs and OCPs levels in snapping turtles (Chelydra serpentina), in [SOURCE: Page_13]

258   Ontario, Canada and sampled in 2001–2004 (Letcher et al., 2015): plasma [SOURCE: Page_13]

259   concentrations of OCPs (sum of 17 contaminants) ranged from 0.2 to 236 ng/g w.w. [SOURCE: Page_13]

260   and the most abundant pesticide was p,p’-DDE (mean ± SE: 27 ± 6 ng/g w.w.). In our [SOURCE: Page_13]

261   study, plasma concentrations of POPs (sum of 16 contaminants) were much lower, [SOURCE: Page_13]

262   ranging from 0 to 2 ng/g w.w and p,p’-DDE levels didn’t exceed 2.3 ng/g ww. [SOURCE: Page_13]

263   Snapping turtles are freshwater turtles with similar feeding habits than European [SOURCE: Page_13]

264   pond turtle: they also consume plant and animal matter, including aquatic [SOURCE: Page_13]

265   invertebrates, fish, frogs, reptiles (Ernst et al. 1994).Other studies of turtles [SOURCE: Page_13]

12 [SOURCE: Page_13]

266   documented plasma concentrations of POPs comparable to those in our study; for [SOURCE: Page_14]

267   instance, for PCB 153 and HCB in another freshwater species, the Western pond [SOURCE: Page_14]

268   turtle (Actinemys marmorata) in Sequoia National Park, USA, in 2012 (Meyer et al., [SOURCE: Page_14]

269   2016), as well as in marine turtle, such as Loggerhead sea turtles (Caretta caretta) in [SOURCE: Page_14]

270   the Eastern Atlantic Ocean in 2011-2012 (Bucchia et al., 2015), and in Green turtles [SOURCE: Page_14]

271   (Chelonia mydas) and Hawksbill turtles (Eretmochelys imbricata) in Cape Verde [SOURCE: Page_14]

272   between 2009 and 2011 (Camacho et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_14]

273         In terms of occurrence of POPs, our results in European pond turtles were low [SOURCE: Page_14]

274   compared to other studies (Bucchia et al., 2015 ; Meyer et al., 2016). The occurrences [SOURCE: Page_14]

275   of dioxin-like PCBs in 8% of European pond turtles, as well as p,p’-DDE in 2% of [SOURCE: Page_14]

276   individuals were much lower than those found in plasma of Loggerhead sea turtles [SOURCE: Page_14]

277   that ranged from 63% in the Atlantic Ocean to 100% in the Adriatic Sea for dioxin- [SOURCE: Page_14]

278   like PCBs and 100 % for p,p’-DDE (Bucchia et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_14]

279         Despite low detection frequencies and levels, we found differences in POP [SOURCE: Page_14]

280   burdens between turtles from the two sampling sites. Turtles in Faïsses, a site [SOURCE: Page_14]

281   receiving water from drainage canals, exhibited significantly higher occurrence and [SOURCE: Page_14]

282   levels of HCB compared to turtles from Esquineau, likely because of the [SOURCE: Page_14]

283   remobilisation of trapped OCPs in soils. On the other hand, the occurrence and levels [SOURCE: Page_14]

284   of PCB 153 were higher in turtles from Esquineau, a site with irrigation channels. A [SOURCE: Page_14]

285   previous study has documented higher concentrations of PCBs in the aquatic fauna [SOURCE: Page_14]

286   in irrigation canals compared to the drainage canals downstream of rice fields (Roche [SOURCE: Page_14]

287   et al., 2009b). We also found that concentrations of PCB-52 were higher in turtles at [SOURCE: Page_14]

288   Faïsses, however, which may be due to a lower sedimentation rate of PCB-52 [SOURCE: Page_14]

13 [SOURCE: Page_14]

289   associated with its lower molecular weight and a greater solubility due to a lower [SOURCE: Page_15]

290   level of chlorination compared to PCB-153 (Gong et al., 1998 ; Alkhatib & Weigand, [SOURCE: Page_15]

291   2002). The processes of deposition and release of PCBs from the irrigation part to the [SOURCE: Page_15]

292   drainage part of the canals need to be further studied. In addition, other populations [SOURCE: Page_15]

293   of pond turtles in the two types of hydraulic systems should be studied to confirm [SOURCE: Page_15]

294   this pattern. [SOURCE: Page_15]

295             The occurrence and levels of PCB-153 were lower in the youngest individuals [SOURCE: Page_15]

296   (4 to 8 year old), which could be attributed to a shorter exposure period. PCB-153 is a [SOURCE: Page_15]

297   recalcitrant chemical with a high hydrophobicity (high octanol-water partition [SOURCE: Page_15]

298   coefficient), high resistance to metabolic transformation, and slow respiratory [SOURCE: Page_15]

299   elimination through air-exhalation (high octanol-air partition coefficient). PCB-153 is [SOURCE: Page_15]

300   also more prone to bioaccumulation than many other PCB congeners (Kelly et al. [SOURCE: Page_15]

301   2007), especially in long-lived air-breathing predatory species (Rowe, 2008). It is also [SOURCE: Page_15]

302   possible that there is a dietary shift during growth in pond turtles leading to the [SOURCE: Page_15]

303   consumption of higher trophic-level prey with age, and these higher trophic level [SOURCE: Page_15]

304   prey would be more PCB contaminated due to biomagnification. This hypothesis, [SOURCE: Page_15]

305   however, is not supported by dietary studies of E. orbicularis in Camargue), showing [SOURCE: Page_15]

306   a shift to a more herbivorous diet with age, using prey items identification in faecal [SOURCE: Page_15]

307   samples (Ottonello et al., 2005) or no difference in the proportion of plants, [SOURCE: Page_15]

308   invertebrates and vertebrates, using metabarcoding analyses (Duccotterd et al. [SOURCE: Page_15]

309   2020).) [SOURCE: Page_15]

310             We did not detect differences in POP loads between male and female pond [SOURCE: Page_15]

311   turtles, despite males often being more contaminated than females in other turtle [SOURCE: Page_15]

14 [SOURCE: Page_15]

312   species (Guirlet et al., 2010 ; Bangma et al., 2019; Lambiase et al., 2021) due to a [SOURCE: Page_16]

313   transfer of pollutants from the mother to the eggs through vitellogenesis (Moss et al., [SOURCE: Page_16]

314   2009 ; de Solla, 2010). [SOURCE: Page_16]
