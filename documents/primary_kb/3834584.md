11   Abstract: The SMOS (Soil Moisture and Ocean Salinity) mission provides surface soil moisture (SM) [SOURCE: Page_1]

12   maps at a mean resolution of ~50 km. However, agricultural applications (irrigation, crop monitoring) and [SOURCE: Page_1]

13   some hydrological applications (floods and modeling of small basins) require higher resolution SM [SOURCE: Page_1]

14   information. In order to overcome this spatial mismatch, a disaggregation algorithm called Disaggregation [SOURCE: Page_1]

15   based on Physical And Theoretical scale Change (DISPATCH) combines higher-resolution data from [SOURCE: Page_1]

16   optical/thermal sensors with the SM retrieved from microwave sensors like SMOS, producing higher- [SOURCE: Page_1]

17   resolution SM as the output. A DISPATCH-based processor has been implemented for the whole globe [SOURCE: Page_1]

18   (emerged lands) in the Centre Aval de Traitement des Données SMOS (CATDS), the French data [SOURCE: Page_1]

19   processing center for SMOS Level 3 products. This new CATDS Level-4 Disaggregation processor [SOURCE: Page_1]

20   (C4DIS) generates SM maps at 1 km resolution. This paper provides an overview of the C4DIS [SOURCE: Page_1]

21   architecture, algorithms and output products. Differences with the original DISPATCH prototype are [SOURCE: Page_1]

22   explained and major processing parameters are presented. The C4DIS SM product is compared against L$_3$ [SOURCE: Page_1]

23   and in situ SM data during a one year period over the Murrumbidgee catchment and the Yanco area [SOURCE: Page_1]

24   (Australia), and during a four and a half year period over the Little Washita and the Walnut Gulch [SOURCE: Page_1]

25   watersheds (USA). The four validation areas represent highly contrasting climate regions with different [SOURCE: Page_1]

26   landscape properties. According to this analysis, the C4DIS SM product improves the spatio-temporal [SOURCE: Page_1]

2 of 48 [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   correlation with in situ measurements in the semi-arid regions with substantial SM spatial variability [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   mainly driven by precipitation and irrigation. In sub-humid regions like the Little Washita watershed, the [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   performance of the algorithm is poor except for summer, as result of the weak moisture-evaporation [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   coupling. Disaggregated products do not succeed to have and additional benefit in the Walnut Gulch [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   needed by DISPATCH. Although further validation studies are still needed to better assess the [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   expected to provide satisfying results over regions having medium to high SM spatial variability. [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   Soil moisture (SM) is an essential component of the water cycle that impacts infiltration, runoff and [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   evaporation processes. In addition, it modulates the energy exchange as well as the carbon exchange at the [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   land surface (Daly & Porporato, 2005). SM has influence over a range of spatial scales: the climatic [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   (Douville, 2004; Laio et al., 2002), the meteorological (Dirmeyer, 2000; Drusch, 2007), the hydrological [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   (Chen et al., 2011; Draper et al., 2012), the parcel and the local scale (Guérif & Duke, 2000). [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   Current satellite missions provide surface SM observations at large scales on a global basis. Passive [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   microwave L-band observations are widely used for surface SM retrievals, but in practice they constrain [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   the resolution of the retrievals to 30-60 km (Kerr & Njoku, 1990; Njoku & Entekhabi, 1996; Schmugge, [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   1998) with current technology. The Soil Moisture Ocean Salinity (SMOS) mission, launched in November [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   2009, incorporates an interferometric radiometer at L-band (1.4 GHz) and provides SM with a resolution [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   of 30-55 km and a sensing depth of 3-5 cm (Kerr et al., 2001, 2010). SMOS Level 2 (L$_2$) and Level 3 (L$_3$) [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   SM products have been validated extensively on a regular basis since the beginning of the mission (Al [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   Bitar et al., 2012; Delwart et al., 2008) and they have been assessed as suitable for hydro-climate [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   applications (Lievens et al., 2015; Wanders et al., 2014). However, most hydro-agricultural applications [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   need SM measurements of sub-kilometer spatial resolution with a still representative temporal coverage [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   (Walker & Houser, 2004). We should strive to provide a high resolution (HR) SM product that would [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   enhance the knowledge of the hydrological processes at local scale. [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   Different satellite-based approaches have been proposed to retrieve SM. One of the most popular is the [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   use of active sensors like the synthetic aperture radars (SAR) (ERS, ALOS, Sentinel 1) or scatterometers [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   (ASCAT). These instruments provide observations with a variety of spatial and time resolutions but they [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   are influenced to a great extent by the scattering produced by vegetation structure and surface roughness, [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   among other factors. Unlike active sensors, passive instruments are much less sensitive to scattering but [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   provide surface SM estimations at coarse resolutions (>40 km). C- and X-band radiometers like AMSR-E [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   and WindSat have shown good results (Mladenova et al., 2011), but because of the frequency used, their [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   sensing depth is shallow (~1cm) and vegetation becomes rapidly opaque. In contrast, L-band radiometer [SOURCE: Page_3]

4 of 48 [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   acquisitions from SMOS provide SM estimations for a much wider range of vegetation conditions, with a [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   sensing depth of around 5 cm and a revisit time of ~3 days. However, the spatial resolution provided is [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   also coarse (35-55 km) as mentioned previously. The main strategies to workaround this issue while [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   maintaining the benefits of L-band consist of merging the L-band acquisitions with HR ancillary data, [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   namely radar and optical observations. [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   Over the past decade, various methods have been proposed to combine active and passive sensors to [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   produce HR SM (Das et al., 2011; Narayan et al., 2006; Zhan et al., 2006). The NASA Soil Moisture [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   Active Passive (SMAP) mission, launched in 2015, intended to combine L-band brightness temperatures [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   (TB) and HR L-band radar backscatter data (Entekhabi et al., 2010a). Despite the radar failure in July [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   2015, related previous studies showed that SM could have been delivered at 9 km and even 3 km [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   resolution (Das et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   Optical sensors (visible/near-infrared/thermal-infrared) can achieve finer spatial resolutions. However, the [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   quality of their observations is critically compromised by the presence of clouds. Examples of optical [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   sensors include the Landsat instruments and the Advanced Spaceborne Thermal Emission and Reflection [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   radiometer (ASTER), with         data at ~100 m resolution, and the MODerate resolution Imaging [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   Spectroradiometer (MODIS), with data at ~1 km resolution. Such data include soil temperature and [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   vegetation cover information, which are variables linked to soil water content (Fang et al., 2013). The [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   relationship between land surface temperature (LST) and normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   was first formalized in the 90s with the triangle (Carlson et al., 1994; Carlson, 2007) and the trapezoid [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   (Moran et al., 1994) approaches. [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   Most of the methods for deriving HR SM from the synergy between optical and microwave observations [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   are based on the triangle/trapezoid approaches. Chauhan et al. (2003) stated that the relationship between [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   LST, NDVI and SM can be formulated as a regression formula specific to the region and climatic [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   conditions. Later, Piles et al. (2011) included SMOS TBs in the equation, which reduced the bias but [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   slightly degraded the spatio-temporal correlation between the obtained HR SM and the in situ [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   measurements. These empirical methods need local calibration of the regression coefficients at low [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   resolution (LR) before applying them to the HR ancillary data. On the contrary, semi-physical methods [SOURCE: Page_4]

5 of 48 [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   replace the polynomial function by physically-based models that use evaporation as a proxy variable for [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   SM variability. Merlin et al. (2008) linked the SM to the soil evaporative efficiency (SEE), defined as the [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   ratio of actual to potential soil evaporation. Kim & Hogue (2012) established a linear relationship [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   between the soil evaporative fraction of Jiang & Islam (2003) and SM. Both approaches improved the [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   satellite SM spatial variability and showed better correspondence with ground measurements in the area of [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   study (SMEX04). [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   The semi-physical methods have three important advantages with respect to the purely empirical methods: [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   (i) the mean SM is preserved across the merging process (which justifies calling it ‘disaggregation’ or [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   ‘downscaling’),    (ii)   a   physical   link   is   established   for   HR     between     SM     and   the [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   evaporation/evapotranspiration rate and (iii) no local calibration or fit is needed. These are key factors in [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   developing a robust and global operational algorithm for HR SM. [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   Recent studies by Merlin et al. (2012, 2013) have improved the evaporation rate calculation and the [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   evaporation-SM link of Merlin et al. (2008). The DISaggregation based on Physical And Theoretical scale [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   Change (DISPATCH) algorithm estimates SEE at high-resolution from soil temperature and vegetation [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   data for modeling the spatial variations inside the microwave SM observation. In Merlin et al. (2012), [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   DISPATCH included corrections for the microwave sensor weighting function and grid oversampling and [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   provided an estimate of the uncertainty in the output disaggregated data. Later, Merlin et al. (2013) [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   demonstrated that the linear approximation of the SEE-SM link model is suitable for kilometer scales and [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   included soil temperature corrections for elevation effects. Both studies were conducted under semi-arid [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   conditions, in a 500x^100 km study area within the Murrumbidgee river catchment, in southeastern [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   Australia, and in a 60x^60 km study area east of Lleida in Catalunya, Spain. They showed that DISPATCH [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   improves the spatio-temporal correlation with in situ measurements, but that the accuracy of disaggregated [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   products is highly dependant on the SM-evaporation coupling. The downscaled resolution of 1 km [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   (Merlin et al., 2009, 2013) and the combination of satellite data from different time stamps in DISPATCH [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   (Malbéteau et al., 2016b; Merlin et al., 2012) have been considered as a good trade-off between spatial [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   representativeness and overall accuracy, given the current status of the algorithm. [SOURCE: Page_5]

6 of 48 [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   Recently, a new Level-4 (L$_4$) processor (C4DIS) based on DISPATCH has been implemented in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   Centre Aval de Traitement des Données SMOS (CATDS), the French ground segment for SMOS Level-3 [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   and Level-4 data. The aim is to disaggregate the SMOS CATDS Level-3 (L$_3$) 1-day SM maps to produce [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   maps of SM at 1 km resolution for any part of the globe on an operational basis. The ancillary temperature [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   and vegetation data are retrieved from the MODIS mission. [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   This paper seeks (i) to provide an overview of the C4DIS architecture, processing algorithms, output [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   products, strengths and weaknesses and (ii) to derive the first conclusions on the performance of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   C4DIS product depending on the climatic and landscape conditions. To do so, we evaluate the C4DIS [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   product against in situ data from the Murrumbidgee catchment and two additional contrasting networks. [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   Former versions of DISPATCH have so far been evaluated mostly in semi-arid conditions (Merlin et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   2012, 2013, Malbéteau et al., 2015). The Murrumbidgee network belongs to these previous studies, and it [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   is included here to serve as a reference for the current version of DISPATCH and the C4DIS processor [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   and for the other validation areas. The two other in situ networks considered in this study are located in [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   the Little Washita watershed in Oklahoma, USA, which exhibits sub-humid conditions, and the Walnut [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   Gulch watershed in Arizona, USA, which exhibits semi-arid to arid conditions. Their relief, soil properties [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   and land use differ from the Murrumbidgee’s. The L$_4$ disaggregated SM product is evaluated using in situ [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   0–5 cm and in situ 0–8 cm measurements taken at the same time as SMOS overpasses (around 6 am, 6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   pm) during the period 01/06/2010 to 31/05/2011 for the Australian network and 01/06/2010 to 31/12/2014 [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   for the USA networks. These networks have been providing ground SM data in a continuous basis and [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   have contributed to the validation of different satellite missions, SMOS among them (Cosh et al., 2004; [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   Jackson et al., 2010, 2012; Leroux et al., 2013; Peischl et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   It is important to note that the DISPATCH algorithm will continue to evolve. Validation activities on the [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   Level-4 processor C4DIS will provide valuable information for the improvement of the algorithm and [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   processing chain. This current study is conducted on the products of the first version of the C4DIS [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   processor. [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   2.1 In situ measurements [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   Three validation networks were selected for this work, the Murrumbidgee Soil Moisture Monitoring [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   Network (MB) in Australia (Smith et al., 2012) and two different USDA (United Stated Department of [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   Agriculture) networks: Little Washita (LW) in Oklahoma (Cosh et al., 2006) and Walnut Gulch (WG) in [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   Arizona (Cosh et al., 2008). They exhibit contrasted types of climate, soil properties, land use and spatial [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   extension. [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   The MB network covers a large extension (82,000 km^2) in southern New South Wales. Its climate ranges [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   from semi-arid in the west (average annual precipitation of 300 mm), to humid in the east (annual [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   precipitation of 1900 mm at the Snowy Mountains). The MB has been studied in previous DISPATCH [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   campaigns (Malbéteau et al., 2016b; Merlin et al., 2012). It is included here for different reasons: it [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   permits to confront results with previous versions of the algorithm, it contains within the Yanco area, [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   which gathers the nominal landscape and climatic conditions for DISPATCH (flat, semi-arid with low [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   vegetation), and it shows a variety of climate, soil and land use cases that can reveal the usefulness of [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   disaggregation. [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   The MB consists in 38 validation stations: 18 of them provide SM integrated over the first 8 cm of soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   (Campbell Scientific water content reflectometers) and the rest provide SM integrated over the first 5 cm [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   of soil (Stevens Hydra Probe). The stations are situated in four areas: 7 stations in the limits of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   catchment near to regional centers; 5 stations in Adelong Creek (145 km^2), a grazing area with steep [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   slopes; 13 stations in Kyeamba creek (600 km^2), a catchment with gentle slopes and grazing and dairy [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   land use; and finally, 13 stations in the Yanco region (3000 km^2). [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   Yanco soils are mainly silty-loam. The climate is semi-arid with an average annual rainfall of about 400 [SOURCE: Page_7]

161   mm, with most of the precipitation occurring in winter and spring. The land use is divided into irrigation [SOURCE: Page_7]

162   and dry land cropping and pastures. This area has been extensively monitored since 2001 (Smith et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

8 of 48 [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   2012) and has been used in a variety of satellite validation campaigns (Mladenova et al., 2011; Panciera et [SOURCE: Page_8]

164   al., 2014; Peischl et al., 2012) [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   The USDA networks have been operating since 2002 and they have been used in the validation of [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer-Earth Observing System (AMSR-E) products (Jackson et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   2010), Aquarius (Bindlish, 2015), ASCAT (Leroux et al., 2013) and SMOS products (Jackson et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   2012). The probes are installed at a depth of 5 cm, with an effective measurement depth between 3 and 7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   cm ( Stevens Hydra Probe). [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   LW is located in southwest Oklahoma and covers an area of about 610 km^2. The climate is sub-humid [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   with an average annual rainfall of 750 mm. Summers are hot and relatively dry while winters are short and [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   temperate. Autumn and spring are when most of the precipitation occurs (Allen & Naney, 1991). The land [SOURCE: Page_8]

173   use is mainly rangeland and crops that include winter wheat and some corn and grasses. Soils include a [SOURCE: Page_8]

174   wide range of textures, with large regions of sands, loams and clays. The topography is moderately rolling [SOURCE: Page_8]

175   with few hills. [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   WG occupies an area of 148 km^2 in southeastern Arizona. The climate is semi-arid, with an average [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   annual rainfall of 324 mm, lower than in the Yanco region. Most of the rains occur in the form of small [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   scale high-intensity thunderstorms during the summer months as part of the North American Monsoon [SOURCE: Page_8]

179   System (Cosh et al., 2008). Soils are mainly sands and gravel with good drainage. Desert shrubs and short [SOURCE: Page_8]

180   grasses dominate the landscape. The topography is considered as rolling with significant rock cover. [SOURCE: Page_8]

181   Although the climate class of WG is defined semi-arid as the Yanco area, the contrasting landscape [SOURCE: Page_8]

182   properties and precipitation conditions make WG an interesting validation area (Table 1). [SOURCE: Page_8]

183   It is important to outline that the area extent covered by the networks is different so it may have an impact [SOURCE: Page_8]

184   on the validation process: the MB comprises multiple SMOS pixels through sparse stations and more [SOURCE: Page_8]

186   around 1/4 and 1/16 of the surface of one SMOS pixel. This does not affect the C4DIS processor, which [SOURCE: Page_8]

187   handles input larger surfaces, but it may affect the validation process since the smaller networks may not [SOURCE: Page_8]

190   The SMOS satellite was launched in November 2009. SMOS has global coverage with a revisit period of [SOURCE: Page_9]

191   3 days at the equator, with ascending (A) overpass at 6:00 am and descending (D) overpass 6:00 pm local [SOURCE: Page_9]

192   solar time. The SMOS instrument is a passive 2D interferometer operating at L band (1.4 GHz) (Kerr et [SOURCE: Page_9]

193   al., 2001, 2010). The spatial resolution ranges from 35 to 55 km, depending on the incident angle. The [SOURCE: Page_9]

225   3. The CATDS Level-4 Disaggregation (C4DIS) processor [SOURCE: Page_11]

226   The CATDS Level-4 (L$_4$) Disaggregation (C4DIS) processor is the first operational version of the [SOURCE: Page_11]

227   DISPATCH algorithm. The C4DIS processor selects the best algorithm and parameter configuration [SOURCE: Page_11]

228   according to past DISPATCH studies and the latest research (Merlin et al., 2006, 2009, 2010a, 2010c, [SOURCE: Page_11]

229   2012, 2013). It also makes possible to obtain disaggregated SM on a global and daily basis (under the [SOURCE: Page_11]

230   assumption of no cloud-covered scenes and availability of input data). The C4DIS products have been [SOURCE: Page_11]

231   marked as ‘scientific’ products because the algorithm is still evolving: their access will be granted on [SOURCE: Page_11]

232   demand for specific areas of the world. In this and the following sections, we describe both the [SOURCE: Page_11]

233   DISPATCH prototype and the C4DIS processor. [SOURCE: Page_11]

234   3.1 DISPATCH algorithm [SOURCE: Page_11]

235   DISPATCH relies on a SEE term to model the spatial variability over the low-resolution (LR) SMOS [SOURCE: Page_11]

236   pixel. The first step is to account for the SEE term at HR, described as a linear function of soil [SOURCE: Page_11]

237   temperature: [SOURCE: Page_11]

SEEHR = (Ts,max – Ts,HR) / (Ts,max – Ts,min) (1) [SOURCE: Page_11]

238   Soil (Ts,HR) and vegetation (Tv,HR) temperatures are derived from LST and NDVI datasets as in Merlin et [SOURCE: Page_11]

239   al., (2012), where the surface temperature is partitioned into its soil and vegetation components according [SOURCE: Page_11]

240   to the trapezoid method of Moran et al. (1994). Soil temperature is calculated as follows: [SOURCE: Page_11]

241                                    Ts,HR = (TMODIS − fv,HRTv,HR) / (1−fv,HR) (2) [SOURCE: Page_11]

242   with TMODIS being the MODIS LST and fv the MODIS-derived fractional vegetation cover. Here, the [SOURCE: Page_11]

243   fractional vegetation cover is calculated as: [SOURCE: Page_11]

244                               fv,HR = (NDVIMODIS –NDVIs) / (NDVIv –NDVIs) (3) [SOURCE: Page_11]

245   with NDVIMODIS being the MODIS NDVI, NDVIs the NDVI for bare soil (set to 0.15), and NDVIv the [SOURCE: Page_11]

246   NDVI for full-cover vegetation (set to 0.90). [SOURCE: Page_11]

12 of 48 [SOURCE: Page_12]

247   The vegetation temperature Tv,HR is calculated according to the “hourglass” approach (Moran et al., 1994, [SOURCE: Page_12]

248   Merlin et al., 2012): [SOURCE: Page_12]

249                                          Tv,HR = (Tv,min + Tv,max) / 2 (4) [SOURCE: Page_12]

250   At this point, vegetation (Tv,min, Tv,max) and soil (Ts,min, Ts,max) temperature end-members are estimated [SOURCE: Page_12]

251   depending on the amount of vegetation. Given the minimum and maximum LST values of the scene [SOURCE: Page_12]

252   TMODIS,min and TMODIS,max, and the fv values associated to the same pixels, fv,Tmin and fv,Tmax, the following [SOURCE: Page_12]

253   approximations hold (Merlin et al., 2013): [SOURCE: Page_12]

254       I)      When the vegetation portion is low at TMODIS,min (fv,Tmin < 0.5), then Ts,min = Tv,min = TMODIS,min [SOURCE: Page_12]

255       II)     When the vegetation portion is considerable at TMODIS,min (fv,Tmin >= 0.5), then Tv,min = [SOURCE: Page_12]

256               TMODIS,min and Ts,min is derived from Eq. 2, with Tv = Tv,min [SOURCE: Page_12]

257       III)    When the vegetation portion is low at TMODIS,max (fv,Tmax < 0.5), then Ts,max = TMODIS,max and [SOURCE: Page_12]

258               Tv,max is derived from Eq. 2, with Ts = Ts,max [SOURCE: Page_12]

259       IV)     When the vegetation portion is considerable at TMODIS,max (fv,Tmin >= 0.5), then Tv,max = [SOURCE: Page_12]

260               TMODIS,max and Ts,max is derived from Eq. 2, with Tv = Tv,max [SOURCE: Page_12]

261   Note that LST has been preliminary corrected for elevation effects (decrease of air temperature with [SOURCE: Page_12]

262   altitude) by using the DEM information at HR (Merlin et al., 2013): [SOURCE: Page_12]

263                                      TMODIS = TMODIS-ori + γ(HHR – HLR) (5) [SOURCE: Page_12]

264   with TMODIS being the topography-corrected LST used in the previous equations, TMODIS-ori the original [SOURCE: Page_12]

265   MODIS LST, γ (°C m−1) the mean lapse rate (set to 0.006 °C m−1), HHR the altitude of the MODIS pixel [SOURCE: Page_12]

266   and HLR the mean altitude within the LR pixel. [SOURCE: Page_12]

267   In a second step, the semi-empirical linear model of Budyko (1956) and Manabe (1969) is used to link the [SOURCE: Page_12]

268   surface SM (0-5 cm) and the SEE terms. According to Merlin et al. (2013), the linear model is a good [SOURCE: Page_12]

269   approximation for kilometer scales so the SEE for each HR pixel can be written as: [SOURCE: Page_12]

13 of 48 [SOURCE: Page_13]

270                                           SEEHR = SMHR / SMp (6) [SOURCE: Page_13]

271   where SMp is a parameter estimated at LR at each execution from daily SM and SEE observations as [SOURCE: Page_13]

272   follows: [SOURCE: Page_13]

273                                            SMp = SMLR / SEELR (7) [SOURCE: Page_13]

274   with SMLR the radiometer-sensed SM and SEELR the average of the SEEHR values inside the LR pixel. [SOURCE: Page_13]

279   3.2 DISPATCH operational implementation [SOURCE: Page_13]

280   Following the methodology introduced in Merlin et al. (2012), C4DIS executes DISPATCH on a set of [SOURCE: Page_13]

281   possible combinations of input datasets, producing multiple HR outputs that are averaged together into a [SOURCE: Page_13]

282   single final disaggregated SM field (SM_HR). The rationale behind this is to account for the uncertainty [SOURCE: Page_13]

283   of the approach and to reduce independent random errors (Malbéteau et al., 2016b; Merlin et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_13]

284   The input ensemble is formed by 4 downsampled instances of the original L$_3$ SM dataset and up to 6 LST [SOURCE: Page_13]

285   datasets corresponding to 3 consecutive days of MODIS acquisitions (Aqua and Terra overpasses). This [SOURCE: Page_13]

286   means that each SM_HR output comes from the composition of up to 24 DISPATCH outputs (up to 24 [SOURCE: Page_13]

287   input SM-LST possible pairs). [SOURCE: Page_13]

288   SMOS original datasets are downsampled in order to work at the radiometer resolution. SMOS L$_3$ [SOURCE: Page_13]

289   products are provided on a 25 km grid, which can be up to half of the original SMOS resolution (35-50 [SOURCE: Page_13]

290   km). The four SM datasets are derived from the original SM map by sampling the data at 50 km and are [SOURCE: Page_13]

291   assumed to be independent. This is not totally true, since grid cells depend on the surrounding cells from a [SOURCE: Page_13]

292   radiometric perspective, but helps to potentially reduce (and provide an estimate of) random errors in the [SOURCE: Page_13]

293   SM_HR data. Regarding the selection of 6 MODIS LST datasets from 3 consecutive days, it is assumed [SOURCE: Page_13]

294   that SM fields are spatially stable for periods of at least 1 day around the SMOS overpass time. This 3-day [SOURCE: Page_13]

339   3.5 Assumptions and applicability domains of the algorithm [SOURCE: Page_16]

340   The application requirements of the C4DIS processor are directly inherited from DISPATCH. The [SOURCE: Page_16]

341   following considerations must be taken into account: [SOURCE: Page_16]

342   -   Cloud free conditions: soil temperature can only been retrieved from optical sensors if clouds are not [SOURCE: Page_16]

343       present. C4DIS products show data gaps associated with clouds. [SOURCE: Page_16]

344   -   Low vegetation cover:        The LST-NDVI trapezoid describes a zone of values where no useful [SOURCE: Page_16]

345       disaggregated data can be produced since LST is mainly controlled by vegetation transpiration, with [SOURCE: Page_16]

346       no sensitivity to surface SM (Merlin et al., 2013). Sites with partial fractional vegetation cover at the 1 [SOURCE: Page_16]

347       km resolution are desired. [SOURCE: Page_16]

17 of 48 [SOURCE: Page_17]

348   -   Moisture-driven evapotranspiration: the disaggregation relies on the dependence established between [SOURCE: Page_17]

349       LST, evapotranspiration and SM. Some climates exhibit low dependency between those variables. [SOURCE: Page_17]

350       Typically, climates characterized as energy-limited, like humid climates, exhibit a weaker moisture- [SOURCE: Page_17]

351       evaporation coupling. [SOURCE: Page_17]

352   -   Medium to high spatial variability: the MODIS-derived SEE is computed with a polygon method that [SOURCE: Page_17]

353       relies on LST and reflectance end-members (Moran et al., 1994). In the current version, DISPATCH [SOURCE: Page_17]

354       is contextual and thus heterogeneous scenes with meaningful dry-wet contrast are needed in order to [SOURCE: Page_17]

355       ensure good end-members accuracy (Merlin et al., 2010a). Note that LST end-members could be [SOURCE: Page_17]

356       estimated using available meteorological data (Moran et al. 1994) independently from the surface [SOURCE: Page_17]

357       (wet/dry) conditions observed at the 1 km resolution within the LR pixel (Stefan et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_17]

358   -   Accuracy of the SMp parameter: the SMp parameter is calculated at LR scale by using a linear [SOURCE: Page_17]

359       relationship that has been studied as suitable for kilometer scales (Merlin et al., 2013). It is based on [SOURCE: Page_17]

360       the assumption that the sub-pixel variability of SMp at HR is negligible. Soil characteristics (texture, [SOURCE: Page_17]

361       porosity, etc.) may impact the relationship between SEE and SM and thus SMp. Hence, the current [SOURCE: Page_17]

362       versions of C4DIS and DISPATCH should perform better in areas with homogeneous soil [SOURCE: Page_17]

363       characteristics where the intra-pixel spatial SM variability is mainly due to forcing agents, namely [SOURCE: Page_17]

364       precipitation and irrigation. [SOURCE: Page_17]

365   -   Mismatch of overpass times: the C4DIS processor uses MODIS LST datasets at 6 different [SOURCE: Page_17]

366       timestamps. This is based on the assumption that the SM pattern is maintained over a period of 3 days, [SOURCE: Page_17]

403   4. Analysis Methodology [SOURCE: Page_21]

404   Our analysis involves two main approaches: qualitative assessment of disaggregated SM maps and [SOURCE: Page_21]

405   statistical evaluation. The statistical evaluation consists on comparing the L$_3$ SMOS product (LR) and the [SOURCE: Page_21]

406   L$_4$ product (HR) against the in situ SM by using standard statistical metrics (e.g. correlation, bias, etc.). [SOURCE: Page_21]

407   This can be accomplished in the spatial or in the time domain. We base the statistical evaluation on the [SOURCE: Page_21]

408   assumption that the 1 km pixel is more representative of the in situ measurement than the whole LR pixel. [SOURCE: Page_21]

409   In order to assess the relative spatial performance of both L$_3$ and L$_4$ products at HR, we directly compare [SOURCE: Page_21]

410   the station measurements to the satellite retrievals, without aggregating them at LR. In the subsequent [SOURCE: Page_21]

411   sections, MB refers to the whole Murrumbidgee network, including Yanco area. Yanco only refers to the [SOURCE: Page_21]

412   12 stations contained in this region. [SOURCE: Page_21]

413   4.1 Data preparation [SOURCE: Page_21]

414   We filter L$_3$ and L$_4$ SM time series for radio frequency interference (RFI) by removing pixels having [SOURCE: Page_21]

415   more than 10 % RFI probability. The RFI information is extracted from the same CLF31A/D product and [SOURCE: Page_21]

416   accounts for the percentage of brightness temperatures acquisitions affected by RFI presence (Kerr et al., [SOURCE: Page_21]

417   2013; Oliva et al., 2012). In addition, regarding the in situ data, we only keep the SM values at the SMOS [SOURCE: Page_21]

418   overpass times. Finally, we filter the three SM time series (in situ, L$_3$ and L$_4$) for common dates with [SOURCE: Page_21]

419   valid SM values (>0.0 m^3/m^3). [SOURCE: Page_21]

420   4.2 Analysis of the temporal and spatial variability of the in situ SM [SOURCE: Page_21]

421   As expected for any data disaggregation approach, the application of DISPATCH is relevant when the SM [SOURCE: Page_21]

422   spatial variability at the downscaled resolution is larger than the output uncertainty. Since the current [SOURCE: Page_21]

423   version of DISPATCH relies on the spatial contrast of LST and SM of the scene, a preliminary study on [SOURCE: Page_21]

424   the spatial SM variability of the validation areas is desired. In homogeneous SM landscapes, the output [SOURCE: Page_21]

425   uncertainty is likely to be greater than the spatial gain provided at HR by disaggregation. [SOURCE: Page_21]

22 of 48 [SOURCE: Page_22]

426   Similarly, it is desirable that the evaluation include in situ time series spanning the full range of SM [SOURCE: Page_22]

427   conditions and seasonal changes. In other words, the temporal standard deviation (σ) should be large [SOURCE: Page_22]

428   enough so that all the states of the SM variable are represented and no selection bias is present. [SOURCE: Page_22]

429   Additionally, stations exhibiting very different temporal σ may suggest landscape spatial heterogeneity: [SOURCE: Page_22]

430   soil characteristics like texture, vegetation and topography affect the dry-down process, generating [SOURCE: Page_22]

431   different extreme values in time. [SOURCE: Page_22]

432   Based upon the considerations discussed above, the evaluation of the performance of the C4DIS products [SOURCE: Page_22]

433   should include a preliminary assessment of the spatial and temporal SM variability of the validation [SOURCE: Page_22]

434   networks. The performance of DISPATCH outputs over MB and Yanco has been identified as rather [SOURCE: Page_22]

435   satisfactory in recent studies (Malbéteau et al., 2016b; Merlin et al., 2012), which makes them good [SOURCE: Page_22]

436   references for spatial and temporal σ. [SOURCE: Page_22]

437   4.3 Classical metrics [SOURCE: Page_22]

438   Given the spatial mismatch between in situ and satellite estimations and the spatial scarcity of ground [SOURCE: Page_22]

439   stations, most classical satellite validation campaigns only evaluate the temporal dimension, by means of [SOURCE: Page_22]

440   metrics like correlation (R), root mean square error (RMSE) and bias (B) (Albergel et al., 2012; Albergel, [SOURCE: Page_22]

441   et al., 2013; Entekhabi, et al., 2010b; Bitar et al., 2012). In this study, we use similar temporal analysis but [SOURCE: Page_22]

442   we also include an evaluation in the spatial domain since disaggregation techniques aim at producing [SOURCE: Page_22]

443   better spatial representation. The spatial statistical analysis consists of computing the metrics between the [SOURCE: Page_22]

444   satellite and in situ values for each day, then, deriving the average of each metric for the whole period. We [SOURCE: Page_22]

445   deliberately establish a minimum of 5 points per day to compute the metrics. [SOURCE: Page_22]

446   Herein, instead of the RMSE, we use as error metric the standard deviation of the error (Eq. 9) (Mood et [SOURCE: Page_22]

447   al., 1974; Salkind, 2010), which is a non-biased estimation of the error and so it is not compromised by the [SOURCE: Page_22]

448   bias in the mean and amplitude of the time series that affects the RMSE. The relationship between both [SOURCE: Page_22]

449   metrics is written in Eq. 10 and 11. Since we already use multiple terms to refer to different standard [SOURCE: Page_22]

450   deviation measures and datasets in this paper (σ, STD), we will refer to this metric as unbiased-RMSE or [SOURCE: Page_22]

451   ubRMSE (Entekhabi et al., 2010b). Given that the 1 km pixels are in general heterogeneous and that the [SOURCE: Page_22]

23 of 48 [SOURCE: Page_23]

452   ground data also present measurement uncertainties, the term ‘error’ has been replaced by ‘difference’ in [SOURCE: Page_23]

453   these metrics, i.e. RMSD and ubRMSD. [SOURCE: Page_23]

ubRMSD = √ E{ [(SMsatellite – E{SMsatellite}) – (SMinsitu – E{ SMinsitu })]2} (9) [SOURCE: Page_23]

RMSD = √ E{(SMsatellite - SMinsitu)2} (10) [SOURCE: Page_23]

ubRMSD = √ (RMSD$_2$ - B$_2$) (11) [SOURCE: Page_23]

454   where E{·} is the expectation operator, SMsatellite and SMinsitu the satellite and the in situ SM time series. [SOURCE: Page_23]

455   We include one additional metric to assess the efficiency gained in spatial representativeness: the slope (S) [SOURCE: Page_23]

456   of the regression line between in situ and satellite estimates: [SOURCE: Page_23]

457                                              S = R · σsatellite / σinsitu (12) [SOURCE: Page_23]

458   with σsatellite and σinsitu being the standard deviations of satellite and in situ SM, respectively. The S metric [SOURCE: Page_23]

459   can help to understand how much better the SM redistribution is represented after the disaggregation [SOURCE: Page_23]

460   process. Whereas aggregation systematically decreases the σsatellite, disaggregation specifically aims to [SOURCE: Page_23]

461   improve the spatial representation of satellite SM by increasing the σsatellite at the level of σinsitu, while [SOURCE: Page_23]

462   keeping a significant R. Mathematically speaking, R is the slope of the standardized regression line, and S [SOURCE: Page_23]

463   is scaled by the σ values of both data ensembles (Rodgers & Nicewander, 1988). Since the σinsitu is fixed, S [SOURCE: Page_23]

464   is more sensitive than R to changes in σsatellite. In summary, an increase in random uncertainties (larger [SOURCE: Page_23]

465   ubRMSD, smaller R) in disaggregated SM might be acceptable if S is closer to 1. Note that the random [SOURCE: Page_23]

466   uncertainties in satellite SM can be significantly reduced via the techniques of data assimilation in land [SOURCE: Page_23]

467   surface models, but the systematic errors associated with the mismatch between data resolution and model [SOURCE: Page_23]

468   application scale are more difficult to take into account at HR (Merlin et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_23]

469   Finally, the metrics here (S, R, ubRMSD, B) assume that a linear relationship exists between the two [SOURCE: Page_23]

470   datasets compared. This means that they cannot replace the visual assessment of the data. In the general [SOURCE: Page_23]

471   case, both SMOS L3 and disaggregated SM may exhibit non-linear behavior with respect to in situ SM. [SOURCE: Page_23]

642   6. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_37]

643   The C4DIS processor is the new SMOS L$_4$ processor of the French ground segment CATDS, which [SOURCE: Page_37]

644   provides global maps of disaggregated SM at 1 km resolution. The C4DIS processor is the operational [SOURCE: Page_37]

645   version of the DISPATCH prototype (Merlin et al., 2012, 2013). DISPATCH disaggregates LR SM [SOURCE: Page_37]

646   observations using HR soil temperature data. It models the physical link between soil temperature, [SOURCE: Page_37]

647   evaporation and moisture with a semi-empirical SEE model and a first-order Taylor series expansion [SOURCE: Page_37]

648   around the SM observation. The soil temperature is derived from the combination of LST, NDVI and [SOURCE: Page_37]

649   elevation information. The C4DIS processor uses the SM dataset of the SMOS 1-day L$_3$ CLF31A/D [SOURCE: Page_37]

650   product from CATDS, the LST dataset of the MODIS MOD11A$_1$ and MYD11A$_1$ products from LP [SOURCE: Page_37]

651   DAAC services, the NDVI dataset from the MOD13A$_2$ product from LP DAAC services, and the [SOURCE: Page_37]

652   elevation dataset from the GTOPO30 product from the USGS Eros Data Center. [SOURCE: Page_37]

653   In this study, the C4DIS products were evaluated for four different geographical areas: the Murrumbidgee [SOURCE: Page_37]

654   validation network and the Yanco area for the period 06/2010 to 05/2011, and the Little Washita and [SOURCE: Page_37]

655   Walnut Gulch networks for the period 01/2010 to 12/2014. The objective was to provide a first assessment [SOURCE: Page_37]

656   of the processor under different climatic and land conditions.       The performance was assessed by [SOURCE: Page_37]

657   comparing the disaggregated (L$_4$) and non-disaggregated (L$_3$) SM datasets against the in situ [SOURCE: Page_37]

658   measurements in both the spatial and temporal domains.. The in situ SM data was statistically analyzed [SOURCE: Page_37]

659   beforehand in order to predict the suitability of the C4DIS processor for each area. We also evaluated the [SOURCE: Page_37]

660   output COUNT and STD datasets as potential sources of information for quality assessment. [SOURCE: Page_37]

661   The evaluation of the disaggregated SM dataset in Murrumbidgee and Yanco brought results in coherence [SOURCE: Page_37]

662   with previous versions of DISPATCH (Malbéteau et al., 2016b; Merlin et al., 2012), and presented [SOURCE: Page_37]

663   improvements on the spatial correlation in the range 0.09-0.17. Similar enhancements were present in the [SOURCE: Page_37]

664   temporal domain. Additionally, C4DIS SM maps succeeded to reveal spatial heterogeneities (rivers, [SOURCE: Page_37]

665   irrigation areas, floods). [SOURCE: Page_37]

38 of 48 [SOURCE: Page_38]

666   Little Washita and Walnut Gulch showed very low spatial metric values for both non-disaggregated and [SOURCE: Page_38]

667   disaggregated SM fields, though disaggregation slightly improved the statistics. For the Little Washita, the [SOURCE: Page_38]

668   scatter plots revealed that the performances were better in the dry section of the SM range (<0.15 m^3/m^3) [SOURCE: Page_38]

669   and during summers, meaning that the improvement in spatial representation was possible under moisture- [SOURCE: Page_38]

670   driven evaporation periods. Visual assessment of C4DIS SM maps showed that the disaggregated product [SOURCE: Page_38]

671   was capable of revealing the presence of water bodies in the surrounding areas namely lakes. [SOURCE: Page_38]

672   For the Walnut Gulch network, the poor spatial correspondence with in situ was easily explained by the [SOURCE: Page_38]

673   preliminary statistical analysis that we conducted on in situ SM data: this revealed very low spatial [SOURCE: Page_38]

674   variability (mean spatial σ was equal to 0.03 m^3/m^3), which is one of the essential conditions for a good [SOURCE: Page_38]

675   performance of the algorithm. The evaluation of this network brought to view that the algorithm needs to [SOURCE: Page_38]

676   be improved to adapt to all types of soil. Although Walnut Gulch watershed also has a moisture-controlled [SOURCE: Page_38]

677   evaporative profile (semi-arid to arid climate) like the Australian areas, the soil is mainly sandy with high [SOURCE: Page_38]

678   infiltration rates, which obstructs the detection of surface SM variations by the algorithm. [SOURCE: Page_38]

679   When evaluating the temporal behavior of the (non-disaggregated and C4DIS) satellite SM series, we [SOURCE: Page_38]

680   found an improvement of the slope of the regression line between C4DIS and the in situ data. The [SOURCE: Page_38]

681   correlation was slightly hampered, especially in LW and WG, and the standard deviation of the [SOURCE: Page_38]

682   differences also increased. This was likely to be caused by the increase in uncertainty associated with the [SOURCE: Page_38]

683   use of multi-satellite data. [SOURCE: Page_38]

684   With the aim of making the C4DIS products useful in a global perspective, we evaluated how the other [SOURCE: Page_38]

685   two output datasets, COUNT and STD, could help in the future definition of a quality flag. We showed [SOURCE: Page_38]

686   that for a homogeneous area like Yanco, spatial and temporal metrics were better as STD decreased. [SOURCE: Page_38]

687   Consistently, large COUNT values helped to decrease the random uncertainties and they improved [SOURCE: Page_38]

688   temporal statistics. In this area, heterogeneity is mainly driven by precipitation and irrigation, and STD [SOURCE: Page_38]

689   was directly linked to such events. On the contrary, STD and COUNT could not give sufficient [SOURCE: Page_38]

690   information for quality control in more heterogeneous areas (like the entire Murrumbidgee), so we [SOURCE: Page_38]

691   concluded that output C4DIS datasets must be combined with ancillary information like precipitation or [SOURCE: Page_38]

692   other heterogeneity-related data sources to implement a good quality flag field. [SOURCE: Page_38]

39 of 48 [SOURCE: Page_39]

693   In conclusion, the C4DIS processor performs well in regions with SM spatial variability mainly produced [SOURCE: Page_39]

694   by external forcing agents (precipitation or irrigation). Additionally, the degree of variability must be [SOURCE: Page_39]

695   enough so the application of a disaggregation technique is advisable. These two characteristics are mainly [SOURCE: Page_39]

696   conditioned by the climate (semi-arid), soil properties (with moderate drainage), and land properties (low [SOURCE: Page_39]

697   topography, quasi-homogeneous land cover). The proper performance of the processor can be predicted by [SOURCE: Page_39]

698   looking at the in situ SM variability and assessing qualitatively the enounced characteristics. The C4DIS [SOURCE: Page_39]

699   SM products can be evaluated by applying ordinary spatial and temporal statistics, visual inspection of [SOURCE: Page_39]

700   maps as well as using the STD and COUNT datasets on homogeneous areas. In the future, including [SOURCE: Page_39]

701   meteorological forcing (solar radiation, air temperature, wind speed and air humidity at 2 m; Stefan et al., [SOURCE: Page_39]

702   2015), precipitation (Djamai et al., 2015), soil texture (Merlin et al., 2016) and solar exposure (Malbéteau [SOURCE: Page_39]

703   et al., 2016a) as ancillary data will help improve DISPATCH and elaborate a quality control dataset that [SOURCE: Page_39]

704   will enlarge the applicability areas of the processor. [SOURCE: Page_39]
