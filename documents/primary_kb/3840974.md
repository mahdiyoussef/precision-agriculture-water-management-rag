26   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   Aims [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   The assumption that climatic growing requirements of invasive species are conserved between [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   their native and non-native environment is a key ecological issue in the evaluation of invasion [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   risk. We conducted a growth chamber experiment to compare the effect of water regime and [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   temperature on growth and mortality of native and invasive populations of common gorse [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   seedlings (Ulex europeaus, L). [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   Methods [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   Seeds were sampled from 20 populations from five areas from both native (continental France [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   and Spain) and non-native areas (New Zealand, Canary and Reunion islands). The seedlings [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   were grown over 36 days in two temperature treatments (ambient and elevated) combined with [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   two water treatments (irrigated or droughted). The elevated temperature was defined as the [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   highest temperature observed at the niche margin in the different countries. [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   Important findings [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   While elevated temperature increased seedlings growth, the drought treatment increased [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   mortality rate and limited seedlings growth. Under elevated temperature and drought, native [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   populations showed a greater mortality rate (53%) than invasive populations (16%). Invasive [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   seedlings also showed higher above- and belowground development than native ones under [SOURCE: Page_2]

44   these constrained climatic conditions. While phenotypic plasticity did not differ between native [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   and invasive populations, the difference between populations in terms of total dry mass could [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   be related to differences in the climate of origin (precipitation in particular). Assessing the [SOURCE: Page_2]

47   importance of phenotypic changes between populations within invasive species is crucial to [SOURCE: Page_2]

48   identify the margins of their climatic distribution range and to highlight areas where [SOURCE: Page_2]

49   management efforts should be concentrated in order to limit its spread. [SOURCE: Page_2]

50   Keywords: invasion, alien plants, drought, climatic niche, establishment [SOURCE: Page_2]

52   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

53          Biological invasion is a major economic and environmental ecosystem threat and is [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   amplified by global change (Vitousek et al. 1996; Andersen et al. 2004). The invasion of islands [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   by plant species is currently a serious threat to endemic species (Caujapé-Castells et al. 2010). [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   The assumption that climatic requirements of invasive species are conserved between their [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   native and invaded range is key in assessing and quantifying invasive risk (Pearman et al. 2008). [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   However, this hypothesis is not fully supported. Even though a review concluded that ~85% of [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   terrestrial invasive plants would not be able to shift their climatic niche (Petitpierre et al. 2012), [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   evidence of climatic niche shifts has been shown for specific invasive plants (Broennimann et [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   al. 2007; Gallagher et al. 2010; Christina et al. 2020). In particular, Webber et al. (2012) [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   suggested that niche conservation for invasive species would be the exception rather than the [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   rule. For the efficient allocation of management resources, we need to improve our [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   characterization of exotic species’ ecological and climatic niches both in their native and [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   invasive environments, so we can understand which factors limit or even prevent the [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   establishment and spread of these species (Leung et al. 2002). [SOURCE: Page_3]

67          Understanding how genetic variation in invasive plants influence climatic niche shifts [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   is becoming crucial under rapid climate change (Lavergne and Molofsky 2007). Climatic niche [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   shifts could occur when change in plant fitness differs between native and invasive individuals [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   or populations from the same species which could result from phenotypic divergences in plant [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   traits influencing survival and reproduction (Violle et al. 2007). In such case, it suggests that [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   invasive populations are adapted to a wider climatic environmental range than native ones, and [SOURCE: Page_3]

73   thus better fit to colonize larger areas. Even though local adaptation of populations is not [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   observed in all invasive species (Li et al. 2015; VanWallendael et al. 2018), invasive species [SOURCE: Page_3]

75   often present rapid evolution (Buswell et al. 2011) and a high genetic variation (Lavergne and [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

76   Molofsky 2007), thus enabling rapid local adaptation to new ecosystems (Colautti and Barrett [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   2013). Additionally, for those introduced species that spread across a wide distributional range, [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   phenotypic plasticity has been proposed as an important contributor to invasive success [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   (Davidson et al. 2011). In particular, the phenotypic plasticity could improve survival rates [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   during the initial establishment in a new environment (Ghalambor et al. 2007). However, there [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   is still debate on the relative contribution of phenotypic plasticity and local genetic variation [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   (i.e. local adaptation) to the ability of widespread invasive plants to colonize new environments [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   (Liao et al. 2016). [SOURCE: Page_4]

84          A new plant species introduced outside its native range has to cross several barriers [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   before becoming an invader (Richardson et al. 2000; Blackburn et al. 2011). An initial stage is [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   the establishment (survival) of young seedlings, which is strongly influenced by abiotic [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   conditions. Many findings pointed out that seedling establishment is restricted by climatic [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   conditions such as temperature, water availability or light (Danner and Knapp 2003; Kellman [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   2004; Hou et al. 2014; Leiblein-Wild et al. 2014; Nguyen et al. 2016), particularly for high- [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   elevation ecosystems (Arévalo et al. 2010; Tecco et al. 2016). Yet, few studies have dealt with [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   the influence of abiotic factors on seedling establishment at the margin of its climatic niche [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   (e.g. Kellman 2004; Laube et al. 2015), which is crucial to understand the mechanisms of niche [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   changes. [SOURCE: Page_4]

94          In this study, we tested the tolerance of an important invasive shrub (common gorse, [SOURCE: Page_4]

95   Ulex europaeus L.) to two imposed abiotic factors (temperature and water availability) at the [SOURCE: Page_4]

96   margin of its native climatic niche. Gorse is a particularly interesting model species as it is [SOURCE: Page_4]

97   among the 32 most invasive land plant species worldwide (IUCN) at latitudes ranging from [SOURCE: Page_4]

98   54°S to 60°N and at altitudes ranging from sea level to 3,550 m a.s.l. (Hornoy et al. 2013). Its [SOURCE: Page_4]

99   world-scale distribution suggests a potentially broad niche with respect to climatic variables [SOURCE: Page_4]

100   (particularly temperature) with a compensatory phenomenon with soil water content and [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

101   atmospheric humidity (e.g. gorse is only present at high altitude for lower latitudes). Recent [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   modeling studies suggested that the climatic niche of introduced gorse populations changed in [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   comparison to native populations, allowing invasive populations to invade warmer and drier [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   environments in North America, South America and Australia (Hernández-Lambraño et al. [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   2016; Christina et al. 2020). Our first objective was to assess the climatic niche margins of [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   gorse in terms of temperature and water availability as a complement to its used (realized) niche [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   assessed in previous studies. Our second objective was to assess the potential phenotypic [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   differences between native and invasive seedlings traits that could explain this climatic niche [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   shift. The hypotheses were that: i) gorse seedling fitness is reduced in warm and dry [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   environments, ii) invasive gorse seedlings are more performant in warm and dry environments [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   than in native ones and iii) the differences in performance among gorse seedlings can be [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   explained by intra-specific trait variability. [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   Assessing the temperature niche of Ulex europaeus in studied countries [SOURCE: Page_5]

117          Ulex europaeus, L. (gorse) native populations from France and Spain and invasive [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   populations from Canary Islands, Reunion Island and New Zealand were used in this study [SOURCE: Page_5]

119   (Table 1). Prior to the experiment, the climatic niche of gorse was assessed in each country. [SOURCE: Page_5]

120   Localization of gorse populations was performed in a previous study using various field [SOURCE: Page_5]

121   reconnaissance and literature queries (Christina et al. 2020). In total, 2627, 1068, 5, 1005 and [SOURCE: Page_5]

122   457 gorse populations were located in France, Spain, Canary Islands, Reunion Island and New [SOURCE: Page_5]

123   Zealand, respectively. The data for these populations were then transformed to a 5 arc min (~10 [SOURCE: Page_5]

124   km) grid of gorse presence and matched with WordClim bioclimatic variables (5 arc min, [SOURCE: Page_5]

125   worldclim.org, version 1.4, Hijmans et al. 2005). We used the WorldClim bioclimatic variables [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

126   to define two temperature ranges, one ambient temperature corresponding to an average [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   temperature where gorse was present and one elevated temperature corresponding to a [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   temperature at the margin of the temperature tolerance of gorse. Climatic data were presented [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   using a beanplot (Kampstra 2008) based on a normal density function. [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   Studied populations [SOURCE: Page_6]

132          Seeds were sampled from 20 populations from five countries and altitudes ranging from [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   sea level to 2000 m asl (Table 1). Eleven populations were considered as natives (seven from [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   continental France and four from Spain) and nine populations sampled in non-native [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   ecosystems (five from Reunion Island, three from New Zealand and one from Canary Island). [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   In each population, 20 different individuals were selected and 50 seed pods per individuals were [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   sampled during the reproductive period. Only mature brown pods were collected. The seeds [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   were stored in the pods at 4ºC until required. The seeds were carefully examined to exclude any [SOURCE: Page_6]

139   seeds that had been attacked by insects. Climatic data at each location were obtained from [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   WorldClim bioclimatic variables (2.5 arc min, worldclim.org, version 1.4, Hijmans et al. 2005). [SOURCE: Page_6]

142   Seed germination [SOURCE: Page_6]

143          All equipment was sterilized in an autoclave and sterile water was used. To further [SOURCE: Page_6]

144   minimize the risk of infestation by fungi and bacteria, seed samples were washed in 2.5% [SOURCE: Page_6]

145   sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) for 10 min and then rinsed during 5 min in sterile water. Seeds [SOURCE: Page_6]

146   were then scarified for 3h using sulfuric acid concentrated at 96%, where two volumes of acid [SOURCE: Page_6]

147   to one volume of seeds were used (Sixtus et al. 2003). Note that there is a high risk of creating [SOURCE: Page_6]

148   chlorine gas due to contact between NaOCl and sulfuric acid. After rinsing the seeds in sterile [SOURCE: Page_6]

149   water, they were placed in petri dishes at 15°C until germination (~2 weeks). Petri dishes were [SOURCE: Page_6]

150   regularly humidified. [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

151      After germination, seeds were planted at 2 cm depth in individual pots (10 cm depth) and [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   grown in a greenhouse. Potting substrate was a typical acid sandy soil representative of southern [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   France where native gorse grows (dry moorland: 5.9 - 12.7 mg C g-1, 0.40 - 0.67 mg N g-1, [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   0.026 - 0.030 mg PTOT g-1, 0.13 - 0.19 mg OxAl g-1, 0.08 - 0.15 mg OxFe g-1). At the end of [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   the growing period, 220 homogeneous seedlings were chosen for the experiment and assigned [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   to the four treatments (76 from seven populations in France; 40 from five populations in Spain; [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   12 from one population in Canary Islands; 52 from six populations in Reunion Island and 40 [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   from five populations in New Zealand). In each country, seedlings were chosen in different [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   populations to take into account phenotypic variability within each country. We sampled a large [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   number of populations rather than individuals in each population to assess differences between [SOURCE: Page_7]

161   populations rather than within populations. The seedlings were pooled by population and range [SOURCE: Page_7]

162   (native vs. invasive) herein after referred to as either “native population” or “invasive [SOURCE: Page_7]

163   population”. [SOURCE: Page_7]

165   Temperature and watering treatments [SOURCE: Page_7]

166          Value of elevated temperature was defined as the highest temperature observed at the [SOURCE: Page_7]

167   niche margin in the different countries, and throughout the experiment, the difference in [SOURCE: Page_7]

168   temperature between the elevated (ET) and ambient (AT) treatments was set at +6°C. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

169   maximum daily temperature (TMAX) and the average daily temperature (TMEAN) were defined [SOURCE: Page_7]

170   based on the WorldClim maximum daily temperature of the warmest month and the daily mean [SOURCE: Page_7]

171   temperature of the driest quarter, respectively (Fig. 1). Seedlings were grown in two [SOURCE: Page_7]

172   environmental growth chambers (INRAE phytotron) each holding 110 pots : one AT chamber [SOURCE: Page_7]

173   characterized by TMAX = 24°C, TMEAN = 17°C, TNIGHT = 10°C; and one ET chamber with TMAX [SOURCE: Page_7]

174   = 30°C, TMEAN = 23°C, and TNIGHT = 16°C. In each chamber four periods of light levels and [SOURCE: Page_7]

175   temperatures were programmed to mimic mean summer day conditions: a night period of 12h [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

176   (TNIGHT and photosynthetically active radiation - PAR=0 µmol m-2 s-1), a morning and an [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   afternoon period of 3h each (TMEAN and PAR≈1000 µmol m-2 s-1), and a high temperature period [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   of 6h in the middle of the day (TMAX and PAR≈1000 µmol m-2 s-1). In each chamber, relative [SOURCE: Page_8]

179   humidity (RH) was adjusted to have similar non-limiting (low enough) vapour pressure deficit [SOURCE: Page_8]

180   (VPD) during the daytime. Consequently, RH in the ET chamber was set to 74% during the [SOURCE: Page_8]

181   night and 70% during the day, corresponding to VPDNIGHT = 0.41, VPDMEAN = 0.7 and VPDMAX [SOURCE: Page_8]

182   = 1.01 kPa periods. In the AT chamber, RH was set to 74% during the night and 60% during [SOURCE: Page_8]

183   the day, corresponding to VPDNIGHT = 0.29, VPDMEAN = 0.67 and VPDMAX = 0.98 kPa periods. [SOURCE: Page_8]

184      Additionally, two watering treatments were applied in each chamber: one where 20 mL of [SOURCE: Page_8]

185   water was added to each pot three times a week to maintain soil at full saturation (wet or well- [SOURCE: Page_8]

186   watered treatment), and a drought treatment where pots were not watered over the 36-days [SOURCE: Page_8]

187   period of the experiment (dry treatment). [SOURCE: Page_8]

189   Seedling growth and chamber acclimation [SOURCE: Page_8]

190      As described above, four treatments were applied using two controlled environment [SOURCE: Page_8]

191   chambers (AT, wet and dry in chamber #1; ET, wet and dry in chamber #2). In this experimental [SOURCE: Page_8]

192   design, due to a limited number of available chamber space, the temperature treatment was [SOURCE: Page_8]

193   nested within a “growth chamber treatment”. Nevertheless, as other climatic factors (PAR, [SOURCE: Page_8]

194   VPD) were controlled and similar between chambers, we assume that this design was adequate [SOURCE: Page_8]

195   to test the temperature effect. At the start of the experiment, both chambers were set up at [SOURCE: Page_8]

196   ambient temperature and all seedlings were watered. During the first ten days, temperature of [SOURCE: Page_8]

197   the chamber #2 increased gradually to reach the desired ET treatment. At the end of this [SOURCE: Page_8]

198   temperature/watering acclimation period (i.e. after ten days), and thus at the onset of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

199   experiment, seedling heights were similar (around 19 mm; p value = 0.59) among all native and [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

200   invasive populations (Fig. S$_1$). Watering treatments started after this acclimation period and [SOURCE: Page_9]

201   were maintained for 36 days. [SOURCE: Page_9]

203   Measurements [SOURCE: Page_9]

204      At the end of the 36-days period, plant traits were measured at the individual seedling level: [SOURCE: Page_9]

205   total dry mass (TDM), height (H), leaf area (LA), leaf (LDM) and root dry mases (RDM), root [SOURCE: Page_9]

206   length (RL), area (RA), and diameter (RD), above to belowground dry mass ratio (ABRATIO), leaf [SOURCE: Page_9]

207   to aboveground dry mass ratio (LARATIO) and specific root length (SRL) and area (SRA). Plant [SOURCE: Page_9]

208   H was defined as the height up the apex. Note that H of a few individuals was lower after 36 [SOURCE: Page_9]

209   days in comparison to the end of the acclimation period due to desiccation. Considering plant [SOURCE: Page_9]

214   weighed to obtain the dry weights. Mortality rate was defined as the proportion of dead [SOURCE: Page_9]

215   seedlings at the end of the experiment in each population. [SOURCE: Page_9]

262   Gorse response to air temperature and watering level [SOURCE: Page_11]

263      Air temperature and watering levels influenced mortality rate (χ2=30.9, df=3, p<0.001) and [SOURCE: Page_11]

267   was not influenced by air temperature but was lower by 17% in the dry treatment in comparison [SOURCE: Page_11]

268   to the wet one (Table 3). Similarly, LDM, RDM, RL, RA, ABRATIO and SRA were statistically [SOURCE: Page_11]

269   influenced by watering level but not by air temperature. Reduction in the dry treatment varied [SOURCE: Page_11]

271   air temperature and watering level (Table 2). The tallest seedlings were found in the ET x Wet [SOURCE: Page_11]

272   treatment with H = 32 mm, while H was 18 mm on average in the AT treatment for both [SOURCE: Page_11]

273   watering levels. The dry treatment induced a reduction in H only when combined with ET. [SOURCE: Page_11]

336   Discussion [SOURCE: Page_14]

337   Establishment at the climatic niche margin [SOURCE: Page_14]

338      Assessing whether the environmental niche of a species may change between different [SOURCE: Page_14]

339   geographical areas or time periods is extremely important for predicting the spread of invasive [SOURCE: Page_14]

340   species in the context of ongoing climate change (Alexander and Edwards 2010; Guisan et al. [SOURCE: Page_14]

341   2014; González-Moreno et al. 2015). While global temperatures rise (Walther et al. 2009), [SOURCE: Page_14]

342   extreme temperature events can reduce invasive plant competitiveness if conditions become [SOURCE: Page_14]

343   unsuitable (Hellmann et al. 2008). Our study suggested that temperature tolerance at the thermal [SOURCE: Page_14]

344   niche margin may change between native and invasive gorse populations. Furthermore, our [SOURCE: Page_14]

345   results also showed that elevated temperature in interaction with water availability can [SOURCE: Page_14]

346   potentially facilitate the survival and reproduction of invasive gorse in regions outside its native [SOURCE: Page_14]

347   range. In particular, the mortality rate of gorse under combined elevated temperature and [SOURCE: Page_14]

348   drought was higher for native seedlings than invasive seedlings (except for seedlings from New [SOURCE: Page_14]

14 [SOURCE: Page_14]

349   Zealand that featured a climate similar to that in the native areas; Table S$_1$). A similar pattern [SOURCE: Page_15]

350   was observed in a greenhouse experiment with Ulmus pumila seedlings (Hirsch et al. 2016), [SOURCE: Page_15]

351   where mortality rate of seedlings from native populations from China was greater than those [SOURCE: Page_15]

352   from non-native populations from Argentina and the U.S.A. under various watering and [SOURCE: Page_15]

353   temperature conditions. In its native area, gorse is an oceanic species, which makes water [SOURCE: Page_15]

354   availability an important ecological factor shaping its niche (Delerue et al. 2015). [SOURCE: Page_15]

355   Consequently, change in gorse seedlings’ thermal and drought tolerance between invasive and [SOURCE: Page_15]

356   native populations may be factors explaining the climatic niche shift observed at the global [SOURCE: Page_15]

357   scale by (Christina et al. 2020) and the niche expansion of this species in South America [SOURCE: Page_15]

358   (Hernández-Lambraño et al. 2016). [SOURCE: Page_15]

359      Under Global Change, many studies support that rising temperatures may increase the risk [SOURCE: Page_15]

360   of invasion (Walther et al. 2009; Bradley et al. 2010; Verlinden and Nijs 2010; Wang et al. [SOURCE: Page_15]

361   2011). Nevertheless, this hypothesis has to be nuanced (Hellmann et al. 2008). For example, in [SOURCE: Page_15]

362   a meta-analysis, (Sorte et al. 2013) showed that native and non-native plant species largely [SOURCE: Page_15]

363   respond similarly to climate manipulations (temperature and precipitation) in terms of survival, [SOURCE: Page_15]

364   growth or fecundity, and that, rather than simply rising temperatures, the major threat for [SOURCE: Page_15]

365   ecosystem invasiveness comes from increasing extreme events. [SOURCE: Page_15]

366 [SOURCE: Page_15]

367   Traits and seedling performance [SOURCE: Page_15]

368      For some plant species, this increase in vegetative growth and/or reproductive effort would [SOURCE: Page_15]

369   result in a competitive advantage of the invasive species in the introduced ranges (Zou et al. [SOURCE: Page_15]

370   2007; Ordonez et al. 2010; Heberling et al. 2016). In terms of vegetative growth, our study [SOURCE: Page_15]

371   showed an increase in height and leaf area for invasive gorse populations as compared to native [SOURCE: Page_15]

372   populations in the early phases of the invasive process (seedling establishment) under warm [SOURCE: Page_15]

373   conditions. This observation is in accordance with previous studies showing an increase in [SOURCE: Page_15]

15 [SOURCE: Page_15]

374   vegetative growth (Hornoy et al. 2011) and reproductive output (Udo et al. 2017; Bakker et al. [SOURCE: Page_16]

375   2019) in introduced Ulex europaeus populations compared to native ones under non-limiting [SOURCE: Page_16]

376   conditions. [SOURCE: Page_16]

377      The better performance of gorse seedlings from invasive populations in extreme [SOURCE: Page_16]

378   environments may result from change in individual traits compared to native populations. In [SOURCE: Page_16]

379   our study, above to belowground ratio in invasive population differed depending on watering [SOURCE: Page_16]

380   level while it remained constant in individuals from native populations. Such differences in [SOURCE: Page_16]

381   biomass allocation could explain the increase in drought resistance of invasive compared to [SOURCE: Page_16]

382   native populations by allowing invasive seedlings to maintain a constant root area and length [SOURCE: Page_16]

383   even in dry conditions, while it decreased in native seedlings. (Song et al. 2010) found that [SOURCE: Page_16]

384   invasive Wedelia (Sphagneticola sp. Asteraceae) seedlings suffered less inhibition in terms of [SOURCE: Page_16]

385   growth rates due to high temperature than the native Wedelia (native to Central America), which [SOURCE: Page_16]

386   was explained by a change in photosystem II activity and efficiency, as well as by higher net [SOURCE: Page_16]

387   photosynthetic rate between native and invasive seedlings. Changes in traits that influence plant [SOURCE: Page_16]

388   fitness under extreme conditions could explain the climatic niche shift towards warmer and [SOURCE: Page_16]

389   drier environments observed for gorse (Hernández-Lambraño et al. 2016; Christina et al. 2020). [SOURCE: Page_16]

390 [SOURCE: Page_16]

391   Climate of origin and phenotypic plasticity [SOURCE: Page_16]

392      Invasive species often present rapid evolution (Buswell et al. 2011) and a high genetic [SOURCE: Page_16]

393   variation (Lavergne and Molofsky 2007) which made many authors to suggest that rapid local [SOURCE: Page_16]

394   adaptation to new ecosystems (Colautti and Barrett 2013) or phenotypic plasticity are key [SOURCE: Page_16]

395   processes in the invasion success (Davidson et al. 2011). In the current study, we have found [SOURCE: Page_16]

396   no difference in phenotypic plasticity at the population level between native and invasive [SOURCE: Page_16]

397   populations for gorse seedlings when exposed to different temperature and water availability [SOURCE: Page_16]

398   conditions. On the contrary, precipitation regime of climate of origin did differ between native [SOURCE: Page_16]

16 [SOURCE: Page_16]

399   and invasive populations in our study (Fig. 5a). Even though local adaptation of populations is [SOURCE: Page_17]

400   not observed in all invasive species (Li et al. 2015; VanWallendael et al. 2018) and that some [SOURCE: Page_17]

401   authors suggested that invasive species are not better adapted to local conditions than native [SOURCE: Page_17]

402   ones (Oduor et al. 2016), our study showed a correlation between the dry mass of gorse [SOURCE: Page_17]

403   populations and precipitations in the climate of origin. This result suggested a relation between [SOURCE: Page_17]

404   the gorse population environment of origin and its growth, which could explain differences in [SOURCE: Page_17]

405   adaptation between native and invasive gorse populations. [SOURCE: Page_17]

406      The evolution of phenotypic traits in invasive gorse populations has been demonstrated in [SOURCE: Page_17]

407   common garden experiments, and has been linked to the hexaploid karyotype of the species, [SOURCE: Page_17]

408   and by its high genetic polymorphism in both native and invasive populations (Hornoy et al. [SOURCE: Page_17]

409   2011, 2013). Considering that in the majority of introduced regions, invasion occurred since [SOURCE: Page_17]

410   the 1800s (Hornoy 2012; Udo 2016), an explanation to the rapid evolution of local adaptation [SOURCE: Page_17]

411   could result from the release of natural enemies as suggested by the evolution of increased [SOURCE: Page_17]

412   competitive ability hypothesis (Blossey and Nötzold 1995; Joshi and Vrieling 2005). Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_17]

413   the specific weevil Exapion ulicis, that can eat up to 80% of the gorse seeds in their native [SOURCE: Page_17]

414   regions, was not introduced in the invaded regions as the same time as the plant (even though [SOURCE: Page_17]

415   it has been introduced in most regions for biological control since then). The absence of this [SOURCE: Page_17]

416   predator may have relaxed the genetic constraints resulting from the complex strategies of seed [SOURCE: Page_17]

417   predation avoidance (Atlan et al. 2010), and thereby may have facilitated the rapid adaptation [SOURCE: Page_17]

418   to new climate and contributed to the niche expansion in introduced regions. [SOURCE: Page_17]

419 [SOURCE: Page_17]

420   Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_17]

421      The study assessed the influence of the interaction between elevated temperature (at the [SOURCE: Page_17]

422   niche margin) and drought on establishment success (seedling survival and growth allocation) [SOURCE: Page_17]

423   of the invasive gorse. Our results highlight changes in seedling phenotypes related to above and [SOURCE: Page_17]

17 [SOURCE: Page_17]

424   belowground development between native and invasive gorse populations, which could explain [SOURCE: Page_18]

425   the increase in drought resistance of invasive populations at extreme temperatures. A common [SOURCE: Page_18]

426   garden approach set up by our research group, and currently taking place in both native and [SOURCE: Page_18]

427   invasive environments along an altitudinal gradient, will allow the completion of observations [SOURCE: Page_18]

428   in a natural environment and validate the role of phenotypic divergences into gorse expansion [SOURCE: Page_18]

429   towards warm and dry areas around the world. [SOURCE: Page_18]
