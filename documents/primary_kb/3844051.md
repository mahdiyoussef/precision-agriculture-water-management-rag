5   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

6   Forest dieback is a high risk factor for the sustainability of these ecosystems in the climate [SOURCE: Page_1]

7   change context. Productivity losses and increased defoliation and mortality rates have [SOURCE: Page_1]

8   already been recorded for many tree species worldwide. However, dieback is a process [SOURCE: Page_1]

9   that depends on complex interactions between many biotic and environmental factors [SOURCE: Page_1]

10   acting at different scales, and is thus difficult to address and predict. [SOURCE: Page_1]

11   Our aim was to build tree- and stand-level foliar deficit models integrating biotic and abiotic [SOURCE: Page_1]

12   factors for Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris), a species particularly threatened in Europe, and [SOURCE: Page_1]

13   especially in the southeastern part of France. To this end, we quantified foliar deficit in 1740 [SOURCE: Page_1]

14   trees from 87 plots distributed along an environmental gradient. We also measured tree [SOURCE: Page_1]

15   annual radial growth and the abundance of two parasites: the pine processionary moth [SOURCE: Page_1]

16   (Thaumetopoea pityocampa Den. & Schiff.) and mistletoe (Viscum album L.). Topographic, [SOURCE: Page_1]

17   soil, climate and water balance indices were assessed for each plot, together with the stand [SOURCE: Page_1]

18   dendrometric characteristics. Given the large number of environmental factors and the [SOURCE: Page_1]

19   strong correlations between many of them, models were developed using a partial least [SOURCE: Page_1]

20   squares (PLS) regression approach. [SOURCE: Page_1]

21   All the models pointed to a preponderance of the biotic factors (processionary moth and [SOURCE: Page_1]

22   mistletoe) in explaining the intensity of foliar deficit at both tree- and stand- levels. We also [SOURCE: Page_1]

23   show that strong interactions between climate, soil, water balance and biotic factors help to [SOURCE: Page_1]

24   explain the intensity of dieback. Dieback was thus greater in the driest topoedaphic and [SOURCE: Page_1]

25   climatic conditions where the mistletoe and processionary moth were present. [SOURCE: Page_1]

38   1.     Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   Ever since the beginning of the industrial era in the 19th century, human activities have [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   been associated with a significant rise in global temperatures, forecast to continue in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   range of +1°C to +5.7 °C by 2100 according to climate scenarios (relative to the period [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   1850-1900 ; Masson-Delmotte et al., 2021). This gradual temperature rise has been [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   accompanied by an increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events and [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   summer droughts in some regions, such as the Mediterranean (Cramer et al., 2018). These [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   climate changes intensify the physiological stress undergone by forest ecosystems, leading [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   to short- and long-term modifications in their carbon and water economies (McDowell et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   2008), and in their structure and composition (Anderegg et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_3]

48 [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   One sign of these dysfunctions at both tree and stand levels is dieback, defined as [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   weakened vitality of a tree relative to a healthy reference tree, or weakened vitality of a [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   large number of trees in a stand (Schütt and Cowling, 1985). Visible clues of dieback [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   include reduced radial growth (Helama et al. 2014; Camarero et al. 2018), changes in [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   crown architecture, branch and root death, fewer or smaller seeds (Girard et al., 2012; [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   Vennetier et al., 2013), and most often a leaf area deficit (Becker et al., 1990; Carnicer et [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   al., 2011; Dobbertin and Brang, 2001). Dieback may degenerate in tree death in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   absence of recovery (Bréda et al., 2006). Besides these visible clues, various physiological [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   and anatomic signs can be reported, including changes in carbon reserves (Adams et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   2013), lower efficiency in the use of resources (Gessler et al., 2018), xylem embolism [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   (Choat et al., 2012), or nutrient imbalance (Gessler et al., 2017). Mechanistically, dieback is [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   described as a complex system in which various biotic and abiotic factors interact in chains [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   (Franklin et al., 1987; Waring, 1987). Commonly cited biotic factors include fungi, insects, [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   bacteria, plant parasites, and inter-tree competition. Abiotic factors are related to climate, [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   topography, soil, and sometimes air pollution. Dieback processes are often triggered by [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   weather events, in particular severe droughts (Bigler et al., 2006; Suarez et al., 2004; Das [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   et al., 2007; Pedersen, 1998; Gaylord et al., 2015), which are increasingly associated with [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   heatwaves (Teskey et al., 2015; Vogel et al., 2021). These factors can be compounded with [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   spells of deep frost, either early or late, but also with biotic agent outbreaks (e.g., Morcillo et [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_4]

69 [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   Forest dieback affects many biomes and species worldwide (e.g., van Mantgem et al. 2009; [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   Allen et al., 2010; Hartmann et al., 2018; Hartmann et al. 2022), including Scots pine (Pinus [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   sylvestris), a main European tree species that covers more than 20 million hectares from [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   Scandinavia to southern Spain (Gonthier et al. 2011; Houston Durrant et al. 2016). It is [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   particularly impacted in the southern part of its distribution range, in the Pyrenees and the [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   Alps, but also in central Europe. These dieback events are often attributed to drought [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   (e.g., Bigler et al. 2006; Bose et al. 2020; Buras et al. 2018; Camarero et al. 2015; Etzold et [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   al. 2019; Martínez-Vilalta et al. 2009), but it also involves biotic factors such as the pine [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   processionary moth (Thaumetopoea pityocampa Den. & Schiff.) (Hódar et al., 2003), and [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   mistletoe (Viscum album L.) a hemiparasitic plant that causes gradual weakening of [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   infested trees (Dobbertin et al. 2005; Galiano et al. 2010; Rigling et al. 2010 ; Kollas et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   2018). [SOURCE: Page_4]

82 [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   Determining the potential interactions between stand attributes, climate, processionary [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   moth and mistletoe in the dieback process is key with regard to climate change. When [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   water and nutrients availability are not limited, pine mistletoes can co-exist with their host [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   tree for years (Solomon et al., 2015; Zuber, 2004). However, during drought, mistletoes [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   maintain high transpiration rates (Zweifel et al., 2012), which may exacerbate the stress [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   experienced by the host pine, with negative consequences on the growth performance and [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   on foliar deficit (Dobbertin and Rigling, 2006; Rigling et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   Considering that epidemic phases of the processionary moth can also cause strong pine [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   defoliation, and that both biotic factors are favoured by sunny and warm positions, and thus [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   potentially by transparent canopies, synergies between these factors, drought, and the [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   microenvironment are likely (e.g,. Gea-Izquierdo et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   Both biotic factors are favored by higher temperature, and upward or northward shifts have [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   been reported in several areas (e.g., Dobbertin et al. 2005; Robinet et al. 2007 and 2014). [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   However, quantification of this process requires long-term data series, which are rarely [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   available and may show a different trend (e.g., Camarero et al., 2022 in the case of [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   processionary moth). [SOURCE: Page_5]

99 [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   In the last 20 years, many numeric models have been developed to predict the endpoint of [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   dieback, namely tree death. On the one side, physiological models explicitly simulate the [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   carbon and hydraulic mechanisms involved in tree death (e.g., McDowell et al., 2013; [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   Cochard, 2020; Davi and Cailleret, 2017). On the other side, phenomenological models [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   predict mortality risk using statistical approaches based on local abiotic environmental data [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   (soil, climate, topography) and dendrometric data (type of stand, density, tree size, etc.) [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   without considering the physical and physiological mechanisms behind tree dieback (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   Taccoen et al., 2019). Along       this continuum are models that couple empirical and [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   mechanistic approaches (e.g., Venturas et al. 2021), and others that use integrative [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   indicators of tree vitality, such as radial growth (e.g., Bigler and Bugmann, 2004; Holzwarth [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   et al., 2013; Hülsmann et al., 2018). Physiological models are considered as more accurate [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   in the context of a changing environment, but they require a large amount and diversity of [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   high-resolution data, and are not yet fully functional for many species and over large areas. [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   Phenomenological models are more parsimonious and robust within their calibration range, [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   but less reliable outside it. Despite the increasing number, diversity and complexity of [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   models that predict whether a tree will live or die, prediction of mortality at a regional scale [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   is still challenging (Trugman et al., 2021), and few models simulate a tree’s level of dieback, [SOURCE: Page_5]

117   owing to lack of data and because dieback displays a wide range of signs. These dieback [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   models, for example those that predict foliar deficit (e.g., Galiano et al., 2010; Gonthier et [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

119   al., 2011), cannot be used directly for forestry diagnostics as they consider only a few [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   factors influencing tree health, either directly or indirectly (through their interactions), and [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   often neglect site factors (e.g., soil and topography). However, in view of the current [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   increase in forest dieback at both local and global scales, operational tools able to predict [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   this risk are urgently needed. [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   The aim of this study was to partly fill this research gap by developing phenomenological [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   models to predict foliar deficit of Scots pine. We used a partial least squares (PLS) [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   regression approach to (1) assess the impact of multiple environmental factors (climate, [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   soil, topography) and of the spatial distribution of pine populations at landscape scale on [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   the abundance of biotic agents : pine processionary moth and mistletoe; (2) disentangle the [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   respective and interactive impacts of these abiotic and biotic drivers on foliar deficit on both [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   tree and stand scales; and (3) develop a model that could be used by forest managers to [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   predict the risk of stand dieback. With this multifactorial and multiscale approach, we aimed [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   at improving our understanding of the dieback process on Scots pine and the [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   methodologies used to predict dieback risk. [SOURCE: Page_6]

262   at a 50 m resolution using data from 88 weather stations, taking into account elevation, [SOURCE: Page_11]

263   latitude and topography (Piedallu and Gégout, 2007). Monthly potential evapotranspiration [SOURCE: Page_12]

284   2.5 Soil and topographic data and indices [SOURCE: Page_12]

285   Local topoedaphic index [SOURCE: Page_12]

286   For each plot, a detailed survey of bedrock, soil and local topography was carried out in the [SOURCE: Page_12]

287   field to calculate a topoedaphic index (TEI) developed and validated over the studied area [SOURCE: Page_12]

288   by Vennetier et al. (2018, for details see Appendix D). This index appraises the local water [SOURCE: Page_12]

12 [SOURCE: Page_12]

289   balance on the plot scale and is correlated with forest productivity. Depending on its value, [SOURCE: Page_13]

290   which ranges from +80 to −80, the water balance is considered as highly favorable (>15), [SOURCE: Page_13]

291   favorable (5 to 15), neutral (−5 to +5), unfavorable (−5 to −15), or highly unfavorable [SOURCE: Page_13]

292   (<−15). [SOURCE: Page_13]

293 [SOURCE: Page_13]

294   Soil characteristics were estimated directly from a hand-dug trial pit at the center of each [SOURCE: Page_13]

295   plot and from several core drillings throughout the plot. The useful water reservoir was [SOURCE: Page_13]

296   quantified using the protocol of Baize and Jabiol (2011), and calculated from the [SOURCE: Page_13]

297   pedotransfer functions developed by Jamagne et al. (1977). The nature of the underlying [SOURCE: Page_13]

298   bedrock was surveyed in the field whenever possible, or else using geological maps. The [SOURCE: Page_13]

299   presence of active limestone was estimated in the top 50 cm using 10% hydrochloric acid. [SOURCE: Page_13]

300   The pH was measured at a depth of 20 cm using a ©SoilStik pH meter. The slope and [SOURCE: Page_13]

301   aspect were measured using a clinometer and a compass. [SOURCE: Page_13]

302 [SOURCE: Page_13]

303 [SOURCE: Page_13]

304   Topographic indices at landscape scale [SOURCE: Page_13]

305   Three landscape topographic indices were calculated using two DEMs from the French [SOURCE: Page_13]

306   National Institute of Geographic and Forest Information available with a respective precision [SOURCE: Page_13]

307   of 25 m and 75 m (BD_ALTI © IGN). The topographic position index (TPI) was calculated in [SOURCE: Page_13]

308   a 100 m radius for microtopography (TPI_100, already used for the sampling stratification), [SOURCE: Page_13]

309   and a 1500 m radius (TPI_1500) for macrotopography (Weiss, 2001), with DEMs at 25 m [SOURCE: Page_13]

310   and 75 m resolution respectively, each adapted to a working scale. Plots located at the top [SOURCE: Page_13]

311   of the slope show high TPI values while plots located at the bottom of the valley have low [SOURCE: Page_13]

312   TPI. The topographic wetness index (TWI) is an estimate of the water accumulation in a [SOURCE: Page_13]

313   defined area. It was calculated with DEMs at 75 m. TWI is defined as the ratio of the area [SOURCE: Page_13]

314   upslope (i.e., from where water would flow) from a given point on the landscape to the local [SOURCE: Page_13]

315   slope at that point (Galiano et al., 2010; Petroselli et al., 2013). A high TWI corresponds to [SOURCE: Page_13]

316   favorable situations where water inputs are higher than the losses. These three indices [SOURCE: Page_13]

13 [SOURCE: Page_13]

317       illustrate the part of the water balance linked to water flows in the landscape, on a larger [SOURCE: Page_14]

318       scale than the plot (i.e., than the TEI). [SOURCE: Page_14]

319       2.6 Statistical approach [SOURCE: Page_14]

320       We built two indices quantifying the intensity of colonization by mistletoe and pine [SOURCE: Page_14]

321       processionary moth, and developed three dieback models that related tree and stand health [SOURCE: Page_14]

322       status with all the biotic and abiotic variables mentioned above (Table 1). [SOURCE: Page_14]

323       Target variables [SOURCE: Page_14]

324       To quantify the intensity of colonization by biotic factors, we focused on the percentage of [SOURCE: Page_14]

325       crown colonized by mistletoe (CrownVa), and the number of nests of processionary moth [SOURCE: Page_14]

326       per tree (NbnestTp). [SOURCE: Page_14]

327       For the first dieback model, the percentage of foliar deficit (FolDef) was analyzed on tree [SOURCE: Page_14]

328       scale, while the second model used the percentage of stems with at least 50% of leaf loss [SOURCE: Page_14]

329       (Xstem^50) estimated on stand scale. Xstem^50 was used instead of the mean foliar deficit [SOURCE: Page_14]

330       as both variables are closely correlated (r = 0.95, p < 0.001), and Xstem^50 is often used [SOURCE: Page_14]

331       operationally by forest managers. Indeed, tree dieback is conventionally defined by a foliar [SOURCE: Page_14]

332       deficit greater than or equal to 50% (DGAL 2018; Linares et Camarero 2012; Sergent [SOURCE: Page_14]

333       2011). The third model was developed to predict the probability that a stand experiences a [SOURCE: Page_14]

334       dieback, i.e., that its Xstem^50 value is greater than 30%. This risk model was designed for [SOURCE: Page_14]

335       direct use in the field by forest managers. Different threshold values of Xstem^50 (20, 25 or [SOURCE: Page_14]

336       30%) are cited in the literature to define dieback in a stand (Brunier et al., 2020; DGAL, [SOURCE: Page_14]

337       2018; Linares and Camarero, 2012), but preliminary analyses enabled us to retain the 30% [SOURCE: Page_14]

338       threshold (Appendix E). [SOURCE: Page_14]

342   Summary statistics and model development [SOURCE: Page_15]

343   A three-way ANOVA was used to determine the pertinence of the stratification variables [SOURCE: Page_15]

344   used for the sampling design: elevation (≥1200 m, <1200 m), IKR (cold, neutral, hot) and [SOURCE: Page_15]

345   TEI (favorable, neutral, unfavorable) on the dieback variables FolDef and Xstem^50. ANOVA [SOURCE: Page_15]

346   validity assumptions were statistically examined, i.e., the normality of residuals with the [SOURCE: Page_15]

347   Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variance with the Levene test. Two-by-two [SOURCE: Page_15]

348   comparisons of means were carried out with the Tukey test, except when the variables did [SOURCE: Page_15]

349   not meet ANOVA validity criteria, in which case the Kruskall-Wallis and Nemenyi tests were [SOURCE: Page_15]

350   used. [SOURCE: Page_15]

351 [SOURCE: Page_15]

352   The two indices of potential colonization by biotic agents and the three dieback models [SOURCE: Page_15]

353   were developed using partial least squares (PLS) regression (Ter-Braak and Juggins, [SOURCE: Page_15]

354   1993). [SOURCE: Page_15]

15 [SOURCE: Page_15]

355   The PLS approach is known to well define complex interacting systems (Fernandes, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_16]

356   This regression method predicts the values taken by the target variables from a series of [SOURCE: Page_16]

357   predictor variables using a multivariate approach. This approach sidesteps two difficulties [SOURCE: Page_16]

358   often met in ecology, namely the collinearity of explanatory variables, and the high number [SOURCE: Page_16]

359   of these variables relative to the number of observations (Cramer III et al., 1988; [SOURCE: Page_16]

360   Tenenhaus, 1998). The method has proved to be effective in forestry : i.e., to study forest [SOURCE: Page_16]

361   productivity (Bikindou et al., 2012; Paulo et al., 2015; Rathgeber et al., 2005), stand health [SOURCE: Page_16]

362   (Tenenhaus, 1998), and flora composition based on a bioclimatic model (Vennetier et al. [SOURCE: Page_16]

363   2008). We chose the PLS approach because the number of variables used to build the [SOURCE: Page_16]

364   models was large (99 in total, see Appendix A) and exceeded the number of plots. Also, [SOURCE: Page_16]

365   some climatic and topographic variables based on digital elevation models are highly [SOURCE: Page_16]

366   correlated. For instance, The AURELHY and DIGITALIS models use topography [SOURCE: Page_16]

367   information to derive high-resolution climatic maps (Bénichou and Lebreton, 1987; Piedallu [SOURCE: Page_16]

368   and Gégout, 2007). A stepwise method based on the Q$_2$ coefficient of Stone-Geisser was [SOURCE: Page_16]

369   used to select the number of components in the PLS regression (Bertrand, F., Maumy- [SOURCE: Page_16]

370   Bertrand, M., 2018; Bastien, P., Vinzi, V.E., Tenenhaus, M., 2005). Only the variables [SOURCE: Page_16]

371   whose partial correlation coefficient was significantly different from zero (p < 0.05) after [SOURCE: Page_16]

372   bootstrapping were then retained. To model the probability of the presence or absence of [SOURCE: Page_16]

373   dieback in a plot, we used a PLS binary logistic regression approach (LOG PLS); the [SOURCE: Page_16]

374   logistic regression being regularly used to predict tree mortality risk (e.g., Cailleret et al., [SOURCE: Page_16]

375   2016; Hülsmann et al., 2018; Monserud, 1976). The LOG PLS combines a PLS regression [SOURCE: Page_16]

376   model with the binary prediction model of logistic regression. The PLS regressions were run [SOURCE: Page_16]

377   with the plsRglm package (Partial Least Squares Regression for Generalized Linear [SOURCE: Page_16]

378   Models; Bastien et al., 2005; Bertrand and Maumy-Bertrand, 2018) from the R 4.0.5 open- [SOURCE: Page_16]

379   source software (RStudio Team, 2021). [SOURCE: Page_16]

380 [SOURCE: Page_16]

381   Finally, we partitioned the variance of the stand dieback models to assess the relative [SOURCE: Page_16]

382   weights of the biotic, climatic, and topoedaphic factors. Venn diagrams were drawn with the [SOURCE: Page_16]

16 [SOURCE: Page_16]

383   eulerr R package (Larsson, 2021) to evaluate the specific and combined weights of these [SOURCE: Page_17]

384   three groups of variables in the explained variance of Xstem^50 and of biotic potential [SOURCE: Page_17]

385   colonization indices. In such diagrams, the area of the ellipse plotted for each group is [SOURCE: Page_17]

386   proportional to the sum of its individual coefficient of determination and its joint contribution [SOURCE: Page_17]

387   with one or several other groups. The joint contributions are represented by the area of the [SOURCE: Page_17]

388   intersections of the individual ellipses. [SOURCE: Page_17]

527   4.     Discussion [SOURCE: Page_26]

528   Tree dieback is a complex process that requires a multifactorial and multiscale approach (Franklin [SOURCE: Page_26]

529   et al. 1987; Manion 1991; Allen et al. 2015). This study, based on multivariate statistical models [SOURCE: Page_26]

530   and conducted on Scots pine, confirms the importance of incorporating a range of biotic factors [SOURCE: Page_26]

531   (tree and stand characteristics, and abundance of mistletoe and processionary moth), topographic [SOURCE: Page_26]

532   and soil factors representing the local- and landscape-scale water balance, and climatic factors, [SOURCE: Page_26]

533   which are important both directly and through their interactions with the other factors. [SOURCE: Page_26]

26 [SOURCE: Page_26]

534   4.1 Biotic factors [SOURCE: Page_27]

535   Mistletoe and the pine processionary moth play essential roles in the Scots pine dieback process. [SOURCE: Page_27]

536   When considering their interaction with the topoedaphic variables, these biotic factors explain [SOURCE: Page_27]

537   64.5% of the variance in the percentage of stems with 50% of leaf deficit (stand model predicting [SOURCE: Page_27]

538   Xstem^50 ; Figure 5). [SOURCE: Page_27]

539   The impact of mistletoe on the health and growth of Scots pine is well documented. Mistletoe does [SOURCE: Page_27]

540   not regulate its transpiration even under drought, and consequently reduces the water available to [SOURCE: Page_27]

541   the host tree, worsening its physiological stress. It also uses a significant proportion of the [SOURCE: Page_27]

542   nutrients from the tree sap, which it stores in its own structure (Mutlu et al., 2016). Consequently, [SOURCE: Page_27]

543   infested trees close their stomata earlier and longer (Zweifel et al., 2012) and produce smaller [SOURCE: Page_27]

544   leaves (Rigling et al., 2010) thereby limiting their carbon absorption capacity and growth potential [SOURCE: Page_27]

545   (Meinzer et al., 2004). Stand-scale productivity losses have been estimated at between 29% in [SOURCE: Page_27]

546   Germany (Kollas et al. 2018) and 64% in Spain for high levels of infestation (Camarero et al., [SOURCE: Page_27]

547   2018), with lower resistance and resilience to severe drought. Infestation by mistletoe is on [SOURCE: Page_27]

548   average more marked in the upper part of the crown due to the parasite’s heliophilous nature [SOURCE: Page_27]

549   (Briggs, 2021; Mellado and Zamora, 2014) and the behavior of the birds that spread it. This [SOURCE: Page_27]

550   explains why it was mostly present in our study area in sunny locations such as on convex terrain [SOURCE: Page_27]

551   (high TPI; Figures 4 and 5), and in open stands. [SOURCE: Page_27]

552   The presence of mistletoe is also linked to climate (Jeffree and Jeffree, 1996), especially to [SOURCE: Page_27]

553   temperatures in the coldest and hottest months, as shown by its significant correlation with the [SOURCE: Page_27]

554   Skre index (Figure 4). Although temperature is the variable that best explains the presence of [SOURCE: Page_27]

555   mistletoe, the population density of fruit-eating birds, and more specifically of its main dispersion [SOURCE: Page_27]

556   agent the thrush genus Turdus, also explains the spatial distribution of mistletoe at landscape [SOURCE: Page_27]

557   scale (Ramsauer et al., 2021). The most significant radius (8 km) used to calculate the area of [SOURCE: Page_27]

558   pines sensitive to mistletoe around our plots is in line with the literature, e.g., with the minimum [SOURCE: Page_27]

559   radius of 3.5 km estimated in Catalonia by Ramsauer et al. (2021). The propagation of mistletoe [SOURCE: Page_27]

560   from a point source is fast, accelerates over time with the progressive increase in population [SOURCE: Page_27]

561   density in neighboring areas, but is still modulated by vegetation distribution in the landscape and [SOURCE: Page_27]

27 [SOURCE: Page_27]

562   especially by the availability of host species (from 0.04 km/year to 0.35 km/year ; Hawksworth and [SOURCE: Page_28]

563   Scharpf 1986, Shaw and Lee 2020). [SOURCE: Page_28]

564   The processionary moth is a forest pest that can cause severe damage to leaves, especially in [SOURCE: Page_28]

565   newly infested areas (Battisti et al., 2005; Stastny et al., 2006). Its distribution in our study area [SOURCE: Page_28]

566   was closely linked to climate, to the area covered by pine stands on the landscape, and to soil and [SOURCE: Page_28]

567   topography factors. The potential evapotranspiration from October to March and minimum [SOURCE: Page_28]

568   absolute temperature were the two dominant climatic variables in the colonization model, in line [SOURCE: Page_28]

569   with the model of Robinet et al. (2007) calibrated in central France. Low minimum temperature [SOURCE: Page_28]

570   limits the expansion of the processionary moth toward higher elevations and latitudes (Hoch et al., [SOURCE: Page_28]

571   2009; Hódar et al., 2003) as it needs heat in winter and large amounts of sunlight to feed and [SOURCE: Page_28]

572   survive (Battisti et al., 2005). It is accordingly found on sunny exposition (e.g., at the top of the [SOURCE: Page_28]

573   slopes, south-facing aspect; Figures 4 and 5) and in open forests where tree canopies are well [SOURCE: Page_28]

574   exposed. The optimal radius to compute its pine hosts density (6 km), is consistent with the study [SOURCE: Page_28]

575   by Robinet et al. (2014b), stating that females can fly 6 km on average, and up to 10 km. [SOURCE: Page_28]

576   The density of both mistletoe and processionary moth populations seem thus reduced when the [SOURCE: Page_28]

577   stands sensitive to these parasites are young, have low stand density, and are scattered over the [SOURCE: Page_28]

578   landscape (low PPI; see also Shaw and Lee 2020). Because Scots pine naturally and gradually [SOURCE: Page_28]

579   colonized abandoned farmland over the last century (Médail, 2001), its age and spatial distribution [SOURCE: Page_28]

580   is irregular within the landscape. This explains why some plots were not or only slightly affected by [SOURCE: Page_28]

581   these parasites, and thus relatively healthy, despite climatic and site conditions favorable to these [SOURCE: Page_28]

582   pests (Figure 6). Such plots, though few in number, reduced the predictive capacity of the dieback [SOURCE: Page_28]

583   models by partly masking the effects of climatic variables. [SOURCE: Page_28]

584 [SOURCE: Page_28]

585   For these two heliophilous parasites, the significant weight of topography and soil is explained by [SOURCE: Page_28]

586   their relationship with the water balance and landscape structure. Stands located at the top of the [SOURCE: Page_28]

587   slopes, on ridges, on convex terrain well-exposed to sunlight, and on shallow or stony soils (often [SOURCE: Page_28]

588   found in such locations due to erosion), (i) usually have a low stem density and canopy cover [SOURCE: Page_28]

589   favoring the infestation of dominant trees with sunny crowns, and (ii) have limited water retention [SOURCE: Page_28]

28 [SOURCE: Page_28]

590   capacities, weakening trees and increasing their vulnerability to these parasites and so [SOURCE: Page_29]

591   accelerating dieback (Mutlu et al., 2016). These stands have especially experienced high [SOURCE: Page_29]

592   defoliation and mortality rates during and following the 2003–2007 drought         (Vennetier et al., [SOURCE: Page_29]

593   2007), opening them, and favoring both mistletoe and processionary moth populations. [SOURCE: Page_29]

594 [SOURCE: Page_29]

595   Independently of the factors cited above, stands with large mean tree DBH had higher foliar deficit [SOURCE: Page_29]

596   (see also Galiano et al. 2010). These large trees generally have larger crowns, which would be [SOURCE: Page_29]

597   preferred by both mistletoe and processionary moth that demand light. They also provide more sites [SOURCE: Page_29]

598   for birds involved in the spread of mistletoe (Kolodziejek J and Kolodziejek A, 2013). Nevertheless, [SOURCE: Page_29]

599   in these stands, shaded trees smaller than the stand average had higher foliar deficits, probably due [SOURCE: Page_29]

600   to the relative intolerance of Scots pine to high levels of shade (Gaudio et al., 2011). This result is [SOURCE: Page_29]

601   supported by the studies from Taccoen et al. (2021) who reported an excess of mortality in shaded [SOURCE: Page_29]

602   Scots pine in South-East France linked to the climate and its change, and from Galiano et al. (2010) [SOURCE: Page_29]

603   in the Spanish Pyrenees. [SOURCE: Page_29]

604 [SOURCE: Page_29]

605   Finally, trees with the lowest foliar deficit were also those with the higher mean growth over the last [SOURCE: Page_29]

606   20 years, and with the higher recovery and resilience after the 2003-2007 drought. The causal link [SOURCE: Page_29]

607   between growth and crown health (i.e., defoliated trees show low growth rates and recovery after [SOURCE: Page_29]

608   drought, or trees with low growth and recovery rates are more sensitive to further drought and prone [SOURCE: Page_29]

609   to dieback) is unclear because the information on the temporal change in foliar deficit is missing. [SOURCE: Page_29]

610   However, the negative relationships between foliar deficit and mean growth rates and resilience to [SOURCE: Page_29]

611   drought were consistent with previous physiological and dendroecological studies (e.g., DeSoto et [SOURCE: Page_29]

612   al., 2020; Dobbertin, 2005; Guada et al., 2016; Poyatos et al., 2013). Interestingly, growth [SOURCE: Page_29]

613   resistance to drought does not seem related to Scots pine dieback, just as it does not seem related [SOURCE: Page_29]

614   to the mortality of gymnosperms (DeSoto et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_29]

615 [SOURCE: Page_29]

616   4.2 Climatic factors [SOURCE: Page_29]

617   Climatic variables alone only partly explained tree dieback in the PLS regression models. The more [SOURCE: Page_29]

618   unfavorable the climatic water balance (P-PET) during the growing and non-growing seasons, the 29 [SOURCE: Page_29]

619   higher the foliar deficit, reflecting the well-known sensitivity to drought of Scots pine (e.g., Camarero [SOURCE: Page_30]

620   et al., 2015; Galiano et al., 2010; Matías et al., 2017; Rigling et al., 2013). These climatic variables [SOURCE: Page_30]

621   were calculated for the period 1981–2010. This period was warmer than the previous decades [SOURCE: Page_30]

622   (Cramer et al., 2018), thus favorable towards the dissemination of mistletoe with more effects on the [SOURCE: Page_30]

623   health of stands. [SOURCE: Page_30]

624   This period also corresponded to the first reports of massive dieback in southeast France, partly [SOURCE: Page_30]

625   linked to the presence of biotic agents and drought (Lieutier et al., 1988; Sardin, 1997). As [SOURCE: Page_30]

626   previously shown, the strong interactions of climate with biotic and topoedaphic factors (Figure 6), [SOURCE: Page_30]

627   added to the irregular spatial distribution of these biotic agents on the regional scale, probably [SOURCE: Page_30]

628   limited the ability of the models to isolate the impact of some climatic variables. [SOURCE: Page_30]

629   4.3 Soil and topography factors [SOURCE: Page_30]

630   Our analysis highlights a strong effect of topographic and edaphic conditions on foliar deficit, which [SOURCE: Page_30]

631   are important in offsetting or worsening climatic water balance. Scots pine presents high foliar [SOURCE: Page_30]

632   deficits on shallow soils (i.e., low TEI) with low water holding capacity (Galiano et al. 2010) but [SOURCE: Page_30]

633   also low growth rate (Bigler et al. 2006). [SOURCE: Page_30]

634   The topoedaphic index (TEI) had a significant effect in all the dieback models developed, [SOURCE: Page_30]

635   confirming its relevance to assess at least roughly the water balance at local scale, independently [SOURCE: Page_30]

636   of the vegetation and climate. A more accurate quantification of this water balance could be [SOURCE: Page_30]

637   obtained from fine soil measurements and from the evaluation of the water demand at stand scale [SOURCE: Page_30]

638   (e.g., with the leaf area index; see Granier et al., 2000), but would require additional work beyond [SOURCE: Page_30]

639   our possibilities. Operationally, the TEI offers the advantage of being of use in varied topographic [SOURCE: Page_30]

640   conditions, notably in conditions where slope or hillside effects are very marked, such as in our [SOURCE: Page_30]

641   study area. [SOURCE: Page_30]

642   However, this index still needs calibration at regional scale and to be evaluated in other contexts, [SOURCE: Page_30]

643   and is not sufficient to estimate water fluxes and pools at landscape level. Topographic indices [SOURCE: Page_30]

644   calculated from DEMs on a scale of 100 m (microtopography) or 1500 m (macrotopography) must [SOURCE: Page_30]

645   also be integrated, as revealed by our tree dieback model and several previous studies (Petroselli [SOURCE: Page_30]

646   et al., 2013; Raduła et al., 2018; Kopecký et al., 2021). 30 [SOURCE: Page_30]

647   4.4 Comparison of dieback models [SOURCE: Page_31]

648   The model developed at tree level had quite low performance (R² = 0.38), which may partly due to [SOURCE: Page_31]

649   the difficulties when estimating tree leaf deficit despite a careful application of the protocols. The [SOURCE: Page_31]

650   presence of understorey may limit the view of the functional crown in several directions and the [SOURCE: Page_31]

651   microphylly, which is very frequent in our study area, is not easy to estimate with binoculars. [SOURCE: Page_31]

652   However, this was mainly caused by the high variability in foliar deficit within stands (see Table 3), [SOURCE: Page_31]

653   which was only partly captured by the variables measured at tree scale (age, diameter, and annual [SOURCE: Page_31]

654   radial growth). The integration of an individual-based competition index (e.g. Heygi index 1974) [SOURCE: Page_31]

655   could have improved the predictive ability of the tree model (e.g. Galiano et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_31]

656   In the geologically complex environments we studied, the vertical and horizontal heterogeneity in [SOURCE: Page_31]

657   soil properties (e.g., texture, percentage of coarse elements) and in bedrock fracture levels was [SOURCE: Page_31]

658   very high, sometimes only a few meters away, and could not be evaluated with a single soil pit. [SOURCE: Page_31]

659   This intra-plot variability in foliar deficit can also be linked to different microclimatic conditions [SOURCE: Page_31]

660   between trees depending on their neighborhood and on the micro-topography. Some trees have [SOURCE: Page_31]

661   also undergone other climatic damages, such as snow breaks, often observed in these [SOURCE: Page_31]

662   environments (Vennetier et al. 2007). They may have different genetic potential, for example [SOURCE: Page_31]

663   causing differences in water use efficiency, even though this effect of genetic differentiation may [SOURCE: Page_31]

664   be negligible (Santini et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_31]

665   At stand scale, the models predicting the percentage of declining tree (i.e., with a foliar deficit [SOURCE: Page_31]

666   higher than 50%), and the probability of having more than 30% of declining trees model offered a [SOURCE: Page_31]

667   higher prediction quality (R² = 0.65 and AUC = 0.88, respectively). The risk model reliably [SOURCE: Page_31]

668   predicted dieback in successive 20% classes (Figure 7), making it operational for a direct use in [SOURCE: Page_31]

669   the field; although a slight tendency to overestimate dieback between 20 and 80% was noted. This [SOURCE: Page_31]

670   model seems to offer a valuable support for decision-making in forest management in a context of [SOURCE: Page_31]

671   increasing drought recurrence and intensity. [SOURCE: Page_31]

672   This modeling approach was designed to be continuously improved, generalized to all tree species [SOURCE: Page_31]

673   and other pedoclimatic contexts, and enhanced by the improved quality and availability of foliar [SOURCE: Page_31]

674   deficit data, for example at European scale (Ferretti, 2021). Another option was to make the risk 31 [SOURCE: Page_31]

675   model available as an application to allow simple diagnostics in the field and make use of data [SOURCE: Page_32]

676   collected by users, so increasing the size and diversity of the database used for calibration and [SOURCE: Page_32]

677   validation. This would make the model more general, i.e., of use in regions where other abiotic or [SOURCE: Page_32]

678   biotic factors are involved in the dieback process (e.g., presence of other pathogens, high [SOURCE: Page_32]

679   competition intensity, hydromorphy; see Oliva et al., 2016 or Taccoen et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_32]

681   5.     Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_32]

682   This work emphasizes the importance of taking into account the respective and interactive effects [SOURCE: Page_32]

683   of multiple abiotic and biotic environmental factors to model tree foliar deficit and dieback. The [SOURCE: Page_32]

684   abiotic factors cannot be reduced to the climatic conditions alone and must also include soil [SOURCE: Page_32]

685   variables observed in the field and the local and landscape topography, which contribute to the [SOURCE: Page_32]

686   local water balance. Finally, the abundance of biotic factors, here processionary moth and [SOURCE: Page_32]

687   mistletoe, is of key importance to accurately predict foliar deficit and dieback of Scots pine. [SOURCE: Page_32]

688   Considering that their potential colonization on the host pine tree is also dependent of biotic, [SOURCE: Page_32]

689   climatic, soil and topographic factors, this study highlights the interdependencies between biotic [SOURCE: Page_32]

690   agents and abiotic factors. [SOURCE: Page_32]
