Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

In semi-arid regions as in India, where agriculture relies on groundwater abstraction, increase of [SOURCE: Page_3]

water resources availability through managed aquifer recharge (MAR) or rainwater harvesting (RWH) [SOURCE: Page_3]

is often perceived as a major solution. Studies on these structures efficiency exists but despite the [SOURCE: Page_3]

interest, limited information is available on the temporal variation of their replenishment. In a [SOURCE: Page_3]

monsoon driven climate, the inter-annual variations are crucial to assess the potential of water [SOURCE: Page_3]

storage and multi-year management especially for these structures. Here, we aim at developing a [SOURCE: Page_3]

methodology to reconstruct water storage of RWH tanks to further improve our understanding on [SOURCE: Page_3]

long term efficiency and multi-years drought management. To tackle this issue, long-term monitoring [SOURCE: Page_3]

of a RWH tank located in Telangana in southern India is achieved by a combination of field [SOURCE: Page_3]

monitoring over 2 years (tanks surface and water levels) and a daily water balance compared to [SOURCE: Page_3]

LANDSAT measurements of the tank area. The procedure allows reconstructing the tank filling [SOURCE: Page_3]

dynamic over a 14-years period at a daily time step and show the extreme variability of the tank [SOURCE: Page_3]

filling level. During this period, the yearly maximum tank volume ranges from 8 650 to ~ 200 000 m^3. [SOURCE: Page_3]

On the 14-years period, the tank reach its maximum capacity only once and, for 1/3 of the time, [SOURCE: Page_3]

yearly maximum replenishment is below 15 % of its capacity. The surface water availability remains [SOURCE: Page_3]

limited in time since the tank dries-up annually, except for 2 years. However, water percolation to [SOURCE: Page_3]

the aquifer is slightly enhanced for some years. During this monitoring period, very few extreme [SOURCE: Page_3]

raining events (6) contribute for more than 50 % of the collected volume. This observation highlights [SOURCE: Page_3]

(1) the dependency of the structure to extreme storm events, (2) the limited capacity for a multi- [SOURCE: Page_3]

year’s management and (3) the farmers vulnerability to successive droughts. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

With the development of mechanized irrigation pumps since the green revolution in the 1970s, India [SOURCE: Page_4]

becomes the country with the highest annual groundwater abstraction (Doll et al., 2012 ; Shah, [SOURCE: Page_4]

2005). Such groundwater exploitation through 26-28 million structures (Mukherji & Shah, 2005 ; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Shah, 2008) represents 85% of the domestic water and 50% of irrigation water. Due to this increasing [SOURCE: Page_4]

intensive extraction (Kumar et al., 2005; Smith et al., 2016), water scarcity is a major issue in [SOURCE: Page_4]

numerous areas of the country (CGWB, 2009) and solutions for a better management at global and [SOURCE: Page_4]

local scale are considered. Comprehensive management of water resources may promote global [SOURCE: Page_4]

water, food security and reduce human vulnerability to climate change. Conjunctive uses of [SOURCE: Page_4]

groundwater and surface water that use surface water for irrigation and water supply during wet [SOURCE: Page_4]

periods, and groundwater during drought, are likely to prove essential (Taylor et al., 2013; Zaveri et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al., 2016). To tackle the problem of water scarcity, India invests in numerous programs both at [SOURCE: Page_4]

federal and local scale. The ‘‘Master plan for artificial recharge’’ (CGWB,2013) recommends building [SOURCE: Page_4]

11 million artificial recharge and rainwater harvesting structures at the national level. It is estimated [SOURCE: Page_4]

that about 36 km^3 of water (1% of the rainfall) could be stored annually (CGWB, 2007). Managed [SOURCE: Page_4]

aquifer recharge (MAR) and rainwater harvesting (RWH) are common practices in India with a sound [SOURCE: Page_4]

historical experience. Sakthivadivel (2007) estimates the number of MAR and RWH structures to be [SOURCE: Page_4]

0.5 million of which 0.25 million are located above crystalline aquifers. Renovation of such existing [SOURCE: Page_4]

structures is also part of the recharge master plans. [SOURCE: Page_4]

MAR solutions are increasing over the world, with successful results worldwide (Dillon et al., 2018) [SOURCE: Page_4]

including in India (Dashora et al., 2018 ; Stiefel et al., 2009). However, water management is complex [SOURCE: Page_4]

with both long term and short-term issues (Batchelor et al., 2003 ; Bitterman et al., 2016; Gleeson et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al., 2012) and impact of large MAR programs remain unclear in semi-arid regions. Despite the [SOURCE: Page_4]

common view and some studies supporting the belief that MAR is a possible solution to the actual [SOURCE: Page_4]

water scarcity problems on those area, various authors such as Dillon et al. (2009), Kumar et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Material and methods [SOURCE: Page_8]

Site presentation [SOURCE: Page_8]

The method is tested on a RWH tank in the Maheshwaram watershed, located 35 km south of [SOURCE: Page_8]

Hyderabad (Telangana, India) which covers an area of 53 km^2 (Figure 1). Detailed studies of this [SOURCE: Page_8]

watershed for more than a decade (Alazard et al., 2015a; Boisson et al., 2014; Dewandel et al., 2006; [SOURCE: Page_8]

Marechal et al., 2004; Maréchal et al., 2006; Perrin et al., 2011) provide a solid baseline for [SOURCE: Page_8]

investigating the functioning and impact of MAR systems. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The region has a semiarid climate with annual monsoon rains (June to October). Mean annual [SOURCE: Page_8]

precipitation (P) is about 750 mm, of which the monsoon accounts for more than 90%. The mean [SOURCE: Page_8]

annual temperature is about 26 °C although during summertime (October to May) maximum [SOURCE: Page_8]

temperatures can reach 45 °C. The watershed is overexploited with more than 700 boreholes used [SOURCE: Page_8]

for agriculture dominated by rice fields. Currently, the water table is 15–25 m deep and is [SOURCE: Page_8]

disconnected from surface water. Maheshwaram watershed is underlain by weathered Archean [SOURCE: Page_8]

biotite granite. Its weathering profile leads to a stratified aquifer with two distinct layers (Dewandel [SOURCE: Page_8]

et al., 2006; Dewandel et al., 2011). The saprolite [10–15 m thick layer from the surface] is [SOURCE: Page_8]

characterized by a sandy-clay composition. Its total porosity is relatively high, up to 10%, but due to [SOURCE: Page_8]

the clay content the effective porosity is low and ranges from 0.5% to 2% (Wyns et al., 2004; [SOURCE: Page_8]

Dewandel et al., 2012). Its hydraulic conductivity is low (1.10-7 m.s-1 to 3.10-5m s-1 – Dewandel et al. [SOURCE: Page_8]

2006) but may contain locally preserved fissures. Below, the fissured layer [10–30 m thick] [SOURCE: Page_8]

constitutes the transmissive part of the aquifer (hydraulic conductivity between 10-6 and 10-4 m.s-1) [SOURCE: Page_8]

with a low effective porosity 1–2% (Dewandel et al., 2012; Marechal et al., 2004). The monitored [SOURCE: Page_8]

tank is close to Tumulur village and has been used for more than 15 years for water storage. No [SOURCE: Page_8]

direct tank water extraction for irrigation occurs. The current tank system can be considered as a [SOURCE: Page_8]

representative example of MAR in semi-arid southern India. Dry season piezometric map shows a [SOURCE: Page_8]

dominant groundwater flow NE to SW. An earth bund dams the natural stream outlet and [SOURCE: Page_8]

Validation [SOURCE: Page_11]

The tank volume (V) is obtained by computing the bathymetry of the tank measured during the dry [SOURCE: Page_11]

period using DGPS (Differential Global Positioning System) for different water levels through SIG [SOURCE: Page_11]

computation (Figure 1). The relationship between water levels and area is estimated from field [SOURCE: Page_11]

volume and surface area is thus established. Estimated volumes from water balance computation [SOURCE: Page_11]
