13    Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

14             In the present study, tomatoes were grown under control conditions (CT), a [SOURCE: Page_2]

15    moderate (MT, up to 2 °C) or a higher rise in temperature (HT, up to 5 °C), and a [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

36          Under glasshouse, different cultural practices and climate management strategies [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   can be used to modulate plant growth conditions in order to obtain the required fruit [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   yield and quality. Among those, temperature control is a useful method for growers to [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   modulate plant growth when other factors such as light, nutrients and water are not [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   limiting (Guichard et al. 2001). [SOURCE: Page_3]

41          Over the last few decades, several studies have been devoted to identifying the [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   minimal temperatures allowing a high yield of tomatoes while limiting the energy costs [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   of heating. Night temperature has long been known to be the determining factor in [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   tomato fruit setting, with optimum temperatures ranging from 15 °C to 20 °C.. In [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   addition, the variation of daily temperature patterns may affect the plant and fruit [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   growth and metabolism, and ultimately, the fruit quality (Adams et al. 2001; Hurd and [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   Graves, 1985; Truffault et al. 2015; Van der Ploeg and Heuvelink, 2005). In the near [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   future, climate change will cause an increase in temperature, which can affect crop [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   yield, due to a reduced fruit set, and change fruit quality (Garg and Cheema, 2011). [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   Increasing temperature generally increases the fruit growth rate but also accelerates fruit [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   maturation and ripening so that fruit weight at harvest can be reduced (Adams et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   2001; Fleisher et al. 2006). Temperatures at the daily scale can affect water and carbon [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   fluxes within the plant. Walker and Ho (1977) reported that increasing fruit temperature [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   favored sugars allocation to the fruits. Similarly, a rapid decrease in air temperature at [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   the end of the day is used by growers to favor assimilate partitioning to the fruits. The [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   tomato fruits dry matter content is not affected by temperature increase from 18 °C to [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   23 °C (Heuvelink, 1995). However, growing plants at higher temperatures (increasing [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   temperature from 25/14 °C to 30/12 °C, day/night respectively) enhances the tomato [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   fruits dry matter content (Rosales et al. 2011). These changes in dry matter content also [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   reflected changes in the tomato fruits composition (Dorais et al. 2010; Rivero et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   2001). Rosales et al. (2011) reported that sugars content increased at high temperatures [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   (30-35 °C) while organic acids decreased. Robertson et al. (1995) found a maximum [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   plateau of tomato lycopene concentration between 18 and 26 °C. Similarly, Brandt et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   (2006), Krumbein et al. (2006), and Dannehl et al. (2012) reported that the lycopene [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   content in tomatoes increases with increasing temperature and reaches its maximum [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   concentration at 25 °C. Temperatures below 12 °C strongly inhibit lycopene [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   biosynthesis and temperatures above 32 °C stop the process (Dumas et al. 2003), [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   affecting the activities of enzymes in the metabolic pathway and consequently, the fruit [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   carotenoid composition (Gautier et al. 2008). Likewise, phenolic compounds (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   chlorogenic acid, rutin and naringenin) increase with increasing mean temperatures [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   (Dannehl et al. 2012). In contrast, increasing the temperature from 27 to 32 °C reduces [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   ascorbate accumulation (Gautier et al. 2008). Thus, high temperature could also have a [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   significant impact on the quality of fruits due to changes in primary or secondary [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   metabolism (Moretti et al. 2010). [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

75          In this sense, in the present work, we intend to study the consequences of [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   different climates (moderate to larger temperature increase) in order to mimic a possible [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   future scenario under climate change. The data obtained may help in the interpretation [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   of the effects of diurnal temperatures in the development and dry mass allocation as [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   well as in the quality and composition of tomatoes. It is imperative to acquire such [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   knowledge since a moderate or larger temperature increase may have different effects. [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   2.1. Tomato plant culture and treatments [SOURCE: Page_5]

84          In June 2013, tomato seeds (Solanum lycopersicum L. cv. “Moneymaker”) were [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   sown into seed trays containing a peat-moss and vermiculite (50/50 v/v) and were [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   germinated in a glasshouse at 25 °C in Avignon (Southern France, 44° N). Ninety plants [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   with five growing leaves were transplanted per treatment into 15 cm diameter pots [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   containing 5 L of potting soil, in a multispan Venlo-type greenhouse, N-S oriented. [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   Plant nutrition, pests and diseases control were carried out in accordance with [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   commercial practices. Water was supplied to the plants using a drip irrigation system to [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   maintain a 20-30% drainage. The flowers were pollinated by vibration once a day [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   during the anthesis phase, the inflorescences of each truss were pruned to six flowers, [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   and lateral shoots were removed as soon as they appeared. [SOURCE: Page_5]

94          Plants were grown in four different compartments, and temperature treatments [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   started on September 2013. The air temperature and humidity inside the different [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   compartments of the greenhouse were measured using ventilated shelters equipped with [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   temperature and humidity probes (HD 9817T$_1$, Delta ohm, France). Temperatures were [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   regulated by various techniques: heating, free ventilation by opening the aeration vents, [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   forced ventilation, and evaporative cooling (cool box). These systems were gradually [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   implemented by the automaton that manages the greenhouse climate. The side walls of [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   the compartments had been bleached to homogenize the radiation and the temperature [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   of each compartment. [SOURCE: Page_6]

103          Temperature scenarios were applied one week before truss flowering and until [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   fruit harvest, by heating the glasshouse during the warmest hours of the day, from 11:00 [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   to 19:00, thus increasing diurnal temperature (+2 or +5 °C). We also used cooling to [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   rapidly decrease temperature at the end of the day and study the possibility to mitigate [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   the consequences of increased temperatures on fruit growth, physiology and quality. [SOURCE: Page_6]

108          The control treatment (CT) was similar to the outdoor climate, and the three [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   other treatments had higher diurnal temperature: [SOURCE: Page_6]

110          - the High temperature Treatment (HT), achieved by heating from 11:00 to 19:00 [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   with an average offset of 2.6 °C up to 5 °C for maximum diurnal temperature (Table 1), [SOURCE: Page_6]

112          - the Moderate temperature Treatment (MT), achieved by heating from 11:00 to [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   19:00 with an average offset of 0.5 °C up to 2 °C for maximum diurnal temperature, [SOURCE: Page_6]

114          - the ‘end-of-day’ treatment (ED), which was similar to MT except at the end of [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   the day, from 19:00 to 22:00, when the temperature was reduced by 3°C in order to [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   favor sugar allocation to the fruits (Walker and Ho, 1977). [SOURCE: Page_6]

303          Despite the fact that the average temperature differences were rather small and [SOURCE: Page_20]

304   close to climate projections for the near future in France, we observed that they affected [SOURCE: Page_20]

305   different processes and resulted in reduced fruit growth and changes in fruit quality [SOURCE: Page_20]

306   traits at harvest. In the present experiment, the most visible quality trait impacted by the [SOURCE: Page_20]

307   temperature treatments was the final fruit size at harvest. We observed that the fruit [SOURCE: Page_20]

308   growth duration decreased in a linear way with increased temperatures: the higher the [SOURCE: Page_20]

309   temperature, the lower the duration of fruit development (from anthesis until red ripe [SOURCE: Page_20]

310   stage). Similar findings have been reported with a reduced developmental duration to [SOURCE: Page_20]

311   reach ripe stage for tomato fruits grown under increased temperature, especially during [SOURCE: Page_20]

312   the fruit later stages of maturation (Adams et al. 2001; Fleisher et al. 2006; Hurd and [SOURCE: Page_20]

313   Graves, 1985). In other species like chili, heat stress following anthesis or from 30 DAA [SOURCE: Page_20]

314   until harvest also reduced the growth period of chili fruits by 15 and 10 days, [SOURCE: Page_20]

315   respectively (Pagamas and Nawata, 2008). High temperatures generally accelerate fruit [SOURCE: Page_20]

316   maturation and ripening, and this could be an adaptive mechanism to stress conditions, [SOURCE: Page_20]

317   enabling the plant to disperse its seeds faster when under stress (Mizhari, 1982). [SOURCE: Page_20]

318   Experiments using locally heated fruits showed that heating fruits reduced fruit size, [SOURCE: Page_20]

319   since it modified the cell division orientation (Fanwoua et al. 2012) and reduced cell [SOURCE: Page_20]

320   expansion (Okello et al. 2014). In another study, rising temperatures from 20/20 [SOURCE: Page_20]

321   day/night to 25/25°C had no effect on tomato fruit size (Bertin, 2005). This might be [SOURCE: Page_20]

322   linked to the fact that i) the plant remains at optimal temperature regarding fruit growth, [SOURCE: Page_20]

323   or that ii) different processes are affected by increased temperatures, but they [SOURCE: Page_20]

324   compensate one another. Indeed, Bertin (2005) reported a compensatory effect between [SOURCE: Page_20]

325   a reduced duration of fruit cell division (which reduced the number of fruit cells per [SOURCE: Page_20]

326   fruit) and an increased period of cell expansion, where the cell expansion rate was not [SOURCE: Page_20]

327   affected. When the temperature further increases, some processes will not be at their [SOURCE: Page_20]

19 [SOURCE: Page_20]

328   optimal temperature and will not be compensated. In addition, other processes can be [SOURCE: Page_21]

329   affected; so the reduction of fruit size could be related to other factors such as limited [SOURCE: Page_21]

330   assimilate supply, as it was reported for higher fruit load under HT (Gautier et al. 2005). [SOURCE: Page_21]

331   Indeed, the fruit dry weight accumulation was reduced under HT (Figure 2), which [SOURCE: Page_21]

332   could be linked to the increased carbohydrate loss by respiration. Furthermore, the [SOURCE: Page_21]

333   increase in temperature causes a higher vapor pressure deficit (VPD), which could lead [SOURCE: Page_21]

334   to a larger water loss by transpiration. However, this higher climatic demand could be [SOURCE: Page_21]

335   partly, fully, or over-compensated by the observed decrease of the fruit skin [SOURCE: Page_21]

336   conductance associated with the HT treatment. Similar findings have been reported for [SOURCE: Page_21]

337   leaves growing under water stress (Cameron et al. 2006), which could be the [SOURCE: Page_21]

338   consequence of changes in the cuticular wax composition or thickness (Vogg et al. [SOURCE: Page_21]

339   2004). If the water conductance of young growing fruits is also decreased, it will impair [SOURCE: Page_21]

340   fruits photosynthesis and transpiration (Blanke and Lenz, 1989). Even if their [SOURCE: Page_21]

341   photosynthetic activity remains low (Carrara et al. 2001), it can contribute to 10 or 15% [SOURCE: Page_21]

342   of the carbon skeleton of the fruits (Tanaka et al. 1974). Thus, reduced fruit [SOURCE: Page_21]

343   photosynthesis alters carbon allocation to seeds and may impact the seeds quality [SOURCE: Page_21]

344   (Lytovchenko et al. 2011). [SOURCE: Page_21]

345          In addition, reducing fruit transpiration helps maintaining fruit water pressure [SOURCE: Page_21]

346   and limits the entry of water and carbon into the fruits through the phloem. This could [SOURCE: Page_21]

347   partly explain the lower rate of fruit growth observed under HT. Similar data have been [SOURCE: Page_21]

348   reported by Li et al. (2001). in covered peach fruits: a decrease in transpiration, [SOURCE: Page_21]

349   respiration, and photosynthesis rates could contribute to reducing the quantity of [SOURCE: Page_21]

350   carbohydrates entering into the fruits, which in turn would result in reducing soluble [SOURCE: Page_21]

351   solids content. We observed a 37% reduction in fruit water conductance from the [SOURCE: Page_21]

20 [SOURCE: Page_21]

352   control to the HT treatment (at red ripe stage), which parallels the 33% decrease in dry [SOURCE: Page_22]

353   mass accumulation. [SOURCE: Page_22]

354          In the present experiment, dry matter and structural dry matter contents were not [SOURCE: Page_22]

355   affected by HT applied during tomato fruits development (Figures 1C and D). However, [SOURCE: Page_22]

356   we observed that the ED treatment tended to increase the pericarp DM content. Under [SOURCE: Page_22]

357   moderate temperatures, Heuvelink (1995) reported no significant difference in the DM [SOURCE: Page_22]

358   content of fruits harvested on plants grown at 18 ºC or 24 °C. In contrast, under higher [SOURCE: Page_22]

359   temperatures, Rosales et al. (2011) found increased dry matter contents in tomato fruits [SOURCE: Page_22]

360   produced at 30/12 °C compared to 25/14 °C (day/night respectively), which could also [SOURCE: Page_22]

361   be linked to water limitation in the summer conditions. In the present experiment, the [SOURCE: Page_22]

362   temperature decrease at the end of the day may have been too low, and it would be [SOURCE: Page_22]

363   interesting to consider broader changes in ED temperature to confirm its potential role [SOURCE: Page_22]

364   in improving fruits dry matter content. In addition, we observed that HT tended to [SOURCE: Page_22]

365   reduce both the carbon influx to the fruit and its partitioning to the seeds, and [SOURCE: Page_22]

366   consequently, seed storage and quality could be altered (Lytovchenko et al. 2011). [SOURCE: Page_22]
