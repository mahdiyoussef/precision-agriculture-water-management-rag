6   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

7   Wetlands are essential for humankind as they provide food security and climate [SOURCE: Page_3]

8   change mitigation through ecosystem services such as water regulation and [SOURCE: Page_3]

9   biodiversity conservation. Peatlands, which make up an integral component of [SOURCE: Page_3]

10   wetlands, are also known for significant carbon storage capacity through peat [SOURCE: Page_3]

11   accumulation. During the period 1970-2013, the area of wetland worldwide [SOURCE: Page_3]

12   decreased by 35%. This loss was more pronounced in the Mediterranean, with up to [SOURCE: Page_3]

13   48 % of wetland surface loss (Gardner et al., 2018) in spite of the fact that wetlands [SOURCE: Page_3]

14   constitute biodiversity and endemism hotspots. The drastic wetland surface loss is [SOURCE: Page_3]

15   mainly attributed to water withdrawal and agricultural land extension (Parish et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

16   2008; Kløve et al., 2011; Malek et al., 2018). The aridity of the Mediterranean climate [SOURCE: Page_3]

17   implies wetlands such as peatlands are expected to be threatened by ongoing and [SOURCE: Page_3]

18   future climate change impacts. Extended drought periods may involve a decrease in [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   the water table, enhancing mineralization of organic matter and turning these [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   peatlands into a source of carbon and drylands (Urák et al., 2017; Huang et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   2018). [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   The crucial need to preserve such peatlands is reflected by a large number of [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   regional to supranational governmental and non-governmental initiatives promoting [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   the need for sustainable development. These include the United Nations Convention [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   to Combat Desertification (UNCCD, 1992), the Paris Climate Agreement (2015), the [SOURCE: Page_3]

1 [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs, 2015) and more specifically [SOURCE: Page_4]

27   the Ramsar Convention (1971), the MedWet Mediterranean Wetlands Initiative [SOURCE: Page_4]

28   (1991) and the Aichi biodiversity targets (2010). In concrete terms, these initiatives [SOURCE: Page_4]

29   aim to identify and mitigate human pressures on the environment. All of them point [SOURCE: Page_4]

30   out that it is critical to obtain detailed knowledge for each site, and specifically on [SOURCE: Page_4]

31   geomorphological, geohydrological and hydroclimatic conditions that are by definition [SOURCE: Page_4]

32   interconnected. It is therefore essential to develop strategies and methodologies able [SOURCE: Page_4]

33   to identify the boundary conditions necessary to sufficiently understand peatlands [SOURCE: Page_4]

34   functioning at the hydrosystem scale. This crucial step will guide sustainable [SOURCE: Page_4]

35   development strategies and help to design integrated water resource management [SOURCE: Page_4]

36   procedures to protect these wetlands and preserve their ecological value [SOURCE: Page_4]

37   (Vlachopoulou et al., 2014; Hettiarachchi et al., 2015; Erostate et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_4]

38   Although peatlands are present in most of the countries bordering the Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_4]

39   Sea, they remain seriously under-investigated: not all are inventoried, their spatial [SOURCE: Page_4]

40   extent and functioning are not properly reported and recharge mechanisms are [SOURCE: Page_4]

41   largely unknown. What knowledge does exist, suggests Mediterranean peatlands are [SOURCE: Page_4]

42   shaped by their local environment, making them small, scattered, and with most [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   presumed to be groundwater-dependent (Finlayson et al., 2018). Nevertheless, peat [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   and gley soils extend over approximately 64,000 km² over the Mediterranean, which [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   means peatlands may cover up to 29 to 42 % of the total known wetland surface [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   (Parish et al., 2008; Gardner et al., 2018). Thus additional information on the [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   peatlands could help delineate their hydrological vulnerability and resilience capacity [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   to climate change. [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   One of the most important knowledge gaps is in characterization and quantification of [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   the water balance components necessary for biodiversity conservation and peat [SOURCE: Page_4]

2 [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   accumulation. Peat accumulation occurs through the maintenance of water levels [SOURCE: Page_5]

52   close to the surface irrespective of seasonal climate variation and over the long term, [SOURCE: Page_5]

53   especially under the semi-arid conditions that characterise the Mediterranean area. [SOURCE: Page_5]

54   Approaches combining complementary methods such as water level monitoring, [SOURCE: Page_5]

55   hydrogeochemistry, and isotope hydrology tools have been widely employed to [SOURCE: Page_5]

56   characterize the origin, periodicity, and recharge mechanisms of very large and well- [SOURCE: Page_5]

57   developed wetlands mainly in cold humid climates (Howie and Van Meerveld, 2013; [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   Isokangas et al., 2017). The same approach to investigation of more seasonal and [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   inhomogeneous recharge mechanisms of Mediterranean peatlands is largely [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   missing. [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   In this study, the Moltifau peatland on the island of Corsica (France) was investigated [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   to characterize the seasonal recharge mechanisms of a low-altitude Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   peatland exposed to extended low precipitation period during the summer. Water [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   level monitoring was combined with major ion hydrochemistry as well as 18O, 2H, 3H, [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   and 87Sr/86Sr isotopes to identify and quantify complex water flows supplying a [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   hydrosystem connected to a larger watershed. The results are used to evaluate the [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   vulnerability and hydrogeological resilience of Mediterranean peatlands based on the [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   origin and renewal dynamics of water bodies contributing to their recharge. Finally, [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   the results are evaluated against existing water and aquatic environmental [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   management policy frameworks. Effective policies are essential to protect the [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   hydrogeological functioning of Mediterranean peatlands and in particular their [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   potential vulnerability to future climate change. [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   The Moltifau peatland was selected for its strategic location in Corsica (France), a [SOURCE: Page_6]

77   mountainous island in the western Mediterranean basin. According to the Köppen [SOURCE: Page_6]

78   climate classification (Peel et al., 2007), Corsican coastal regions are characterized [SOURCE: Page_6]

79   by a Csa climate (hot-summer), mid-mountain regions by a Csb climate (warm- [SOURCE: Page_6]

80   summer), and small areas of highest altitude by a subarctic Dfc climate (snow with [SOURCE: Page_6]

81   dry and cool summer). Such a Mediterranean hydroclimatic context also implies [SOURCE: Page_6]

82   strong inter-seasonal and inter-annual heterogeneity of temperature and rainfall [SOURCE: Page_6]

83   conditions. Corsica is therefore an excellent observatory island giving access to all [SOURCE: Page_6]

84   continental hydrological processes on a small spatial scale. [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   The Moltifau peatland, which extends over 40,000m^2, lies on an alluvial plain [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   between 240 and 270 m a.s.l, within the Ascu River valley, an alpine watershed of [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   365 km² reaching a maximum elevation of 2706 m. a.s.l. Its inland location at low [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   altitude implies a Csb climate with severe summer drought conditions, the intensity, [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   and duration of which are continuously increasing due to climate change which in [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   turn increases the risk of the peatland drying out. The peatland is delimited by the [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   Ascu River at the north, and by the Punta di Ciufellu massif (820 m. a.s.l.) at the [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   south. The average annual temperature is 14.4°C (from +4°C in winter to +25 °C in [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   summer), and the average annual rainfall amount is 726 mm (from 13 mm in summer [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   to 99 mm in fall). The Ascu River has a torrential regime with a mean annual flow of [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   about 5.1 m^3/s, from 0.8 m^3/s in summer to 8.9 m^3/s in fall. The Ascu regime is also [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   influenced by the high altitude Dfc climate involving spring snowpack melting [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   contribution to the flow. Private housing settlements, which are part of the alluvial [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   plain upstream, extend to the west of the peatland (Figure 1). Over the Moltifau area, [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   population and urbanism are growing strongly, by around +56% in 49 years and [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   +245% in 47 years, respectively. The drinking water supply mainly comes from the [SOURCE: Page_6]

4 [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   Ascu River (upstream of the peatland) and numerous private boreholes pump the [SOURCE: Page_7]

102   alluvial groundwater for crop production. [SOURCE: Page_7]

103   The peatland shelters a high plant and animal biodiversity including species [SOURCE: Page_7]

104   protected at National, European, and International levels as well as several endemic [SOURCE: Page_7]

105   species justifying its internationally recognized ecological value (Reille, 1997; [SOURCE: Page_7]

106   Burguet-Moretti et al., 2011). It is referenced as the largest active raised bog on [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   Corsica, is mainly acidic (pH ~4-5), young (14C dating ~ 515 +/- 45 years), and of little [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   thickness (1 to 2.5 m). Although there are some residues remaining from past landfill [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   activities around the alder forest (glass, rubble, and car bodies), the peatland area [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   itself can be considered as “pristine” (Poher, 2017). However, the peatland area is [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   not delimited by any barrier, signage, or other physical materialization and most of [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   the inhabitants of the town of Moltifau consider the peatland as an unhealthy place [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   and underestimate its ecological value. [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   The peatland is underlain by well-fractured, pink perthitic granites that are part of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   Punta di Ciufellu massif (Figure 1). Undifferentiated colluvium, resulting from the [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   erosion of the Ciufellu massif accumulates on its piedmont to a thickness of a few [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   meters and extends in the south of the peatland (Figure 1).The alluvial plain is made [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   of river alluvium organized in terraces of different ages deposited on the granitic [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   bedrock with a thickness of up to several tens of meters and permeability of about [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   6.4×10-4 m/s. Deposits consist mainly of more or less altered pebbles, nested in a [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   clayey orange paleosol close to the piedmont or a grey sandy matrix close to the [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   riverbed (Rossi et al., 1994). [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   Alder forest extending over 120,000 m² surrounds the peatland and hosts tiny [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   temporary streams supplied by runoff flows from the Ciufellu massif but also by [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   numerous cold-water springs on the ground. The cold-water springs origin is not [SOURCE: Page_7]

5 [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   documented and probably related to the alluvial aquifer and colluvium discharge. The [SOURCE: Page_8]

127   water level in the alluvial aquifer and the peatland displays little variation within the [SOURCE: Page_8]

128   peatland irrespective of the season. The peatland is interpreted to be fed upstream [SOURCE: Page_8]

129   by alluvial groundwater overflow due to a topographic threshold affecting alluvium [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   terraces (Genevier et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   3. Method [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   Five quarterly field surveys were conducted under contrasting hydrological conditions [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   to represent a complete hydrological cycle from: May 2018 and June 2019 for spring, [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   August 2018 for summer, December 2019 for fall, and February 2019 for winter. Data [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   June 2019 to constrain the local δ18O variation. Thirdly, the data acquisition includes [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   R12, R16, R19), the granitic massif (F$_6$), the local rainwater (rain gauge near F$_6$), the [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   are not exploited and are used here as piezometers. Their depth increase with [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   distance to the river: 5.7 m at F$_1$, 3.0 m at F$_3$, 6.7 m at F$_4$, 8.2 at F$_5$, 10 m at F$_8$, 30 [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   m at F$_7$, and 103 m at F$_6$. Peatland piezometers develop only within the peat, with a [SOURCE: Page_8]

6 [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   depth of about 1.24 m at T$_1$, 1.20 m at T$_2$, 1.45 m at T$_4$, 0.51 m at T$_5$, and 1.27 m at [SOURCE: Page_9]

152   T$_6$. Water table elevation of the alluvial aquifer and the peatland is averaged [SOURCE: Page_9]

154   the spring 2018 campaign, 3H was not sampled. During the June-2019 campaign, a [SOURCE: Page_9]

156   strontium isotopes (87Sr/86Sr) were measured in 15 samples representative of all [SOURCE: Page_9]

157   water bodies. [SOURCE: Page_9]

191      4.1. Recharge components identification [SOURCE: Page_10]

192   The river flow and the alluvial aquifer water level are strongly correlated with the [SOURCE: Page_10]

193   monthly rainfall amount (Figure 2-A). Three peaks corresponding to high river water [SOURCE: Page_10]

194   level events are observable in the alluvial aquifer during Nov-2018, Feb-2019, and [SOURCE: Page_10]

195   Jun-19. The water level in the peatland appears quite stable but two peaks were [SOURCE: Page_10]

196   recorded and correlate with the two high river water level events of Nov-2018 and [SOURCE: Page_10]

197   Feb-2019. There is no time-lag between these events and the water level varies in [SOURCE: Page_10]

198   the alluvial aquifer and the peatland on a monthly timescale. Except during summer, [SOURCE: Page_10]

199   the EC in the peatland is generally lower than in the alluvial aquifer most likely due to [SOURCE: Page_10]

200   a greater rainfall contribution. The high river water level events are always associated [SOURCE: Page_10]

8 [SOURCE: Page_10]

201   with a decrease in the EC in the alluvial aquifer with values tending towards those [SOURCE: Page_11]

202   observed in the peatland. This information coupled with the observation of the water [SOURCE: Page_11]

203   level map (Figure 1) highlighting a clear continuity over the alluvial plain and the [SOURCE: Page_11]

204   peatland area argue in favour of a strong groundwater-dependence of this peatland. [SOURCE: Page_11]

205   Furthermore, the water level in the alluvial aquifer is generally higher than in the [SOURCE: Page_11]

206   peatland, except during Aug-18 and Sept-18 (Figure 2-B). This highlights the [SOURCE: Page_11]

207   contribution of other water bodies to recharge to the peatland and the possibility for [SOURCE: Page_11]

208   the alluvial aquifer to be fed by the peatland. [SOURCE: Page_11]

209   Most of the samples from Aug-2018 and Dec-2018 plot along the Western [SOURCE: Page_11]

210   Mediterranean Meteoric Water Line (WMMWL) of Celle‐Jeanton et al. (2001) [SOURCE: Page_11]

211   whereas those of May-2018, Feb-2019 and June-2019 plot closer to the Moltifau [SOURCE: Page_11]

212   meteoric water line defined as δ2H = 7.30 (± 0.49) × δ18O + 7.37 (OLSR approach; n [SOURCE: Page_11]

213   = 12; r² = 0.96; Figure 3). No significant δ18O enrichment is observed, indicating that [SOURCE: Page_11]

214   evaporation processes are negligible despite the usual severe low precipitation [SOURCE: Page_11]

215   period during the Mediterranean summer (Jasechko, 2019). Usually, water in [SOURCE: Page_11]

216   peatland appears depleted in 18O and 2H during the high flow period and turns more [SOURCE: Page_11]

406      4.5. From hydrogeological resilience to the vulnerability of Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_19]

407          peatlands [SOURCE: Page_19]

408   At first glance, the peatland's hydrogeological resilience to climate change appears [SOURCE: Page_19]

409   limited because the river-alluvial aquifer system on which the peatland depends is [SOURCE: Page_19]

410   highly responsive to the seasonality of hydroclimatic conditions. In particular, there is [SOURCE: Page_19]

411   no significant time lag between the rain events and the alluvial aquifer water level [SOURCE: Page_19]

412   response supplying the peatland. This finding testifies to the high reversibility of the [SOURCE: Page_19]

413   peatland hydrological status and vulnerability to low precipitation period during the [SOURCE: Page_19]

414   Mediterranean summer. However, the peatland has proven to be resilient during the [SOURCE: Page_19]

415   low precipitation period, as shown by the water level monitoring (Figure 2). This can [SOURCE: Page_19]

416   be explained by the reservoir function of the peat (pore water) and by the multiple [SOURCE: Page_19]

417   groundwater sources supplying the peatland, making it not solely dependent on [SOURCE: Page_19]

418   precipitation feeding into the alluvial aquifer and the river. The peatland benefits from [SOURCE: Page_19]

419   low but perennial inflows from reservoirs with longer renewal dynamics (colluvium [SOURCE: Page_19]

420   and granite groundwater) as well as from the more important but very short time [SOURCE: Page_19]

421   inflows from summer storms governing the river flow. [SOURCE: Page_19]

422   The hydrogeological functioning of Mediterranean peatlands is an intrinsic asset [SOURCE: Page_19]

423   making them well adapted to the Mediterranean climate. From a qualitative point of [SOURCE: Page_19]

424   view, groundwater bodies with long renewal dynamics constitute perennial and [SOURCE: Page_19]

425   presumed good quality recharge components. However, their connection with surface [SOURCE: Page_19]

17 [SOURCE: Page_19]

426   water bodies makes them vulnerable to any land-use changes likely to affect water [SOURCE: Page_20]

427   quality and quantity within the watershed: population and urban growth (drinking [SOURCE: Page_20]

428   water supply, sanitation), agricultural practices (irrigation, fertilizers, phytosanitary [SOURCE: Page_20]

429   products) as this is more widely the case in the Mediterranean. During the summer [SOURCE: Page_20]

430   low precipitation period, the river, the colluvium, and the granite groundwater may be [SOURCE: Page_20]

431   insufficient to maintain a sufficient water level in the alluvial aquifer to support [SOURCE: Page_20]

432   peatland hydrological functioning, leading to a water table inversion (Figure 2B). [SOURCE: Page_20]

433   During such unfavourable hydrological conditions, the peatland survival relies on the [SOURCE: Page_20]

434   peat reservoir function. This demonstrates the precarious hydrogeological equilibrium [SOURCE: Page_20]

435   within the peatland. Any increase in the water table inversion duration can then lead [SOURCE: Page_20]

436   to the peatland drying up. Critically, the drying up of peatland, even temporarily, is [SOURCE: Page_20]

437   likely to permanently alter the plant and animal species it contains. As a [SOURCE: Page_20]

438   consequence, any slight external pressure on the alluvial groundwater level from [SOURCE: Page_20]

439   abstractions to support irrigation and drinking water supply, or through climate [SOURCE: Page_20]

440   change (by increasing the temperature or the drought length, and decreasing or [SOURCE: Page_20]

441   modifying the rainfall scheme) can result in the drying up, where the worst-case [SOURCE: Page_20]

442   scenario is complete desiccation. [SOURCE: Page_20]

445          Mediterranean region [SOURCE: Page_20]

446   The Moltifau peatland can be considered a representative peatland from the western [SOURCE: Page_20]

447   Mediterranean region at low altitude, where worsening vulnerability to drought [SOURCE: Page_20]

448   conditions has been documented (Parish et al., 2008). As part of the territory of [SOURCE: Page_20]

449   France, one of the Mediterranean rim countries offering the strictest legislative [SOURCE: Page_20]

18 [SOURCE: Page_20]

450   framework in favour of wetlands preservation, the peatland provides an important [SOURCE: Page_21]

451   perspective on deficiencies in the legislation and management of such ecosystems. [SOURCE: Page_21]

452   At the peatland scale, management strategies are theoretically developed to [SOURCE: Page_21]

453   preserve its ecological value. Nevertheless, the lack of willingness, financial [SOURCE: Page_21]

454   resources as well as the involvement of several environmental institutions such as [SOURCE: Page_21]

455   the French national forestry board (ONF) and the Directorate of the Environment [SOURCE: Page_21]

456   (DREAL) make policies ineffective. Added to this are potential administrative [SOURCE: Page_21]

457   difficulties that might arise through the transfer of wetland management strategies to [SOURCE: Page_21]

458   the local authorities through the Regional Environmental Office (OEC). This situation [SOURCE: Page_21]

459   is due to the State decentralization which recognizes the Corsica administrative [SOURCE: Page_21]

460   Region as fully in charge of environmental management and conservation, blurring [SOURCE: Page_21]

461   the situation and making coordination even more complicated from the European to [SOURCE: Page_21]

462   the local level.. Furthermore, the preservation strategy presently developed only [SOURCE: Page_21]

463   deals with biodiversity aspects and is applied only within the strict area of the [SOURCE: Page_21]

464   peatland. None of the strategies takes into account the need to preserve the water [SOURCE: Page_21]

465   body it contains and those inter-connected to it in a wider context. Indeed, the [SOURCE: Page_21]

466   peatland water body does not appear in any management document for aquatic [SOURCE: Page_21]

467   environments related to the local transcription of the European Water Framework [SOURCE: Page_21]

468   Directive of 2000 (WFD, 2000/60/EC). The fate of the peatland is therefore in the [SOURCE: Page_21]

469   hands of the local authorities who are forced to reach compromises between the [SOURCE: Page_21]

470   peatland ecosystem services preservation, and the necessary socio-economic [SOURCE: Page_21]

471   development of the territory. Due to its small size and lack of visibility, both in the [SOURCE: Page_21]

472   landscape (no barriers, signage, or other physical materialization) and in the minds [SOURCE: Page_21]

473   (little communication and education about its ecological value), this peatland is [SOURCE: Page_21]

474   suffering from increasing human pressure. [SOURCE: Page_21]

19 [SOURCE: Page_21]

475   The WFD is widely accepted as the most ambitious and substantial European [SOURCE: Page_22]

476   sustainable development legislation to date. By nature, the WFD aims at reaching [SOURCE: Page_22]

477   qualitative and quantitative “good status” for identified surface and groundwater [SOURCE: Page_22]

478   bodies supplying wetlands by the end of the second or third management cycles [SOURCE: Page_22]

479   extending during 2015-2012 and 2021 to 2027, respectively. Although this approach [SOURCE: Page_22]

480   attempts to address the most serious health-threatening impacts on the environment, [SOURCE: Page_22]

481   many wetlands of international importance remain outside the WFD. On the one [SOURCE: Page_22]

482   hand, as the number of water bodies is defined according to political criteria, most [SOURCE: Page_22]

483   often in the way to reduce costs (Moss, 2008), the managed groundwater body size [SOURCE: Page_22]

486   1/80,000 of the groundwater body's total size. On the other hand, pollution sources [SOURCE: Page_22]

487   being often multiple in nature and difficult to constrain due to the complexity of [SOURCE: Page_22]

488   human activities on the territory, basic measurements for water body quality [SOURCE: Page_22]

491   2017). [SOURCE: Page_22]

492   As demonstrated in this study, Mediterranean peatlands are groundwater-dependent [SOURCE: Page_22]

493   (GDE) and well connected to the whole hydrographic network. In its present form, the [SOURCE: Page_22]

494   WFD requires the establishment of separate Programmes of Measures (PoMs) for [SOURCE: Page_22]

495   groundwater and surface water bodies without taking into account the complex [SOURCE: Page_22]

496   hydrological functioning of such transitional environments. Water flow connects the [SOURCE: Page_22]

501   economic drivers (Varela-Ortega et al., 2011; Vlachopoulou et al., 2014). Such an [SOURCE: Page_23]

502   integrated and coordinated approach should be implemented in the WFD by the [SOURCE: Page_23]

506   over the relevant territory, and finally identifying relevant indicators for evaluating the [SOURCE: Page_23]

510   anthropized and regional river basins (Tavakoly et al., 2019). On a smaller scale, as [SOURCE: Page_23]

511   is the case for the Moltifau peatland, it is difficult to implement this approach because [SOURCE: Page_23]

512   of the lack of willingness and coordination between all environmental institutions and [SOURCE: Page_23]

513   land users. A solution would be to propose the concept of scientifically-piloted PoMs [SOURCE: Page_23]

514   at the hydro-ecosystem level for the third WFD cycle (2021-2027). Based on the [SOURCE: Page_23]

515   present study, its delimitation would be based on the peatland's "optimal functioning [SOURCE: Page_23]

516   conditions zone” including both surface and groundwater. Quality indicators would [SOURCE: Page_23]

517   facilitate assessment of the whole ecosystem status, including new groundwater [SOURCE: Page_23]

518   degradation trends and impacts on GDEs by the implementation of relevant research [SOURCE: Page_23]

519   tools such as the monitoring of water levels, physico-chemical parameters, [SOURCE: Page_23]

520   hydrochemistry, and isotopes (Erostate et al., 2020). The advantage of this proposal [SOURCE: Page_23]

521   is also the consideration of heterogeneities in hydroclimatic conditions and [SOURCE: Page_23]

526   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_24]

527   This study represents one of the first comprehensive analyses of the seasonal [SOURCE: Page_24]

528   recharge mechanisms of a representative Mediterranean peatland, integrating a [SOURCE: Page_24]

529   range of geochemical and isotope hydrology tools (Na+, δ18O, 3H, 87Sr/86Sr). Such an [SOURCE: Page_24]

530   approach is relevant to identify and quantify complex water flows supplying [SOURCE: Page_24]

531   Mediterranean peatlands and could be adopted in other peatlands worldwide [SOURCE: Page_24]

532   potentially connected to multiple surface and groundwater bodies. This study [SOURCE: Page_24]

533   confirmed   the    groundwater-dependent       status   of   Mediterranean       peatlands, [SOURCE: Page_24]

534   emphasizing the strong seasonal heterogeneity of the recharge component [SOURCE: Page_24]

535   contributions despite a very stable water level throughout the year. Peatlands are [SOURCE: Page_24]

536   dependent on surface and shallow flows during fall, winter, and spring, and are highly [SOURCE: Page_24]

537   dependent on shallow and deep groundwater during summer. This important finding [SOURCE: Page_24]

538   suggests, on the one hand, an intrinsic resilience to low precipitation period during [SOURCE: Page_24]

539   the Mediterranean summer and on the other hand, the connectivity between all [SOURCE: Page_24]

540   surface and groundwater bodies of the watershed. This important hydrological [SOURCE: Page_24]

541   functioning makes the peatlands vulnerable to any change in land use within the [SOURCE: Page_24]

542   watershed or in hydroclimatic conditions as expected in the context of global change. [SOURCE: Page_24]

543   The peatland vulnerability is also increased by poor consideration of their specific [SOURCE: Page_24]

544   functioning into water and aquatic environment management policies such as the [SOURCE: Page_24]

545   European WFD. A paradigm-shift with management based on the hydrosystem [SOURCE: Page_24]

546   concept is necessary and implies a downscaling adapted to Mediterranean peatlands [SOURCE: Page_24]

547   size and their transitional situation connecting surface and groundwater. Aquatic [SOURCE: Page_24]

548   environments,     surface,   and   groundwater    bodies     currently   being   managed [SOURCE: Page_24]

549   independently, need to be coordinated and efforts between environmental institutions [SOURCE: Page_24]

22 [SOURCE: Page_24]
