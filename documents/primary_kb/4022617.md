41   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

42      Conservation agriculture was developed to enhance the sustainability of agricultural systems [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   and reduce drawbacks of conventional agriculture, especially soil degradation due to erosion [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   (Hobbs et al., 2008). Conservation agriculture combines three main interrelated soil conservation [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   techniques: (i) little or no soil disturbance, (ii) permanent soil cover by crop residues and/or living [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   cover crops and (iii) diversification of plant species (FAO, 2016). Interactions among these three [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   techniques lead to complex and interrelated modifications in soil physical, chemical and biological [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   properties. Considering these changes is crucial to assess performances of such agricultural systems [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   properly. However, studies of impacts of conservation agriculture on soil properties show many [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   inconsistencies, especially for soil hydraulic processes (Green et al., 2003; Strudley et al., 2008; [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   Verhulst et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_3]

52      Effects of soil cultivation practices on soil properties has received much research attention in [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   recent decades, but clear trends have not been established due to differences in location, soils and [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   agricultural practices (Green et al., 2003; Strudley et al., 2008). Tillage tends to decrease bulk 2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   density and increase macroporosity, thus increasing the saturated and near-saturated hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   conductivity of the tilled layer. These effects are, however, strongly time-dependent and usually [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   disappear rapidly after tillage (Mapa et al., 1986), due to natural soil reconsolidation caused by [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   wetting and drying cycles (Ahuja et al., 1998). Simultaneously, tillage interrupts macropore [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   connectivity between the soil surface and the untilled deeper soil, thus decreasing water movement [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   throughout the entire soil profile (Cameira et al., 2003). Conversely, untilled soils have higher bulk [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   density and greater pore connectivity (Gozubuyuk et al., 2014). Cover crops may (partially) [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   counterbalance negative effects of no tillage on bulk density by, for example, creating stable [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   biopores through their root development during the growing season (Williams and Weil, 2004; [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   Abdollahi and Munkholm, 2014). Moreover, after cover crop destruction, the dead residues form a [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   mulch that physically protects the soil surface from crusting (Baumhardt and Lascano, 1999). [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   Maintaining crop residues on the soil surface also leads to accumulation of soil organic matter in [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   topsoil layers (Kay and VandenBygaart, 2002) and improves aggregate stability (Devine et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   2014). In parallel, increased macrofauna activity (especially of earthworms) in conservation [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   agriculture systems forms biomacropores that improve water infiltration (Shipitalo et al., 2000). [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   Finally, soils under conservation agriculture also tend to have a larger proportion of finer pores [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   (micropores) (Hill et al., 1985). These changes in pore-size distribution could improve the storage [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   of plant-available water (Bescansa et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_4]

73      The variety and complexity of the counteracting effects of conservation agriculture on soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   properties call for developing new tools to properly assess and model these effects. Development of [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   water- and solute-transport models has received much research attention in recent decades. The lack [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   of accurate data on soil hydraulic properties, especially for soils under conservation agriculture, [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   however, hinders the use of models, as they require water-retention and hydraulic conductivity data [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   as inputs (Wösten et al., 1999). Despite significant improvements in measuring techniques, [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   researchers agree that directly measuring water-retention curves remain expensive, time consuming [SOURCE: Page_4]

82      Predicting hydraulic properties may be accurate enough to be used in water- and solute- [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   transport models (Wösten et al., 2001). One promising solution to managing the scarcity of [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   head or water-retention curve parameters using continuous input variables such as granulometric [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   fractions, bulk density and soil organic carbon content (Al Majou et al., 2008a; Rawls et al., 1982). [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   More recently, novel machine-learning methods have been used to develop PTFs based on [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   regression trees (i.e. “tree-PTFs”) (Toth et al. 2015). [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   Land cover (Nemes et al., 2003) or soil management (Tóth et al., 2015) have also been proposed, [SOURCE: Page_5]

203           2.4.2 Regression tree [SOURCE: Page_10]

204           Regression tree methods consist of recursive binary partitions of a dataset. At each node, [SOURCE: Page_10]

205   observations are split according to a decision rule based on only one predictor. Splitting continues [SOURCE: Page_10]

206   until all of the subsets (i.e. “terminal nodes” of the tree) are as homogeneous as possible with [SOURCE: Page_10]

207   reference to the response variable (Hastie et al., 2009; Prasad et al., 2006). Splitting stops when the [SOURCE: Page_10]

208   subset reaches a minimum size of 5 data points or when no more improvement can be made. The [SOURCE: Page_10]

209   criterion used to decide which predictor splits the data best is based on ANOVA. First, a maximum [SOURCE: Page_10]

210   tree is grown that likely overfits the training data. To reduce the size of the tree and avoid [SOURCE: Page_10]

211   overfitting, the tree is then pruned using cost-complexity pruning (10 cross validation). Briefly, for [SOURCE: Page_10]

212   each pair of terminal leaves with a common parent node, the error in classifying the testing dataset [SOURCE: Page_10]

213   is calculated to see whether the sum of squares would be smaller by turning the parent nodes into a [SOURCE: Page_10]

214   terminal leaf. The procedure is repeated until the pruning does not decrease the error in the testing [SOURCE: Page_10]

215   data. The resulting pruned tree is usually smaller than the initial maximum tree, but in theory, [SOURCE: Page_10]

216   pruned trees can range from the maximum size to minimum size (no partitions, no tree). The size of [SOURCE: Page_10]

217   the pruned tree can depend on the cross-validation method used. The pruned tree to be used as a [SOURCE: Page_10]

218   model for each water content was then randomly selected. The response variable was volumetric [SOURCE: Page_10]

219   water content at a given matric head, and the terminal nodes of the tree represented mean water [SOURCE: Page_10]

220   content in the partitions. The “rpart” R package (Therneau and Atkinson, 2019) was used to build [SOURCE: Page_10]

221   the trees. [SOURCE: Page_10]

395      The published PTFs may have had low-quality predictions for several reasons. First, differences [SOURCE: Page_18]

396   in the sampling or measurement protocol between the databases used to develop the PTFs and the [SOURCE: Page_18]

397   dataset that we used may be a source of uncertainty (Román Dobarco et al., 2019). For example, Al [SOURCE: Page_18]

398   Majou et al. (2008b) measured water content using undisturbed aggregates (10-15 cm^3), whereas we [SOURCE: Page_18]

399   used undisturbed soil cylinders (50 cm^3). Several studies have also highlighted the influence of [SOURCE: Page_18]

400   sample size on soil water retention and the quality of PTFs developed (Ghanbarian et al., 2015; [SOURCE: Page_18]

401   Silva et al., 2018). Furthermore, some of these PTFs were developed from large databases collated [SOURCE: Page_18]

402   in the USA or Europe and covered a wide range of sand, silt, clay and organic matter contents and [SOURCE: Page_18]

403   bulk densities (Rawls et al., 1982; Tóth et al., 2015). Like Cornelis et al. (2001), we calculated the [SOURCE: Page_18]

404   ranges of the soil properties of our samples and found that all lay within those in the databases from [SOURCE: Page_18]

405   the USA and Europe; nevertheless, the predictions were unsatisfactory according to the criteria. [SOURCE: Page_18]

406   Nemes et al. (2003) suggested that using a small set of relevant data rather than a larger, more [SOURCE: Page_18]

407   general dataset can produce more accurate PTFs. Indeed, for predicting Hungarian soils, they found [SOURCE: Page_18]

408   that PTFs that had been developed by neural networks from data from throughout the USA and [SOURCE: Page_18]

409   Europe performed worse than PTFs that had been developed from a smaller dataset that considered [SOURCE: Page_18]

410   the pedoclimatic context (e.g. the subset of Hungarian soils). Testing published PTFs developed [SOURCE: Page_18]

411   from large and general datasets with our dataset may explain the poor prediction in our study. [SOURCE: Page_18]

412   However, most of the PTFs tested were developed from French databases (Bruand et al., 2004; Al [SOURCE: Page_18]

413   Majou et al., 2007, 2008a, 2008b; Román Dobarco et al., 2019) and should have been more [SOURCE: Page_18]

414   appropriate for predicting water content of the soils in our dataset. These French PTFs, however, [SOURCE: Page_18]

415   did not necessarily perform better than those developed by Tóth et al. (2015) at the European scale. [SOURCE: Page_18]

416   They did, however, perform better than those of Rawls et al. (1982), which were developed from [SOURCE: Page_18]

417   soil samples from the USA, which appeared to be unsuitable (criteria among the worst for each PTF [SOURCE: Page_18]

418   evaluated), except when using other water contents as predictors. The poor performance of the 17 [SOURCE: Page_18]

Soil and Tillage Research [SOURCE: Page_19]

419   French PTFs was not related to the ranges of soil properties in our dataset, because all of them fell [SOURCE: Page_19]

420   within the domain of applicability of the PTFs tested. Moreover, a metric distance representing a [SOURCE: Page_19]

421   PTF’s domain of applicability, developed by Tranter et al. (2009), was calculated for two of the [SOURCE: Page_19]

422   published PTFs whose training dataset was available (M9_Co and M10_Co). Overall, 97% of the [SOURCE: Page_19]

427   than point PTFs, as relations between water-retention curve parameters and basic soil properties are [SOURCE: Page_19]

428   complex. The same basic soil properties do not necessarily describe the variability in water content [SOURCE: Page_19]

469   important for predicting water content due to an indirect influence, such as organic carbon, which [SOURCE: Page_21]

470   plays both an indirect role, by improving soil structure, and a direct role, through its adsorption [SOURCE: Page_21]

471   properties (Tóth et al., 2015). Cropping practices influence soil properties greatly, especially soil [SOURCE: Page_21]

472   structure (Strudley et al., 2008), and can thus influence water content indirectly. Román Dobarco et [SOURCE: Page_21]

473   al. (2019) suggest that land use should be considered in future PTFs, even though PTFs are [SOURCE: Page_21]

474   generally suitable for most agricultural soils. [SOURCE: Page_21]

475          However, given the similar cross-validation performances of PTFs developed from random [SOURCE: Page_21]

476   forests and multiple linear regression (which were even better than regression trees), our results do [SOURCE: Page_21]

477   not support the hypothesis that cropping practices are essential for predicting water content in the [SOURCE: Page_21]

478   topsoil (0-30 cm). We also set new parameters for two multiple linear regressions (M22_Co and [SOURCE: Page_21]

479   M28_Co), developed by Román Dobarco et al. (2019), that were among the published PTFs that [SOURCE: Page_21]

480   predicted best; thus, recalibrating existing PTFs rather than developing new ones may be sufficient. [SOURCE: Page_21]

481   Finally, when we developed PTFs from regression trees and random forests without including [SOURCE: Page_21]

482   cropping practices, we obtained nearly identical results. [SOURCE: Page_21]

483          In terms of quality of adjustment, random forests performed the best, with almost perfect [SOURCE: Page_21]

484   fits. This was likely due to the nature of machine-learning methods, which “learn” from the dataset [SOURCE: Page_21]

485   provided and thus perform well with it. Consequently, we also expected regression trees to have [SOURCE: Page_21]

486   high quality of adjustment, but their results were similar to those of multiple linear regressions. This [SOURCE: Page_21]

487   result was likely related to the pruning, as adjustment to the training data is purposely reduced so [SOURCE: Page_21]

488   that the model performs better with a test dataset. In our study, however, performance of regression [SOURCE: Page_21]

489   trees and random forests decreased between adjustment (i.e. the training dataset) and cross- [SOURCE: Page_21]

490   validation (the test dataset) (Fig. 5). While the poor prediction by the regression trees can be [SOURCE: Page_21]

491   explained easily by their well-known instability (i.e. a small difference in the training dataset can [SOURCE: Page_21]

492   result in a different tree) (Gey and Poggi, 2006; Yang et al., 2016), the instability of the random [SOURCE: Page_21]

493   forests was more surprising. Conversely, multiple linear regression was a stable method whose [SOURCE: Page_21]

494   quality of prediction was as good or better than that of the machine-learning methods. The [SOURCE: Page_22]

495   similarity between its adjustment and cross-validation performances demonstrates its robustness. [SOURCE: Page_22]

496   Overall, however, the cross-validation quality of prediction remained unsatisfactory in this study, [SOURCE: Page_22]

497   mainly for EFCV, which never reached satisfactory values for any of the PTFs despite having [SOURCE: Page_22]

498   satisfactory MECV (close to 0). [SOURCE: Page_22]

499          In France, few water-retention data are available in conservation agriculture, and the small [SOURCE: Page_22]

500   size of the dataset may have contributed to unsatisfactory predictions. Indeed, our study was located [SOURCE: Page_22]

501   in a single French region and contained data for relatively few soils (140 samples from 61 [SOURCE: Page_22]

502   agricultural fields). The dataset thus may not represent the wide range of French soil diversity. [SOURCE: Page_22]

503   Moreover, the lack of an independent dataset to validate the new PTFs led us to use cross [SOURCE: Page_22]

504   validation, which estimated only the quality of prediction of the modelling approach. Indeed, as [SOURCE: Page_22]

505   predicted parameter values of the PTFs changed for each soil, the structure of the model could not [SOURCE: Page_22]

506   be tested. Cross validation revealed that even the highly performing random forest method was [SOURCE: Page_22]

507   unstable, which may have resulted from the small sample size. Supplementing the scarce water- [SOURCE: Page_22]

508   retention data would advance development of reliable tools for conservation agriculture. In [SOURCE: Page_22]

509   particular, more data could have helped us better assess the quality of prediction of the PTFs [SOURCE: Page_22]

510   developed. The unsuitability of basic soil properties for predicting water retention remains a major [SOURCE: Page_22]

511   limitation in the development of PTFs (Vereecken et al., 2010). As demonstrated by the study, more [SOURCE: Page_22]

512   relevant predictors of water retention still need to be identified, as using three methods to select the [SOURCE: Page_22]

513   best predictors objectively still yielded unsatisfactory results. [SOURCE: Page_22]

514   Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_22]

515      We tested the performance of several published PTFs and newly developed PTFs using multiple [SOURCE: Page_22]

516   linear regressions, regression trees and random forests to predict water content at field capacity (h= [SOURCE: Page_22]

517   -100 or -300 cm) and wilting point (h= -15 000 cm). Although some PTFs approached satisfactory [SOURCE: Page_22]

518   performance according to the three criteria, none of them managed to reach it, which limits their use [SOURCE: Page_22]

21 [SOURCE: Page_22]

Soil and Tillage Research [SOURCE: Page_23]

519   in soil transport models for conservation agriculture. Most of our soil samples belonged to the [SOURCE: Page_23]

520   domain of applicability of the PTFs, so the poor results obtained are likely related to (i) the use of [SOURCE: Page_23]

521   unsuitable predictors, (ii) the use of PTFs developed at an inappropriate scale or (iii) differences in [SOURCE: Page_23]

522   soil management between databases. [SOURCE: Page_23]

523       This study, the first to develop PTFs specifically calibrated for conservation agriculture, [SOURCE: Page_23]

524   demonstrated that cropping practices were not necessary to predict water contents. The small size of [SOURCE: Page_23]

525   our dataset was a major obstacle and probably partly explains the unsatisfactory performance of our [SOURCE: Page_23]

526   PTFs, despite using methods designed to yield high performance. Future studies should use larger [SOURCE: Page_23]

527   datasets of soils under conservation agriculture, at more locations, to verify the preliminary results [SOURCE: Page_23]

528   of this study. [SOURCE: Page_23]

529       The machine-learning methods often selected CEC, which had not been used to develop the [SOURCE: Page_23]

530   PTFs. However, because of low performance, even by random forests, the results suggest that the [SOURCE: Page_23]

531   development of PTFs still lacks suitable predictors. Including more relevant soil properties when [SOURCE: Page_23]

532   developing PTFs thus remains a research path for improving PTFs. [SOURCE: Page_23]
