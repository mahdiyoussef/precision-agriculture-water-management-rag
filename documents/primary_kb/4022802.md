43   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   Urban densification has resulted in an increase of impervious surfaces, leading to increased runoff [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   rates and volumes, losses of infiltration (Fletcher et al., 2013) and other environmental hazards (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   heavy metals’ pollution) (Chu et al., 2019). Studies have shown that urban soils have an unpredictable [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   and heterogeneous layer organisation, poor structure, poor vegetation development and sometimes [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   high concentrations of persistent contaminants such as trace elements (TE) (De Kimpe and Morel, [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   2000). Besides climate hazards, the urban population is exposed to toxic agents, as the result of [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   industrial activities and traffic (Khalifa et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   Nature-Based Solutions (NBSs) are defined as the use of nature in tackling societal or urban [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   challenges (UCs) and maintaining biodiversity in a sustainable manner (Lafortezza and Sanesi, 2019). [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   For the scientific community, the concept is still very open and needs to be more clearly defined, also [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   to clarify its links with other nature related concepts (e.g. green infrastructure, ecosystem-based [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   approaches) (Nesshöver et al., 2017). Nevertheless, there is a growing consensus on the key aspects [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   that frame the concept. NBS can be developed in different environments, from natural and rural areas [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   to more anthropized areas and cities. Promoting the idea of getting more nature in cities, the EU is [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   currently especially pushing to develop NBS in urban context (Faivre et al., 2017). The presence of [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   NBSs in urban areas instead of surfaces with high thermal inertia that store heat limits the overheating [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   of the urban surface (Emmanuel and Loconsole, 2015) and thus improves the thermal comfort of [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   people (Depietri and McPhearson, 2017; Kabisch et al., 2017). Moreover, as cities have been blamed [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   for contributing disproportionately to global climate change (Dodman, 2009), NBSs associating [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   vegetation and soil can provide a mean to mitigate climate change by storing/sequestering carbon and [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   thus reduce the overall greenhouse gases emissions (GHG) of the urban areas (Velasco et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   NBSs used in urban water management help get closer to a natural water cycle (Wild et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   They are currently named under diverse terminologies, according to the country and/or the language [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   (sustainable urban drainage systems “SUDS”, low impact development “LID”, best management [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   practice “BMP”, see Fletcher et al., 2015). They are usually based on increasing storage, infiltration [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   and/or evapotranspiration processes. Indeed, NBSs have the ability to limit surface runoff, increase [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   water retention during stormy episodes and flood protection compared to impervious surfaces (Liquete [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   et al., 2016) and improve rainwater and runoff waters quality (Husseini et al., 2013). Thus they can [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   mitigate floods by source storm water storage, they lead to a more sustainable urban water [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   management by favouring groundwater recharge and decreasing impervious surfaces (Granados‐ [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   Olivas et al., 2016). However, due to possible contamination of water and urban soils by heavy metals [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   (Krishna and Govil, 2008; Szekeres et al., 2018), salts and hydrocarbon in particular, the potential [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   transfer of pollutants to the groundwater by recharge during water management should be considered [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   (Carlson et al., 2011; Ostendorf et al., 2009)(Ostendorf et al., 2009; Carlson et al., 2011). The [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   treatment performance will highly depend on the design of SUDS and both quality of runoff waters [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   and soils (Fardel et al., 2020). Urban water management and quality (UWMQ) challenges are usually [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   examined at the catchment scale (e.g. (Obropta and Kardos, 2007)) that can also be compared to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   neighbourhood scale (extended areas of a city that have relatively uniform land use with dimensions [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   ranging from 0.5 to 4.0 kilometers) for climate issues, for example (Stein, 2014). Indeed, the [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   neighbourhood scale is the smaller scale that integrates a certain complexity of urban pattern [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   (associating buildings, streets, green spaces, etc.) and enables the observation of the interactions [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   between the urban pattern's individual elements. However, the performance and the hydrological [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   behaviour of NBSs are more often studied at the local scale (Golden and Hoghooghi, 2018). As a [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   combination of different NBSs stands as the solution rather than only one type of NBSs, and as [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   sustainable water management is more a large spatial and time scales issue, the city scale has also to [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   be studied, municipal or metropolitan perimeters being especially relevant because they also coincide [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   with administrative boundaries within which the strategies of local authorities are developed. The [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   different scales are described in the supplementary material (Section A$_1$). Evaluation of mixed [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   scenarios of NBSs at the city scale can also make it possible to avoid negative joint effects or to [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   promote positive ones (Gunawardena et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   In urban areas, soils are often stripped, filled, mixed, compacted and supplemented with artificial [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   materials. The soil structure is modified, and built structures and drainage infrastructures are [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   introduced. NBSs can improve soil properties on two aspects. The first category consists of solutions [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   based on soil modifications, improvement of soil functions and, consequently, the resilience of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   built ecosystem to external factors. These solutions are based on the concept of soil health (Brady et [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   al., 2008). The second category includes solutions that are based on altering flows (water, sediment, [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   nutrients, pollutants) based on the concept of connectivity (Parsons et al., 2015). It include water [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   catchment, a range of aspects and topography, varied climatic conditions, sufficient species richness [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   and ecosystem function to allow for multi-functionality of interactions, a number of habitats and [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   sufficient area to permit the ingress of plant species and movement of vectors and animals (Turner, [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   2005) and understood in the landscape ecology meaning, that is to say an assemblage of [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   ecosystems interacting occurring in a geo-graphically defined region (Haber, 1990). The [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   inclusion of NBSs in the urban planning strategy would contribute to the mitigation of some major [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   environmental problems (e.g. loss of organic carbon) (Bouzouidja et al., 2019). Indeed, Foucault et al. [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   (2013) observed at a battery recycling site in Toulouse (France), that green manure increased soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   respiration rate by 25 to 50%, leading to a better organic matter humidification process and thus an [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   increase in organic carbon stock. The objective of adding NBSs related to soil quality and [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   management is to allow it to perform two essential functions, which are as follows: (i) to be a support [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   for vegetation. This function is possible if the urban soil has an appropriate agronomic quality (Morel [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   et al., 2015), (ii) to promote the filtration and epuration of urban water. [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   On the one hand, we have highlighted the complexity of the current challenges in urban areas. On the [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   other hand, it appears that nature-based solutions (NBSs) can efficiently address current environmental [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   issues (climate change adaptation, urban water and soil management) and adapt to each local context. [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   Implementing NBSs in urban projects would contribute to the mitigation of major environmental [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   issues and to the development of sustainable and resilient cities. This objective requires a [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   reconsideration of the management of urban areas and the development of adapted methodology, [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   methods and models. Currently, various studies and projects funded by the EU’s Horizon 2020 [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   benefits and to guide the design and implementation of urban development projects and cross-sectoral [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   policies. Cooperation between urban scientists (climate change, water and soil management) should [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   therefore be promoted. Consequently, to develop sustainable management of urban areas, it is of [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   utmost importance to use expert models and methods (EMMs) that take into account UCs and the [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   benefits of NBSs. These EMMs inform us about the performance of NBSs using various urban [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   performance indicators (UPIs) (e.g. Peak flow variation) (Montazeri et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   multiple challenges. Our issue is that the NBS assessment framework cannot be the sum of indicators [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   and EMMs of each challenge addressed. The main aim of this study is to analyse a set of current [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   simulations of EMMs dedicated to urban microclimate, water and soil management, in order to answer [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   the following questions: What are the main variables or list of indicators computed in these EMMs? [SOURCE: Page_6]

238   The performance assessment of water management and quality for NBS is conducted in N4C project [SOURCE: Page_16]

239   at “parcel”, neighbourhood and city (a set of catchments and sub catchments) scales for swales, green [SOURCE: Page_16]

240   roof and vegetated areas (SUDS) by using different methods. The EMM was parameterised with [SOURCE: Page_16]

241   respect to cover rate and vegetation height (low vegetation and high vegetation mixed). For urban [SOURCE: Page_16]

242   stormwater quantity purposes, the method consists of using urban hydrological modelling. For urban [SOURCE: Page_16]

243   stormwater quality purposes, the method uses the adapted comparison between physico-chemical [SOURCE: Page_16]

244   properties, nutrients concentrations and “micro-pollutants concentrations of water” and European [SOURCE: Page_16]

245   standards for surface waters. Concerning storm water quality, the “good status” of surface waters [SOURCE: Page_16]

246   defined in the EU Water Framework Directive (WFD) relies both on the ecological and the chemical [SOURCE: Page_16]

247   status. The former includes biological indicators, physicochemical parameters controlling biological [SOURCE: Page_16]

248   status, specific pollutants of ecological status and hydro-morphological parameters. The latter is based [SOURCE: Page_16]

249   on the compliance with environmental quality standards (EQSs). The EQSs in Directive 2008/105/EC [SOURCE: Page_16]

250   are concentration limits of the priority substances (45) and 8 other pollutants in water (or biota), i.e. [SOURCE: Page_16]

251   concentration limits. In the case of water quality evaluation for SUDS, the WFD scheme is simplified [SOURCE: Page_16]

252   as biological indicators and hydro-morphological parameters are not relevant. Table 4 summarizes the [SOURCE: Page_16]

253   selected UPI attached to the EMMs related to the water management challenge. Additionally, the [SOURCE: Page_16]

254   groundwater quality, potentially impacted by NBS stormwater infiltration, should be taken in [SOURCE: Page_16]

255   consideration with the same significance as the surface water quality, given the value of groundwater [SOURCE: Page_16]

256   for drinking water supply (Standen et al., 2020). In terms of storm water runoff, NBSs can positively [SOURCE: Page_16]

257   influence the regulation of surface runoff due to their retention potential. Zölch et al., (2017) used a [SOURCE: Page_16]

258   PFV indicator and found that the vegetation together with the substrate store rainwater and make it [SOURCE: Page_16]

259   available for evapotranspiration (1.4% and 14%). In addition, NBSs could increase water retention [SOURCE: Page_16]

260   during rain events. For example, 20% to 100% (10 mm h-1 to 3 mm h-1 respectively) of rainfall were [SOURCE: Page_16]

261   stored (e.g. (Carter and Rasmussen, 2006). Trees planted on prairie slopes increased water storage [SOURCE: Page_16]

262   under their canopy, again reducing erosion and surface water runoff (Joffre and Rambal, 1993). For [SOURCE: Page_16]

263   example, Ellis et al., (2006) found that treed roads could reduce runoff from a grassy slope by 32 to [SOURCE: Page_16]

264   68% in a 10-year storm (24.5 mm in 30 min) and by 100% in a two-year storm (48 mm h-1 for [SOURCE: Page_16]

656   4. Towards the Expert models based decision support systems [SOURCE: Page_34]

657   The role of Expert model based decision support systems EMB DSS is to underline the enhancement [SOURCE: Page_34]

658   of strategies with scientific evidence and quantitative illustrations to help stakeholders for the [SOURCE: Page_34]

659   sustainable implementation of NBSs in cities. In the wider sense, this work leads to the development [SOURCE: Page_34]

660   of an integrated European reference framework on robust cost-benefit assessment of nature-based [SOURCE: Page_34]

661   solutions. In this way, a flowchart is presented in Figure 2. [SOURCE: Page_34]

662   The first stage of the EMB DSS aims to acquire data on the type of NBS to be characterised and the [SOURCE: Page_34]

663   UCs to be improved. It requires the definition and selection of UCs. Such an approach is carried out [SOURCE: Page_34]

664   through a combination of data collection. [SOURCE: Page_34]

665   The second step is the integration of the data into the first module of the EMB DSS, which aims at [SOURCE: Page_34]

666   identifying the interactions between NBSs and UPIs, as well as the classification of suitable NBSs. [SOURCE: Page_34]

667   This allows to assess the initial status of NBSs with respect to UCSs. This step also allows a first [SOURCE: Page_34]

668   selection of EMMs. [SOURCE: Page_34]

669   Then, the third and last step aims to give a semi-quantitative evaluation, depending on the scale [SOURCE: Page_34]

670   requirement and on the scores of the EMMs in relation to the targeted challenge. This evaluation is [SOURCE: Page_34]

671   determined from a selection of criteria (parameterization, documentation, reliability, modelling time, [SOURCE: Page_34]

672   user-friendliness) relevant for each EMM. These criteria are weighted according to their relevance to [SOURCE: Page_34]

673   the requirements of the EMMs. Finally, this flowchart makes it possible to generate scores (1) to [SOURCE: Page_34]

674   evaluate the relevance of an NBS in a given context, and (2) to propose a selection of NBSs adapted or [SOURCE: Page_34]

675   capable of improving UCs. [SOURCE: Page_34]

680   5. Conclusion and outlooks [SOURCE: Page_35]

681   This study has investigated the main features expected by Expert Models and methods (EMMs) to [SOURCE: Page_35]

682   improve and therefore optimize the services provided by nature-based solutions (NBSs) in an urban [SOURCE: Page_35]

683   microclimate, water and soil context. After a categorization of EMMs tools that may be applied to [SOURCE: Page_35]

684   NBSs evaluation in cities, the various tools developed in Nature^4Cities (N4C) project have been [SOURCE: Page_36]

685   labelled along with the various spatial cases and their ability to evaluate NBSs performance. [SOURCE: Page_36]

686   Key findings are: [SOURCE: Page_36]

687   - for each urban scale (object, neighbourhood/district, and city), specific EMMs have been proposed, [SOURCE: Page_36]

688   in relation with several relevant Urban Performance Indicators (UPIs). Although the studied EMMs [SOURCE: Page_36]

689   are not exhaustive, and could be replaced by specific tools proposed by other research teams, the tools [SOURCE: Page_36]

690   proposed here are totally relevant and advanced in the field of the main urban challenges treated [SOURCE: Page_36]

691   here, i.e. climate, water and soil management. [SOURCE: Page_36]

692   - A specific toolbox has been developed, regrouping the different EMMs. This decision support [SOURCE: Page_36]

693   system called EMB DSS, as “Expert Model based decision support system”, is innovative through its [SOURCE: Page_36]

694   multi-disciplinary co-construction between urban micro climatologists, urban hydrologists and soils [SOURCE: Page_36]

695   scientists. Thanks to a smart description and linkage of UPIs, urban challenges and NBSs features, this [SOURCE: Page_36]

696   DSS makes it possible to assess the ecosystem services provided by NBSs, including (i) reduction of [SOURCE: Page_36]

697   urban heat island; (ii) limitation of surface warming; (iii) increase of the thermal comfort of people; [SOURCE: Page_36]

698   (iv) limitation of the overheating and runoff of surfaces due to their imperviousness and the use of [SOURCE: Page_36]

699   materials that favour energy storage; (v) increase of the water retention during stormy episodes, (vi) [SOURCE: Page_36]

700   improvement of surface storm water quality at the outlet of the NBS; (vii) promotion of the filtration [SOURCE: Page_36]

701   and epuration of urban water and (viii) support in order to guide managers in the decision-making [SOURCE: Page_36]

702   processes required for the sustainable construction of urban areas [SOURCE: Page_36]
