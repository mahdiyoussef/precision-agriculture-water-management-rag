11                                               Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

12 [SOURCE: Page_1]

13   Drought is expected to increase in frequency and severity with climate change, leading to [SOURCE: Page_1]

14   more intense impacts on grasslands and their associated ecosystem services. Complementary [SOURCE: Page_1]

15   to ground experiments, remote sensing technologies allow for the study of drought impacts [SOURCE: Page_1]

16   with large spatio-temporal coverage in real-life-conditions. We aimed to quantify the [SOURCE: Page_1]

17   variability of grassland sensitivity to drought using a long-term satellite image time series of [SOURCE: Page_1]

18   394 temperate permanent grassland plots to identify factors influencing these sensitivities. [SOURCE: Page_1]

19   Accordingly, we assessed the slope of the linear relationship between satellite-based [SOURCE: Page_1]

20   vegetation status, using the standardized anomalies of the vegetation indices (VIs), and [SOURCE: Page_1]

21   drought severity, using a modified version of the Standardized Precipitation [SOURCE: Page_1]

22   Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI), from 1985 to 2019. The process was repeated for 24 VIs [SOURCE: Page_1]

23   and five SPEI timescales. We then conducted a linear model selection procedure, using the [SOURCE: Page_1]

24   grassland sensitivity derived from the most responsive VIs (i.e., VIs for which anomalies [SOURCE: Page_1]

25   indicated a tighter linear relationship with the modified SPEI), to identify which grassland [SOURCE: Page_1]

1 [SOURCE: Page_1]

26   properties influenced sensitivity to drought. A total of 29 properties, grouped into [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   pedoclimate, agricultural management, and vegetation diversity factors, were derived from [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   ground measurements. Overall, we demonstrated that the influence of predictors on grassland [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   sensitivity to drought varied across the drought integration timescales. Our results highlighted [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   the significant mitigating effect of soil water holding capacity on sensitivity to drought for [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   short timescales of fewer than 30 days. The date of first herbage use by farmers was positively [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   related to grassland sensitivity to drought across all timescales. We also demonstrated that [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   higher vegetation diversity significantly reduced sensitivity to drought. However, for the long [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   timescales of drought integration, such influence was mainly redundant with management [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   (i.e., shared partition of variance) suggesting complex cascading effects between agricultural [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   practices and plant community structure that still need to be addressed comprehensively in [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   future studies. [SOURCE: Page_2]

85   1.       Introduction [SOURCE: Page_5]

86        Meteorological droughts – in other words, deficits in the climatic water balance – of [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   varying severity, frequency, and duration affect several components of agroecosystems, with [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   serious consequences for agricultural production and environmental health (Howden et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   2007). Similar to other agroecosystems, managed grasslands are influenced by drought [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   impacts. The increasing frequency and severity of drought threaten the multiple ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   services – provision, regulation, and cultural – provided by grasslands and their associated [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   biodiversity (Bengtsson et al., 2019; Chang et al., 2021; Hofer et al., 2016; Zwicke et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   2013). Grasslands contribute significantly to milk and meat production (O'Mara, 2012) and [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   provide an estimated one billion jobs around the world (Buisson et al., 2022). In addition to [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   provisioning services, grasslands securely store an estimated 30.6% of terrestrial carbon [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   below ground in the roots and soil (Bai and Cotrufo, 2022; Lei et al., 2016) and host a large [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   number of species, some of which are endangered (Dengler et al., 2014). Unfortunately, [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   extreme drought events are well recognized to be detrimental to grassland biodiversity and [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   ecosystem function (Newbold et al., 2016; Strömberg and Staver, 2022). One of the most [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   evident consequences is the reduction of net ecosystem productivity, which reduces [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   agricultural production but also converts grasslands from sinks to sources of carbon (Ciais et [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   al., 2005; Lei et al., 2016; Nagy et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_5]

103        Knowledge of grassland sensitivity to drought and its determinants has emerged from field [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   experiments and, more recently, from Earth surface observations. Field observations and [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   semi-controlled experiments have provided, thus far, the most comprehensive insights [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   regarding grassland properties that either promote or suppress vegetation sensitivity to [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   drought. The most obvious properties, or drivers, are related to pedoclimatic conditions. [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   Higher sensitivity to drought has been found in grasslands that are topographically exposed to [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   solar radiation (Yang et al., 2020), situated at low elevations (Catorci et al., 2021; Gharun et [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   al., 2020), and found on soils with low water retention capacity (Buttler et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   Additionally, grassland management practices, which refer to the modalities of fertilizer [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   application and herbage usage by mowing and/or grazing, have been tested partially and [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   sometimes have revealed mixed effects. High fertilizer addition can either increase sensitivity [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   to drought (Bharath et al., 2020; Klaus et al., 2016; Rose et al., 2012) or have no effect (Vogel [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   et al., 2012; Weisser et al., 2017). More frequent mowing events have been related to stronger [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   negative effects of drought (Vogel et al. 2012; Weisser et al., 2017; Zwicke et al., 2013), and [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   grazing has been associated with greater sensitivity to drought than mowing (Deléglise et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   2015). Finally, experimental studies have further highlighted the mixed influences of [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   grassland diversity. Higher taxonomic or functional diversity has often been associated with [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   lower sensitivity to drought (Grange et al., 2021; Griffin-Nolan et al., 2019; Isbell et al., 2015; [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   Kreyling et al., 2017), but some studies have indicated an opposite effect of species richness [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   (Vogel et al., 2012; Weisser et al., 2017) or the absence of effect (de Boeck et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   According to these findings from drought experiments conducted in managed grasslands, the [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   properties influencing vegetation sensitivity to water deficit can be categorized into [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   pedoclimatic, management, and biodiversity drivers. [SOURCE: Page_6]

126      Despite their incontestable scientific value, the results provided by semi-controlled [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   experiments conducted at the field level reveal some limitations. These experiments are, in [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   essence, restricted in their design (e.g., limited combinations of rainfall regimes, levels of [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   diversity, type of soils, etc.) and geographic coverage. These limitations hinder the analysis of [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   complex combinations of potential drivers that prevail in real-life conditions (Fraser et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   2013; Matos et al., 2020) and prevent the generalization of the results to all biogeographic [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   contexts on Earth. In addition, those experiments usually report limited temporal coverage of [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   grassland responses to drought over one or few successive growing seasons (Hoover and [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   Rogers, 2016). Although coordinated and long-term observations and experiments (Fraser et [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   al., 2013; Knapp et al., 2017a, 2017b; Lemoine et al., 2016) push those limitations, spatially [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   and temporally wider analyses of existent grasslands are needed. [SOURCE: Page_7]

137      The rapid development of Earth observation techniques tremendously increases both [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   spatial and temporal coverage of agroecosystem monitoring (Ali et al., 2016; Anderson, 2018; [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   Arun Kumar et al., 2021; Reinermann et al., 2020). Therefore, recent studies have assessed [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   the response of natural ecosystems and agricultural lands to drought severity using satellite [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   images at a wide range of spatial scales (Jiao et al., 2019; Maurer et al., 2020; Vicente- [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   Serrano, 2007; Vicente-Serrano et al., 2013). Such assessment is based either on the [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   quantification of the relationship between the local satellite reflectance and climatic variables [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   (Cabello et al., 2012; Graw et al., 2017; Nanzad et al., 2019), or it is based on the satellite [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   product anomalies and the measured standardized drought indices (e.g., Li et al., 2015; Li et [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   al., 2022; Ye et al., 2020). Consequently, these relationships depict the sensitivity of vegetated [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   surfaces to drought events (Vicente-Serrano, 2013). Afterward, remotely sensed sensitivity [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   can be related to geographic variations of a set of environmental parameters, considered to be [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   the hypothetical drivers of vegetation response to drought. [SOURCE: Page_7]

150      Remote sensing (RS) analyses of drought effects on vegetated surfaces are based on [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   various methodological choices. Regarding drought estimates, studies frequently used the [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI), Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   (SPEI), and Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI). From here, the standardized precipitation [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   indices can be used to determine drought severity at different timescales (Vicente-Serrano et [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   al., 2010; Nanzad et al., 2019), but seldom considered in studies (Almeida-Ñauñay, et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   2022; An et al., 2020; Dong et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2018). Research that considered multiple [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   drought timescales has identified grassland and cultivated vegetation response to drought to [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   be best correlated at a timescale of one to three months (e.g., Almeida-Ñauñay et al., 2022; [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   An et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2018). However, these studies used monthly meteorological data. [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   Finer climate data resolution, such as weekly or daily, is needed to reveal more accurate [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   impacts of meteorological variations on vegetation property changes (Salehnia et al., 2018; [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   Wang et al., 2015). Regarding RS-based vegetation condition estimates, studies generally [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   used Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) or the Enhanced Vegetation Index [SOURCE: Page_8]

164   (EVI), or their derivatives, such as the Vegetation Condition Index (VCI), and the Vegetation [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   Health Index (VHI; Graw et al., 2017; Kogan et al., 2004; Picoli et al., 2019; Vicente-Serrano, [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   2007). Aside from these greenness-based satellite proxies, indices related to the hydric status [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   of vegetation, such as the Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI) or Land Surface [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   Water Index (LSWI), have emerged in other studies (Bajgain et al., 2015; Picoli et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   However, vegetation indices (VI), such as the NDVI, are used to represent multiple vegetation [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   properties and do not always perform well in the assessment of drought when implemented in [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   other ecoregions (Bajgain et al., 2015; Ebrahimi et al., 2010; Maurer et al., 2020). These [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   discrepancies in methodological choices between studies limit the generalization of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

173   published results and their comparison. [SOURCE: Page_8]

174      Thus far, the RS studies have attempted to identify the drivers of vegetation sensitivity to [SOURCE: Page_8]

175   drought through a focus on specific categories of drivers, namely, the abiotic environment, [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   land management, and vegetation properties, usually in isolation. Some of these categories [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   have been understudied in grasslands. The investigated drivers are in topographic factors for [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   forests and shrublands (Cartwright, 2020), and soil properties, such as the soil water holding [SOURCE: Page_8]

179   capacity for different land covers (Ji and Peters 2003; Thoma et al., 2019). Some studies [SOURCE: Page_8]

180   further considered the influence of land use (Burrell et al., 2020; Munson et al., 2016; [SOURCE: Page_8]

181   Tollerud et al., 2020) and, in the case of grasslands, the type of agricultural management [SOURCE: Page_8]

182   (Burrell et al., 2020; Catorci et al., 2021; Graw et al., 2017; Wagle et al., 2019). A final group [SOURCE: Page_8]

183   of studies has highlighted the importance of vegetation cover (De Keersmaecker et al., 2015) [SOURCE: Page_8]

184   and vegetation diversity (De Keersmaecker et al., 2016; van Rooijen et al., 2015) through the [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

185   lens of taxonomic diversity rather than functional diversity. These studies have contributed to [SOURCE: Page_9]

186   a better understanding of why some types of vegetation are more sensitive to drought than [SOURCE: Page_9]

187   others, although the influence of abiotic factors in grassland deserves more attention. [SOURCE: Page_9]

188   However, an important gap of knowledge remains in the assessment of the relative influences [SOURCE: Page_9]

189   of these different drivers – classified as pedoclimatic, agricultural management, and [SOURCE: Page_9]

190   biodiversity factors – at the same time. [SOURCE: Page_9]

191        In this study, we pursued two main objectives. First, we aimed to quantify the sensitivity of [SOURCE: Page_9]

192   managed grassland to drought at various timescales using satellite-based VI anomalies that [SOURCE: Page_9]

193   were best related to irregularities of climatic water balance (i.e., SPEI). This was conducted [SOURCE: Page_9]

194   over a 34-year period for a vast geographic region predominantly covered by typical Western [SOURCE: Page_9]

195   European grasslands managed for cattle and sheep breeding. Second, we aimed to assess the [SOURCE: Page_9]

196   relative influence of pedoclimate, agricultural management practices, and vegetation diversity [SOURCE: Page_9]

197   factors on grassland sensitivity to drought. To do so, RS-based assessments of sensitivity to [SOURCE: Page_9]

198   drought were analysed against 29 grassland descriptors measured at the ground level for the [SOURCE: Page_9]

199   394 vegetation plots of the study area. [SOURCE: Page_9]

203        2.1. Study area [SOURCE: Page_9]

204        The Massif central is a mountainous region ranging from 300 to 1,885 metres above sea [SOURCE: Page_9]

205   level in France. It exhibits four climatic zones: mountainous and semi-continental in the major [SOURCE: Page_9]

206   center areas, with influences of oceanic climate in both the northern and western parts, and of [SOURCE: Page_9]

207   Mediterranean climate near the southeastern part (Joly et al., 2010). The mean annual [SOURCE: Page_9]

208   cumulative precipitation, between 1985 and 2019, was 1,067 millimetres (mm) with a [SOURCE: Page_9]

209   standard deviation of 348 mm, while the mean annual temperature was 9.3 °C with a standard [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

210   deviation of 1.96°C. The 85,000 square kilometres (km^2) region is covered mostly by [SOURCE: Page_10]

211   managed perennial grasslands representing 60% of agricultural areas, which comprise one- [SOURCE: Page_10]

212   third of the French permanent grasslands. [SOURCE: Page_10]

214   areas of management with heterogeneous vegetation, topography, and soil characteristics. An [SOURCE: Page_10]

216   one and maximum of 10 plots). The subsequent analyses, therefore, were based on the 394 [SOURCE: Page_10]

217   vegetation plots distributed among the 143 parcels (Figure II - 1). These plots have an average [SOURCE: Page_10]

218   area of 25 square metres (m^2) and range from 2 to 100 m^2. The sampling design aimed to [SOURCE: Page_10]

219   represent the main types of grassland vegetation within the Massif central region (Galliot et [SOURCE: Page_10]

220   al., 2020; Hulin et al, 2012, 2019; Le Hénaff et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_10]

236          2.2.1. Drought estimates over the 1985–2019 period [SOURCE: Page_12]

30 [SOURCE: Page_30]

574      Using long-term satellite image time series and meteorological data, we demonstrated the [SOURCE: Page_31]

575   significant variability of grassland sensitivity to drought over a vast geographic region [SOURCE: Page_31]

576   dominated by open habitats maintained for cattle and sheep grazing. We further quantified the [SOURCE: Page_31]

577   influence of a set of factors related to the pedoclimate, agricultural management, and [SOURCE: Page_31]

578   vegetation diversity on the assessed vegetation responses. We found that their relative effect [SOURCE: Page_31]

579   and explanatory power varied with the duration and frequency of drought events. [SOURCE: Page_31]

581      4.1. Quantifying geographic variations of grassland sensitivity to drought [SOURCE: Page_31]

582      We improved the current satellite-based methods of quantification of vegetation response [SOURCE: Page_31]

583   to drought in two ways. First, we demonstrated, based on the comparison of 24 VIs, that [SOURCE: Page_31]

584   indices accounting for SWIR bands (shortwave infrared bands between 1.57 and 1.65 [SOURCE: Page_31]

585   nanometres (nm) for SWIR$_1$ and between 2.11 and 2.29 nm for SWIR$_2$) outperformed other [SOURCE: Page_31]

586   indices for detecting the effects of meteorological drought on vegetated surfaces. Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_31]

587   indices such as the NDWI and the GVMI were specifically developed for remote sensing of [SOURCE: Page_31]

588   vegetation water content (Ceccato et al., 2002; Gao 1996;) and have an immediate response to [SOURCE: Page_31]

589   moisture changes, while greenness indices – specifically, NDVI – exhibit lagged effects (Liu [SOURCE: Page_31]

590   et al., 2017; Tong et al., 2017) and are not directly related to the hydric status of vegetation, [SOURCE: Page_31]

591   especially during moderate drought intensity (Bajgain et al., 2015; Chandrasekar et al., 2010; [SOURCE: Page_31]

592   Gu et al., 2007). Although many studies have proved the usefulness of greenness indices such [SOURCE: Page_31]

593   as NDVI (Catorci et al., 2021; De Keersmaecker et al., 2016; Ji and Peters, 2003; Nanzad et [SOURCE: Page_31]

594   al., 2019) or EVI (Cabello et al., 2012; Cartwright et al., 2020; Munson et al., 2016; Zhou et [SOURCE: Page_31]

595   al., 2019), these were outperformed by moisture-based indices in this study. Second, we [SOURCE: Page_31]

596   highlighted the importance of the timescale of calculation of standardized drought severity [SOURCE: Page_31]

597   indices such as the SPEI. The estimated sensitivities differed significantly between timescales [SOURCE: Page_31]

598   ranging from 15 to 120 days. Generally, previous studies have considered only one timescale [SOURCE: Page_31]

31 [SOURCE: Page_31]

599   (Horion et al., 2019; Hossain and Li, 2021; Lu et al., 2021; Maurer et al., 2020). Other studies [SOURCE: Page_32]

600   that scrutinized multiple timescales considered much coarser ones, than we did, with monthly [SOURCE: Page_32]

601   meteorological data (Almeida-Ñauñay et al., 2022; Li et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2017; Xu et al., [SOURCE: Page_32]

602   2021). [SOURCE: Page_32]

603      Despite recent developments, satellite-based assessments of vegetation response to drought [SOURCE: Page_32]

604   may still suffer from a few limitations. First, the relationships between VI anomalies and the [SOURCE: Page_32]

605   modified SPEI were noisy overall. Indeed, anomalies of grassland reflectance may arise from [SOURCE: Page_32]

606   multiple natural phenomena, including pest attacks (e.g., voles increasing bare soil), [SOURCE: Page_32]

607   vegetation diseases, or compositional changes in the vegetation. Anomalies of the climatic [SOURCE: Page_32]

608   water balance index (SPEI) were computed from the SAFRAN data with fine daily temporal [SOURCE: Page_32]

609   resolution but coarse spatial resolution (8 km x 8 km grid). Despite the high correlation with [SOURCE: Page_32]

610   field meteorological stations (Appendix A), our estimates of the modified SPEI still may not [SOURCE: Page_32]

611   fully capture the fine-scale climatic variations, especially in mountainous regions. Second, our [SOURCE: Page_32]

612   procedure for calculating the long-term normal reflectance of each day and each vegetation [SOURCE: Page_32]

613   plot tolerates the 30-day variation of grazing and mowing events between years. We assumed [SOURCE: Page_32]

614   that management practices were closely similar from 1985 to 2019, however, we cannot [SOURCE: Page_32]

615   guarantee that sporadic changes in management over time have not occurred. Further [SOURCE: Page_32]

616   developments may address this issue in two ways: (i) detection of management events with [SOURCE: Page_32]

617   fine temporal resolution satellite products (e.g., Sentinel 1 and 2; Griffiths et al., 2020; [SOURCE: Page_32]

618   Kolecka et al., 2018; Lobert et al., 2021), despite the fact that the temporal extents of Sentinel [SOURCE: Page_32]

619   images are currently too short – in other words, eight years for Sentinel 1 and seven years for [SOURCE: Page_32]

620   Sentinel 2 – to estimate the normal vegetation reflectance along the growing season, or (ii) [SOURCE: Page_32]

621   precise recording of the daily sequence of practices along the growing season with the help of [SOURCE: Page_32]

622   farmers. Regarding other sources of disturbance, new RS techniques should be developed to [SOURCE: Page_32]

623   better discriminate the spectral signature of drought from other natural or anthropogenic [SOURCE: Page_32]

32 [SOURCE: Page_32]

624   disturbances and stresses (McDowell et al., 2015). Despite these methodological limitations, [SOURCE: Page_33]

625   we argue that our procedure provided at least an unbiased, although noisy, estimate of [SOURCE: Page_33]

626   grassland sensitivity to drought. This allowed us to provide better understanding of its main [SOURCE: Page_33]

627   drivers. [SOURCE: Page_33]

629      4.2. The strong pedoclimatic influence prevails at short timescales [SOURCE: Page_33]

630      We revealed the buffering effects of the soil water holding capacity (SWHC; Buttler et al., [SOURCE: Page_33]

631   2019; Thoma et al., 2019) and topographic exposure to solar radiation (Gharun et al., 2020; [SOURCE: Page_33]

632   Jiao et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2020) on vegetation sensitivity to climatic water balance deficit, [SOURCE: Page_33]

633   as demonstrated by previous studies. Obviously, these were highly expected. However, our [SOURCE: Page_33]

634   findings further indicated that these strong buffering effects hold true only for short and [SOURCE: Page_33]

635   frequent droughts, then completely vanish from the 60 days timescale (Bodner et al., 2015; [SOURCE: Page_33]

636   Finn et al., 2018). Interestingly, for longer timescales, our results revealed the emerging but [SOURCE: Page_33]

637   moderate role of the TWI. This indicates that large-scale hydrological processes related to [SOURCE: Page_33]

638   land surface topography may relay local pedoclimatic buffers when the water deficit becomes [SOURCE: Page_33]

639   too long, which may have implications for the management of agricultural drains. Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_33]

640   such land preparation either hampers or promotes horizontal movements of water in soils. [SOURCE: Page_33]

641   Depending on the topographic context, the removal of an existing drain or the installation of [SOURCE: Page_33]

642   new ones may thus help mitigate the impact of drought on grasslands. [SOURCE: Page_33]

643      The influence of soil chemical properties also prevailed for the short timescale. High [SOURCE: Page_33]

644   values of MgO and C:N ratio increased sensitivity to drought, especially for the 15-day [SOURCE: Page_33]

645   timescale, but the MgO influence was still significant for longer timescales. Magnesium [SOURCE: Page_33]

646   limitation is recognized to impede several ecophysiological processes that enhance drought [SOURCE: Page_33]

647   tolerance (Shao et al., 2021; Tränkner and Jaghdani, 2019; Waraich et al., 2011). In this [SOURCE: Page_33]

648   respect our results are contradictory. A first alternative explanation is that the selection of soil [SOURCE: Page_33]

33 [SOURCE: Page_33]

649   magnesium (Mg) concentration in our model does not reflect an effect of this chemical [SOURCE: Page_34]

650   component on vegetation sensitivity to drought but is a consequence of repeated droughts in [SOURCE: Page_34]

651   some of the vegetation plots. Indeed, it has been demonstrated that, under water deficit [SOURCE: Page_34]

652   conditions, Mg accumulates in the soil because of a reduced plant uptake (Sardans et al., [SOURCE: Page_34]

653   2008). A second alternative explanation is the influence of an unknown factor correlated with [SOURCE: Page_34]

654   soil Mg concentration. The soil C:N ratio response is directly modified by N fertilization [SOURCE: Page_34]

655   (Soussana and Lemaire, 2014), and it is expected to mirror fertilization response to drought [SOURCE: Page_34]

656   sensitivity, but in the opposite way because N is the denominator. [SOURCE: Page_34]

658      4.3. On the importance of herbage use [SOURCE: Page_34]

659      The date of first use by farmers was the primary management factor explaining grassland [SOURCE: Page_34]

660   sensitivity to drought for whatever timescale of SPEI considered. This was expressed in [SOURCE: Page_34]

661   thermal time (cumulative growing degree days). Doing so, the date of first use better reflects [SOURCE: Page_34]

662   grassland phenology than calendar dates and allows comparisons among plots located at [SOURCE: Page_34]

663   different altitudes while it minimizes the influence of between-year variation of [SOURCE: Page_34]

664   meteorological conditions. Our results indicated that late agricultural uses during the growing [SOURCE: Page_34]

665   season were associated with higher sensitivity to drought. The effect of the date of first use on [SOURCE: Page_34]

666   grassland sensitivity to drought has not been tested in isolation thus far; instead, it is often [SOURCE: Page_34]

667   mixed with cutting frequency (Zwick et al., 2013). We may still interpret our result in light of [SOURCE: Page_34]

668   the timing of herbage use and the occurrence of droughts during the growing season. The [SOURCE: Page_34]

669   timing of drought occurrence has already been highlighted to play a key role in drought [SOURCE: Page_34]

670   impacts on grasslands (Denton et al., 2017; Edwards and Chapman, 2011; Hahn et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_34]

671   Although droughts do not have identical occurrences between years, they often occur in late [SOURCE: Page_34]

672   spring and summer in the Massif central. Thus, late uses are more likely to coincide with [SOURCE: Page_34]

673   strong water deficits. However, it is well recognized that defoliation combined with water [SOURCE: Page_34]

34 [SOURCE: Page_34]

674   stress depletes carbohydrate reserves on which plant regrowth and stress tolerance depend [SOURCE: Page_35]

675   (Kahmen et al., 2005; Volaire et al., 1994) and lessens the maintenance of aboveground [SOURCE: Page_35]

676   productivity (Ma et al., 2020). Additionally, the influence of the date of use of farmers may [SOURCE: Page_35]

677   also arise indirectly from its effect on plant community structure, as we discuss in the next [SOURCE: Page_35]

678   section. [SOURCE: Page_35]

679      We further found strong evidence of greater sensitivity of vegetation to drought in [SOURCE: Page_35]

680   preferentially grazed paddocks than in preferentially mown ones. It should be noted that [SOURCE: Page_35]

681   usually mown grasslands may be grazed early in spring or during the autumn regrowth. Our [SOURCE: Page_35]

682   results confirm previous findings from grassland experiments (Deléglise et al., 2015). The [SOURCE: Page_35]

683   role of repeated defoliation by grazers along the course of the growing season, compared to [SOURCE: Page_35]

684   sudden cuts, tends to maintain grassland vegetation in the vegetative phase (Bloor et al., 2020; [SOURCE: Page_35]

685   Lei et al., 2016). As a result, plants allocate fixed carbon to leaf regrowth at the expense of [SOURCE: Page_35]

686   carbohydrate storage and root growth necessary to ensure soil water and nutrient uptake, [SOURCE: Page_35]

687   which can reduce their tolerance to drought (Amiard et al., 2003; Frank, 2007; Xu et al., [SOURCE: Page_35]

688   2013). Nevertheless, further research is needed to determine whether grazing pressure has [SOURCE: Page_35]

689   additive or combined effects on the drought response of grasslands (Ruppert et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_35]

691      4.4. The joint influence of vegetation diversity and agricultural management [SOURCE: Page_35]

692      Overall, vegetation diversity explained a substantial part of the variance of grassland [SOURCE: Page_35]

693   sensitivity to drought. Several descriptors had weak to moderate individual effects, but once [SOURCE: Page_35]

694   they were summed together, they had substantial effects, especially for longer timescales. [SOURCE: Page_35]

695   Such effects were largely shared with agricultural management. In this respect, we interpret [SOURCE: Page_35]

696   the role of vegetation diversity on grassland sensitivity to drought together with the effect of [SOURCE: Page_35]

697   N fertilization and the date of first use. [SOURCE: Page_35]

35 [SOURCE: Page_35]

698      Our results suggest a complex cascade of effects involving the influence of N fertilization [SOURCE: Page_36]

699   on vegetation diversity and the influence of vegetation diversity on drought tolerance. We [SOURCE: Page_36]

700   found that the Shannon diversity index increased grassland sensitivity to drought, whereas [SOURCE: Page_36]

701   functional diversity and N fertilization had the opposite effect. Regarding taxonomic diversity [SOURCE: Page_36]

702   and N fertilization, our findings seem to contradict those of several grassland experiments [SOURCE: Page_36]

703   (Kübert et al., 2019; Bharath et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2021). However, N fertilization is also [SOURCE: Page_36]

704   recognized to reduce taxonomic diversity (Humbert et al., 2016; Louault et al., 2017; Niu et [SOURCE: Page_36]

705   al., 2014; Socher et al., 2013) but, at the same time, increase functional diversity of growth [SOURCE: Page_36]

706   syndromes and the CWM SLA (Louault et al., 2017; Niu et al., 2014). Nevertheless, greater [SOURCE: Page_36]

707   functional diversity of growth syndromes may result in greater asynchrony of species [SOURCE: Page_36]

708   responses to drought, which has been related to better grassland resilience (Loreau and de [SOURCE: Page_36]

709   Mazancourt, 2013; Muraina et al., 2021). The role of functional diversity has even been [SOURCE: Page_36]

710   suggested to be more important than the potential effect of taxonomic diversity on grassland [SOURCE: Page_36]

711   stability (Valencia et al., 2020). Therefore, the positive effect of the Shannon diversity index [SOURCE: Page_36]

712   that emerged from our results may be interpreted as a spurious effect. We must warn that this [SOURCE: Page_36]

713   conclusion should be taken with caution for management recommendations. Indeed, the effect [SOURCE: Page_36]

714   of N fertilization in other contexts or at much higher levels of application may reduce species [SOURCE: Page_36]

715   richness to a greater extent and result in a reduction of grassland functional diversity and, [SOURCE: Page_36]

716   ultimately, an increase in grassland sensitivity to drought. [SOURCE: Page_36]

717      Beyond the direct influence of the date of first use on sensitivity to drought, as discussed in [SOURCE: Page_36]

718   the preceding section, the greater sensitivity of late-use grasslands may also be mediated by [SOURCE: Page_36]

719   changes in vegetation. However, our results do not allow to infer the underlying causal [SOURCE: Page_36]

720   relationships. Delays in mowing or grazing have been demonstrated to increase taxonomic [SOURCE: Page_36]

721   diversity when postponed from early to late spring or summer (Humbert et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_36]

722   However, taxonomic diversity had only a weak effect in our study and, thus far, the [SOURCE: Page_36]

36 [SOURCE: Page_36]

723   consequences of delaying mowing or grazing on functional diversity remain unknown. [SOURCE: Page_37]

724   Otherwise, delayed mowing or grazing may favor species with late phenology and reduce [SOURCE: Page_37]

725   light use efficiency (Gaujour et al., 2012), which may result in a lower CWM SLA. This is [SOURCE: Page_37]

726   consistent with our finding that lower drought sensitivity was associated with high SLA. [SOURCE: Page_37]

727   However, SLA reduction usually works as a phenotypic adjustment to water stress (Wellstein [SOURCE: Page_37]

728   et al., 2017), which contradicts our results. [SOURCE: Page_37]

729        Finally, we found that plant communities with heavier seeds were associated with lesser [SOURCE: Page_37]

730   sensitivity to drought. This has already been reported in semi-arid grasslands (Martínez-López [SOURCE: Page_37]

731   et al., 2020) dominated by annual species. Indeed, in stressful conditions, the post-drought [SOURCE: Page_37]

732   establishment and survival of seedlings are more successful for large seeds that contain more [SOURCE: Page_37]

733   reserves. Regeneration in permanent grasslands is mostly clonal and, in normal conditions, [SOURCE: Page_37]

734   depends more on buds than seeds (Benson and Hartnett, 2006). However, in a long-term [SOURCE: Page_37]

735   drought experiment conducted in mountainous grasslands dominated by perennials, Stampfli [SOURCE: Page_37]

736   and Zeiter (2004) found that post-drought vegetation dynamics were driven largely by [SOURCE: Page_37]

737   recruitment from seeds. We were unable to clearly discriminate how the CWM seed mass was [SOURCE: Page_37]

738   influenced by agricultural practices. Our result highlights the need to conduct new studies on [SOURCE: Page_37]

739   drought mitigation through agricultural management, with an explicit focus on how different [SOURCE: Page_37]

740   practices influence the composition and diversity of the regeneration syndromes of grassland [SOURCE: Page_37]

747   from Landsat images. Through the indices, vegetation was most responsive to drought for the [SOURCE: Page_37]

37 [SOURCE: Page_37]

748   60 and 90 days timescales. We demonstrated that variations of satellite-based sensitivity to [SOURCE: Page_38]

749   drought within and between grassland parcels can be explained by pedoclimatic, agricultural [SOURCE: Page_38]

750   management, and vegetation diversity factors. We underlined that the soil water holding [SOURCE: Page_38]

751   capacity (SWHC) worked logically as a strong buffer for meteorological droughts but only for [SOURCE: Page_38]

752   the shortest time scales of fewer than 30 days. Additionally, agricultural management had also [SOURCE: Page_38]

753   a strong influence, either independent or largely shared with vegetation diversity. This [SOURCE: Page_38]

754   suggests complex indirect effects involving changes in functional composition and diversity [SOURCE: Page_38]

755   of the grassland plant communities. Accordingly, such complexity may be disentangled by [SOURCE: Page_38]

756   future experimental studies focusing on the ecological consequences of the timing of herbage [SOURCE: Page_38]

757   use, tests of interactions between several management practices, and analyses of multivariate [SOURCE: Page_38]

758   causal relationships. Finally, better RS-based assessment of vegetation sensitivity to drought [SOURCE: Page_38]

759   is required to discriminate between drought events and other types of disturbances, whether [SOURCE: Page_38]

760   natural or agricultural. [SOURCE: Page_38]
