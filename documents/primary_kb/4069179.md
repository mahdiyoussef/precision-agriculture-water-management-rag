49   1.      Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

50        Mountain pastures are important livelihood systems in the European Alps, with a [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   multifunctional form of land use encompassing agriculture, outdoor recreation, and tourism as well [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   as conservation needs (Wanner et al., 2021). Rich in terms of biodiversity (Kurtogullari et al., 2020) [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   and cultural heritage (Jourdain-Annequin and Duclos, 2006), alpine pastures fulfil economic, social [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   and environmental functions at the same time (Bengtsson et al., 2019). They provide low-cost [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   fodder for grazing livestock during the summer period and - where traditional transhumance [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   systems are present – represent a complementary resource for Mediterranean pastoral systems [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   (Caballero et al., 2009). Shaped by pastoral activities, alpine pastures have undergone multiple [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   transformations over the centuries, mainly driven by the fragile balance between maximising [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   agricultural productivity and the limits imposed by the temporal and spatial dynamics of the climate [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   and forests-grasslands interactions (Kurz, 2013). However, alpine pastoralism manifests its [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   fragility in the face of the changes induced by recent global warming. Climate changes and their [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   impacts are visible in the alpine region, which has experienced a temperature increase of almost 2 [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   °C over the last century, along with an important reduction of precipitation in the summer season [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   (Gobiet et al., 2014). Specifically, droughts have been one of the main manifestations of climate [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   variability. Corresponding to periods of abnormally low precipitation, they alter grassland [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   productivity and quality (Nettier et al., 2010; Dibari et al., 2016) by offsetting the positive effect [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   of summer heatwaves on canopy greenness (Corona-Lozada et al., 2019), as seen in the European [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   Alps following a series of droughts (Calanca, 2007). The response of European mountain plant [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   assemblages to increasing temperatures (thermophilisation) also suggests a progressive decline of [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   cold-tolerant high-altitude grassland communities (Gottfried et al., 2012) and landscape [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   modifications with warming-induced upward range shifts (Engler et al., 2011). This may lead to [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   both a decrease in areas suitable for pasture and a reduction in pasture diversity driven by low- 3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   quality vegetation types in the Alpine chain (Dibari et al., 2020), together with changes in grazing [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   practices (Dibari et al., 2021). This is critical because most impacts on grassland ecosystems can [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   be related to overgrazing and changes in the timing of livestock transhumance, with high stocking [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   densities in particular causing a range of negative impacts on plant and animal communities, as [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   observed in central France (Dumont et al., 2009) and in the Italian Maritime Alps (Negro et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   2011). [SOURCE: Page_5]

79      In this context, appropriate management can preserve grassland biodiversity, maintain socio- [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   ecological systems (Altaweel et al., 2015; Alessa et al., 2018) and counteract climate-change [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   impacts (Nori and Gemini, 2011; Felber et al., 2016). Specifically for the western Alps, global [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   warming and the increased frequency of extreme climate events such as heatwaves and droughts [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   have raised awareness of the need to adapt, due to the combined effects of climate and changes in [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   pastoral practices (Bonet et al., 2016). However, in many alpine zones, specific measures to [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   manage pastures in the face of climate change are still not implemented, despite the implementation [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   of agri-environmental and climate measures in the Common Agricultural Policy (EC, 2013). Since [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   proper management is needed to ensure the environmental, social and economic sustainability of [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   mountain permanent grasslands, a multi-disciplinary approach is a fundamental starting point, [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   involving the co-responsibility of livestock farmers and local officers, as well as cooperation based [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   on observation, modelling and intervention (Della-Vedova and Legeard, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_5]

91      This posture forms the basis of the design and implementation of this study started in 2017 in [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   two representative areas of the western alpine territory: the Écrins (France) and Gran Paradiso [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   (Italy) national parks (PNE and PNGP, respectively). In the pasturelands of the two parks, ground- [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   based and remotely sensed observation systems, as well as model-based simulations were used to [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   identify efficient management strategies able to support pastoral management and the sustainability [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   of pastoral systems. Modelling adaptation strategies was supported by a participatory-based 4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   process bringing together different local stakeholders in the two case study areas. The target of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   modelling concerned the performance of pastoral systems and in particular the definition of [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   production while minimising environmental impacts. Remote sensing supports such modelling by [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   offering information on the spatial and temporal variation of important canopy state variables [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   which would be difficult to obtain otherwise. The involvement of local pastoralists was the basis [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   for the design and assessment of the analytical framework concerning the climate-change [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   adaptation strategies. [SOURCE: Page_6]

104        In the context of these alpine pastures, the objectives of this study were: (1) to inform modelling [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   via calibration with remotely sensed data; (2) to use the calibrated models to project climate-change [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   impacts, and (3) to assess a set of adaptation options for pastoral management identified by [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   stakeholders. [SOURCE: Page_6]

115        Established in 1973, the Parc National des Écrins (PNE) covers an area of ~91800 ha [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   (approximately in the range 44° 03’-45° 05’ N and 06° 05’-06° 35’ E) in the two French [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   departments of Hautes-Alps (region Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur) and Isère (region Auvergne- [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   Rhône-Alpes). It includes c. 70,000 ha of summer pastureland (~30% of the park area), which is [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   grazed by about 115,000 sheep (75% of the total stocking rate), 5,800 cows and >1,000 of goats [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   and horses. Transhumance (which is declining across Europe) is still relevant in the study area, 5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   with ~1/3 of the total sheep stocking rate in summer pasture being involved in transhumance (Brien, [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   2018). [SOURCE: Page_7]

123      The Parco Nazionale Gran Paradiso (PNGP) is Italy’s oldest national park (founded in 1922), [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   established in the core of the former Piedmontese royal hunting reserve of the alpine ibex, a species [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   of wild goat (Capra ibex) that lives in the mountains of the European Alps. It covers over 71,000 [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   ha approximately in the range 45° 25’-45° 45’ N and 07° 00’-07° 30’ E in the two Italian regions [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   of Piedmont and Aosta Valley. Most of the territory (c. 60%) is used for non-agricultural purposes, [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   a small part (c.11.5%) is covered by forests, while the areas of stable grasslands are constantly [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   decreasing. [SOURCE: Page_7]

130      The surface of both parks is represented by mountainous environments, located from low valleys [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   to very high mountains, with the highest peaks of 4,102 m a.s.l. (Barre des Écrins) and 4,061 m [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   a.s.l. (Gran Paradiso Mountain) for the PNE and PNGP, respectively. The territories of the two [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   protected areas are characterised by forests, from broadleaf in the lower parts to coniferous in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   higher parts, and by mountain and alpine grasslands and pastures. The climate is generally alpine, [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   but with different microclimatic conditions due to high variability in topographical features [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   (elevation, aspect and slope). In addition, there are different lithological formations. All these [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   complex and variable conditions produce a large typology of different plant communities [SOURCE: Page_7]

169   2.2.    Data collection [SOURCE: Page_10]

170      The Normalised Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) is a standard way to determine vegetation [SOURCE: Page_10]

171   cover and productivity. High NDVI values (approximately >0.7-0.8) indicate healthy vegetation, [SOURCE: Page_10]

172   dense and productive canopies, while low NDVI values indicate land with little or no vegetation [SOURCE: Page_10]

234   2.5.   Grassland modelling [SOURCE: Page_13]

235      Process-based models are important tools in agricultural and environmental research to [SOURCE: Page_13]

236   extrapolate local observations over time and space, and to assess the impact of climate and [SOURCE: Page_13]

237   agricultural practices on the soil-plant-atmosphere continuum through plant-soil feedback effects. [SOURCE: Page_13]

238   These widely tested models are also recognised as effective tools for studying the magnitude and [SOURCE: Page_13]

239   spatial-temporal patterns of C-N (carbon-nitrogen) fluxes, playing a prominent role in testing the [SOURCE: Page_13]

240   effect of specific changes in management, plant properties or environmental factors, and in [SOURCE: Page_13]

241   designing policies specific to the soil, climate and agricultural conditions of a location or region. [SOURCE: Page_13]

242   However, the results from different models often differ, presenting a range of possible impacts and [SOURCE: Page_13]

243   adaptation responses (Brilli et al., 2017), which are influenced by the models’ users’ knowledge [SOURCE: Page_13]

244   and expertise, and their understanding of the variables determined in the target agroecosystems [SOURCE: Page_13]

245   (Albanito et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_13]

246      Here, the soil-vegetation generic model DayCent (Parton et al., 1994, 1998) and the grassland- [SOURCE: Page_13]

247   specific model PaSim (Riedo et al., 1998) were chosen to simulate alpine pastures. Both provide a [SOURCE: Page_13]

248   mechanistic view of the multiple processes and interactions occurring in grassland systems and are [SOURCE: Page_13]

249   able to simulate grassland productivity and C and N fluxes under alternative management options. [SOURCE: Page_13]

250   DayCent is the daily time-step adaptation of the biogeochemical model CENTURY (Parton et al., [SOURCE: Page_13]

251   1994), which simulates plant growth, soil C dynamics, N leaching, gaseous emissions (e.g. nitrous 12 [SOURCE: Page_13]

252   oxide) and C fluxes (e.g. net ecosystem exchange) in a variety of managed ecosystems. PaSim is a [SOURCE: Page_14]

253   grassland-specific ecosystem model consisting of detailed sub-models for vegetation, animals, [SOURCE: Page_14]

254   microclimate, soil biology, soil physics and management to simulate grassland productivity and C- [SOURCE: Page_14]

255   N fluxes. [SOURCE: Page_14]

572   4.1.     Uncertainty in climate-change impact assessments [SOURCE: Page_35]

573        The issue of uncertainty in model outputs remains a real challenge for the implementation of a [SOURCE: Page_35]

574   modelling framework in decision-making or information processes. Uncertainty can arise from [SOURCE: Page_35]

575   several sources (model structure, parameterisation, input data, initialisation), and the way it [SOURCE: Page_35]

576   manifests itself in model estimates can be difficult to determine and requires interpretation by [SOURCE: Page_35]

577   stakeholders. In this study on climate-change and impact projections in alpine pasturelands, there [SOURCE: Page_35]

578   are different levels of uncertainty on multiple elements (e.g. biophysical, socio-economic). The [SOURCE: Page_35]

579   lack of certainty about the future is primarily related to prospective views at various scales: global [SOURCE: Page_35]

580   (socio-economic scenarios), regional (land and soil use) and field (pastoral systems and [SOURCE: Page_35]

581   management). In addition, the chaotic character of the climate system, with its interannual [SOURCE: Page_35]

582   variability, limits the reliability of climate projections. For instance, the CNRM-ALADIN RCM [SOURCE: Page_35]

583   used to derive forcing factors for downscaling in the alpine context (Rousselot et al., 2012) is [SOURCE: Page_35]

584   considered relevant for high temporal frequency climate studies in the Euro-Mediterranean region [SOURCE: Page_35]

585   (Nabat et al., 2020), but substantial improvement in simulating spatial patterns and annual cycles [SOURCE: Page_35]

586   can be achieved with an ensemble of RCMs (Fantini et al., 2018). In our study, the use of ensemble- [SOURCE: Page_35]

587   mean results to estimate mean trends for alternative RCMs does not take into account the variability [SOURCE: Page_35]

588   associated with different RCMs, which respond differently to the same emission scenarios [SOURCE: Page_35]

589   (Supplementary material, section 3), and represents a simplification in the modelling design. [SOURCE: Page_35]

590        Also in the case of vegetation response to CO$_2$ enrichment, there are large uncertainties in the [SOURCE: Page_35]

591   (complex) climate model projections and underlying scenarios. The extent of our imperfect [SOURCE: Page_35]

592   knowledge of the processes (and their interactions) embedded in models (epistemic uncertainties) [SOURCE: Page_35]

593   is reflected in climate modelling, where it mainly concerns atmospheric and biosphere physics, [SOURCE: Page_35]

594   ocean-atmosphere coupling, embedded empirical relationships, parameterisation and spatial 34 [SOURCE: Page_35]

595   resolution. At smaller scales, impact models (such as the grassland models used here) may suffer [SOURCE: Page_36]

596   from the omission or lack of consideration of long-term climate-related processes (e.g. plant [SOURCE: Page_36]

597   acclimation; Sándor et al., 2018) and their interactions (model structure), as well as changes in [SOURCE: Page_36]

598   parameter values due to new climate conditions (which may require re-parameterisation of the [SOURCE: Page_36]

599   model, e.g. Ben Touhami et al., 2015). Estimates of future conditions should be presented for [SOURCE: Page_36]

600   review by stakeholders, who can then assess the different levels of uncertainty in the multiple [SOURCE: Page_36]

601   elements and estimate their own relevant projections based on their own area of expertise. In [SOURCE: Page_36]

602   climate-change impact studies, a range of emission scenarios are used to feed climate models and, [SOURCE: Page_36]

603   in turn, impact models. Uncertainty accumulates and propagates throughout the process of climate- [SOURCE: Page_36]

604   change projection and impact assessment, which is carried out by developing fine-scale climate [SOURCE: Page_36]

605   data from coarse-scale climate models and feeding the resulting local-scale scenarios into impact [SOURCE: Page_36]

606   models to determine impacts and assess possible adaptations (Bellocchi et al., 2015). Emission [SOURCE: Page_36]

607   scenarios are neither forecasts nor policy recommendations (Moss et al., 2010) but are selected to [SOURCE: Page_36]

608   map a broad range of climate outcomes for further research and assessment, including impact- [SOURCE: Page_36]

609   modelling studies. In particular, policies contrary to those established to discuss extreme situations [SOURCE: Page_36]

610   (Cao et al., 2022) should not lead to a misuse of RCP$_8$.5 as a no-climate-policy baseline (Pilke and [SOURCE: Page_36]

611   Ritchie, 2021). [SOURCE: Page_36]

612      Our work demonstrates the value gained by conducting a process to assess the ability of a [SOURCE: Page_36]

613   modelling framework and remotely sensed products to represent past (observational) and future [SOURCE: Page_36]

614   (projected) conditions (two-time frames), based on the outputs of two climate scenarios (radiative [SOURCE: Page_36]

615   forcing), three climate models (climate forcing) and two grassland models (impacts and [SOURCE: Page_36]

616   adaptations). For the latter, the study identified grassland models sufficiently contrasted in their [SOURCE: Page_36]

617   ability to represent processes controlling the dynamics of energy, water and C-N cycles. This [SOURCE: Page_36]

618   choice was made in order to better assess changes in model estimates as a result of changing 35 [SOURCE: Page_36]

619   assumptions and to identify the most significant ones that reflect reality. In addition, this approach [SOURCE: Page_37]

620   helps to identify the range of variability over which model outputs may vary. In the selection phase, [SOURCE: Page_37]

621   two models were identified in which processes are represented at different levels of detail. While [SOURCE: Page_37]

622   PaSim is a complex (grassland-specific) model, simulating C-N and water cycles in detail (with a [SOURCE: Page_37]

623   module dedicated to livestock impact and livestock-atmosphere feedbacks), DayCent is a more [SOURCE: Page_37]

624   empirical (generic) model, with relatively simple relationships between driving variables and [SOURCE: Page_37]

625   fluxes. The two models differ in the representation of soil properties, vegetation type, agricultural [SOURCE: Page_37]

626   practices and environmental forcing, as well as in the initialisation of C pools. The common feature [SOURCE: Page_37]

627   of both models is that they were both designed to be applied considering the grassland community [SOURCE: Page_37]

628   as a crop with parameters describing the morphological and physiological characteristics of the [SOURCE: Page_37]

629   vegetation set at values that should represent the mean traits of the community. Consequently, their [SOURCE: Page_37]

630   ability to characterise interactions in multi-species grasslands is limited, beyond simple mixtures [SOURCE: Page_37]

631   of legumes and grasses (Van Oijen et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_37]

633   4.2.   Analysis of climate-change impacts and adaptation strategies [SOURCE: Page_37]

634      The two impact models adopted agreed in the representation of impacts such as the timing and [SOURCE: Page_37]

635   extent of the growing season and C-N fluxes, whilst divergences were observed for other outputs [SOURCE: Page_37]

636   (e.g. biomass production and peak production). The longer growing season simulated by both [SOURCE: Page_37]

637   models was mainly driven by the extension of the potential growing season in spring, whilst it was [SOURCE: Page_37]

638   limited during autumn-winter, reflecting long-term observations in large alpine regions [SOURCE: Page_37]

639   (Barichivich et al., 2013; Ernakovich et. al., 2014; Chen and Yang, 2020). The agreement in the [SOURCE: Page_37]

640   GS outputs suggests the ability of the models to reproduce the changing seasonality of [SOURCE: Page_37]

641   photosynthesis in vegetation, including the beginning and end of the growing season. Although [SOURCE: Page_37]

642   both models were widely applied in various contexts (e.g. Calanca et al., 2007; Abdalla et al., 2010; 36 [SOURCE: Page_37]

643   Vital et al., 2013; Ma et al., 2015; Ben Touhami and Bellocchi, 2015; Pulina et al., 2018; Fitton et [SOURCE: Page_38]

644   al., 2019; Fucks et al., 2019, 2020; Melo-Damian et al., 2021), this is one of the few studies [SOURCE: Page_38]

645   reproducing the dynamics in alpine pastoral environments, whose multifaceted structure of territory [SOURCE: Page_38]

646   and vegetation coupled with extreme weather conditions is difficult to parameterise due to limited [SOURCE: Page_38]

647   ground-based data for initialisation, calibration and assessment, the complex response of the [SOURCE: Page_38]

648   vegetation growth and information on critical thresholds (e.g. air temperatures, water needs, [SOURCE: Page_38]

649   radiation use efficiency) for mixed plant communities. As evidence of this, the mean plant growth [SOURCE: Page_38]

650   trend simulated with both models (20-year means, Fig. 5) does not seem to reflect the observed [SOURCE: Page_38]

651   pattern of very slow or no growth during the snow season, followed by a rapid increase in biomass [SOURCE: Page_38]

652   accumulation as the snow melts. Generally, after the onset of growth in May or early June, plants [SOURCE: Page_38]

653   grow rapidly, and daily dry matter accumulation can reach a maximum within a few weeks. [SOURCE: Page_38]

654   Grassland models in their current state are not designed to properly represent such a progression [SOURCE: Page_38]

655   of biomass accumulation with the rapid attainment of the first biomass peak. If this behaviour was [SOURCE: Page_38]

656   roughly captured with the calibration work (over three years of AGB data; Fig. S$_2$), and [SOURCE: Page_38]

657   discrepancies appeared less evident with PaSim. Also, the lack of overlap between snow-cover [SOURCE: Page_38]

658   periods and vegetation growing seasons was due to limitations inherent to the modelling of [SOURCE: Page_38]

659   complex ecosystems. Specifically, biogeochemical models are often unable to discriminate the [SOURCE: Page_38]

660   presence of snow and biomass at the same time on a given surface unit, but instead work separately [SOURCE: Page_38]

661   on it. Thus, to avoid speculation on the residual amount of snow cover and, therefore, period [SOURCE: Page_38]

662   overlap, the two components were showed separately. Considering that biophysical data show a [SOURCE: Page_38]

663   large degree of variability, and that discrepancies between model estimates and actual data are [SOURCE: Page_38]

664   common despite calibration efforts, research avenues can be opened from this assessment to [SOURCE: Page_38]

665   improve simulation models for such harsh environments as high altitude mountains, where snow [SOURCE: Page_38]

666   beds melt late and plants grow rapidly, but the rapid growth period is short during the middle of 37 [SOURCE: Page_38]

667   the growing season, and this pattern evolves with changing climate (e.g. Wang et al., 2020). The [SOURCE: Page_39]

668   extension of data series is indeed critical, as while calibrated models provide an adequate [SOURCE: Page_39]

669   description of the available data, they may not capture known trends over long time horizons [SOURCE: Page_39]

670   (Bellocchi et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_39]

671      Regarding C-N fluxes, the two grassland models agreed to some extent across study areas and [SOURCE: Page_39]

672   pasture macro-types, with differences in magnitude and patterns likely associated with the inherent [SOURCE: Page_39]

673   structure of each specific C and N sub-model (e.g. Cavalli et al., 2019). The higher C uptake [SOURCE: Page_39]

674   estimated by DayCent compared to PaSim (Figs. S12, S13 and S14) reflects the fact that PaSim [SOURCE: Page_39]

675   estimates the animal contribution to ecosystem respiration, which is not accounted for in DayCent’s [SOURCE: Page_39]

676   C budget. Similarly, DayCent estimated limited CH$_4$ emissions since (unlike PaSim) it does not [SOURCE: Page_39]

677   account for fermentative digestion in its C-N sub-model. The magnitude of N2O emissions was [SOURCE: Page_39]

678   instead similar between the two models for each study area and macro-type. Overall, the analysis [SOURCE: Page_39]

679   also revealed that GHG dynamics were mainly driven by weather variables in DayCent and by [SOURCE: Page_39]

680   livestock management in PaSim. In this way, PaSim estimates of CH$_4$, and CO$_2$ emissions may [SOURCE: Page_39]

681   better reflect observational studies that underpin the impact of management on the annual C cycle [SOURCE: Page_39]

682   of grassland systems (as well as cropping systems), in addition to the variability of local [SOURCE: Page_39]

683   environmental drivers (Pinares-Patiño et al., 2007; Ceschia et al., 2010; Zeeman et al., 2010, 2019). [SOURCE: Page_39]

684   In particular, the importance of quantifying direct CO$_2$ emissions from grazing animals is [SOURCE: Page_39]

685   emphasised (e.g. Pinares-Patiño et al., 2007). In the absence of observational data to compare with [SOURCE: Page_39]

686   the simulation results, we refer to the literature on the C sequestration capacity of grasslands, which [SOURCE: Page_39]

687   reports contrasting results similar to those of Table 4, which do not exclude that mountain [SOURCE: Page_39]

688   grasslands may oscillate between being sinks and moderate sources (e.g. Zeeman et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_39]

689      Although the two grassland models generally agree in their impact projections, they often differ [SOURCE: Page_39]

690   in essential details, for instance with regard to future peak pasture production. Among the reasons 38 [SOURCE: Page_39]

691   for these different results is the uncertainty inherent in the structure of the models, as well as [SOURCE: Page_40]

692   uncertainty in their parameterisation (and the generalisation of the resulting sets of parameter [SOURCE: Page_40]

693   values to broad regional studies), which in turn makes the projections themselves uncertain. When [SOURCE: Page_40]

694   considering the influence of these uncertainties on the interpretation and understanding of the [SOURCE: Page_40]

695   projections, but also on the direction of the research, it is of great value to know the factors behind [SOURCE: Page_40]

696   these uncertainties (Dietze et al., 2018). DayCent and PaSim showed different responsiveness to [SOURCE: Page_40]

697   water-related factors. The water-limited growth of DayCent rather hinders biomass growth, which, [SOURCE: Page_40]

698   according to climate scenarios, peaks before the start of grazing. With DayCent, the projected [SOURCE: Page_40]

699   scenarios indicate that the water deficit could be the limiting factor for summer growth, which [SOURCE: Page_40]

700   could be lower than in the near-past climate baseline. Consequently, DayCent projections towards [SOURCE: Page_40]

701   a greater C sourcing in the PNE are logically associated with water stress and water-limited biomass [SOURCE: Page_40]

702   production estimated by this model under future scenarios (Fig. 7), which limits photosynthetically [SOURCE: Page_40]

703   assimilated C (gross primary production). This condition requires further study of the uncertainty [SOURCE: Page_40]

704   associated with the model processes. Thus, the mean responses of alpine pasture production (and [SOURCE: Page_40]

705   related outputs) to climate change, obtained with two impact models, should be considered as two [SOURCE: Page_40]

706   extreme situations with respect to plausible future realisations: without water stress (liberal PaSim [SOURCE: Page_40]

707   approach) and with water stress (conservative DayCent approach). [SOURCE: Page_40]

708      However, there may also be biases in the simulation of SWC, with grassland models that may [SOURCE: Page_40]

709   not be accurate enough to estimate these dynamics. This is often associated with an unrealistically [SOURCE: Page_40]

710   low amplitude of the annual cycle (fluctuation damping, after Wu et al., 2002) of the soil water [SOURCE: Page_40]

711   content curve compared to field measurements (Sándor et al., 2017). It is known that the quality of [SOURCE: Page_40]

712   soil water content simulations can seriously affect model outputs. In case of poor estimation of soil [SOURCE: Page_40]

713   water content, model calibration may result in biased parameter values. Several factors, such as [SOURCE: Page_40]

714   permanent wilting point, root distribution and maximum transpiration rate, are in fact related to the 39 [SOURCE: Page_40]

715   rate of water infiltration into the soil during precipitation events and snowmelt periods (Philip, [SOURCE: Page_41]

716   1993), which would require detailed datasets for an accurate description. Because of the known [SOURCE: Page_41]

717   role of soil water content in determining evapotranspiration rates, stomatal conductance and other [SOURCE: Page_41]

718   processes, this issue has obvious implications for sites and seasons where water shortage is a typical [SOURCE: Page_41]

719   feature. The response of the models to water-limited conditions is thus questionable, which means [SOURCE: Page_41]

720   that the applicability of the models in semi-arid or arid pastoral systems may not always be [SOURCE: Page_41]

721   supported. This is to some extent related to the ability of roots to extract water from the soil (Volaire [SOURCE: Page_41]

722   and Lelièvre, 2001). The usefulness of soil water content estimation is not as straightforward as for [SOURCE: Page_41]

723   other variables and it is clear the development of improved models is fundamentally necessary for [SOURCE: Page_41]

724   soil hydrology, to rectify structural errors in models and to avoid systematic errors associated with [SOURCE: Page_41]

725   some of the model parameters. These may involve further study of runoff, diffusion and percolation [SOURCE: Page_41]

726   processes, while accounting for features such as ponding water formation, and snowmelt and [SOURCE: Page_41]

727   groundwater movement (Hidy et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_41]

729   4.3.   Effect of adaptation measures [SOURCE: Page_41]

730      The climate-change scenarios exhibited an increase in the air temperature together with higher [SOURCE: Page_41]

731   winter precipitation and prolonged drier conditions during the spring-summer period. This [SOURCE: Page_41]

732   condition logically translates into an accentuation of seasonality, with faster snowmelt and a longer [SOURCE: Page_41]

733   growing season (i.e. +15 to +40 days), providing higher estimated yields and evapotranspiration in [SOURCE: Page_41]

734   both study areas. In this perspective, earlier grazing dates and changes in livestock density, which [SOURCE: Page_41]

735   were a priori hypothesised as adaptation options, proved to be coherent to cope with these projected [SOURCE: Page_41]

736   changes. [SOURCE: Page_41]

737      The earlier grazing date better matched the future biomass peaks simulated by DayCent, also [SOURCE: Page_41]

738   agreeing with observations in alpine regions (i.e. Xu et al., 2016) and thus resulting in a more 40 [SOURCE: Page_41]

739   efficient use of pastures. Although this pattern paved the way for increasing the number of grazing [SOURCE: Page_42]

740   events during the growing season, reduced biomass regrowth due to dry summer conditions [SOURCE: Page_42]

741   inhibited the possibility of further grazing. Reduced soil water availability and increased number [SOURCE: Page_42]

742   of days of heat stress can lead to stomatal closure and inhibit biomass production in summer, which [SOURCE: Page_42]

743   must be taken into account in addition to the increase in plant photosynthetic rates with increasing [SOURCE: Page_42]

744   CO$_2$ concentration in the atmosphere, not to mention the possible degradation of grasslands due to [SOURCE: Page_42]

745   severe summer drought episodes (Moreau and Lorgeou, 2007). For that, any excess water in winter [SOURCE: Page_42]

746   can be used to reduce the water deficit when the soil is drier than the field capacity. While this [SOURCE: Page_42]

747   would hint at the possibility of using irrigation even at high altitudes to extend the number of [SOURCE: Page_42]

748   grazing events, the balance between costs (e.g. of energy and irrigation systems) and benefits (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_42]

749   increased end product) would need to be thoroughly investigated. In contrast, in the scenario [SOURCE: Page_42]

750   depicted by PaSim, which would be too liberal in the sense that it simulates optimal growing [SOURCE: Page_42]

751   conditions in midsummer (i.e. without an expectation of summer water stress), this adaptation [SOURCE: Page_42]

752   strategy would not be necessary. [SOURCE: Page_42]

753      The model results indicate higher biomass production when LD is increased (although changes [SOURCE: Page_42]

754   in livestock density do not particularly modify production levels). This result is likely due to a [SOURCE: Page_42]

755   higher N availability to plants provided by a higher amount of N excreted in faeces and urine which, [SOURCE: Page_42]

756   together with a simulated non-linear effect of grazing on production, led to a faster and higher [SOURCE: Page_42]

757   biomass regrowth rate. While both models consider N from excretion and the effect of grazing on [SOURCE: Page_42]

758   production, detrimental effects on biomass production due to the impact of soil compaction when [SOURCE: Page_42]

759   animal density increases were not considered. However, higher animal numbers may increase soil [SOURCE: Page_42]

760   compaction, resulting in poor water retention and altered (slower) mineralisation processes that [SOURCE: Page_42]

761   may reduce biomass regrowth and forage quality (Li et al., 2017). In this perspective, the response [SOURCE: Page_42]

762   of biomass growth to changes in LD could be partly overestimated or affected by a certain level of 41 [SOURCE: Page_42]

763   uncertainty in both models. Despite these limitations, we can conclude that the alpine region is set [SOURCE: Page_43]

764   to become warmer and wetter, and that yields in these areas are highly dependent on both water [SOURCE: Page_43]

765   availability and the type of management adopted. The projected climate scenarios and adaptation [SOURCE: Page_43]

766   options considered are not expected to substantially worsen the GHG balance, although a caveat is [SOURCE: Page_43]

767   that C sequestration by pasturelands may be reduced in a warmer climate. However, there is a need [SOURCE: Page_43]

768   to further develop and evaluate grassland models for key processes and outputs, such as CO$_2$ and [SOURCE: Page_43]

769   non-CO$_2$ emissions, as well as to systematically and more accurately characterise the extent and [SOURCE: Page_43]

770   timing of human intervention in a range of grazing areas covering broader climatic gradients. [SOURCE: Page_43]

772   5.       Concluding remarks [SOURCE: Page_43]

773        Research on mountain pastures in two western alpine parks shows that variations in climate- [SOURCE: Page_43]

774   change impacts and adaptations of these systems are linked to natural and anthropogenic factors to [SOURCE: Page_43]

775   different degrees depending on the pastoral macro-type class studied (defined by an altitudinal [SOURCE: Page_43]

776   productivity gradient). While the use of modelling approaches and remote-sensing products in [SOURCE: Page_43]

777   vulnerability studies is not new per se, the integration of these tools within alpine pastoral [SOURCE: Page_43]

778   communities has a point of originality, as the analysis carried out can help to solve [SOURCE: Page_43]

779   multidisciplinary challenges such as which areas are vulnerable and how they compare under harsh [SOURCE: Page_43]

780   climatic conditions. The findings of this study indicate an increase in the length of the growing [SOURCE: Page_43]

781   season by 15 to 40 days, leading to expected changes in the timing and amount of biomass [SOURCE: Page_43]

782   production and a likely decrease in biomass regrowth during the summer season due to prolonged [SOURCE: Page_43]

783   drought conditions. The greatest uncertainties were found in the GHG balance and mitigation [SOURCE: Page_43]

784   capacity of alpine pastures, where contrasting patterns were observed between the impact models [SOURCE: Page_43]

785   used (ranging from -350 to +100 g C m-2 yr-1 for NEE), mainly due to the different flux simulation [SOURCE: Page_43]
