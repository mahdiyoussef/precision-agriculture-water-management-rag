9 10   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

11   Pedotransfer functions are the most widely used method to estimate common soil hydraulic properties [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   at regional scale. Since they rely on an empirical link between textural and structural soil properties [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   observed in the laboratory on undisturbed soil samples, one must check whether the pedotransfer [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   functions built elsewhere also apply to the location of interest. Alternative methods to laboratory [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   analysis, such as infiltration tests, exist but are difficult to carry out at large scales. Here we propose a [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   method for selecting appropriate soil hydraulic properties based on the physical link between the soil [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   water diffusion properties and the plant water stress, which has been named the “evaporation test”. It [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   consists in (i) detecting water stress from remote sensing data in the Thermal Infra Red spectrum and [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   a simulated unstressed surface temperature, then (ii) estimating the date of the last irrigation/rainfall [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   event, the water content at the end of this irrigation/rainfall event, the unstressed evapotranspiration [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   rate and the average root depth and (iii) reducing the range of possible values for the hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   parameters to those that compute a time-to-stress that is consistent with the observed one, i.e. the [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   difference between the observed water stress date and the date of the end of the last irrigation/rainfall [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   event. The performance of this method is then checked for two sites within the frame of the SudMed [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   and SALSA experiments by comparing the resulting properties to those obtained by other methods, [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   namely the Beerkan infiltration test and the most commonly used pedotransfer functions. While not [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   providing a unique set of hydraulic properties, the “evaporation test” is a good mean to refine the [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   range of appropriate hydraulic parameter values at the scale of the Thermal Infra Red data. [SOURCE: Page_2]

4   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

5 6           Most water balance models, from the simplest ones (the FAO56 method, Allen et al., 1998) to [SOURCE: Page_3]

7   the most complicated ones (complex SVAT schemes, hydrological models based on the Richards [SOURCE: Page_3]

8   equation …) require an a priori knowledge of the soil hydraulic properties. Soil hydraulic properties can [SOURCE: Page_3]

9   be classified in two types: textural properties, that describe the statistical distribution of the size of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

10   pores, and structural properties, that describe the spatial organization and connectivity of the pores. [SOURCE: Page_3]

11   Both property groups are linked since the biggest pore sizes induce the largest connectivity and [SOURCE: Page_3]

12   therefore the highest conductivity. Texture maps are the most largely, and sometimes the only [SOURCE: Page_3]

13   available information on soil properties at regional scale. Therefore, for most agronomical or [SOURCE: Page_3]

14   hydrological applications, pedotransfer functions are applied to textural classes to derive soil hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_3]

15   properties (Bouma, 1989). Pedotransfer functions are usually obtained from selected laboratory soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

16   sample databases. Multivariate regression analyses are carried out to produce polynomial functions [SOURCE: Page_3]

17   that link common retention and hydraulic conductivity curves (like the Brooks and Corey or the van [SOURCE: Page_3]

18   Genuchten equations) to extended textural properties, at minimum clay and sand contents. Since [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   these regressions are obtained from a limited number of samples, either stemming from a single [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   region or from several laboratories (e.g. the Grizzly database, Haverkamp et al., 1997), their generality [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   is rather questionable. Moreover, given the wide range of published pedotransfer functions (See [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   Wagner et al., 2001, for a review) and the poor statistical link between structural and textural [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   properties of a given soil, it is advisable to test the relevance of each of them before considering using [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   one for a particular region/site of interest. [SOURCE: Page_3]

25           One possible way to check if one particular pedotransfer function can be applied locally is to [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   perform lab analysis, but it is costly, both in time and money. A second approach is to carry out simple [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   infiltration tests such as the Beerkan tests (Braud et al., 2005). Interpreting infiltration tests consists in [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   matching cumulative infiltration curves for ponding (well permeameter, Beerkan and double-ring tests) [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   or non-ponding (disc infiltrometer) conditions with simple analytical functions or more complex [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   mathematical expressions such as the Richards equation (Zou et al., 2001; Ritter et al., 2004; Mynasni [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

1   et al., 2005) that depend on the local hydraulic properties. Since these tests rarely provide a single set [SOURCE: Page_4]

2   of valid soil hydraulic parameters, alternative methods must be carried out to reduce the space of [SOURCE: Page_4]

3   acceptable solutions. At the same (local) scale, evaporation tests have been carried out using vapour [SOURCE: Page_4]

4   flux measurements (at the laboratory: Schneider et al., 2006). The physics of the extraction of vapour [SOURCE: Page_4]

5   from the soil porous medium is strongly dependent on the movement of the liquid water below the [SOURCE: Page_4]

6   evaporation front (Boulet et al., 1997) and, in turn, on the hydraulic properties. By minimizing the [SOURCE: Page_4]

7   difference between the simulated and the observed vapour fluxes one can evaluate the hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_4]

8   properties if an extra measurement of matric head and/or water content with time is available. Given [SOURCE: Page_4]

9   the size of the ring or the evaporation chamber used in these methods, these tests provide point [SOURCE: Page_4]

10   estimates of soil properties, whereas a global estimate at the scale of interest (usually the field) is [SOURCE: Page_4]

11   required. There is therefore no direct means to access field-scale hydraulic data without a costly and [SOURCE: Page_4]

12   time consuming lab or field study with multiple samples. Alternatively, eddy-correlation measurements [SOURCE: Page_4]

13   of latent heat fluxes are usually based on a much larger footprint and can be considered as [SOURCE: Page_4]

14   representative of the field; inverse methods consisting in minimizing the difference between the [SOURCE: Page_4]

15   observed and the measured latent heat flux have been proposed, from synthetic (Jhorar et al., 2002) [SOURCE: Page_4]

16   or observed (Gutmann and Small, 2007) evaporation data. But eddy-correlation systems are [SOURCE: Page_4]

17   expensive and need well-trained staff to operate and maintain them. Furthermore, it is not possible to [SOURCE: Page_4]

18   cover a large region in a short period except with a costly set-up. [SOURCE: Page_4]

19           Information on the water balance at the regional scale can be obtained routinely through the [SOURCE: Page_4]

20   use of remote-sensing data. Several experiments have been carried out to test the assimilation of [SOURCE: Page_4]

21   surface soil moisture inferred from active (Santanello et al., 2007) or passive (Burke et al., 1998) [SOURCE: Page_4]

22   microwave remote-sensing data to constrain soil hydraulic properties. But soil moisture is not easy to [SOURCE: Page_4]

23   observe through remote-sensing at an adequate resolution (for example the SMOS satellite mission [SOURCE: Page_4]

24   will provide data on 0-5 cm soil moisture at ~50*50 km resolution) or with sufficient precision (radar [SOURCE: Page_4]

25   data for instance is very sensitive to roughness and is usually acquired with a very sparse revisit [SOURCE: Page_4]

26   period that is incompatible with most hydrological applications). [SOURCE: Page_4]

27           Amongst recent methods based on remote-sensing data, several papers have proposed to [SOURCE: Page_4]

28   assimilate Thermal Infra Red (TIR) data into Soil Vegetation Atmosphere Transfer (SVAT) models [SOURCE: Page_4]

29   (e.g. Demarty et al., 2004). In the near future, we expect that TIR remote-sensing data will be acquired [SOURCE: Page_4]

30   every day or so at a resolution of less than 100m, which is consistent with the size of most agricultural [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

1   fields. Remote Sensing data in the TIR part of the spectrum provides indirect estimates of water stress [SOURCE: Page_5]

2   – defined as a function of the ratio between actual and potential evaporation rates - at the earth [SOURCE: Page_5]

3   surface. During the first stage of evaporation (“energy limited” evaporation) of an interstorm period, [SOURCE: Page_5]

4   water availability is large enough to sustain evaporation at a potential rate and the ratio between [SOURCE: Page_5]

5   actual and potential evapotranspiration is close to one: evapotranspiration depends only on the [SOURCE: Page_5]

6   available energy at the surface. During the second stage of evaporation (“soil controlled” evaporation), [SOURCE: Page_5]

7   the water content has dropped below a critical value and the diffusion processes within the soil are [SOURCE: Page_5]

8   considerably reduced. This critical value depends on the hydraulic properties of the soil. Since the [SOURCE: Page_5]

9   amount of soil water that can be easily extracted by roots is entirely controlled by the diffusion within [SOURCE: Page_5]

10   the soil, transpiration decreases with diffusion, and stress occurs; as a result the ratio drops below [SOURCE: Page_5]

11   one. During water stress, stomatal resistance is increasing and the available energy (mostly net [SOURCE: Page_5]

12   radiation) is no longer converted into evaporation but in sensible heat. The latter is less efficient in [SOURCE: Page_5]

13   dissipating energy and the surface of the leaves warms up. Surface temperature is thus strongly [SOURCE: Page_5]

14   related to water stress. Since surface temperature can be deduced from TIR remote sensing data, [SOURCE: Page_5]

15   stress and no-stress conditions can be observed from space. Data assimilation readjusts dynamically [SOURCE: Page_5]

16   the soil water content and/or the soil parameters to minimize the difference between the simulated and [SOURCE: Page_5]

17   the observed surface temperatures. Again, these methods are difficult to put into practice, and are [SOURCE: Page_5]

18   meant to be applied over a long period of time. Indeed, potential differences in surface temperature [SOURCE: Page_5]

19   are not always explained by inaccurate soil moisture content, but often as well by large errors in [SOURCE: Page_5]

20   estimating the water and energy balance parameters. SVAT models have indeed a large number of [SOURCE: Page_5]

21   unknown parameters, and the resulting observing system based on TIR data and SVAT models is [SOURCE: Page_5]

22   usually underdetermined. Here, we propose a simpler procedure based on the same idea: can one [SOURCE: Page_5]

23   relate water stress observed by TIR remote sensing for a given sensor resolution to the underlying soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

24   physical properties at the same spatial resolution ? For that purpose, a simple yet robust evaporation [SOURCE: Page_5]

25   equation (Boulet et al., 2000 and Boulet et al., 2004) for the second stage evaporation is used as a [SOURCE: Page_5]

26   tool to check what soil parameter values are computing the same date for the onset of stress as what [SOURCE: Page_5]

27   is observed under given conditions of uncertainty in the system. By restricting our study to water [SOURCE: Page_5]

28   stress periods, i.e. periods for which second stage evaporation exists, and by using a simple but [SOURCE: Page_5]

29   robust model, we ensure that the information extracted from TIR data is tightly linked to the diffusion [SOURCE: Page_5]

30   processes within the soil and therefore to the prevailing soil hydraulic properties. The duration of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

1   first-stage drying, or time-to-stress, is so closely related to the hydraulic properties of the soil that it [SOURCE: Page_6]

2   has been used as a surrogate to soil hydraulic properties by Salvucci (1997) for short vegetation. [SOURCE: Page_6]

3   Levine and Salvucci (1999) have later on extended the approach to all vegetation types but this [SOURCE: Page_6]

4   method requires time-to-stress observations for all interstorm periods, which gives little predictive [SOURCE: Page_6]

5   value to the method. In order to get a comprehensive and predictive estimate of the whole water [SOURCE: Page_6]

6   balance, one must have access to the soil hydraulic properties themselves. Contrarily to those [SOURCE: Page_6]

7   approaches, that model evaporation by replacing the hydraulic properties with a function of an [SOURCE: Page_6]

8   observed time-to-stress, Boulet et al. (2004) provide an analytical expression linking the time-to-stress [SOURCE: Page_6]

9   to an average potential evapotranspiration rate, an average initial condition, and given soil hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_6]

10   properties. Time-to-stress can thus be used to infer the latter if the two other inputs (potential [SOURCE: Page_6]

11   evapotranspiration and initial soil moisture) are known. This paper builds on this hypothesis and [SOURCE: Page_6]

12   presents an evaporation test designed to refine the range of acceptable soil hydraulic properties for [SOURCE: Page_6]

13   use in a variety of water balance models. This test is based on the evaluation of the time-to-stress [SOURCE: Page_6]

14   from TIR remote-sensing data and an analytical expression relating the time-to-stress to the hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_6]

15   properties, the initial water content and a mean potential evaporation rate. The latter is derived from a [SOURCE: Page_6]

16   simple energy balance equation driven by routinely available meteorological and ancillary remote- [SOURCE: Page_6]

17   sensing data. In the second part of the paper, the performance of various methods to constrain soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

18   hydraulic properties is analysed for data acquired during two international field experiments. These [SOURCE: Page_6]

19   methods are (i) the proposed evaporation test, (ii) fitting the daily evaporation simulated by the [SOURCE: Page_6]

20   SVATsimple model on the observed one, (iii) Beerkan infiltration tests and (iv) three commonly used [SOURCE: Page_6]

21   pedotransfer functions. [SOURCE: Page_6]

23   2. Theoretical basis of the “evaporation test” [SOURCE: Page_6]

24 [SOURCE: Page_6]

25   2.1. Concept [SOURCE: Page_6]

26 [SOURCE: Page_6]

27           The principle of the evaporation test has been briefly outlined above and is relatively simple: [SOURCE: Page_6]

28   the time-to-stress of a given surface is closely related to the amount of water that has been extracted [SOURCE: Page_6]

29   at a potential rate from the beginning of the interstorm period (say, after a heavy rainfall or an irrigation [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

1   event); since this amount depends in turn on the hydraulic properties and the initial water content, it is [SOURCE: Page_7]

2   possible to infer some information on those properties from an estimate of the time-to-stress if the [SOURCE: Page_7]

3   latter is observable with TIR remote sensing data. The evaporation test consists in two successive [SOURCE: Page_7]

4   modelling steps: First, a simple energy balance equation is solved to derive an average potential [SOURCE: Page_7]

5   evapotranspiration rate and a related unstressed equilibrium surface temperature during each [SOURCE: Page_7]

6   interstorm period for a given site. The difference between the unstressed and the observed surface [SOURCE: Page_7]

7   temperature is then computed for all interstorm periods as a baseline to detect water stress (section [SOURCE: Page_7]

8   2.2). Second, a modelled time-to-stress (section 2.3) is calculated for all interstorm periods for which a [SOURCE: Page_7]

9   reduction in evaporation due to water shortage is observed, using a range of realistic hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_7]

10   parameter values. The parameter values that provide the smallest differences between the observed [SOURCE: Page_7]

11   and the modelled time-to-stress are then kept as “appropriate”. [SOURCE: Page_7]

13   2.2. Detecting water stress using information in the Thermal Infra Red Spectrum [SOURCE: Page_7]

14 15           Water stress is classically related to a sharp increase in the difference between the surface [SOURCE: Page_7]

16   temperature and the air temperature. However, it was shown in Boulet et al. (2007) that using solely [SOURCE: Page_7]

17   the difference between the actual and a theoretical unstressed surface temperature is more efficient [SOURCE: Page_7]

18   than using the classical index as a baseline to monitor water stress. To illustrate this, Figure 1 shows [SOURCE: Page_7]

19   two dry-down periods selected for the B123 wheat field during the SudMed experiment (Chehbouni et [SOURCE: Page_7]

20   al., 2008) in 2003, one when the field is a bare soil (top), the other at the end of the growing season of [SOURCE: Page_7]

21   winter wheat (bottom). On the left hand side, evaporation time series in potential and real conditions [SOURCE: Page_7]

22   are displayed; potential evaporation rates are simulated with a simple energy balance equation (Boulet [SOURCE: Page_7]

23   et al., 2000), and the divergence between both evaporation curves corresponds to the time-to-stress. [SOURCE: Page_7]

24   On the right hand side, time series of unstressed to observed surface temperature difference show a [SOURCE: Page_7]

25   significant increase around the time-to-stress, while the difference between the surface temperature [SOURCE: Page_7]

26   and the air temperature does not show a clear trend around that time. [SOURCE: Page_7]

27           In general, the simple energy balance model used to compute both the unstressed [SOURCE: Page_7]

28   temperature Tsp and potential evaporation rate ep is a simple “big-leaf” model with a single “bulk” [SOURCE: Page_7]

29   source of energy. This description is consistent with the remote sensing data that do not discriminate [SOURCE: Page_7]

30   between the different elements within a pixel. The interesting feature is that since the “evaporation [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

1   test” will be performed at the scale of the remote-sensing data, it provides an integrated or “effective” [SOURCE: Page_8]

2   estimate of the hydraulic properties. An example of a simple “big leaf” model is provided in Boulet et [SOURCE: Page_8]

3   al. (2007) and is given in Appendix. Following this approach, the data requirement to compute Tsp and [SOURCE: Page_8]

4   ep is then: (i) Meteorological forcing data and (ii) time series of Leaf Area Index (LAI), usually derived [SOURCE: Page_8]

5   from time series of Normalised Differential Vegetation Index (NDVI) obtained from a combination of [SOURCE: Page_8]

6   remotely sensed reflectances, and a given LAI/NDVI relationship (Duchemin et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_8]

13   4. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_17]

14           The principles of an “evaporation test” using remotely sensed TIR data have been presented [SOURCE: Page_17]

15   in this paper. The “evaporation test” consists of two parts: [SOURCE: Page_17]

16           (i) detecting water stress as a sharp divergence between the observed and the unstressed [SOURCE: Page_17]

17   surface temperature time series and inverting the unknown parameters of the energy budget by [SOURCE: Page_17]

18   minimizing the difference between both temperatures before the onset of stress (information used : [SOURCE: Page_17]

19   TIR and Normalized Differential Vegetation Index NDVI data at representative scale; NDVI/Leaf Area [SOURCE: Page_17]

20   Index relationship; meteorological data) and [SOURCE: Page_17]

21           (ii) selecting the hydraulic parameters [hg, Ksat] that give a simulated time-to-stress consistent [SOURCE: Page_17]

22   with the observed time-to-stress (information used: difference between the date of last irrigation or [SOURCE: Page_17]

23   rainfall and the time-to-stress derived in the previous step; water content at the beginning of the [SOURCE: Page_17]

24   interstorm period; particle size distribution; estimate of the root zone depth). [SOURCE: Page_17]

25   This method allows for refining the range of valid hydraulic properties at the scale of the remote- [SOURCE: Page_17]

26   sensing measurements. The obtained range of values has been compared to i) the amount of [SOURCE: Page_17]

27   information one can retrieve from observed evaporation time series and ii) local estimates of the [SOURCE: Page_17]

28   hydraulic properties deduced from infiltration tests. It has been shown that deriving a rough estimate of [SOURCE: Page_17]

29   the time-to-stress from remote-sensing yields significant information on the appropriate hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_17]

30   properties compared to the evaporation time series measured by an eddy-correlation device. [SOURCE: Page_17]

16 [SOURCE: Page_17]

1    Moreover, the soil hydraulic properties inferred from this estimate, although spanning a wide range of [SOURCE: Page_18]

2    values in the hydraulic conductivity / retention curve parameter space, are consistent with the [SOURCE: Page_18]

3    estimates obtained by other means (pedotransfer functions and infiltration tests). The main advantage [SOURCE: Page_18]

4    of this method is that the information retrieved from TIR data is representative of the pixel size of the [SOURCE: Page_18]

5    TIR imagery. The main limitation of this method is that a field average initial soil moisture is difficult to [SOURCE: Page_18]

6    assess, especially with remote-sensing; a possible way to bypass this for irrigated agriculture is to [SOURCE: Page_18]

7    assume that initial water content is close to field capacity, but this remains a very crude estimate. [SOURCE: Page_18]

8    Moreover, if several land uses or irrigation practices are present in the pixel, the results of the method [SOURCE: Page_18]
