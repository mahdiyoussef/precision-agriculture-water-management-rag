11           Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   Riverine ecosystems form a dendritic network in which landscape and catchment-scale properties [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   influence freshwater community structure. Placed in a restoration framework, this suggests that [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   regional drivers can overrule the benefit of measures aiming at improving local habitat quality. [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   Disentangling the relative influence of local and regional drivers on freshwater communities is thus [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   crucial for ecosystem management and restoration. Along riverbanks, soil bioengineering is often [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   used to both control erosion and improve ecological conditions. Soil bioengineering techniques aim [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   at copying naturally functioning riverbank models and can thus be viewed as riparian ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   restoration. Nevertheless, these techniques are mostly designed at the local scale and are [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   implemented in a broad range of rivers. This implies large variations in regional drivers, which may [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   greatly influence the response of freshwater communities to restoration efforts. We studied 37 [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   riverbanks, from civil engineering to soil bioengineering, plus natural willow stands, in the foothills of [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   the Alps and Jura Mountains, and assessed the relative influence of local (terrestrial and aquatic [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   habitat conditions) and regional (water quality, hydrological context and land cover composition) [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   drivers on benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages. Using multivariate GLM and structural equation [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   modelling, we investigated variations in the taxonomic and functional composition and in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   diversity of native, exotic, shredder and scraper taxa to both set of drivers. Our results show that soil [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   bioengineering improved local habitat conditions, with an increase in the vegetation density and in [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   the aquatic habitat quality. Related changes influenced the functional composition but not diversity [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   patterns. Instead, we found that natives and shredders richness increased from civil engineered to [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   soil bioengineered structures. This direct influence of riverbank protection techniques suggest that [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   soil bioengineered improves instream habitat quality by inducing other changes in local abiotic [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   conditions (i.e., shade, water temperature, input of organic material). However, our results also show [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   that macroinvertebrates were more influenced by regional than by local drivers. Thus, the [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   hydrological context best explained the composition of taxa feeding habits and variation in taxa [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   diversity, with larger abundance and richness of scrapers and shredders in small headwater streams. [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   anthropogenic pressures at larger scales. To improve the efficiency of restoration efforts, integrated [SOURCE: Page_3]

50       1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   Advances in ecological theory, formalized within the metacommunity concept (Leibold et al., 2004), [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   have evidenced that community assembly resulted from the joint action of local – i.e niche-based – [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   and regional – i.e., dispersal – drivers. The ability of organisms to disperse and colonize areas is [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   largely dependent on landscape attributes, i.e., variations in habitat area, quality and connectivity [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   and properties of the matrix environment (Hodgson et al., 2011). Thus, beyond the availability of [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   local suitable habitat conditions, community composition in targeted areas remain largely dependent [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   on landscape attributes at multiple scales (e.g., Bruno et al., 2014; Kail and Wolter, 2013; Stoll et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   2016; Townsend et al., 2003). In a context of ongoing global biodiversity loss (Butchart et al., 2010) [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   and in order to enhance our understanding of biodiversity patterns, it therefore appears of primary [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   importance to balance the relative influence of local and regional drivers. [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   Lotic ecosystems are intrinsically structured in a hierarchical way (Ward, 1989) and form a dendritic [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   network in which landscape structure and physical flows dictate dispersal events (Altermatt, 2013). [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   Changes in community structure cannot thus be explained by considering a single set of local habitat [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   variables, but a combination of local and regional drivers (Kuglerová et al., 2015). Investigating the [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   impact of multiple stressors on freshwater communities, Leps et al. (2015) showed that linkages [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   between communities and different scales of land use and stream type groups varied strongly, [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   suggesting that the improvement of local habitat conditions might be overwhelmed by catchment [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   and landscape-scale properties. Placed in a restoration framework, this suggests that the benefit of [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   measures aiming at improving local habitat quality can be overruled by regional drivers (Palmer et [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   al., 2010). Indeed, a few studies have documented how the success of river restoration on benthic [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   macroinvertebrate communities depends on the species pool in surrounding areas (Sundermann et [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   al., 2011; Tonkin et al., 2014), the regional hydromorphological habitat quality (Stoll et al., 2016) and [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   the position of the restored reach within the river network (Swan and Brown, 2017). To achieve long [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   lasting successes in ecological restoration, it is thus of vital importance to consider the influence of [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   potential large-scale drivers. [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   In riparian zones, nature-based solutions are often used to both control erosion and improve [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   ecological conditions along riverbanks (Keesstra et al., 2018). By using the physical properties of [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   living plants, soil bioengineering techniques aim at copying naturally functioning riverbank models [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   and can thus be viewed as riparian ecosystem restoration (Rey et al., 2019). Indeed, through the [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   active introduction of pioneer native tree and shrub species, soil bioengineering have been showed [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   to increase riparian habitat quality and biodiversity (Janssen et al., 2019; Li et al., 2006; Schmitt et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   2018), as compared to civil engineering solutions. The ecological benefit of soil bioengineering are [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   however not only restricted to the terrestrial part of the riverbank. By increasing tree coverage and [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   diversity, these stabilization structures may induce changes in river abiotic conditions (i.e., light, [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   temperature, flow velocity, sediment deposition, water quality) and foster greater input of organic [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   material in riverbed (Corenblit et al., 2007; Cummins et al., 1989; Garner et al., 2017; Osborne and [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   Kovacic, 1993), which directly benefit aquatic biodiversity (Cavaillé et al., 2018; Pander et al., 2017; [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   Sudduth and Meyer, 2006). Nevertheless, soil bioengineering techniques are mostly designed at the [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   local scale and are implemented in a broad range of rivers, from mountain streams to large alluvial [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   rivers. This imply large variations in the properties of regional attributes (e.g., water quality, [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   hydrological conditions and landscape composition), which may greatly influence the structure of [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   freshwater communities and their response to restoration efforts. [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   Here, we aimed to study how different riverbank structures, including civil engineering, soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   bioengineering and natural willow stands, influence the diversity and composition of benthic [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   macroinvertebrates in the French and Swiss foothills of the Alps and Jura Mountains. Beyond the [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   direct effect of the type of structure (Cavaillé et al., 2018), we wanted to assess the relative [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   ecological added-value of soil bioengineering techniques on benthic macroinvertebrate communities [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   as compared to influential regional drivers. Indeed, though our study design was built in a way that [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   limits significant variations in confounding factors between treatments, we could not avoid quite [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   large variations in hydrological, land-cover and water quality conditions within treatments. Thus, [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   moving beyond simple categories of riverbanks, we took advantage of these variations to contrast [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   the influence of local and regional drivers on benthic macroinvertebrates. By using a taxonomic and a [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   functional approach, we aimed to understand whether the ecological benefit of using soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   bioengineering techniques to stabilize riverbanks can be overruled by catchment and landscape-scale [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   properties. From a restoration perspective, this was done to identify which are the key factors in [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   stabilization structure, and regional environmental conditions, that has the best potential for hosting [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   diverse benthic macroinvertebrate communities, and thus increase the effectiveness of stream [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   restorations (Bernhardt and Palmer, 2011). [SOURCE: Page_6]

110       2. Materials and Methods [SOURCE: Page_6]

111       2.1.       Study area and sampling design [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   The study was carried out in the French and Swiss foothills of the Alps and Jura Mountains (Fig. 1), [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   which are characterized by a limestone substratum and a temperate climate. We selected a large [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   array of rivers (n = 23), between the Drôme River (44°43’N; 4°58’E) and the Doubs River (47°21’N; [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   7°10’E), at elevations ranging from 200 to 700 m asl. The area of the upstream watersheds of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   sampled rivers ranged from 5 to 5,700 km² and was occupied to a large extent by forested areas (48 [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   %) and agricultural areas (39 %) and to a lesser extent by sparsely vegetated areas (8 %) and urban [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   areas (5 %). [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   In 2011, we sampled 37 riverbank sites: 29 were engineered for erosion control and 8 were young [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   natural riparian willow stands (Table A.1., see also Cavaillé et al., 2018). Among the engineered [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   riverbank sites, four different stabilization structures were investigated: riprap protection (n = 8), [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   mixed protection, i.e., riprap at lower part combined with soil bioengineering at the upper part of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   bank (n = 7), vegetated crib wall (n= 6) and willow fascines (n= 8). All streambank protection occurred [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   between 3 and 9 years prior to the study. [SOURCE: Page_6]

125       2.2.       Benthic macroinvertebrates assessment [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   Benthic macroinvertebrates were sampled following a standardized protocol (AFNOR, 2009), in [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   September and October 2011. To explore a representative range of aquatic habitats along each [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   riverbank site, five surber samples (500 µm mesh size, sampling area of 1/20 m²) were taken in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   five most qualitative submerged habitats (see 2.3. for details). Incremental samples were taken from [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   downstream to upstream to avoid any disturbance caused by walking in the riverbed. The material [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   collected was fixed in 70 % ethanol and sorted in laboratory. Independent experts identified benthic [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   macroinvertebrates to the lowest practical taxonomic level. [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   To avoid identification or quantification bias in the analyses, the taxa Hydrozoa, Bryozoa, Porifera, [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   Hydrachnidia, Nematoda and Oligochaeta were removed from the dataset and taxon identifications [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   were harmonized at the family level (Floury et al., 2013). In addition, to highlight how soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   bioengineering techniques, as well as regional drivers, induce changes in diversity patterns, we [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   distinguished between native and exotic species in subsequent analyses (i.e., based on species [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   chorology and expert knowledge, Table A.2.). Focus was made on exotic species because they [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   represent potential threats to native communities and pose challenges to restoration measures [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   (Leuven et al., 2009; Strayer, 2010). Finally, beyond the taxonomic approach, we used a functional [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   approach by considering groups of species based on their feeding habits. Specifically, we considered [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   the affinity of each taxa to 8 categories describing behavioural aspects of nutrition: absorber, [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   deposit-feeder, shredder, scraper, filter-feeder, piercer, predator and parasite (Tachet et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   The affinity index is an integer score ranging from 0 (no affinity) to 3 (strong affinity), based on expert [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   knowledge, which integrates possible variation in feeding habits during the life stage of a taxa. [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   Following Bady et al. (2005), this index was treated as frequency distribution which allowed us to [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   compute a new matrix giving approximate abundances for each class of feeding habits at each [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   riverbank site (see raw data in Table A.2.). We then decided to more specifically focus on diversity [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   patterns of shredders and scrapers, because theses feeding groups rely on coarse organic material [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   and are dominant in headwaters (Vannote et al., 1980). Indeed, given that input of organic material [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   are expected to be higher along riverbanks stabilized by soil bioengineering, as compared to those [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   stabilized by civil engineering, shredders and scrapers were hypothesized to be the best candidates [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   to investigate the relative influence of local and regional drivers on macroinvertebrates. [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

154       2.3.    Riparian habitat variables [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   To assess the effect of soil bioengineering on benthic macroinvertebrates, we used three terrestrial [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   and three aquatic habitat variables that were expected to highlight differences in habitat quality [SOURCE: Page_8]

157   among rivebank structures (Table 1). For the terrestrial part of bank, we considered the cover density [SOURCE: Page_8]

158   of herbaceous, ligneous and exotic plant species. Data were extracted from riparian vegetation [SOURCE: Page_8]

159   surveys, conducted from May to July 2011, along three 25 m transects located parallel to the [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   riverbank (i.e., one transect close to the water line, one in the middle and one on the upper part, [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   close to the edge of the riverbank). Along each transect, measurements of plant species frequency [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   were taken every meter using the Point Contact Method. For the aquatic part of the bank, we [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   considered the richness of habitats and the percentage cover of low and high potential habitats for [SOURCE: Page_8]

164   macroinvertebrates. Data were extracted from substrate inventories, conducted from September to [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   October 2011 along each submerged part of riverbank. Substrates covering more than 1/20 m² were [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   assessed and assigned to one of the 12 following substrate types: slab (Hab = 0), algae (Hab = 1), [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   sand/silt (Hab = 2), mud (Hab = 3), helophyte (Hab = 4), gravel (Hab = 5), block (Hab = 6), pebble (Hab [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   = 7), root (Hab = 8), litter (Hab = 9), hydrophyte (Hab = 10) and bryophyte (Hab = 11). Each substrate [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   type was a priori ranked in relation to its ability to host organisms using “habitability” score (MEDAD, [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   2007). Based on this, percentage cover of low potential habitats were defined as the sum of [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   substrate types having a score ≤ 5 while percentage cover of high potential habitats were defined as [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   the sum of substrate types having a score ≥ 6. [SOURCE: Page_8]

173       2.4.    Water quality, hydrological and land cover variables [SOURCE: Page_8]

174   In addition to terrestrial and aquatic habitat variables, we used three set of three a priori biologically [SOURCE: Page_8]

175   important regional drivers for benthic macroinvertebrates measured at each riverbank site (Table 1). [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   Water quality variables, i.e., conductivity (µS/cm) and temperature (degrees Celsius) measured using [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   the WTW 340i - TetraCon® 325 and dissolved oxygen (mg/L) measured using the Hach HQ30D. [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   Hydrological variables, i.e., stream width (m) assessed using a laser rangefinder and watershed area [SOURCE: Page_8]

179   (km²) and slope gradient (m/km) derived from Digital Elevation Model analysis, by calculating flow [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

180   direction grids and then delimiting individual drainage areas for each site along river networks with [SOURCE: Page_9]

181   the GRASS GIS software (GRASS Development Team, 2017). Land cover variables (Bissardon et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

182   1997), i.e., forest, agricultural and urban area proportions in the surrounding landscape measured [SOURCE: Page_9]

183   within a 500-m-radius around each riverbank site with the QGIS software (QGIS Development Team, [SOURCE: Page_9]

184   2015). [SOURCE: Page_9]

185       2.5.    Statistical analyses [SOURCE: Page_9]

186   Analyses were performed with the R version 3.5.1 software (R Core Team, 2019). [SOURCE: Page_9]

187   For each set of independent continuous variables we built a synthetic latent variable using values of [SOURCE: Page_9]

188   the first axis of a principal component analysis (PCA). This PCA axis represented variations from low [SOURCE: Page_9]

189   to high vegetation density for terrestrial habitat variables, from low to high habitat quality for [SOURCE: Page_9]

190   aquatic habitat variables, from high to low water quality for water quality variables, from large to [SOURCE: Page_9]

191   small rivers for hydrological variables and from urban dominated to agricultural dominated [SOURCE: Page_9]

192   landscapes for land cover variables (Fig. A.3.). Because it appears more logical for water quality to [SOURCE: Page_9]

193   represent variations from low to high quality, values of the PCA first axis were reversed. We then test [SOURCE: Page_9]

194   if latent variables varied significantly among the five categories of riverbank sites using one-ways [SOURCE: Page_9]

195   ANOVAs with Tukey’s post hoc tests. Finally, to explore the direct influence of riverbank protection [SOURCE: Page_9]

196   techniques on assemblage patterns, we created an ordinal variable that combined the effect of [SOURCE: Page_9]

197   technique with the age of the structure (named “riverbank structure [SOURCE: Page_9]

198   ”). Based on the few available studies (Cavaillé et al., 2018; Li et al., 2006; Sudduth and Meyer, 2006), [SOURCE: Page_9]

199   riverbank protection techniques were ranked from low to high habitat quality (i.e., riprap -> mixed -> [SOURCE: Page_9]

200   crib wall -> fascine -> natural), and recent structures (i.e., 3 years old) were considered to have lower [SOURCE: Page_9]

201   habitat quality than did older ones (i.e., 9 years old). The resulting ordinal variable ranged from a [SOURCE: Page_9]

202   minimum value of 1, for 3-year-old riprap protection, to a maximum value of 19, for natural [SOURCE: Page_9]

203   riverbanks (see the column “Score” in Table A.1.). [SOURCE: Page_9]

204   To determine whether the taxonomic and functional composition of benthic macroinvertebrates [SOURCE: Page_9]

205   were shaped by the riverbank structure ordinal variable as well as by terrestrial habitat, aquatic [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

206   habitat, water quality, hydrological and land cover latent variables, we used multivariate generalized [SOURCE: Page_10]

207   linear models (mvabund package, Wang et al., 2012). We fitted the full model to each family and [SOURCE: Page_10]

208   feeding groups of benthic invertebrates that were present in > 10 % of the studied sites, and [SOURCE: Page_10]

209   summed across the univariate response to estimate their multivariate response with a negative [SOURCE: Page_10]

210   binomial distribution. The significance of independent variables in the multivariate GLM was [SOURCE: Page_10]

211   assessed using an analysis of variance with the PIT-trap method and 999 bootstrap resamples [SOURCE: Page_10]

212   (Warton et al., 2017). Moreover, to determine which family and feeding group best contribute to the [SOURCE: Page_10]

213   overall model deviance, we calculated univariate test statistics and p-values, adjusted to correct for [SOURCE: Page_10]

214   multiple testing (Holm’s step-down procedure), for each family/feeding group (Wang et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_10]

215   Finally, to provide a graphical representation of the variation in family/feeding groups composition, [SOURCE: Page_10]

216   we used a canonical analysis of principal coordinates (Anderson and Willis, 2003), with a Bray-Curtis [SOURCE: Page_10]

217   distance. [SOURCE: Page_10]

218   To determine whether diversity patterns of benthic macroinvertebrates were influenced by the [SOURCE: Page_10]

219   riverbank structure ordinal variable as well as by terrestrial habitat, aquatic habitat, water quality, [SOURCE: Page_10]

220   hydrological and land cover latent variables, we used structural equation modelling (piecewiseSEM [SOURCE: Page_10]

221   package, Lefcheck, 2016). This was done to jointly test the direct and indirect effect of soil [SOURCE: Page_10]

222   bioengineering techniques on diversity patterns, while controlling for a priori important drivers of [SOURCE: Page_10]

223   benthic macroinvertebrates. Using literature review based on empirical data, we built an a priori [SOURCE: Page_10]

224   model that was expected to be the most plausible causal structure in explaining diversity patterns, [SOURCE: Page_10]

225   while keeping in mind that the ratio of the total number of samples to the number of variables [SOURCE: Page_10]

226   should not fall below 5 (Grace et al., 2015). Our model hypothesized that local terrestrial and aquatic [SOURCE: Page_10]

227   habitat quality latent variables would mediate the indirect effect of the type of riverbank structure [SOURCE: Page_10]

228   on benthic macroinvertebrates; and that water quality, hydrological and land cover latent variables [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   would directly influence diversity patterns. We fit the same a priori model structure to four [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   dependent variables, i.e., native, exotic, shredder and scraper taxa, and used a Gaussian distribution [SOURCE: Page_10]

231   to model the relationship between both terrestrial and aquatic habitat latent variables and the [SOURCE: Page_10]

10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

232   riverbank structure ordinal variable. To model the relationship between native, exotic, shredder and [SOURCE: Page_11]

233   scraper taxa and local and regional drivers we used a Poisson distribution for richness and a Negative [SOURCE: Page_11]

234   Binomial distribution for abundance. The goodness-of-fit of the model was assessed using Fisher’s C [SOURCE: Page_11]

235   statistic and a test of directed separation, while the variance explained was estimated using the [SOURCE: Page_11]

236   coefficient of determination. Finally, because part of the model relied on GLMs, the results report [SOURCE: Page_11]

237   only unstandardized coefficients. [SOURCE: Page_11]

291       4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_13]

292   Our results clearly showed that benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages were more influenced by [SOURCE: Page_13]

293   regional than by local drivers. Although we have found evidences that soil bioengineering improve [SOURCE: Page_13]

294   terrestrial and aquatic habitat conditions and benefit to benthic macroinvertebrates, the hydrological [SOURCE: Page_13]

295   context, land cover composition and, to a lower extent, water quality conditions performed much [SOURCE: Page_13]

296   better in explaining variations in composition and diversity. Along river networks, catchment-scale [SOURCE: Page_13]

297   properties and landscape attributes surrounding riverbank sites appeared to be major drivers of [SOURCE: Page_13]

298   benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages. To achieve ecological improvement, river practitioners and [SOURCE: Page_13]

299   managers should therefore take into account the hierarchical structure of the regional and local [SOURCE: Page_13]

300   drivers in designing and implementing restoration operations. [SOURCE: Page_13]

301   Soil bioengineering improves local habitat conditions and benefit to benthic macroinvertebrates [SOURCE: Page_13]

302   As expected, both terrestrial and aquatic habitat qualities increased along riverbanks stabilized with [SOURCE: Page_13]

303   soil bioengineering techniques, specifically for stabilization structures made of willow fascines and [SOURCE: Page_13]

304   vegetated crib wall (Fig. 2). Given that soil bioengineering consists in integrating vegetation into [SOURCE: Page_13]

305   engineering design (Evette et al., 2009), the increase in vegetation density reported herein reflects [SOURCE: Page_13]

306   the fact that pure bioengineering techniques used a higher proportion of plant material and may also [SOURCE: Page_13]

307   better promote the recruitment and growth of plant species along stabilized riverbanks (Li et al., [SOURCE: Page_13]

308   2006; Schmitt et al., 2018). Moreover, we showed that the immerged part of the riverbank benefited [SOURCE: Page_13]

309   from these nature-based solutions. Thus, when comparing soil bioengineering structures to mixed [SOURCE: Page_13]

13 [SOURCE: Page_13]

310   and civil engineering structures, an increase in the proportion of high potential habitats for [SOURCE: Page_14]

311   macroinvertebrates was highlighted. Even more, we found that the quality of aquatic habitat was [SOURCE: Page_14]

312   quite similar between willow fascine structures and natural willow stands (Fig. 2). As reported in [SOURCE: Page_14]

313   other studies (Li et al., 2006; Pander et al., 2017; Sudduth and Meyer, 2006), our results confirm that [SOURCE: Page_14]

314   the introduction of tree species to stabilize riverbanks could benefit instream habitats by e.g., [SOURCE: Page_14]

315   fostering the development of fine root systems and the inputs of woody debris and leaf litter into the [SOURCE: Page_14]

316   rivers. However, restoration efforts relying on replanting strategies only (Giling et al., 2015; Parkyn et [SOURCE: Page_14]

317   al., 2003) and/or stabilization structures using riprap at the lower submerged part (Cooperman et al., [SOURCE: Page_14]

318   2007; Schmitt et al., 2018) have been showed to be of low ecological value for freshwater [SOURCE: Page_14]

319   communities. In accordance, we found that mixed and riprap structures induced an increase in the [SOURCE: Page_14]

320   proportion of low potential habitats for macroinvertebrates (e.g., slab). To foster the ecological [SOURCE: Page_14]

321   benefit of stabilization structures along riverbanks, it therefore appears essential to promote [SOURCE: Page_14]

322   substrate heterogeneity deeper in the aquatic environment and to avoid, whenever possible, [SOURCE: Page_14]

323   submerged bank protection dominated by ripraps (e.g., Schmitt et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_14]

324   Related changes in aquatic habitat conditions influenced the composition of macroinvertebrate [SOURCE: Page_14]

325   feeding groups. Even though the deviance explained by the aquatic habitat variable was lower than [SOURCE: Page_14]

326   the one explained by regional drivers, a significant effect was found. Specifically, we showed that [SOURCE: Page_14]

327   deposit-feeders and, to lower extend, piercers were more abundant along banks dominated by low [SOURCE: Page_14]

328   potential aquatic habitats. This may underlines thicker layers of fine detrital organic matter that [SOURCE: Page_14]

329   settle out and accumulate between the rock blocs of riprap and mixed stabilization structures (Feld [SOURCE: Page_14]

330   and Hering, 2007). However, despite the fact that numerous studies have established a clear link [SOURCE: Page_14]

331   between aquatic habitat quality and macroinvertebrate diversity (Buffagni et al., 2019; Cogerino et [SOURCE: Page_14]

332   al., 1995; Li et al., 2018; Verdonschot et al., 2016), in the foothills of the Alps and Jura Mountains, [SOURCE: Page_14]

333   diversity patterns were not influenced by local terrestrial and aquatic habitat conditions. Instead, we [SOURCE: Page_14]

334   found that the richness of natives and shredders increased from civil engineered to soil [SOURCE: Page_14]

335   bioengineered stabilization structures (i.e., with direct increasing value of the riverbank structure [SOURCE: Page_14]

14 [SOURCE: Page_14]

336   ordinal variable). This suggests that beyond changes in riparian vegetation density and in the [SOURCE: Page_15]

337   proportion of high potential habitats, soil bioengineering induces other changes in local abiotic [SOURCE: Page_15]

338   conditions which benefit to overall benthic macroinvertebrates as well as specialized species, i.e., [SOURCE: Page_15]

339   relying on coarse particulate of organic matter. Especially, we argued that willow fascines and [SOURCE: Page_15]

340   vegetated crib wall increase resource input of coarse organic material (i.e., woody debris, leaves, [SOURCE: Page_15]

341   roots) at the reach-scale in a manner comparable to that of natural willow stands, which directly [SOURCE: Page_15]

342   benefit to shredders (Cummins et al., 1989; Turunen et al., 2019). More generally, we inferred that [SOURCE: Page_15]

343   the introduction of tree species to stabilize riverbanks increases shade over parts of the river, [SOURCE: Page_15]

344   preventing thus the heating of water (Dugdale et al., 2018; Garner et al., 2017), and increases [SOURCE: Page_15]

345   biophysical interactions with water and sediment flows, enhancing thus the trapping of sediments, [SOURCE: Page_15]

346   organic matter and nutrients and favouring the development of additional fluvial landforms [SOURCE: Page_15]

347   (Corenblit et al., 2007; Hortobágyi et al., 2018). These changes in environmental conditions, if they [SOURCE: Page_15]

348   occur over a river segment embedded in a landscape dominated by low riparian habitat quality, may [SOURCE: Page_15]

349   be very attractive for macroinvertebrate populations and foster the co-occurrence of various taxa at [SOURCE: Page_15]

350   local scale. Thus, in accordance with the few available studies (Cavaillé et al., 2018; Pander et al., [SOURCE: Page_15]

351   2017; Sudduth and Meyer, 2006), our results pointed out that soil bioengineering can be a good [SOURCE: Page_15]

352   compromise solution to support both erosion control and freshwater communities. However, the [SOURCE: Page_15]

353   ecological benefits of these nature-based solutions should be rationalized, as it appears clear that the [SOURCE: Page_15]

354   improvement of riparian habitat quality induces by soil bioengineering alone cannot solve [SOURCE: Page_15]

355   environmental degradations due to human activities in surrounding landscape. [SOURCE: Page_15]

356   Regional drivers primarily shape benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages [SOURCE: Page_15]

357   Consistently with studies that have investigated the relative influence of multiple scale drivers on [SOURCE: Page_15]

358   benthic macroinvertebrates (Bruno et al., 2014; Feld and Hering, 2007; Heino et al., 2003; Townsend [SOURCE: Page_15]

359   et al., 2003), we found that composition and diversity patterns were more strongly explained by [SOURCE: Page_15]

360   regional than by local drivers. Specifically, we highlighted that the hydrological context, i.e., a [SOURCE: Page_15]

361   variable combining stream width, watershed area and slope gradient, best explained [SOURCE: Page_15]

15 [SOURCE: Page_15]

362   macroinvertebrate assemblages (Townsend et al., 2003). Thus, in accordance with the river [SOURCE: Page_16]

363   continuum concept (Vannote et al., 1980), the composition of taxa feeding habits was structured in a [SOURCE: Page_16]

364   predictable manner, i.e., communities dominated by scrapers, shredders and predators were located [SOURCE: Page_16]

365   along small headwaters streams. Thanks to the structural equation modelling approach, which allows [SOURCE: Page_16]

366   balancing the relative importance of parameters in models, it appears clear that changes in [SOURCE: Page_16]

367   communities structure was firstly due to the larger abundance of scrapers and shredders taxa in [SOURCE: Page_16]

368   small streams, secondly to their larger richness. Due to close relationship between taxa abundance [SOURCE: Page_16]

369   and resource availability, framed within the species-energy hypothesis (Wright, 1983), this may [SOURCE: Page_16]

370   confirms the leading role played by organic materials in shaping benthic macroinvertebrate [SOURCE: Page_16]

371   communities (Vannote et al., 1980). Also, contrary to empirical evidences (Heino et al., 2003; [SOURCE: Page_16]

372   Minshall et al., 1985; Paller et al., 2006), we found that total richness of native taxa decreased with [SOURCE: Page_16]

373   increasing river size. The river continuum concept predicts that biotic diversity increases with stream [SOURCE: Page_16]

374   size, reaching maximum values in midreaches and then decreasing in very large rivers (Vannote et al., [SOURCE: Page_16]

375   1980). However, this biodiversity-stream size relationships has been showed to be rather weak, [SOURCE: Page_16]

376   especially for macroinvertebrate communities (Vander Vorste et al., 2017). Moreover, because [SOURCE: Page_16]

377   stream size correlates well with a large number of other variables, a mechanistic explanation is [SOURCE: Page_16]

378   needed to disentangle the drivers controlling biodiversity in riverine ecosystems (Vander Vorste et [SOURCE: Page_16]

379   al., 2017). In this regard, our results also showed that the richness of exotic taxa increased from small [SOURCE: Page_16]

380   to large rivers. Beyond the confirmation that exotic taxa benefit from more connected river networks [SOURCE: Page_16]

381   (Leuven et al., 2009), those results give evidence that the biodiversity-stream size relationships [SOURCE: Page_16]

382   should not be investigated based solely on synthetic metrics of diversity, such as total richness. We [SOURCE: Page_16]

383   thus inferred that the pattern observed can be attributed to a combination of multi-stressors, [SOURCE: Page_16]

384   probably linked to the fact that largest rivers in our study area are more likely to be subject to [SOURCE: Page_16]

385   channeling, which induces physicochemical and morphological degradations to the benefit of exotic [SOURCE: Page_16]

386   taxa (Früh et al., 2012) and to the detriment of native taxa. [SOURCE: Page_16]

16 [SOURCE: Page_16]

387   Beside the influence of the hydrological context, it bah been shown that the landscape attributes [SOURCE: Page_17]

388   surrounding the riverbanks strongly influence benthic macroinvertebrates communities (Lorenz and [SOURCE: Page_17]

389   Feld, 2013). Thus, land cover explained most of the variation in the taxonomic composition, even [SOURCE: Page_17]

390   though not significant, and ranked second in explaining variation in the functional composition. In [SOURCE: Page_17]

391   addition, richness and abundance of native, scraper and shredder taxa increased with a decreased [SOURCE: Page_17]

392   proportion of urban dominated landscapes. This confirms the substantial effect that human [SOURCE: Page_17]

393   infrastructures have in the direct vicinity of rivers on benthic macroinvertebrates (Feld and Hering, [SOURCE: Page_17]

394   2007; Kail and Wolter, 2013; Moore and Palmer, 2005; Roy et al., 2003). Indeed, by increasing built- [SOURCE: Page_17]

395   up areas around rivers, human activities can alter riparian habitat quality (e.g., by reducing the width [SOURCE: Page_17]

396   of the forest buffer) and water properties (e.g., by increasing organic pollution, acidity and turbidity) [SOURCE: Page_17]

397   (King et al., 2005). However, contrary to close linkages established between urban land cover and [SOURCE: Page_17]

398   water quality, and related negative effects on the diversity of benthic macroinvertebrates (Roy et al., [SOURCE: Page_17]

399   2003), in our study area, land cover and water quality were independent (i.e., the test of directed [SOURCE: Page_17]

400   separation was not significant). This may underlines the fact that most of the riverbanks studied were [SOURCE: Page_17]

401   embedded in an agricultural dominated landscape (see Table 1), with only 9 riverbank sites having [SOURCE: Page_17]

402   more than 50 % of urban areas in the surrounding landscape. Thus, when comparing the relative [SOURCE: Page_17]

403   influence of land cover and water quality drivers on benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages, we [SOURCE: Page_17]

404   found less direct influence for the latter. Indeed, only the abundance of scraper and native taxa [SOURCE: Page_17]

405   increased with increasing value of water quality, while the richness of exotic taxa decreased. This [SOURCE: Page_17]

406   result may be linked to the higher success of non-native benthic macroinvertebrates with increased [SOURCE: Page_17]

407   loads of pollutants as it has already been observed (Früh et al., 2012). Also, these opposite patterns [SOURCE: Page_17]

408   give weight to the view that exotic taxa share different traits than native taxa, i.e., they are mostly [SOURCE: Page_17]

409   collector-filterers, belong to the molluscs or crustaceans taxonomic groups, and are more tolerant to [SOURCE: Page_17]

410   organic pollution (Karatayev et al., 2009). Overall, our results highlight the hierarchical structure of [SOURCE: Page_17]

411   the regional drivers on macroinvertebrates and confirm the importance of considering multiple scale [SOURCE: Page_17]

17 [SOURCE: Page_17]

412   parameters for a better understanding of the structure of freshwater communities (Bruno et al., [SOURCE: Page_18]

413   2014; Feld and Hering, 2007; Heino et al., 2003; Kail and Wolter, 2013). [SOURCE: Page_18]

415       5. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_18]

416   The response of freshwater communities to instream habitat restoration has been shown to be [SOURCE: Page_18]

417   inconsistent (Palmer et al., 2010). As opposed to terrestrial biodiversity, which benefits more strongly [SOURCE: Page_18]

418   from local restoration efforts, the recovery of aquatic biodiversity remains largely under the control [SOURCE: Page_18]

419   of large-scale stressors, which prevent taxa recolonization (Pilotto et al., 2019). As such, the few [SOURCE: Page_18]

420   previous studies that have investigated the response of fish and benthic macroinvertebrate [SOURCE: Page_18]

421   communities to soil bioengineering have found either a positive significant effect (Cavaillé et al., [SOURCE: Page_18]

422   2018; Dolph et al., 2015; Pander et al., 2017; Sudduth and Meyer, 2006) or a non-significant effect [SOURCE: Page_18]

423   (Cooperman et al., 2007; Ernst et al., 2012; Schmitt et al., 2018; Selvakumar et al., 2010). By moving [SOURCE: Page_18]

424   beyond simple categories of individual riverbank stabilization structures and by accounting for local [SOURCE: Page_18]

425   and regional potential important abiotic drivers, we showed that soil bioengineering has a positive [SOURCE: Page_18]

426   effect on benthic macroinvertebrates. However, as compared to regional drivers, especially [SOURCE: Page_18]

427   catchment-scale properties and landscape attributes, local drivers were of lower importance in [SOURCE: Page_18]

428   shaping macroinvertebrate assemblages. Thus, if stabilization structures are implemented with the [SOURCE: Page_18]

429   aim both of controlling erosion and of improving ecological properties of riverbanks for aquatic [SOURCE: Page_18]

430   biodiversity, the potential impact of large scales anthropogenic pressures on ecological restoration [SOURCE: Page_18]

431   success should be addressed as soon as possible (Kail and Wolter, 2013; Pilotto et al., 2019). Finally, [SOURCE: Page_18]

432   from an operational point of view, our results suggest that the ecological benefits of soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

433   bioengineering for freshwater communities can be maximized along small size rivers embedded in an [SOURCE: Page_18]

434   agricultural dominated landscape. [SOURCE: Page_18]
