## 13 ABSTRACT

14 Yield improvement certainly depends on breeding new genotypes, but also on identifying the [SOURCE: Page_1]

15 best genotype for a given location and crop management system. Hence we need to quickly [SOURCE: Page_1]

16 evaluate        the performance of each new variety in different cropping systems and 17 environmental conditions. [SOURCE: Page_1]

18 Our objective was to develop a model which can help to improve genotypic assessment in the [SOURCE: Page_1]

19 sunflower           crop (SUNFLO). The present work aimed at identifying, quantifying and 20 modelling the phenotypic variability of crop performance in response to the main abiotic [SOURCE: Page_1]

21 stresses occurring in the field (light, temperature, water, nitrogen) but also in the expression [SOURCE: Page_1]

22 of genotypic variability. [SOURCE: Page_1]

23 We therefore include just enough genetic information to enable the models to be used with [SOURCE: Page_1]

24 new genotypes. Each genotype was thus defined by chosen phenotypic traits which were [SOURCE: Page_1]

25 transcribed into a set of 12 genotype-specific parameters. [SOURCE: Page_1]

26 The model’s performance was evaluated in both specific field experiments and generic multi- [SOURCE: Page_1]

27 environment trials (MET). The first evaluation assessed model robustness: no variables had a [SOURCE: Page_1]

28 large prediction error, indicating that the final output error results more from poor prediction [SOURCE: Page_1]

29 for all variables than from error compensation. An ANOVA on the simulated MET dataset [SOURCE: Page_1]

30 showed that although the model simulates less variability than in reality (60%), there was [SOURCE: Page_1]

31 genotype-environment interaction and the ranking of the ANOVA factors was identical in [SOURCE: Page_1]

32 both observed and simulated networks. The model's accuracy was sufficient to discriminate [SOURCE: Page_1]

33 between genotypes from different breeding periods, but was similar to the difference in -1 34 performance between actual genotypes (~ 0.2 t.ha ). [SOURCE: Page_2]

35 To improve the understanding of crop physiology and crop-environment interactions, this [SOURCE: Page_2]

36 kind   of model shows weaknesses, especially when dealing with environmental stress 37 integration or biomass allocation. On the other hand, SUNFLO seems sufficiently robust to [SOURCE: Page_2]

38 estimate the influence on yield of breeding traits or to explore new management practices. [SOURCE: Page_2]

39 [SOURCE: Page_2]

40 Key words:     crop model, genotypic variability, water stress, nitrogen stress, yield, oil 41 content, sunflower (Helianthus annuus L.) [SOURCE: Page_2]

## 42 INTRODUCTION

43 [SOURCE: Page_3]

44 Sunflower is a major world oilseed crop (ca. 35 Mt, 9% oil production after palm and [SOURCE: Page_3]

45 rapeseed oil) which recently acquired new interest for biodiesel development (Pereyra-Irujo [SOURCE: Page_3]

46 et al., 2009). Considering sunflower seed production (source : FAOSTAT 2009), major [SOURCE: Page_3]

47 production regions are Europe (62 % of world production, mainly by Ukraine, Russia, [SOURCE: Page_3]

48 France), Americas (19 %, mainly Argentina, USA) and Asia (15%, mainly China, India). [SOURCE: Page_3]

49 Yield improvement certainly depends on breeding new genotypes, but also on identifying the [SOURCE: Page_3]

50 best genotype for a given location and crop management. The improvement of genotype [SOURCE: Page_3]

51 assessment is obvious at the three steps of genotype development: (1) breeding: evaluation of [SOURCE: Page_3]

52 the impact of a morpho-physiological trait (or an ideotype) on the field performance, (2) [SOURCE: Page_3]

53 cultivar   registration: assessment of the performance of elite plant materials in multi- 54 environment trials and (3) cultivar dissemination: providing advice on stable and high- [SOURCE: Page_3]

55 yielding combinations of genotype, environment and crop management to farmers. Although [SOURCE: Page_3]

56 Genotype x Environment x Management (G x E x M) interactions may sometimes appear to [SOURCE: Page_3]

57 breeders as an hindrance to the stable phenotypic response of a genotype, they could be [SOURCE: Page_3]

58 exploited more fully by advisers to recommend the best cultivar-management combination in [SOURCE: Page_3]

59 a given environment, especially in a context of low-input production. [SOURCE: Page_3]

60 Modelling can help in genotype assessment. During breeding, yield improvement does not [SOURCE: Page_3]

61 result directly from the accumulation of sequencing or functional genomic information due to [SOURCE: Page_3]

62 very variable and unpredictable cropping environments (Miflin, 2000;       Sinclair et al., 2004). 63 For example, in sorghum, a QTL for the stay-green physiological trait was identified (Tao et [SOURCE: Page_3]

64 al., 2000) but its effect on yield was shown to vary greatly (Borell and Hammer, 2000). [SOURCE: Page_3]

65 Predicting the effects of genotypic traits on yield for contrasting environments is thus a [SOURCE: Page_3]

66 halfway step for the exploitation of genomic results (Hammer et al., 2005; Hammer et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

67 2006). During cultivar evaluation for official registration and advice, field experimentation [SOURCE: Page_3]

68 remains an essential tool, but modelling could provide additional information on genotype [SOURCE: Page_3]

69 response to varying soil-weather combinations and could limit the number of trials needed by [SOURCE: Page_3]

70 siting them in the most appropriate environments (Messina et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_3]

71 Crop models, by their capacity to simulate phenotypic plasticity in response to environmental [SOURCE: Page_3]

72 variability (weather, soil or practices) can help to solve the inherent difficulty of genotype [SOURCE: Page_3]

73 evaluation.   For sunflower, crop physiology has been incorporated in different and 74 complementary ways in a few simulation models. In most of these (Steer et al., 1993 ; [SOURCE: Page_3]

75 Villalobos et al., 1996; Pereyra-Irujo and Aguirrezabal, 2007) biomass accumulation is [SOURCE: Page_3]

76 driven by radiation (RUE); only Q-Sun (Chapman et al., 1993) switches to a water-driven [SOURCE: Page_4]

77 (WUE) submodel if water is the main limiting factor. These models include limiting factors [SOURCE: Page_4]

78 based on their target environments: in those of Steer et al. (1993) and Pereyra-Irujo and [SOURCE: Page_4]

79 Aguirrezabal (2007) yield is only limited by temperature and light. To these two factors, [SOURCE: Page_4]

80 Chapman et al. (1993) added water stress and Villalobos et al. (1996) widened its scope by [SOURCE: Page_4]

81 the inclusion of nitrogen stress. [SOURCE: Page_4]

82 How do crop models deal with genotypic variability? Genotypic variability does not appear to [SOURCE: Page_4]

83 be as explicitly included as environmental responses in current sunflower crop models [SOURCE: Page_4]

84 (Connor and Hall, 1997). Two models consider different parameterization sets between [SOURCE: Page_4]

85 genotypes: Villalobos et al. (1996) introduced a statistical optimization to represent three [SOURCE: Page_4]

86 genotypes through five parameters (phenological stages, yield potential) and Chapman et [SOURCE: Page_4]

87 al.’s (1993) model did not isolate the values for genotypic parameters but rather indicated [SOURCE: Page_4]

88 genotypic variability for key parameters (water use efficiency, phyllochrons, phenological [SOURCE: Page_4]

89 stages). Moreover, the values of these parameters are mostly estimated by optimization, [SOURCE: Page_4]

90 which limits the parameterization at a larger scale for numerous genotypes. Several generic [SOURCE: Page_4]

91 crop models have been adapted to the sunflower crop, but they lack genotypic refinement [SOURCE: Page_4]

92 (Kiniry et al., 1992). [SOURCE: Page_4]

93 The value of a crop model for evaluating genotypes results mainly from its predictive quality [SOURCE: Page_4]

94 but also from its ability to be updated for yearly cultivar releases from breeding companies [SOURCE: Page_4]

95 and the official registration process. A model’s predictive quality is usually linked to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

96 relevance of simulated stress factors, the description of canopy growth, the robustness of [SOURCE: Page_4]

97 parameterization and the quality of input data. Updating can be made possible by improving [SOURCE: Page_4]

98 the ease of genotypic parameterization, which depends as much on the total number of [SOURCE: Page_4]

99 genotypic parameters as on the methods used to estimate their values. Therefore, an ideal [SOURCE: Page_4]

100 genotypic parameterization would aim to limit the total parameter number while maintaining [SOURCE: Page_4]

101 a useful predictive capacity. [SOURCE: Page_4]

102 Two steps can be distinguished when modelling the link between a phenotype (i.e. leaf area [SOURCE: Page_4]

103 or grain yield) and a genotype. Firstly, a physiological trait (i.e. reduced conductance with [SOURCE: Page_4]

104 water stress) could be transcribed as a model parameter (slope of the response curve) [SOURCE: Page_4]

105 (Casadebaig et al., 2008). Then the robustness of the relation between the trait and the plant [SOURCE: Page_4]

106 genome should be evaluated (Yin et al., 2004). The study of Chapman et al. (2002) illustrates [SOURCE: Page_4]

107 this view well, although the sorghum lines evaluated by the model differed only by four [SOURCE: Page_4]

108 alleles. [SOURCE: Page_4]

109 Our approach here concerns only the first step: to link a complex phenotype to a set of [SOURCE: Page_4]

110 accessible genotype traits. Each genotype is thus defined by chosen traits which were [SOURCE: Page_5]

111 transcribed into a set of genotype-specific parameters. These genotypic parameters are, [SOURCE: Page_5]

112 despite their name, under uncertain genetic control (Slafer, 2003). [SOURCE: Page_5]

113 Unlike estimating parameters by optimization, direct measurement allows parameter values [SOURCE: Page_5]

114 to be more representative of crop physiology than the paired data / optimization algorithm [SOURCE: Page_5]

115 (Jeuffroy et al., 1996). In this way, the time and complex procedures needed to introduce new [SOURCE: Page_5]

116 genotypes can be reduced, improving the accessibility of the model among technical services [SOURCE: Page_5]

117 (Mavromatis et al., 2001). [SOURCE: Page_5]

118 Why develop a new sunflower crop model? It was the need to differentiate genotypic [SOURCE: Page_5]

119 response with as few parameters as possible, rather than a lack of suitability of existing [SOURCE: Page_5]

120 sunflower crop models, that led us to develop a new crop model for sunflower. An analysis of [SOURCE: Page_5]

121 the comparative performance of genotypes from different breeding generations (Vear et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

122 2003) through the generic approach of biomass production of Monteith (1977) allowed [SOURCE: Page_5]

123 phenotypic variability to be quantified in this system (Debaeke et al., 2004). That study [SOURCE: Page_5]

124 confirmed the role of (1) plant leaf area and its control (persistence, efficiency) after [SOURCE: Page_5]

125 flowering, (2) vertical distribution of this leaf area and (3) phenology (duration from [SOURCE: Page_5]

126 flowering to maturity) for genotype performance. This knowledge was incorporated into a [SOURCE: Page_5]

127 crop model intended to evaluate the contribution of identified parameters to the variability of [SOURCE: Page_5]

128 yield potential (Lecoeur et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_5]

129 Our objective was to identify, quantify and model phenotypic variability of sunflower at the [SOURCE: Page_5]

130 individual plant level in response to the main abiotic stresses occurring at field level (light, [SOURCE: Page_5]

131 temperature, water, nitrogen) but also in the expression of genotypic variability (G x E [SOURCE: Page_5]

132 interactions). [SOURCE: Page_5]

133 This objective underlies a parsimonious addition of genetic information to increase the [SOURCE: Page_5]

134 model’s versatility and usefulness. Using a crop model to help to evaluate genotypes thus [SOURCE: Page_5]

135 requires (1) choosing a suitable modelling framework, (2) setting up genotypic parameters, [SOURCE: Page_5]

136 (3) estimating their values with a simple and robust methodology, (4) evaluating the model’s [SOURCE: Page_5]

137 performance on a small-scale evaluation trial network. [SOURCE: Page_5]

## 237 RESULTS : MODEL DEVELOPMENT

238 [SOURCE: Page_8]

239 1.   Modelling approach 240 Starting from a simple modular modelling basis, we used the model SUNFLO_V$_0$ (Lecoeur [SOURCE: Page_8]

241 et al., 2009) as an initial modelling framework. It estimates the above-ground biomass [SOURCE: Page_8]

242 production of a sunflower crop from incident radiation and mean air temperature. It works on [SOURCE: Page_8]

243 a daily time step and describes plant phenology, leaf expansion and biomass production and [SOURCE: Page_8]

244 its allocation to the grain. Rather classically, actual growth depends on potential growth [SOURCE: Page_8]

245 modulated by temperature and radiation. Genotypic variability is taken into account by a [SOURCE: Page_8]

246 small number (12) of parameters (Lecoeur et al., 2009). Moreover, a fine resolution in [SOURCE: Page_9]

247 describing biomass production is not always the best starting point (Hammer et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_9]

248 When building a model framework, several groups of equations and parameters can be [SOURCE: Page_9]

249 proposed to describe a given physiological process. If their inputs and outputs are measurable [SOURCE: Page_9]

250 and effectively measured, treating these modules as independent sub-models can have several [SOURCE: Page_9]

251 advantages. Firstly it allows easier knowledge sharing among the scientific community [SOURCE: Page_9]

252 (Jones et al., 2001). Secondly, evaluating the model response module by module gives to the [SOURCE: Page_9]

253 overall performance more credibility throughout the simulated growth period and prevents [SOURCE: Page_9]

254 accidental   error compensation (Sinclair and Seligman, 2000). SUNFLO_V$_1$, described 255 below, consists of 7 modules whose structure appears in Figure 1. [SOURCE: Page_9]

256 [SOURCE: Page_9]

257 Adding some genotypic variability. Our objective was to evaluate the possibility of coupling a [SOURCE: Page_9]

258 biophysical model and a phenotyping approach to quantify phenotypic variability of yield [SOURCE: Page_9]

259 and oil content in contrasting cropping environments. Each genotype was defined by a set of [SOURCE: Page_9]

260 parameters whose values are assumed to be constant among environments, thus trying to [SOURCE: Page_9]

261 mimic gene functioning (Colson et al., 1995; Boote et al., 2003). [SOURCE: Page_9]

262 When using experimentation to measure genotypic parameters, there are two kinds of [SOURCE: Page_9]

263 constraint. The first is the amount of work involved, where the number of new genotypes and [SOURCE: Page_9]

264 time available for phenotyping limit the number of genotypic parameters which can be [SOURCE: Page_9]

265 investigated in the future. The second is due to the environmental effects on the values of [SOURCE: Page_9]

266 measured parameters: the use of phenotypic information, as parameter values can only be [SOURCE: Page_9]

267 taken to be genotypic if they prove to be stable in different environments. We finally [SOURCE: Page_9]

268 proposed four criteria before choosing a parameter as genotype-specific; it should (1) present [SOURCE: Page_9]

269 a significant amount of genotypic variability, (2) be stable over environments, (3) markedly [SOURCE: Page_9]

270 affect the model outputs and (4) be easily measurable. [SOURCE: Page_9]

271 [SOURCE: Page_9]

274 2.1 Phenology [SOURCE: Page_9]

275 Plant phenology is driven by thermal time (Aiken, 2005). Cumulative thermal time since [SOURCE: Page_9]

276 emergence (TTE, °C.d) was calculated [EQ$_2$] as the sum of the daily mean air temperature [SOURCE: Page_9]

277 from emergence using a base temperature (Tb) of 4.8°C (Granier and Tardieu, 1998) [SOURCE: Page_9]

278 common to all genotypes. Four key stages, expressed as genotype-dependent thermal dates, [SOURCE: Page_9]

279 delimited periods of plant growth with changes in plant physiology: floral initiation (FI), [SOURCE: Page_9]

280 beginning    of flowering (F), beginning of grain filling (early maturation, EM) and 281 physiological maturity (PM) (CETIOM, 2004; Lecoeur et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_10]

282 Photoperiodic effects on phenology were not included in the model: (Leon et al., 2001) [SOURCE: Page_10]

283 showed that flowering date was unaffected by photoperiods between 14.5 and 16h at [SOURCE: Page_10]

284 emergence. Extreme locations and practices for French sunflower crops could lead to [SOURCE: Page_10]

285 photoperiods at emergence from 13.8 h (southern early sowings) to 16.5 h (northern late [SOURCE: Page_10]

286 sowings), thus showing a very slight effect on flowering date in a few cases. [SOURCE: Page_10]

287 Through the reduction of transpiration, water stress causes overheating of the plant. This [SOURCE: Page_10]

288 heating can accelerate crop development and was modelled using a multiplicative effect with [SOURCE: Page_10]

289 thermal time accumulation [EQ$_2$]. [SOURCE: Page_10]

304 2.2.1 Temperature [SOURCE: Page_10]

305 Temperature effects on processes are mostly accounted for in the model by using thermal [SOURCE: Page_10]

306 time based relationships. However, we needed to include a direct effect of temperature on -1 307 radiation use efficiency (RUE, g.MJ ) and on nitrogen net mineralization rate (dMin, -1 308 Kg.j.ha ). [SOURCE: Page_10]

309 A depressive function of non-optimal temperature (T.RUE) was applied to RUE, calculated [SOURCE: Page_10]

620 2.6 Allocation : calculating crop variables as a statistical model input. [SOURCE: Page_20]

621 Up    to the simulation of crop aerial biomass, the model used process-based dynamic 622 relationships. We propose to calculate yield and oil content by using simple statistical models [SOURCE: Page_20]

623 that depend on a set of 11 previously simulated crop variables and or used genotypic [SOURCE: Page_20]

624 parameters. New genotypic variability was introduced at this point by measuring 2 additional [SOURCE: Page_20]

625 parameters : harvest index (glasshouse experiment, Lecoeur et al., 2009) and oil content [SOURCE: Page_20]

626 (trials #1-40) in low stress conditions (potential). [SOURCE: Page_20]

627 This information, mostly reused from the mechanistic part of the model could be classified in [SOURCE: Page_20]

628 different categories: climatic, abiotic stress, canopy indexes and genotypic parameters. Table [SOURCE: Page_20]

629 2 describes more precisely these indexes and their method of calculation. [SOURCE: Page_20]

630 Previously calculated indexes were used as covariables (independent variables) in two [SOURCE: Page_20]

631 multiple linear regression models to explain the value of harvest index and oil content at [SOURCE: Page_20]

632 harvest time. When building these statistical models, we applied a simple selection procedure [SOURCE: Page_20]

633 (stepwise selection on BIC) to prune non-contributing variables, going from 13 to 7 or 8 [SOURCE: Page_20]

634 covariables, depending on the model. [SOURCE: Page_20]

635 Parameters for the additive statistical models were estimated on the “development” dataset [SOURCE: Page_20]

636 and their robustness was tested in the “evaluation” set of experiments. [SOURCE: Page_20]

637 We adopted statistical modelling as, for example, Hammer and Broad (2003) stated large [SOURCE: Page_20]

638 variations of HI dynamics with environment and, on our data, the statistical approach proved [SOURCE: Page_20]

639 to be more precise for yield prediction (Casadebaig, 2008) than the parametrisation of a [SOURCE: Page_20]

640 simple mechanistic HI model (Moot et al., 1996). We followed a similar statistical approach [SOURCE: Page_20]

641 for   modeling oil content to be more exhaustive in the driving factors, whereas the 642 mechanistic models considered only post-flowering light interception (Aguirrezabal et al., [SOURCE: Page_20]

643 2003) or grain nitrogen content (Triboi and Triboi-Blondel, 2002). In this case the process- [SOURCE: Page_20]

644 based approach performed better on low stress conditions, but showed inconsistencies on a [SOURCE: Page_20]

645 larger panel of stress conditions (Casadebaig, 2008). [SOURCE: Page_20]

## 647 RESULTS : MODEL PERFORMANCE

648 [SOURCE: Page_20]

649 1. Model performance in cases where the plant environment is well characterized [SOURCE: Page_20]

650 The model was first evaluated on a dataset where crop environment (soil, weather and [SOURCE: Page_20]

651 management) was accurately characterized, thus minimizing errors in input variables. Seven [SOURCE: Page_20]

652 crop variables were sampled and compared to the simulated ones (Figure 2) covering nearly [SOURCE: Page_20]

653 every module presented in Figure 1. [SOURCE: Page_20]

657 Phenology (sowing – flowering period) is accurately predicted (3.5%); further analysis [SOURCE: Page_21]

658 revealed   that most of the error came from the sowing-emergence phase. Trends for 659 dynamically sampled variables (absorbed nitrogen, leaf area, RIE and biomass) over the crop [SOURCE: Page_21]

660 season were predicted with most of the error coming from LCS, indicating that the model [SOURCE: Page_21]

## 730 DISCUSSION

731 The “model performance” in itself is not an easy term to define, so improving it can be [SOURCE: Page_23]

732 difficult. For statistical models, the error level calculation is a systematic and important part [SOURCE: Page_23]

733 of the analysis. A confidence interval is often estimated and associated with the model but [SOURCE: Page_23]

734 this approach is not easily applied to crop models. The general method is to compare, in [SOURCE: Page_23]

735 different situations (weather patterns, soils), the model predictions and crop observations to [SOURCE: Page_23]

736 estimate a mean error level. But this method is not ideal. First, the model has often been fitted [SOURCE: Page_23]

737 to the data (through parameter estimation); in this case, the measured error level corresponds [SOURCE: Page_23]

738 to   the fitting error and underestimates the prediction error. The representativeness or 739 reliability of observed data is rarely discussed, although the error level can vary widely in [SOURCE: Page_23]

740 situations where the model is used. In this section, we will discuss the model’s use-cases in [SOURCE: Page_23]

741 relation with its performance. [SOURCE: Page_23]

742 The   SUNFLO crop model can simulate variations in genotypic performance between 743 different environments (GEM interactions). These interactions played a significant part in [SOURCE: Page_23]

744 yield variability in both the actual and simulated network although they were higher in the [SOURCE: Page_23]

745 actual network. Such results raise several methodological points for discussion concerning [SOURCE: Page_23]

746 the relevance of a dynamic crop model for genotype evaluation, the limitations of the [SOURCE: Page_23]

747 modelling approach and finally the prospects for the use of the model. [SOURCE: Page_23]

748 1. Does SUNFLO succeeded in evaluating a range of sunflower genotypes? [SOURCE: Page_23]

749 The ability of the model to rank genotypes (relative performance) was independent from its [SOURCE: Page_23]

750 absolute predictive quality which means that the model can still be used even if simulation [SOURCE: Page_23]

751 biases are detected. [SOURCE: Page_23]

752 Ranking performance. The ranking capacity of SUNFLO is mainly due to an appropriate [SOURCE: Page_23]

753 description of the phenotypic variability among genotypes and to a correct parameterization [SOURCE: Page_23]

754 (statistical or measurement). Half of the genotypic parameters were related to resource [SOURCE: Page_23]

755 acquisition (leaf area) or resource management (water response parameters) processes, which [SOURCE: Page_23]

756 play a significant role in the environmental conditions in which sunflower is grown. But this [SOURCE: Page_24]

757 overview of crop behaviour through genotypic parameters is incomplete, as a recent study [SOURCE: Page_24]

758 showed extra phenotypic variability in rooting depth and water extraction among commercial [SOURCE: Page_24]

759 sunflower hybrids (Guilioni et al., 2008). Extending the crop description to the rooting [SOURCE: Page_24]

760 system would probably improve the model’s capacity to generate more complex interactions [SOURCE: Page_24]

761 in dry environments. [SOURCE: Page_24]

762 Absolute prediction performance. In addition, improving the model’s capacity to predict [SOURCE: Page_24]

763 absolute yield is a more demanding objective as it depends on the quality of input data and [SOURCE: Page_24]

764 observed values as well as on the nature of the dataset (for instance, the relative importance [SOURCE: Page_24]

765 of yield-limiting factors not simulated by the model). The model prediction error (15%, 0.45 -1 766 t.ha ) when estimated on a dataset was however within the range of the other sunflower crop [SOURCE: Page_24]

767 models although the input variables lacked site-specific values. This error remained very [SOURCE: Page_24]

768 close to the least significant difference or between genotypes in microplots but the list of [SOURCE: Page_24]

769 genotypes covered a long period of genotype release (30 years) and potential yield range. [SOURCE: Page_24]

770 This error would probably be too large to discriminate between genotypes from the same -1 771 release period because of their similar performance level ( ~ 0.2 t.ha ). [SOURCE: Page_24]

772 The model performance strongly relies on the quality of the dataset used for evaluation (input [SOURCE: Page_24]

773 data or field assessments). Poor reliability of input data may be attributable to weather being [SOURCE: Page_24]

774 recorded too far from the experimental location, from a rough estimation of available soil [SOURCE: Page_24]

775 water content, or, sometimes, from omissions in the components of crop management (timing [SOURCE: Page_24]

776 and amounts). The prediction error was doubled at the less reliable sites of the network (data [SOURCE: Page_24]

777 not shown). The different sampling methods used in the experimental network and the soil [SOURCE: Page_24]

778 variability within each    trial resulted in different yield or precision levels, with direct 779 consequences for the evaluation of model performance. [SOURCE: Page_24]

780   As we evaluated the model in multi-environment trials used for official cultivar registration, 781   we accepted a default environmental characterization. Trials on experimental stations would 782   have facilitated model evaluation by giving a more complete and reliable environment 783   description, but the number of locations would have been limited. In spite of its limitations, 784   the users’ network was a good opportunity for testing the model’s robustness in real 785   conditions. [SOURCE: Page_24]

790   Internal limitations. Modelling the action of combined environmental stresses (temperature, 791   light, water, nitrogen) on a given biological variable is a conceptual problem to which little 792   attention has been paid. Separating the effect of stresses is an oversimplification of field 793   reality which cannot be observed on a daily basis. Experimenting in controlled 794   environments (greenhouse, CE chambers) helps to separate stresses, but plant growth is 795   suboptimal and population effects are lost. A common solution to this problem is to create 796   stress scalars which are multiplicative or to assume a hierarchy of stresses (the main limiting 797   factor concept). These approaches can lead to an erratic sensitivity of the model: 798   multiplicative solutions tend to overestimate stress effects while ordering constraints using 799   min/max functions introduces threshold effects into the simulation. In our approach, the 800   model slightly overestimated crop performance when using multiplicative stresses, which 801   might lead to bias if new stresses are to be included in the future. [SOURCE: Page_25]

802   Environmental stress modelling can also be viewed as a trade-off between a mechanistic and 803   an empirical integration. In the first case, modelling the mechanisms can be complex: for 804   example, assuming an acceleration of leaf senescence under drought stress may result in a 805   lower light interception and a lower grain yield. On the other hand, the need for simplicity 806   leads one to adopt a more implicit and integrative view whereby drought stress can directly 807   affect the harvest index (instead of active leaf area) and thus yield. It is reasonable to believe 808   that the first solution should generate more realism in plant-environment interactions while 809   demanding more physiological insight. [SOURCE: Page_25]

810   We finally decided to mix mechanistic and statistical approaches to deal with highly 811   integrative variables such as harvest index (HI) or oil content (OC). These variables were 812   modelled at harvest using a simple statistical model dependent on covariables previously 813   simulated by the mechanistic part of the crop model throughout the growing season. But by 814   doing so, feedback effects between different dynamic variables of the model and static 815   output variables cannot be taken into account. However, this statistical solution made it 816   possible to account for environmental variability in the prediction of HI and OC and the 817   large dataset used for parameterization conferred some robustness to the prediction of these 818   final variables.
