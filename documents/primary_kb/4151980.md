### 1. Introduction

Infrastructure represent major economic, health, social and environmental challenges. Their [SOURCE: Page_3]

long-term maintenance therefore requires adapted techno-economic management rules to offset the [SOURCE: Page_3]

negative effects of their ageing and their possible obsolescence, by ensuring their gradual adaptation [SOURCE: Page_3]

to changes in service requirements. Infrastructure asset management (IAM) is defined here as a series [SOURCE: Page_3]

of actions aimed at the long-term maintenance of the capacity of an infrastructure to provide an [SOURCE: Page_3]

efficient service for users, at costs and with impacts that are tolerable for society and the environment [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Amaral et al., 2017; Le Gat et al., 2016; Ugarelli & Saegrov, 2022). In this context, the system [SOURCE: Page_3]

concerned by IAM includes infrastructure, the service with its employees, and the individuals, users [SOURCE: Page_3]

or beneficiaries of the services rendered (Rozan et al., 2017; Serag et al., 2020). The natural [SOURCE: Page_3]

environment (resource, ecosystem) is an external component in interaction with this system; it will [SOURCE: Page_3]

not be dealt with as such in this article, since it has been chosen to focus more specifically on the [SOURCE: Page_3]

human environment. [SOURCE: Page_3]

The actions implemented concern in particular the inventory of infrastructures, their [SOURCE: Page_3]

monitoring, inspection and maintenance, the analysis of their state and performance, their [SOURCE: Page_3]

reinforcement, renovation, and the definition of long-term technical-financial policies. The term [SOURCE: Page_3]

‘Infrastructure Asset Management’ links this research subject to the disciplines of engineering [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

sciences relating to the technical and physical dimension of ‘infrastructure’, and to those of [SOURCE: Page_4]

management science/public management, economics and sociology regarding the ‘asset’ dimension [SOURCE: Page_4]

(i.e. management, use, transmission, perception, value, etc.). This therefore calls for an [SOURCE: Page_4]

interdisciplinary construction in view to aiding decision-making (Le Gat et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_4]

The establishment of principles and regulations by the public authorities regarding the IAM of [SOURCE: Page_4]

structures considered critical, such as large dams and road bridges, has been driven in western [SOURCE: Page_4]

countries by the occurrence of disasters such as the rupture of the Malpasset dam (France) on 2 [SOURCE: Page_4]

December 1959, the collapse of Silver Bridge in Ohio (USA) on 15 December 1967, and that of [SOURCE: Page_4]

Wilson bridge in Tours (France) on 9 April 1978 (Cavalline et al., 2015; Le Gat et al., 2016). This [SOURCE: Page_4]

interest for IAM seems to have been increasing constantly since the 2000s, as much regarding the [SOURCE: Page_4]

owners, operators and the socioeconomic actors involved in infrastructure management and the [SOURCE: Page_4]

contracts linked to their construction, maintenance and renovation, as for the persons responsible for [SOURCE: Page_4]

public policies and users’ associations (Alegre et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_4]

Besides issues of safety and convenience, this interest is often explicitly motivated by the [SOURCE: Page_4]

importance of the infrastructure and the public services that they provide to ensure competitiveness [SOURCE: Page_4]

and economic dynamism. For some, infrastructure are considered critical, i.e. essential for [SOURCE: Page_4]

maintaining the vital functions of society, health, safety, security and the economic and social well- [SOURCE: Page_4]

being of the population (European Union, 2008). [SOURCE: Page_4]

This interest in IAM is also associated with the fear frequently expressed by many decision- [SOURCE: Page_4]

makers regarding the insufficiency of the resources devoted by the public authorities to maintaining [SOURCE: Page_4]

and renovating ageing assets with finite lifetimes. Very recently, in 2021, the Biden Build Back Better [SOURCE: Page_4]

Framework in the USA proposed public financing amounting to US$1,200 billion for the renovation [SOURCE: Page_4]

and construction of infrastructure among which those linked to water took up a considerable share. [SOURCE: Page_4]

The current French recovery plan proposes €300 million for drinking water and drainage [SOURCE: Page_4]

infrastructure. The interest for Water Infrastructure Asset Management (WIAM) can also be seen in [SOURCE: Page_4]

the recent evolution of the regulations, standards and institutional framework (for instance, at the [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

European level, the Water Framework Directive, Flood Directive) which gives new directions [SOURCE: Page_5]

regarding many types of infrastructure (in particular, water network, drainage, flood protection [SOURCE: Page_5]

systems), but which also affects investment choices. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Relatively early, needs for research emerged to understand and model infrastructure [SOURCE: Page_5]

deterioration processes (Jiang & Sinha, 1989), and to know the costs of maintaining and renovating [SOURCE: Page_5]

structures (Cavalline et al., 2015; Saegrov, 2005, 2006). The responsibility taken by institutions and [SOURCE: Page_5]

research for managing the risk pertaining to different critical structures spread at the end of the 1980s [SOURCE: Page_5]

to all collective infrastructure rendering essential services to society, especially those relating to [SOURCE: Page_5]

transport, energy and water (protection and network structures). Since the beginning of the 1990s, [SOURCE: Page_5]

INRAE (French National Research Institute for Agriculture, Food and Environment) carried out [SOURCE: Page_5]

research works aimed at improving the techno-economic management of infrastructure linked to [SOURCE: Page_5]

water as a resource – raw water, drinking water, wastewater, irrigation water, water stored in [SOURCE: Page_5]

reservoirs – or as a source of risks for human populations – floods, torrential phenomena in mountain [SOURCE: Page_5]

areas (Le Gat et al., 2016). In the middle of the 2010s, in order to consider these highly interdependent [SOURCE: Page_5]

socio-technical systems as a whole, the issue of linking the engineering sciences and the human and [SOURCE: Page_5]

social sciences emerged, as did the development of synergies between approaches and the work [SOURCE: Page_5]

carried out by teams studying heterogeneous objects: drinking water and drainage networks, dams, [SOURCE: Page_5]

flood protection systems, and protection against avalanches and debris flows. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Starting from these interdisciplinary reflections and the diversity of the infrastructure studied [SOURCE: Page_5]

and, largely supported by an analysis of the literature, four emerging themes have been identified for [SOURCE: Page_5]

which the need to produce knowledge and tools for reflection and aiding decision-making appear to [SOURCE: Page_5]

proposals based on the authors’ experience faced with an analysis of the perspectives set out in recent [SOURCE: Page_6]

### 3. The temporal and spatial dimensions of WIAM and their consistency

3.1 WIAM activities and temporal dimension [SOURCE: Page_9]

WIAM activities are by nature operational, decisional and informational; they are linked around [SOURCE: Page_9]

four temporal aspects (Alegre et al., 2012): [SOURCE: Page_9]

    the real time is that of monitoring the operation of the infrastructure, the implementation of [SOURCE: Page_9]

inspection, corrective and preventive maintenance and renovation operations (renovation, [SOURCE: Page_9]

replacement, or simple shutdown) of components of the infrastructure, the reporting of these [SOURCE: Page_9]

actions within the information system of the management service; [SOURCE: Page_9]

    the short-term, yearly or two-yearly, is that of establishing programs of equipment, inspection [SOURCE: Page_9]

and renovation, in particular requiring the prioritisation of the infrastructure components [SOURCE: Page_9]

concerned, besides the choice of technologies and suppliers; [SOURCE: Page_9]

    the medium-term, typically ten years, concerns the budgetary planning of annual and biennial [SOURCE: Page_9]

programs of equipment resulting from multi-annual investment plans, inspection and [SOURCE: Page_9]

renovation, and the consistency of their funding (subsidy, loan, or pricing access to the [SOURCE: Page_9]

service), and possibly the type of organisation of the management service (e.g., public [SOURCE: Page_9]

corporation vs. delegation); [SOURCE: Page_9]

    the long-term (several decades) is that of defining the strategic objectives of WIAM, aimed at [SOURCE: Page_9]

transmitting to future generations assets whose state will ensure a quality service in return for [SOURCE: Page_9]

tolerable maintenance and investment costs. [SOURCE: Page_9]

A central question in WIAM is whether or not the investment budget is sufficient to fund the [SOURCE: Page_9]

(long-term) strategic objectives on the one hand, and the constraints of implementing the annual [SOURCE: Page_9]

program of renovation works on the other. Logically, this question of consistency between timeframes [SOURCE: Page_9]

must be included when considering the fate of assets. It therefore entails remedying situations that [SOURCE: Page_9]

are rather caricatural though nevertheless encountered in practice (Large et al., 2014), such as that in [SOURCE: Page_9]

which the annual programming of renovating the piping of a large drinking water network begins [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

with ranking sections according to the risk due to their deterioration and their context of operation [SOURCE: Page_10]

(using the multicriteria method: rate of failure and hydraulic and socioeconomic impacts, [SOURCE: Page_10]

coordination with roadworks, increasing diameter, etc. - cf. Le Gauffre et al. (2007)), whereas [SOURCE: Page_10]

renovation budgets are planned over periods of ten years with the sole aim of limiting the average [SOURCE: Page_10]

age of the pipe sections. [SOURCE: Page_10]

This quest for temporal consistency leads to proposing two complementary directions. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Orientation 1 is aimed at linking the short-term management of defined objects (management of a [SOURCE: Page_10]

specific structure) and that of the medium-term management of the same objects considered as [SOURCE: Page_10]

statistical items (‘pool management’). Orientation 2 dedicated to ensuring the cohesion of the [SOURCE: Page_10]

operational and tactical decisions of budget planning (Figure 2). The research proposals from 1 to 7 [SOURCE: Page_10]
