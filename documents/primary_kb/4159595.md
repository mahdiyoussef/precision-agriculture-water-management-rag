Sierra) 17 18   12 19   13 20 21   14    ABSTRACT 22   15 23 24   16    Yam (Dioscorea spp.) is an important source of food for millions of people throughout the 25 26   17    tropics. Yam yields are constrained by several factors such as low soil fertility and a poor 27   18    response of the crop to nutrient supply. Here we present an experimental and modeling 28 29   19    study performed to test the hypothesis that N leaching in the humid tropics is a key factor 30   20    that explains the poor response of water yam (Dioscorea alata L.) to N fertilization. A field 31 32   21    experiment was carried out to assess the impact of three levels of N supply (50, 125 and 200 33 34   22    kg N ha-1) on crop growth and soil N availability. The N fertilizer was split into two equal 35   23    doses (i.e., 50%/50% split ratio), one applied just before emergence and the other a month 36 37   24    after tuber initiation, which corresponds to the current management used by farmers. The 38   25    CropSyst-Yam model was adapted to include a new soil-crop N component describing the 39 40   26    effects of limiting N conditions on yam growth, and then to test different N fertilizer 41 42 27    managements. The experimental results indicated that all variables linked to the soil-crop 43   28    system were affected by the increase in N supply (soil N availability, crop biomass, leaf area 44 45   29    and N uptake), and the model well described these impacts. Nitrogen leaching throughout 46   30    the field experiment represented about one third of the total N supplied by each treatment. 47 48   31    Observed and predicted data revealed that N leaching reduced yam growth under the N50 49 32    and N125 treatments by reducing soil N availability near the tuber initiation stage. This effect 50 51   33    was not observed under the N200 treatment. Model results showed that N leaching and its 52 53   34    negative impact near tuber initiation in N50 and N125 could be diminished by advancing the 54   35    second N application by two or three weeks, and by using a split ratio of 70%/30%. Model 55 56   36    results also indicated that this fertilizer strategy would even be suitable during very rainy 57   37    growing seasons. 58 59   38 60 61 62 63 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_2]

Introduction 6 7 43 8   44          Yam is a multispecies tuber crop grown throughout the humid subtropics and tropics. 9 10   45   Despite increasing demand, particularly in West Africa, Central America, the Caribbean, 11   46   Pacific Islands and Southeast Asia, yam yields remain low (Onyeka et al., 2006; Asiedu and 12 13   47   Sartie, 2010). Because declining soil fertility is a major problem in tropical regions, higher 14 15 48   yields could be achieved using mineral fertilizers (Hgaza et al., 2012). However, many 16   49   authors have indicated a limited effect of fertilizer on yam tuber yield (Cornet, 2005; Hgaza et 17 18   50   al., 2019). Hgaza et al. (2012) highlighted the very shallow rooting systems of yams and 19   51   proposed a need to investigate the relationship between yield and limitations on nutrient 20 21   52   uptake. In many tropical regions, N is the main limiting nutrient for the photosynthetic 22   53   process, plant hormone and proteomic changes and the growth-development of plants in 23 24   54   order to complete their life cycle (Anas et al. 2020). In humid tropics high annual rainfall 25 26   55   considerably exceeds potential evapotranspiration (Sansoulet et al., 2007; Jankowski et al., 27   56   2018), and then N leaching combined with the shallow rooting system might explain the poor 28 29   57   response of yam to N fertilization. 30   58          Below-ground soil-plant interactions are difficult to assess in field experiments (Toal 31 32   59   et al., 2000). However, a mechanistic understanding of such interactions is clearly needed to 33 34   60   increase current yam yields by improving the management of N supply in the humid tropics 35   61   (O’Sullivan, 2010). The development of comprehensive models, aimed mainly at increasing 36 37   62   our understanding of the interactions between crops and their main growth factors, has 38   63   enabled the investigation of these interactions (Marcos et al., 2009). In a detailed review of 39 40   64   the models developed for tuber crops, Raymundo et al. (2014) reported that although more 41 42 65   than thirty had been proposed for potato, only three yam models had been described in the 43   66   literature. They pointed out that the detailed field experiments and agronomic research 44 45   67   necessary to test and improve crop models were rare regarding yam crops. The CropSyst- 46   68   Yam model has thus proved to be a powerful tool to identify the underlying mechanisms 47 48   69   affecting yam yields and to assess the climatic factors involved in the high year-to-year 49 70   variability observed in yam yields (Marcos et al. 2009, 2011). 50 51   71          As the original version of CropSyst-Yam only performs under non-limiting N 52 53   72   conditions, in the present study we modified the model to include a more detailed simulation 54   73   of N balance in the soil-yam system, in order to evaluate yam performance under N limited 55 56   74   conditions. More specifically, the goal of the study was to test the hypothesis that losses of 57   75   available N by leaching in the humid tropics is a key factor to explain the poor response of 58 59   76   water yam (Dioscorea alata L.) to N supply. The model was calibrated using experimental 60 61 62 63 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_3]

The CropSyst-Yam model 9 10 159 11 160          In this section we present the main traits of the model, further details on its 12 13 161   functioning and on the crop N component are provided in Appendix A. 14 15 162          Marcos et al. (2009, 2011) developed CropSyst-Yam based on the CropSyst- 16 163   Simpotato model (Alva et al., 2004). Under CropSyst-Yam, the duration of the EM-TI and TI- 17 18 164   harvest (HA) phases of water yam are calculated as a function of photoperiod and 19 165   temperature (Marcos et al., 2009). Yam growth is partially described by the CropSyst- 20 21 166   Simpotato approach to simulate C partitioning (Marcos et al., 2011). During the EM-TI phase, 22 167   roots, leaves and stems are the dominant sinks for C, while tubers priority increases from TI 23 24 168   and becomes the major sink of C after the inflection point of the sigmoid curve of growth. 25 26 169   Changes in LAI are controlled by C assimilation, the canopy's relative mean leaf age and the 27 170   competition with tubers for C and N mainly after TI. Leaf senescence begins when old leaves 28 29 171   reach their maximum duration, which corresponds approximately to the inflection point of the 30 172   curve of tuber growth. The model describes the variation in Radiation Use Efficiency (RUE) 31 32 173   after TI as a function of the canopy's relative mean leaf age, a season-dependent maximum 33 34 174   RUE value and the development stage (Marcos et al., 2011). The model was calibrated and 35 175   tested using datasets from 15 field experiments carried out in Guadeloupe under non-limiting 36 37 176   conditions for water and nutrients and over a wide range of planting dates and photoperiods 38 177   (Marcos et al., 2009, 2011). 39 40 178          During the present study, we modified the original CropSyst-Yam model in order to 41 42 179   include a more detailed simulation of N balance in the soil-yam system, and then to be able 43 180   to evaluate yam performance under N limiting conditions. The soil in the ridge (i.e., 0.4 m 44 45 181   depth) was divided into eight 0.05 m-layers, and soil processes were computed for each soil 46 182   layer. The soil N component of the original model was modified to describe separately 47 48 183   mineralization and nitrification, and then soil available N is characterized by NO$_3$ and NH$_4$ 49 184   contents. Nitrification is controlled by soil acidity, which can partially inhibit this process, as 50 51 185   proposed by Sierra et al. (2003). We also included a retention factor for NO$_3$ to simulate the 52 53 186   effect of the anion exchange capacity (AEC) of acid ferralsols on the reduction of NO$_3$ 54 187   leaching (Sierra et al., 2003; Sansoulet et al., 2007). Although plant N uptake is proportional 55 56 188   to the content in each mineral N form (with no preference for either form), leaching only 57 189   affects NO$_3$. It is important to note that N volatilization and denitrification were not considered 58 59 60 61 62 63 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

190    during this study because some properties of our soil, such as acidity and fast water 1 2 191   drainage, do not favor these processes (Sierra et al., 2001, 2003). 3 192          Concerning plant N, we included new, detailed N partitioning and redistribution 4 5 193   schemes between plant organs so that the model would be appropriate with more flexible N 6 7 194   dynamics while retaining the simplicity of a field scale crop model. Nitrogen limited growth 8 195   was thus computed based on the balance between available N (soil N uptake plus 9 10 196   remobilized N and seed-tuber N) and the N plant demand calculated using these additional N 11 197   partitioning and redistribution schemes. If daily available N is not sufficient to meet plant N 12 13 198   demand at critical N concentrations, growth is reduced accordingly. After available N and N 14 15 199   plant demand are compared, actual soil N uptake is calculated and used to update soil 16 200   mineral N status. Prior to leaf senescence, the leaf N/steam N ratio is not affected by N 17 18 201   availability and varies as a function of crop development. In other words, stem N is not a 19 202   buffering compartment between leaf N and tuber N (see Appendix A). After leaf senescence, 20 21 203   a limitation of N availability induces a sharply decrease in this ratio as a consequence of a 22 204   strong remobilization of leaf N to tubers. 23 24 205 25 26 206   2.4. Model calibration and simulations 27 207 28 29 208          Model parameter values were those reported by Marcos et al. (2009, 2011), except 30 209   for maximum daily N uptake and the soil N concentration at which N uptake limitation begins, 31 32 210   which were set for this study. The maximum N uptake was set at 1.5 kg N ha-1 d-1, which 33 34 211   corresponds to the value observed by Hgaza et al. (2012) for water yam under non-limiting N 35 212   conditions. We assumed that N uptake limitation starts at 1 mg N kg-1, which corresponds to 36 37 213   the mean value reported by Greenwood and Draycott (1995) for potato. We assessed the 38 214   performance of the model by comparing observed and predicted data of soil mineral N, dry 39 40 215   and N biomass, LAI, the N content of aboveground biomass and yield. Comparisons 41 42 216   between observed and predicted data were made using regression analysis and Root Mean 43 217   Square Error (RMSE) values (Marcos et al., 2009). 44 45 218          After model calibration, several simulations were carried out to assess the impact of 46 219   changes to the management of N fertilizer on N leaching and yam growth (e.g., timing, rate 47 48 220   and split of the N supply). Simulations of the timing of N applications were performed using 49 221   the same fertilizer rates and split ratios applied in the field experiment, and consisted in 50 51 222   advancing the second N application by two weeks (i.e., at DAP 99), without changing the first 52 53 223   application. Simulations to test the fertilizer rate and split ratio were carried out considering 54 224   the same timing of N fertilization applied in the field experiment. We tested nine fertilizer 55 56 225   rates (ranging from 0 to 200 kg N ha-1) and five split ratios (from 50%/50% to 100%/0%). We 57 226   also performed some simulations for the 2005-2006 growing season, which was 58 59 227   characterized by the highest level of rainfall in French Antilles in the past twenty years. The 60 61 62 63 64 65 [SOURCE: Page_7]

416           During the present study, the observed yam growth and yield were significantly 1 2 417   affected by the increase in N supply. In a detailed review of 218 field studies about the 3 418   response of several yam species (e.g., D. alata, D. rotundata, D. cayenensis) to N 4 5 419   fertilization, Cornet et al. (2005) reported that only 25% of them presented a significant 6 7 420   response. The percentage was the lowest for D. alata (e.g., 15%). Some authors suggested 8 421   that the poor response of yam to N supply might be linked to the shallow-rooting and low root 9 10 422   density of yam species (Hgaza et al., 2012, 2019). Hgaza et al. (2019) observed that root 11 423   length density in the yam mound averaged 0.3 cm cm-3. However, this value seem to be too 12 13 424   small in relation to the root density data reported for other crops; e.g., from 11 cm cm-3 to 16 14 15 425   cm cm-3 for potato (Ahmadi et al., 2014; Nyawade et al., 2018). Although we did not measure 16 426   root density in our study, model results indicated that yam root density in the three N 17 18 427   treatments were of the same order of magnitude but 2-3 times lower than those reported for 19 428   potato (e.g., 5.4 cm cm-3 at tuber maturation, Fig. A13). Nevertheless, as highlighted by 20 21 429   Milroy et al. (2019) for potato, we consider that while the rooting characteristics of yam 22 430   species may explain the variable response to N supply, it does not appear to be an absolute 23 24 431   limitation because yields as high as 50 Mg fresh matter ha-1 are frequently reported in many 25 26 432   tropical regions; e.g., Diby et al. (2011) in Ivory Coast, and Marcos et al. (2011) in 27 433   Guadeloupe. The observed and simulated data on soil mineral N obtained during the present 28 29 434   study suggested that the leaching of available N was a major factor to explain the differences 30 435   between N treatments. This is in line with the results reported by several authors concerning 31 32 436   the impact of N leaching on the poor response of yam to N supply in humid tropics (e.g., 33 34 437   O'Sullivan, 2010; Hgaza et al., 2012). Indeed, the negative effect of N leaching on crop 35 438   growth might be exacerbated in a crop such as yam which has a shallow root system; e.g., 36 37 439   up to 0.4 m according to Melteras et al. (2008) and O’Sullivan (2008). 38 440          Under our modeling approach the limitation of crop N uptake occurred when the 39 40 441   mineral N content was <1 mg N kg-1 in at least one soil layer, being the value observed by 41 42 442   Grenwood and Draycott (1995) for potato. For N50, this threshold was attained between DAP 43 443   83 (start of tuber growth) and DAP 113 (second N application) and from DAP 120 to the end 44 45 444   of the growth cycle. The limitation of N uptake during these periods was linked to N leaching 46 445   and also to the rapid increase in biomass production and N demand by the crop after TI 47 48 446   (Marcos et al., 2011). Although the second N application removed the N limitation observed 49 447   under N50 near TI, this N supply was not sufficient to compensate for leaching and crop N 50 51 448   demand after DAP 120. For N125, the threshold for N limitation was attained between DAP 52 53 449   100 and the second N fertilization at DAP 113. No limitation on N uptake was observed for 54 450   this treatment after DAP 113. In other words, just two weeks of N limitation during the start of 55 56 451   tuber growth caused an important reduction in LAI and biomass production compared to the 57 452   N200 treatment, which did not display any N limitation during the growth cycle. Changes in leaf N content, radiation interception and radiation use efficiency 6 7 458 8 459          In our study, leaf N content was sensitive to reflect the low soil N availability observed 9 10 460   in the N50 treatment. This was in line with the results obtained by Cornet (2005) concerning 11 461   a field experiment on the same yam variety as that used here (i.e., Belep). From our theoretical approach, this 22 468   result reflected the higher priority given by the model to maintaining leaf N status at the 23 24 469   expense of an increase in leaf area under slight N stress, as observed under N125. From a 25 26 470   physiological point of view, this would imply a trade-off between leaf N content and LAI. 27 471   Quantifying the relevance of such an effect requires further experimental and analytical work 28 29 472   on the response of yam to N restriction. 30 473          The linear relationship observed between crop N biomass and LAI up to LAImax (Fig. 31 32 474   A$_6$) is similar to that reported by Lemaire et al. Impact of N fertilizer management 3 494 4 5 495          Model simulations concerning N fertilizer management focused on overcoming the 6 7 496   negative effects of N leaching around the start of tuber growth, as observed N50 and N125. 8 497   In this respect, advancing the second N application by two weeks was useful in reducing the 9 10 498   effect of N leaching on yam growth and induced an increase in yields in N50 and N125. 11 499   However, advancing this application requires a determination of the time of TI, which might 12 13 500   be difficult under field conditions because it varies as a function of planting date (Marcos et 14 15 501   al., 2009); e.g., the earlier the planting date, the longer the duration of the EM-TI phase. It 16 502   thus appears that advancing the second N application by two weeks (for later dates) or three 17 18 503   weeks (for earlier dates) might be an acceptable practice to reduce the risk of N limitation at 19 504   TI. 20 21 505          The splitting of N fertilization is an appropriate approach to improve the balance 22 506   between N need and supply while reducing the risk of N leaching (Goffart et al., 2008), and it 23 24 507   is a practice that is currently applied in the humid tropics (Hgaza et al., 2019; Raphael et al., 25 26 508   2020). Several authors have reported that the positive effects of splitting the N application on 27 509   yields and N leaching in potato were particularly noticeable in a context of rainy growing 28 29 510   seasons inducing high N leaching (Goffart et al., 2008; Rens et al., 2018). This is in line with 30 511   our model results which indicated that for fertilizer rates >125 kg N ha-1, a single N 31 32 512   application near EM (e.g., split ratio 100%/0% in Fig. 6) was less effective in terms of yield 33 34 513   and N leaching than the other split ratios. Moreover, under that fertilizer rate, a split ratio of 35 514   70%/30% was more effective than the 50%/50% ratio in ensuring sufficient N availability at TI 36 37 515   and increasing yam yields. This was also found during the 2005-2006 season which 38 516   experienced higher rainfall and N leaching. 39 40 517          We performed additional calculations to test an N fertilization strategy that combined 41 42 518   a split ratio of 70%/30% with advancing the second N application by two weeks. We found 43 519   that yam yield achieved under N125 was close to that obtained for the initial simulations for 44 45 520   N200 with a 50%/50% split ratio. This result was associated with higher N availability at TI in 46 521   the tested combination (Fig. A10). It appears that the fertilizer rate could be reduced while 47 48 522   maintaining yields and reducing N leaching, by combining a change in the current split ratio 49 523   and in the time of the second N application. Moreover, the reduction of the fertilizer rate 50 51 524   would diminish the risk of diffuse pollution of water resources, which is a major concern in 52 53 525   French Antilles. 54 526 55 56 527 57 528   5. 530           During this study we tested a hypothesis regarding the key role played by N leaching 1 2 531   in the humid tropics in terms of controlling the response of yam to N supply. One major result 3 532   obtained from the field experiment was that N leaching near the start of tuber growth strongly 4 5 533   impacted the subsequent crop phases and yield. Although model results indicated that a 6 7 534   change in the current N fertilizer practices (timing and split ratio) would allow reduce soil N 8 535   limitation at tuber initiation, N leaching was always very high and accounted for about one 9 10 536   third of the fertilizer rate under all the experimental and simulated conditions analyzed during 11 537   this study. Indeed, other practices need to be implemented, together with the change of the 12 13 538   N fertilizer strategy, in order to assure yam yields while reducing the risk of diffuse pollution 14 15 539   of water resources. Among these practices, the use of organic fertilizers and soil mulching 16 540   could be easily adopted by smallholder farmers involved in yam production. 17 18 541          Leaf N status correctly reflected severe soil N constraints (i.e., in N50), but it failed to 19 542   match the negative effect on yam yields of N limitation occurring during short-term periods 20 21 543   (i.e., two weeks in N125).
