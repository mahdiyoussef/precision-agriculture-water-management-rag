9    Wastewater reuse (WW-reuse) is an alternative water resource that may answer present and future [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   1     Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

40   In the context of climate change, the evolving state of water resources has become an issue of [SOURCE: Page_4]

41   worldwide importance. While more than half of the world's population lives in conditions of severe [SOURCE: Page_4]

42   physical water scarcity during at least one month per year (Mekonnen and Hoekstra, 2016), global [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   water withdrawals have increased by a factor of six over the past 100 years (AQUASTAT, n.d.). Due to [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   population growth, economic development and increasing urbanization, urban water supply has [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   become particularly vulnerable. To address this issue and meet future water demands, alternative [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   water resources must be identified and securely deployed. Wastewater reuse (WW-reuse) [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   represents a promising response to water scarcity issues (WWAP, 2017) in various fields of [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   application. Agriculture, for which the water supply accounts for more than 70% of the world’s water [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   withdrawals (FAO, 2016), is the first WW-reuse market (Lautze et al., 2014). However, reclaimed [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   water as well as artificial groundwater recharge can also supply industrial or domestic water uses [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   (Lazarova et al., 2013). Furthermore, in agricultural irrigation, wastewater can provide nutrients for [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   fertigation (Sala and Serra, 2004) and its nitrogen or phosphorus content largely depends on the type [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   of wastewater treatments (Iannelli and Giraldi, 2011). [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   The environmental efficiency of WW-reuse largely depends on the “fit to purpose” water [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   regeneration treatment, which is required to meet the local water quality policies of each water [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   sector (agriculture, industry or domestic). Under certain conditions (wastewater origin, reclaimed [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   water use, location) raw sewage even can be directly reused while in other cases advanced [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   wastewater treatment is required. The environmental impacts of water regeneration directly depend [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   on the nature of the treatment technology, while the high amount of energy required for advanced [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   or tertiary treatments (Pintilie et al., 2016) is a significant driver of indirect impacts (Lane et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   2015). When WW-reuse results in water resource savings (as in coastal situations or when [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   groundwater is used in a non-renewable way), local water scarcity also becomes a key parameter [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   when the environmental impact of avoided local water deprivation is assessed. Thus, the [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   environmental evaluation of WW-reuse as a non-conventional water resource depends on a water- [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   energy nexus linking local water availability with the impact of water treatment. [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   In terms of decision support for the choice of reclaimed water as a relevant water resource among [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   other options, not only economic but also environmental issues should be taken into account. Hence, [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   it is necessary to identify the conditions for which the environmental impacts of WW-reuse (energy, [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   infrastructure, etc.) are lower than the expected environmental benefits (water resource and [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   nutrients saving). With Life Cycle Assessment (LCA), as a holistic tool, the global environmental [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   efficiency of WW-reuse can be evaluated, including the impacts of water deprivation, the type of [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   treatment technology and the energy consumption. In this context, a literature review must be [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   conducted to identify the various approaches used and the types of case studies already [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   investigated. On this basis, it should become possible to define the problems to be solved in order to [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   propose and apply a coherent and harmonised approach. [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   2     Literature review and purpose of the study [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   Several studies on the environmental assessment of WW-reuse have been carried out, many of [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   which have implemented LCA methodology. In order to draw the lessons to be learned from these [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   publications, a literature review was conducted (keywords “LCA” and “wastewater reuse” within [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   exclusively scientific journals, i.e. excluding grey literature) and summarized in Figure 1 (see Appendix [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   A, Table A.1 for the complete literature review). Among the 30 articles, two categories of reuse [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   situations have been identified: (i) the reuse of urban wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) effluent [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   (i.e. wastewater collected on a territorial scale and originating from domestic, industrial and/or run- [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   off sources) and (ii) the reuse of raw wastewater (i.e. before collection), directly at the site of [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   generation (in-situ). This second type of effluent mainly derives from domestic (source separation) or [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   industrial (recycling) reuse cases. In both situations: post-WWTP reuse and raw wastewater reuse, [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   the effluents can be regenerated (generally through tertiary treatment) or not (direct reuse) [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   according to the reuse application and local water-use policies. Therefore, three major characteristics [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   can define a WW-reuse situation: the origin of the effluent, its optional treatment and the intended [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   wastewater user. [SOURCE: Page_6]

91 [SOURCE: Page_6]

92       Figure 1 - Classification synthesis of WW-reuse LCA studies from a literature review of 30 publications. [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   Only 7 out of the 30 reviewed articles attempt to address WW-reuse issues in a virtual or partially [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   generic manner (Figure 1). However, none of them are truly generic because they do not vary by [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   more than one or two parameters, such as applied technologies, electricity mix, water stress or [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   reference scenario. Thus, the vast majority of studies assessing the environmental performance of [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   WW- reuse (23 out of 30 reviewed papers) are directly linked to a particular geographical location [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   and to a specific case of application (water supply and/or wastewater user). In these case studies, the [SOURCE: Page_7]

99   climatic context, and in particular the level of water scarcity, then becomes an important parameter [SOURCE: Page_7]

100   (especially affecting the baseline water supply scenario). Unsurprisingly, for more than 80 % of [SOURCE: Page_7]

101   reviewed cases, the WW-reuse situation is located in semi-arid to arid geographical areas. [SOURCE: Page_7]

102   The necessary comparison between the burdens of a WW-reuse situation (including zero discharge of [SOURCE: Page_7]

103   wastewater which is reused with or without regeneration) and a baseline scenario (wastewater [SOURCE: Page_7]

104   discharge and local or average conventional water supply) is not always achieved. Out of thirty [SOURCE: Page_7]

105   studies, only thirteen compare the WW-reuse supply scenario with an average tap-water supply [SOURCE: Page_7]

106   (conventional treatment from databases) and nine with a local water resource supply. In several [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   cases, since water resource saving represents a basis hypothesis in WW-reuse, regardless of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   initial destination of the reused effluent, a water consumption balance is not necessarily carried out [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   between the baseline scenario and the WW-reuse scenario. Indeed, half of the studies that actually [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   account for water consumption in both baseline and WW-reuse scenarios (in order to reveal water [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   savings by reuse) use a volumetric method that only account for freshwater withdrawals at user. This [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   hypothesis is not consistent with the ISO 14046 water footprint (based on LCA) which is very clear on [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   this point: water consumption is water that has been removed from, but not returned to, the same [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   waterbody (i.e. water withdrawal minus release). Indeed, on one hand, the presumption that WW- [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   reuse systematically generates water savings is only valid in certain specific cases that will be [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   described further on. On the other hand, although the energy consumption of the WW-reuse system [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   (wastewater regeneration and supply system) is a major factor in its environmental performance, the [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   effects of energy mix are lacking in most studies. Indeed, only four reviewed publications examine [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   the influence of the electricity mix on WW-reuse environmental efficiency. In more than half of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   studies, irrigation is identified as a potential wastewater use. Other wastewater consumers are [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   investigated for non-potable domestic or urban use, groundwater artificial recharge or industrial use. [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   Consequently, LCA that includes water footprint indicators should support decision-making by [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   identifying and differentiating situations where WW-reuse is environmentally advantageous from [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   those where the local water resource remains less impacting. However, this literature review [SOURCE: Page_8]

125   highlights two challenges in state of the art environmental assessments of WW-reuse: (i) the [SOURCE: Page_8]

126   heterogeneity of the assumptions of WW-reuse LCA studies (especially concerning the water [SOURCE: Page_8]

127   balance, the perimeter of the system and the lack of comparison with a reference system); (ii) the [SOURCE: Page_8]

128   fact that most case studies concern coastal areas where water is scarce. This paper aims at answering [SOURCE: Page_8]

129   these two issues by proposing a homogeneous and robust LCA framework for WW-reuse [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   environmental assessment and applying it to a global case study, across contrasting situations [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   representative of current WW-reuse problematics. To be consistent with the main water use [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   targeted (and thus mostly concerned by water scarcity and climate change), the chosen case study [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   corresponds to the main WW-reuse application scenario: urban WWTP effluent reuse for irrigation. [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   As discussed previously, water scarcity, geographical location (littoral or continental), and energy mix [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   are fundamental parameters whose influence have been examined. [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   3      Material and methods [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   The methodological framework of this study is based on the four steps of Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   as defined in ISO 14040 and 14044 standards (ISO 14040, 2006; ISO 14044, 2006): (i) Goal & scope [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   definition (section 2.1), (ii) Life Cycle Inventory (LCI, section 2.2), (iii) Life Cycle Impact Assessment [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   (LCIA, section 2.3) and (iv) Interpretation of results (section 3). [SOURCE: Page_8]

141    3.1    Goal and scope definition [SOURCE: Page_8]

142          3.1.1 Goal [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   The goals of this study are (i) to elaborate a robust and homogeneous framework for the evaluation [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   of WW-reuse environmental efficiency and (ii) to apply it to a few worldwide archetype situations to [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   better identify the main drivers of the environmental efficiency of wastewater reuse. In this view, a [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   generic evaluation of the eco-efficiency of WW-reuse was achieved through the life cycle assessment [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   (LCA) of the most commonly applied WW-reuse scenario (urban WW-reuse for irrigation). By [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   comparing the environmental burdens of the WW-reuse scenario with those of a baseline water [SOURCE: Page_9]

149   supply scenario, under contrasting climatic and geographical conditions, the present objective is to [SOURCE: Page_9]

150   identify situations (and associated driving parameters) where WW-reuse proves to be of [SOURCE: Page_9]

151   environmental relevance. The proposed approach should allow for generic results to be generated [SOURCE: Page_9]

152   from any specific case study for guidance in decision making. Indeed, the results could then be [SOURCE: Page_9]

153   optimised by adjustment of parameters). [SOURCE: Page_9]

154         3.1.2 Scope of the study, functional unit and system boundaries [SOURCE: Page_9]

155   The WW-reuse application selected for this study is an urban WWTP effluent reused for local [SOURCE: Page_9]

156   irrigation. This scenario is compared to a baseline scenario (Figure 2) that corresponds to a local [SOURCE: Page_9]

157   water supply mix for irrigation, based on the WSmix (Water Supply mix) model (Leão et al., 2018). In [SOURCE: Page_9]

158   order to avoid masking effects, all other parameters that are equivalent in WW-reuse vs. WSmix [SOURCE: Page_9]

159   comparisons are removed from LCA calculations (ceteris paribus, Figure 2 and Figure 3). The [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   functional unit is the supply of 1 m^3 of water at the user gate (irrigated plot). The WWTP effluent [SOURCE: Page_9]

161   discharge to the local water body is included in the system boundary as an avoided impact in the [SOURCE: Page_9]

162   reuse scenario. In agriculture, WW-reuse can also provide nutrients for fertigation (Meneses et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

163   2010) and their nitrogen or phosphorus content is largely dependent on the type of wastewater [SOURCE: Page_9]

164   treatments. Thus, the nutrient content of reclaimed water for irrigation entails an environmental [SOURCE: Page_9]

165   benefit since it avoids fertilizer production. [SOURCE: Page_9]

166 [SOURCE: Page_10]

167                     Figure 2 - System boundaries for both WW-reuse and Baseline scenario [SOURCE: Page_10]

168   An exhaustive representation of system boundaries is available in the supplementary Appendix A, [SOURCE: Page_10]

169   Figure A.1. The water balance of the studied systems differs according to the geographical location: [SOURCE: Page_10]

170   whether it is coastal or continental. In a continental water basin, the amount of water consumed, [SOURCE: Page_10]

171   defined as the difference between withdrawal and discharge (ISO 14046), is the same for both [SOURCE: Page_10]

172   scenarios (Figure 3). This balance is valid, assuming a renewable use of groundwater resources and [SOURCE: Page_10]

173   considering the recharge of the local water basin through the discharge of treated wastewater. In [SOURCE: Page_10]

174   this case, WW-reuse does not lead to water saving. However, when wastewater is discharged into [SOURCE: Page_10]

175   the sea, all water withdrawals from the catchment area are considered as water consumption. Thus, [SOURCE: Page_10]

176   as long as these assumptions are made, WW-reuse only leads to water saving in coastal areas. [SOURCE: Page_10]

177   This work is limited to the following two contrasting case studies: (i) coastal areas where treated [SOURCE: Page_10]

178   wastewater is discharged into the sea and therefore lost and (ii) continental areas where treated [SOURCE: Page_10]

179   wastewater is returned to the local water body (i.e. not lost). It is obvious that many other specific [SOURCE: Page_10]

180   configurations exist, such as WWTP discharge into intermittent streams in dry areas where [SOURCE: Page_10]

181   discharges never return to the waterbody were water was originally extracted. It would be difficult to [SOURCE: Page_10]

182   consistently take this type of very specific situation into account as long as LCA does not comprise a [SOURCE: Page_10]

183   water fate model that enables the interactions between different water compartments to be [SOURCE: Page_11]

184   assessed (surface water, groundwater, soil, vegetation, atmospheric moisture, etc.), as discussed by [SOURCE: Page_11]

185   Núnez et al., 2018. [SOURCE: Page_11]

188         3.1.3 Sets of parameters and experimental protocol [SOURCE: Page_11]

189   The goals of the study are to compare (i) some archetypes of wastewater reuse with (ii) a set of 3 [SOURCE: Page_11]

190   contrasting water supply mixes (WSmix) described in section 2.2.1. For each of the two studied [SOURCE: Page_11]

191   systems (irrigation water provided by an agricultural WSmix or treated urban WWTP effluent), the [SOURCE: Page_11]

192   main key parameters are identified in order to build a versatile experimental protocol, which would [SOURCE: Page_11]

193   reflect the different WW-reuse situations. First, the coastal locations are distinguished from the [SOURCE: Page_11]

194   continental locations as this directly affects the water balance and thus the water saving potential by [SOURCE: Page_11]

195   WW-reuse. Water scarcity levels and electricity mix are two other parameters which have been [SOURCE: Page_11]

196   chosen to reflect impact of the water-energy nexus on WW-reuse environmental efficiency. [SOURCE: Page_11]

197   Concerning the baseline scenario, three WSmix are investigated by varying the origin of the water [SOURCE: Page_11]

198   resource: a world average conventional WSmix, a non-renewable WSmix from groundwater resource [SOURCE: Page_11]

199   and an unconventional WSmix from desalinated seawater. The regeneration technology train (RT, [SOURCE: Page_12]

200   combination of several treatment units) mainly defines the WW-reuse scenario. “Fit-to-purpose” [SOURCE: Page_12]

201   wastewater regeneration generally involves tertiary (or advanced) treatment technologies. The range [SOURCE: Page_12]

202   of technologies for reclaimed water treatment for irrigation depends both on the quality of the [SOURCE: Page_12]

203   effluent and on the regulations for reclaimed water quality. It spans from mild treatment, generally [SOURCE: Page_12]

204   physico-chemical processes and disinfection (e.g. advanced oxidation processes in Arzate et al., 2019, [SOURCE: Page_12]

205   sand filtration and UV disinfection in Carré et al., 2017 or coagulation-flocculation, chlorination and [SOURCE: Page_12]

206   UV disinfection in Meneses et al., 2010), to intensive treatment by membrane filtration (e.g., reverse [SOURCE: Page_12]

207   osmosis in Bravo and Ferrer, 2011 and Hsien et al., 2019 or ultrafiltration in Büyükkamaci and Karaca, [SOURCE: Page_12]

208   2017 and Carré et al., 2017). Thus, two representative regeneration trains have been investigated: a [SOURCE: Page_12]

209   mild-treatment RT$_1$ (sand filtration, coagulation-flocculation, ultra-violet disinfection) and an [SOURCE: Page_12]

210   intensive treatment RT$_2$ (sand filtration, coagulation-flocculation, microfiltration, reverse osmosis, [SOURCE: Page_12]

211   ultra-violet disinfection). Therefore, the implementation of the environmental efficiency evaluation [SOURCE: Page_12]

212   of a selected WW-reuse application is based on the comparison (by LCA) of the environmental [SOURCE: Page_12]

213   burdens of three representative water supply alternatives (Baseline, WW-reuse RT$_1$, WW-reuse RT$_2$) [SOURCE: Page_12]

214   in contrasting climatic and geographical situations (supplied by different electricity mix grids). [SOURCE: Page_12]

215    3.2    Life cycle inventory and data source (LCI) [SOURCE: Page_12]

216   A life cycle inventory (LCI) was assembled for each scenario, according to a three-step structure [SOURCE: Page_12]

217   (following WSmix structure by Leão et al., 2018): 1. Extraction, transportation and storage, 2. Water [SOURCE: Page_12]

218   treatment and 3. Distribution to user (Appendix A, Table A.2). LCI data were derived from Ecoinvent [SOURCE: Page_12]

219   v^3.5 database (Wernet et al. 2016) using world-average processes for most inputs (except electricity [SOURCE: Page_12]

220   and water flows) and modelled in the SimaPro 9.0 LCA software. [SOURCE: Page_12]

221          3.2.1 WSmix (Water Supply mix) [SOURCE: Page_12]

222   The world average WSmix considered in the baseline scenarios is based on an annual average WOmix [SOURCE: Page_12]

223   (water origin mix) for irrigation in thirty seven developed countries (weighted with country [SOURCE: Page_12]

224   population) using local conventional water sources (surface and groundwater) and no water- [SOURCE: Page_13]

225   treatment (weighted average model and data from Leão et al., 2019). A non-renewable WSmix based [SOURCE: Page_13]

226   exclusively on over-exploited groundwater (i.e. groundwater from a fossil stock or extracted from a [SOURCE: Page_13]

227   water table with a withdrawal rate greater than the annual renewal rate) is also considered. Finally, [SOURCE: Page_13]

228   the third WSmix studied in the baseline scenarios is an unconventional WSmix entirely composed of [SOURCE: Page_13]

229   desalinated seawater (desalination process from the Ecoinvent v^3.5 database, modified to take into [SOURCE: Page_13]

230   account the specific energy mix considered in the experimental design). [SOURCE: Page_13]

258    3.3   LCIA methods [SOURCE: Page_14]

259   Endpoint indicators were used so comparison could be possible between the impacts related to [SOURCE: Page_14]

260   water deprivation and those related to energy consumption on the same basis. Indeed, when dealing [SOURCE: Page_14]

261   with water-energy nexus, end-point impact indicators provide more useful information for decision- [SOURCE: Page_14]

262   making purposes than mid-point ones (as shown in Risch et al., 2014). Calculations are made [SOURCE: Page_14]

263   according to the ReCiPe 2016 method (Huijbregts et al., 2016) as it includes recent water deprivation [SOURCE: Page_14]

264   indicators for two of the three protection areas (impacts on human health and terrestrial vegetation [SOURCE: Page_14]

265   based on Pfister et al., 2009 and impacts on aquatic ecosystems from Hanafiah et al., 2011). In this [SOURCE: Page_14]

266   method, damage of water consumption to human health results from water shortages for irrigation, [SOURCE: Page_14]

267   which potentially leads to malnutrition in countries with a human development index (HDI) equal to [SOURCE: Page_14]

268   or less than 0.88. This is why the present experimental protocol distinguishes two locations where [SOURCE: Page_14]

269   water is scarce and where the HDI is high or low. In this study, it is particularly fundamental that [SOURCE: Page_14]

270   reclaimed water can be compared with conventional water from the extraction of non-renewable [SOURCE: Page_14]

271   groundwater. This led the authors to add an end-point impact to ReCiPe 2016 which would [SOURCE: Page_14]

272   contribute to area of protection “Resource depletion” based on Pfister et al., 2011. In this method, [SOURCE: Page_14]

273   the impact of the use of 1 m^3 of non-renewable groundwater on endpoint resources is equivalent to [SOURCE: Page_15]

274   the cost of extracting and processing seawater as an alternative resource (desalination, set at 1 [SOURCE: Page_15]

275   $/m^3). [SOURCE: Page_15]

276    3.4     Uncertainty management [SOURCE: Page_15]

277   Life cycle inventory (LCI) data can entail three main types of uncertainties: (i) quantitative [SOURCE: Page_15]

278   uncertainties related to the amount of technical flows (e.g. measured amount of energy consumed [SOURCE: Page_15]

279   daily or mass of materials used in an infrastructure), (ii) quantitative uncertainties related to the [SOURCE: Page_15]

280   amount of environmental flows (e.g. uncertainty linked to the amount of pollutant emitted in water [SOURCE: Page_15]

281   or air using an emission model) and (iii) qualitative uncertainties (data pedigree in Simapro software) [SOURCE: Page_15]

282   related to the choice of datasets in existing databases (e.g. is the steel inventory chosen within [SOURCE: Page_15]

283   Ecoinvent database to model an infrastructure representative of the steel actually used for this [SOURCE: Page_15]

284   infrastructure in this region of the world?). In order to provide consistent results to support decision- [SOURCE: Page_15]

285   making, it is essential to assess the uncertainty margins in the results, especially when comparing [SOURCE: Page_15]

286   data (in the present case: impacts of WSmix versus WW-reuse). [SOURCE: Page_15]

287   Consequently, an uncertainty analysis (Monte Carlo simulation available in SimaPro 9.0) has been [SOURCE: Page_15]

288   carried out. The Monte-Carlo method consists of repeating LCIA calculations, and generating for each [SOURCE: Page_15]

289   run a randomised value for every input of the inventory. For comparative LCAs (scenarios A versus B), [SOURCE: Page_15]

290   Monte-Carlo analysis calculates, for each impact category, the probability for the impacts of Scenario [SOURCE: Page_15]

291   A to be greater than those of Scenario B. Somehow, the Monte-Carlo method allows for a [SOURCE: Page_15]

292   combination between an uncertainty assessment and a sensitivity analysis of the results (variations [SOURCE: Page_15]

293   of inventory parameters in compliance with their statistical distribution laws). [SOURCE: Page_15]

294   This requires that the majority of the inventory data ought to be stochastic, i.e. LCI foreground and [SOURCE: Page_15]

295   background data sets should include distribution laws as well as the associated parameters for each [SOURCE: Page_15]

296   of their components. The uncertainty of 52% of the unit processes used for background activities [SOURCE: Page_15]

297   from Ecoinvent database (Ecoinvent, 2018) for this study were quantified by the “data pedigree” [SOURCE: Page_15]

298   (DQI) available in SimaPro as proposed by Ciroth et al., 2016. This algorithm relates the data [SOURCE: Page_16]

299   uncertainty to its source characteristics based on 6 indicators: reliability of the source, [SOURCE: Page_16]

300   representativeness of the sample, temporal correlation and geographical correlation, further [SOURCE: Page_16]

301   technological correlation and in some cases sample size. The uncertainty in the primary foreground [SOURCE: Page_16]

302   input data used for the Baseline and WW-reuse scenarios is mainly quantitative. It is described in [SOURCE: Page_16]

303   Table 1 and detailed DQI selection criteria are available in Table A.6. The Monte-Carlo calculations [SOURCE: Page_16]

304   were halted after a fixed number of runs (set at 1000, as recommended by PRé, 2016), producing [SOURCE: Page_16]

305   cumulative LCA results with a 95% confidence interval. [SOURCE: Page_16]

306   The issue of water consumption uncertainty for background activities in Ecoinvent: [SOURCE: Page_16]

307   In Ecoinvent processes, water consumption data are provided by both input water withdrawal flows [SOURCE: Page_16]

308   (input from nature) and output water discharge flows (emission to water). Water consumption [SOURCE: Page_16]

309   impacts (water consumption, water scarcity, etc.) depend on the difference between withdrawal [SOURCE: Page_16]

310   flows (CF>0) and discharge flows (CF<0). The uncertainty distribution is therefore independently [SOURCE: Page_16]

311   filled out for water withdrawals and water discharges. Thus, in a Monte-Carlo uncertainty analysis, [SOURCE: Page_16]

312   the water consumption balance (withdrawals - discharges) can be disturbed by random values and [SOURCE: Page_16]

313   cause both negative impact values (water deprivation credit) and a wide range of uncertainties. In [SOURCE: Page_16]

314   certain processes characterised by high water withdrawals and discharge flows (such as hydropower [SOURCE: Page_16]

315   production), the uncertainty generated for water consumption impact indicators can reach very high [SOURCE: Page_16]

316   values and mask all other uncertainties. As this masking effect concerns all background processes [SOURCE: Page_16]

317   (especially the electricity mixes, used in almost all processes), it cannot be manually corrected. This [SOURCE: Page_16]

318   issue has previously been identified by the LCA community and has not yet been solved in Ecoinvent. [SOURCE: Page_16]

319   In this context, uncertainty analyses are performed after elimination of the problematic impact [SOURCE: Page_16]

320   categories (i.e. no Monte-Carlo procedure for water consumption impact categories) and exclusively [SOURCE: Page_16]

321   for comparisons where the foreground water balance is identical between scenarios (This is possible [SOURCE: Page_16]

322   since background water consumption has a negligible impact – less than 2% of total impacts). For all [SOURCE: Page_16]

323   comparisons where this procedure was possible, stochastic calculations confirmed deterministic ones [SOURCE: Page_17]

324   (i.e. for each comparison the same scenario has the lowest impacts for each end-point indicator). [SOURCE: Page_17]

325    3.5    Decision rules for scenario comparison [SOURCE: Page_17]

326   Synthesis of the results should lead to the evaluation of the overall eco-efficiency of the investigated [SOURCE: Page_17]

327   scenarios and to the identification of circumstances in which WW-reuse is better or worse than the [SOURCE: Page_17]

328   local water supply mix. In this view, end-point results have been compared and a scenario A can only [SOURCE: Page_17]

329   be considered better than a scenario B if all 3 end-point impact categories are greater or equal for A [SOURCE: Page_17]

330   (i.e. the 3 impacts of scenario A lower than those of B by at least 10%, calculated with Monte-Carlo [SOURCE: Page_17]

331   uncertainty analysis as described in paragraph 2.4). It is thus noteworthy that no weighting or [SOURCE: Page_17]

332   preference (explicit or implicit) between the 3 end-point categories had been implemented according [SOURCE: Page_17]

333   to the LCA decision-support procedure proposed in Guérin-Schneider et al., 2018, where impact [SOURCE: Page_17]

334   categories characterised by an insignificant difference (<10%) are considered to be equal. [SOURCE: Page_17]

377    4.2   Water scarcity effect [SOURCE: Page_20]

378   In continental areas, regardless of the type of groundwater management, the level of water scarcity [SOURCE: Page_20]

379   has an equal effect on all three scenarios, since they all have an equal water balance. Only in the case [SOURCE: Page_20]

380   of a non-renewable groundwater resource does the water balance differ for the assessment of [SOURCE: Page_20]

381   endpoint resource impacts. Indeed, in these latter cases, the discharge of water to the surface of [SOURCE: Page_20]

382   water bodies does not recharge non-renewable groundwater reservoirs (considered as fossil or with [SOURCE: Page_20]

383   a recharge time greater than consumption time). Water discharges therefore do not present any [SOURCE: Page_20]

384   benefit to groundwater resources. Furthermore, in these situations the WW-reuse scenarios do not [SOURCE: Page_20]

385   have any water consumption impact on resources (avoided discharges do not affect the non- [SOURCE: Page_20]

386   renewable underground resource). In all continental climate areas, when the WOmix represents an [SOURCE: Page_20]

387   overexploitation of groundwater, the RT$_1$ WW-reuse scenario is more environmentally efficient than [SOURCE: Page_20]

388   the baseline scenario. [SOURCE: Page_20]

389   Coastal groundwater renewable use [SOURCE: Page_20]

390   In littoral cases, water scarcity increases the water-consumption impacts of the baseline scenario. [SOURCE: Page_20]

391   When the HDI is low (below 0.88), water consumption also generates impacts on human health [SOURCE: Page_20]

392   (related to malnutrition resulting from water deprivation for the benefit of irrigation). This is once [SOURCE: Page_20]

393   more unfavourable for the Baseline scenario in coastal areas. However, the global average WSmix [SOURCE: Page_20]

394   still shows a lower impact on the resource end-point indicator. [SOURCE: Page_20]

395   Coastal groundwater overuse [SOURCE: Page_20]

396   Water depletion induced by excessive exploitation of groundwater is expressed in the endpoint [SOURCE: Page_20]

397   indicator “Resources” as a surplus cost of desalinated water (Pfister et al., 2011). Thus, in arid coastal [SOURCE: Page_20]

398   areas, and when the use of groundwater is non-renewable, the intensive WW-reuse scenario (RT$_2$) [SOURCE: Page_20]

399   treatment is more eco-efficient than the baseline scenario for two of the three endpoint indicators: [SOURCE: Page_21]

400   ecosystems and resources. [SOURCE: Page_21]

401    4.3   Effect of water origin [SOURCE: Page_21]

402   Since the energetic expense of the desalination treatment is even greater than that of the RT$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_21]

403   treatment, its environmental impacts are also highest. Therefore, the WW-reuse RT$_2$ solution [SOURCE: Page_21]

404   becomes more eco-efficient than desalination in the three protection areas. In this case, the water- [SOURCE: Page_21]

405   distribution distance is another key parameter in order to differentiate an intensive regeneration [SOURCE: Page_21]

406   treatment reuse solution from an unconventional WSmix based on desalination. For instance, when [SOURCE: Page_21]

407   the WWTP is closer to the irrigated plot than the coast, the reuse RT$_2$ scenario would be even more [SOURCE: Page_21]

408   legitimate. When compared to a desalinated water supply, reuse RT$_1$ is at least 67 % more efficient [SOURCE: Page_21]

409   for the three environmental areas of protection. [SOURCE: Page_21]

410    4.4   Electricity mix effect [SOURCE: Page_21]

411   The composition of the energy mix influences the most energy-intensive contributors, such as the [SOURCE: Page_21]

412   membrane processes of the RT$_2$ reuse scenario. A more renewable energy mix would tend to favour [SOURCE: Page_21]

413   reuse scenarios in coastal situations, because the extra cost of treatment can be offset by the [SOURCE: Page_21]

414   achieved water savings (case of scenario RT$_1$ on all impacts if a country with low HDI and a non- [SOURCE: Page_21]

415   renewable use of resources is considered). Energy mix effects in urban WW-reuse environmental [SOURCE: Page_21]

416   efficiency evaluation are limited because they affect freshwater treatment as well as regeneration [SOURCE: Page_21]

417   water treatment. [SOURCE: Page_21]

470   6     Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_24]

471   A literature review pointed out that most WW-reuse LCAs were based on very specific case studies, [SOURCE: Page_24]

472   in which the conclusions were directly related to local conditions. The present study first clarified [SOURCE: Page_24]

473   how to calculate the water consumed and/or saved on the basis of a mass balance between [SOURCE: Page_24]

474   withdrawals and discharges, thus demonstrating that WW-reuse does not save water in all cases. [SOURCE: Page_24]

475   Then, the study led to the definition of a robust and homogeneous framework to assess the [SOURCE: Page_24]

476   environmental efficiency of WW-reuse as an alternative to a baseline water supply scenario. This [SOURCE: Page_24]

477   framework has been applied to a representative WW-reuse application case (urban WW-reuse for [SOURCE: Page_24]

478   agriculture) in contrasted but generic locations (in terms of water scarcity, continental or littoral [SOURCE: Page_24]

479   location, water origin for irrigation supply and nature of electricity mix). Thus, situations were [SOURCE: Page_24]

480   identified where wastewater reuse is clearly an environmentally good water supply solution (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_24]

481   massive use on non-renewable groundwater, arid region, littoral location i.e. when WWTP effluent is [SOURCE: Page_24]

482   discharged into the sea, tertiary treatment not too energy-intensive). On the contrary, this study also [SOURCE: Page_24]

483   highlighted situations where reclaimed water supply for irrigation was less eco-efficient than the [SOURCE: Page_24]

484   local WSmix (low water scarcity, inland location, advanced tertiary treatments for reclaimed water). [SOURCE: Page_24]

485   Between these two types of situations, there are numerous cases where the situation cannot be [SOURCE: Page_24]

486   easily distinguished and for which specific studies, accounting for each local situation, would be [SOURCE: Page_24]

487   necessary for a significant comparison. [SOURCE: Page_24]

488   Finally, the proposed methodological framework and its illustrative application are addressed to LCA [SOURCE: Page_24]

489   practitioners for future WW-reuse case studies. Furthermore, the studied experimental protocol and [SOURCE: Page_24]

490   the generic conclusions associated with the WW-reuse case for agriculture provide insights for [SOURCE: Page_24]

491   decision making and water supply system designers. [SOURCE: Page_24]

492   Research perspectives would be to extend this study to other cases of WW-reuse that are less [SOURCE: Page_25]

493   common than the one studied (WW-reuse with tertiary treatment for irrigation) and to integrate the [SOURCE: Page_25]

494   fate of pathogens into LCA as soon as scientific knowledge becomes more advanced in this field. [SOURCE: Page_25]

495   Finally, it would be necessary to study in more detail the treatment plants of the future where the [SOURCE: Page_25]

496   objective would no longer be to process a given waste (wastewater) but rather to recover its energy [SOURCE: Page_25]

497   and nutrient content. [SOURCE: Page_25]
