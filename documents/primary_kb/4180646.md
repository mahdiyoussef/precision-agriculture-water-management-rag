15   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

16   Current mechanistic anaerobic digestion (AD) models cannot accurately represent the [SOURCE: Page_3]

17   underlying processes occurring during food waste (FW) AD. This work presents an update of [SOURCE: Page_3]

18   the Anaerobic Digestion Model no. 1 (ADM$_1$) to provide accurate estimations of free ammonia [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   concentrations and related inhibition thresholds, and model syntrophic acetate oxidation as [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   acetate-consuming pathway. A modified Davies equation predicted NH$_3$ concentrations and pH [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   more accurately, and better estimated associated inhibitory limits. Sensitivity analysis results [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   showed the importance of accurate disintegration kinetics and volumetric mass transfer [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   coefficients, as well as volatile fatty acids (VFAs) and hydrogen uptake rates. In contrast to the [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   default ADM$_1$, the modified ADM$_1$ could represent methane production and VFA profiles [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   simultaneously (particularly relevant for propionate uptake). The modified ADM1 was also 1 [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   able to predict the predominant acetate-consuming and methane-producing microbial clades. [SOURCE: Page_4]

27   Modelling results using data from reactors dosed with granular activated carbon showed that [SOURCE: Page_4]

28   this additive improves hydrogen uptake. [SOURCE: Page_4]

33   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

34   Anaerobic digestion (AD) is a key technology for the sustainable management of several [SOURCE: Page_4]

35   organic waste streams, including sewage sludge, food waste (FW), animal manure, agri- [SOURCE: Page_4]

36   industrial waste, and industrial wastewater (Appels et al., 2008). AD is a multistage biochemical [SOURCE: Page_4]

37   process that offers a triple role: (i) waste stabilization, (ii) production of renewable energy in [SOURCE: Page_4]

38   the form of biogas, and (iii) nutrient recovery by digestate application. These benefits, together [SOURCE: Page_4]

39   with new regulations penalizing cheaper alternatives (i.e. landfilling and incineration; European [SOURCE: Page_4]

40   Directive 2018/850) and imposing circular economy action plans (European Commission [SOURCE: Page_4]

41   Communication COM(2020)98), ensure a bright future for this biotechnology. [SOURCE: Page_4]

42   A clear example of the success of AD is the rapidly expanding treatment of concentrated wastes, [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   such as FW or animal manure (Banks et al., 2008). The case of FW is particularly relevant, as [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   its production is rapidly increasing due to population/economic growth, and policies imposing [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   separate source selection and FW valorisation are being implemented (European Directive [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   2008/98/CE). These factors call for developing sustainable processes that can provide efficient [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   FW valorisation, with AD standing among the most suitable options (Capson-Tojo et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   AD mathematical modelling is well-stablished, and has largely been used for design purposes, [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   operational analysis, technology development and process control (Regmi et al., 2019). The [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   IWA Anaerobic Digestion Model no. 1 (ADM1), the most used AD model, is a mechanistic 2 [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   model based on the underlying biological and physicochemical processes (Batstone et al., 2002; [SOURCE: Page_5]

52   Weinrich and Nelles, 2021). The ADM$_1$ was primarily developed to model sewage sludge AD [SOURCE: Page_5]

53   in wastewater treatment plants. These digesters are characterised by relatively diluted solid [SOURCE: Page_5]

54   concentrations (20-70 g TS·L-1; TS being total solids) and by relatively low risks of process [SOURCE: Page_5]

55   inhibition and acidification (Appels et al., 2008; Astals et al., 2013). Accordingly, the default [SOURCE: Page_5]

56   ADM$_1$ is not able to accurately predict the performance of digesters treating concentrated [SOURCE: Page_5]

57   organic streams or leading to high concentrations of inhibitors, such as ammonia. To overcome [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   these limitations, several ADM$_1$ modifications have been carried out in the last years. Relevant [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   examples are the recent modifications of the ADM$_1$ to consider variable mass/volume contents [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   during high-solids AD (Pastor-Poquet et al., 2018), to account for non-ideal aqueous-phase [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   chemistry (Patón et al., 2018; Solon et al., 2015), to include the syntrophic acetate oxidation [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   (SAO) pathway (Montecchio et al., 2017; Rivera-Salvador et al., 2014), or to consider trace [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   element (TE) complexation and precipitation (Flores-Alsina et al., 2016; Frunzo et al., 2019; [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   Maharaj et al., 2019). As more research is carried out in AD, the knowledge on the underlying [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   mechanisms governing the process increases, allowing to improve and modify models to [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   accurately predict a broader spectrum of substrates, configurations, and operational conditions. [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   A challenge in FW AD is free ammonia nitrogen (FAN) inhibition, caused by its high [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   biodegradable protein concentrations and the low free water availability. High FAN [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   concentrations cause inhibition of acetoclastic methanogenesis (AM; the predominant methane- [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   producing pathway in digesters fed with sewage sludge). At FAN concentrations over 200-400 [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   mg FAN·L-1, hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis (HM) becomes predominant (Banks et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   2012), coupled with SAO (Jiang et al., 2017). This two-step methane production process relies [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   on acetate oxidation to CO$_2$ and H$_2$ by SAO bacteria, followed by their conversion into methane [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   by hydrogenotrophic archaea. This process is only thermodynamically favourable at low H$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   partial pressures (10-80 Pa), and constant H2 removal by hydrogenotrophic archaea is crucial 3 [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   for making SAO energetically feasible (Rivera-Salvador et al., 2014). SAO has been already [SOURCE: Page_6]

77   included into the ADM$_1$, improving the model accuracy in digesters treating poultry litter and [SOURCE: Page_6]

78   pretreated waste sludge (Montecchio et al., 2017; Rivera-Salvador et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_6]

79   AD processes dominated by SAO and HM are known to be prone to propionic acid [SOURCE: Page_6]

80   accumulation, a major inhibitor in AD reactors (Banks et al., 2012). Syntrophic propionate [SOURCE: Page_6]

81   oxidation (SPO) also requires low H$_2$ partial pressures to be thermodynamically favourable, [SOURCE: Page_6]

82   due to product-induced inhibition at high H$_2$ levels. For the same reason, SPO also depends on [SOURCE: Page_6]

83   the concentrations of acetic acid (Batstone et al., 2002; Capson-Tojo et al., 2017). Therefore, [SOURCE: Page_6]

84   AD instabilities leading to increases in the H$_2$ partial pressures can easily result in accumulation [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   of acetic acid, which will further favour the accumulation of propionic acid. Because of its [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   relevance, SPO is generally considered in mechanistic AD models (Batstone et al., 2002). [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   The high ionic strength in FW digesters causes another issue when considering traditional AD [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   models, since the ion-pairing behaviour cannot be simplified to that of an ideal solution. Studies [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   focusing on modelling ion speciation in concentrated AD systems have proved that assuming [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   an ideal equilibrium can lead to overestimate FAN concentrations by up to 30% (Capson-Tojo [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   et al., 2020; Hafner and Bisogni, 2009; Patón et al., 2018; Solon et al., 2015). Activity [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   corrections have been applied to account for the effect of ionic strength on ion speciation, [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   generally using the Davies equation for FAN quantification (Capson-Tojo et al., 2020; Patón et [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   al., 2018; Solon et al., 2015). Despite its importance, this practice has been frequently omitted [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   in the literature, even in publications devoted to FAN inhibition (Capson-Tojo et al., 2020; [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   Rajagopal et al., 2013). FW digesters have the inherent risk of FAN inhibition and therefore a [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   precise quantification of FAN is crucial to obtain coherent inhibitory limits that can be used to [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   better predict process performance and inhibitory events (De Vrieze et al., 2015). The ADM$_1$ [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   does not include the SAO pathway nor the effect of the ionic strength on ion speciation. This [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   limits its applicability for FW AD, particularly in dry systems. These limitations are particularly 4 [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   relevant as full-scale dry digesters (treating undiluted substrates with TS contents over 15%) [SOURCE: Page_7]

102   are becoming more common worldwide (Karthikeyan and Visvanathan, 2013; Motte et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

103   2013). [SOURCE: Page_7]

104   Recent modelling efforts on FW AD modelling have improved the ADM$_1$ performance [SOURCE: Page_7]

105   (Montecchio et al., 2019; Poggio et al., 2016; Rathnasiri, 2016). However, to the best of our [SOURCE: Page_7]

106   knowledge, no previous publication on FW AD has assessed the impact of including SAO and [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   media ionic strength on the ADM$_1$ performance. Zhao et al. (2019) modified the ADM$_1$ to [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   account for FW composition, and calibrated relevant parameters after a sensitivity analysis. [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   They concluded that hydrolysis, disintegration, and acetate uptake were the most influential [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   processes on methane production. Zhao et al. (2019) did not assess the importance of FAN [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   inhibition. Poggio et al. (2016) proposed a substrate characterisation methodology based on [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   substrate fractionation to enhance the ADM$_1$ performance. Hydrolysis was also identified as a [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   relevant kinetic process, and two particulate fractions were needed to accurately model FW AD [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   (i.e. a readily and a slowly particulate biodegradable fraction). Poggio et al. (2016) concluded [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   that their approach led to good predictions for methane yields and solid destruction, being less [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   accurate for the prediction of methane flow rates, pH and VFA profiles. Rathnasiri (2016) [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   applied the ADM$_1$ after FW dilution with water, and Montecchio et al. (2019) for FW co- [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   digestion with sewage sludge, a co-substrate with lower N concentration and higher water [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   content. Both approaches reduced the impact of TAN concentration on the digester [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   performance, which eased fitting the experimental results with the default ADM$_1$. Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   Montecchio et al. (2019) stated that the ADM$_1$ was only adequate for AD at high bacterial and [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   methanogenic activities (achieved when co-digesting FW and sludge). [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   The main goal of this study was to design a modified ADM$_1$ able to accurately simulate FW [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   AD. The ADM$_1$ was modified to consider: (i) SAO as acetate-consuming pathway, (ii) FAN [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   estimation using the Davies equation (to account for non-ideal behaviour), and (iii) 5 [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   methanogenic inhibition due to FAN using a threshold inhibition function (Astals et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_8]

127   The modified and default ADM1s were compared, considering their ability to predict both the [SOURCE: Page_8]

128   AD performances and the predominant microbial communities. The influence of AD additives [SOURCE: Page_8]

129   (e.g. granular activated carbon (GAC)) on the resulting model parameters was also assessed. [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   2. Materials and methods [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   2.1. Inoculum source and substrate characteristics [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   The inoculum was collected from a territorial-industrial plant in the South of France treating a [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   mixture of different organic streams at high total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) concentrations (7.3 [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   ± 0.5 g N·L-1). FW was used as representative concentrated substrate. The FW was collected [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   from different producers from the region of the Grand Narbonne (France). A proportional [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   mixture (wet weight) of the different FWs was used as substrate. The characteristics of the FW [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   and the inoculum, shown in Table 1, correspond to the average from two different FW sampling [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   campaigns and to triplicate measurements for the inoculum. [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   2.2. Batch anaerobic digestion [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   Results from different sets of sequential batch digesters (with a working volume of 430 ± 2 mL) [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   treating FW were used as input data to calibrate and validate the default and the modified [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   ADM1s (Capson-Tojo et al., 2018a). Data from the 2nd feeding of the sequential batch reactors [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   was used to ensure proper inoculum adaptation and reactor operation. The digesters (in [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   triplicate) were started with 60 g of FW as substrate (raw) at a substrate to inoculum ratio of 1 [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   g VS·g VS-1 (with resulting FW concentrations of around 30 g VS FW·L-1; VS being volatile [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   solids). The reactors were incubated at 37 ± 0.2 °C. The incubation system was an Automated [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   Methane Potential Testing System (AMPTSII) (Bioprocess Control, Sweden) consisting of 15 [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   parallel reactors with a total volume of 500 mL (of which 12 were used). To determine the [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   methane flow rate, the headspace of each rector was connected to a carbon dioxide trap (NaOH 6 [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   5% solution) and then to a gas flow meter. The reactors were automatically stirred for 1 minute [SOURCE: Page_9]

152   every 10 minutes at 40 rpm. Before starting the incubation, the headspace was flushed with [SOURCE: Page_9]

153   pure N$_2$ to ensure anaerobic conditions. To account for endogenous respiration, a blank reactor [SOURCE: Page_9]

154   containing only inoculum was also run (in triplicate). The methane production from the blank [SOURCE: Page_9]

155   was subtracted from the biogas produced by the reactors fed with FW. The batches were stopped [SOURCE: Page_9]

156   after 34 days, once the biogas production stopped in all reactors, and the total volatile fatty acids [SOURCE: Page_9]

157   (VFAs) concentration was assumed to be negligible. [SOURCE: Page_9]

158   To assess the applicability of the proposed model modifications, data from reactors working [SOURCE: Page_9]

159   under different conditions were used: (i) control conditions (solely fed with FW), and (ii) [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   supplemented with GAC (dosed at 10 g·L-1). A detailed explanation of the experimental design [SOURCE: Page_9]

161   and the sampling procedure can be found in Capson-Tojo et al. (2018a). [SOURCE: Page_9]

162   2.3. Analytical methods [SOURCE: Page_9]

163   2.3.1. Physicochemical characterization of the FW [SOURCE: Page_9]

164   TS and VS contents were measured according to the standard methods of the American Public [SOURCE: Page_9]

166   method (Dubois et al., 1956), and lipid contents via accelerated solvent extraction using an [SOURCE: Page_9]

167   ASE®200, DIONEX (California, United States of America) coupled to a MULTIVAPOR P-12, [SOURCE: Page_9]

168   BUCHI (Aquon, Netherlands) with heptane as solvent (100 bar, 105 °C, 5 cycles of 10 min [SOURCE: Page_9]

169   static and 100 s purge) (APHA, 2017). Total Kjeldahl Nitrogen (TKN) contents were [SOURCE: Page_9]

170   determined with an AutoKjehdahl Unit K-370, BUCHI, and the protein contents were estimated [SOURCE: Page_9]

171   from TKN values using a factor of 6.25 g protein·g organic N-1 (Galí et al., 2009). The pH was [SOURCE: Page_9]

172   measured using a WTW (London, United Kingdom) pHmeter series inoLab pH720. The FW [SOURCE: Page_9]

173   biochemical methane potential (BMP) was determined according to Motte et al. (2014), [SOURCE: Page_9]

174   following Angelidaki et al. (2009). The chemical oxygen demand (COD) content of the FW [SOURCE: Page_9]

175   was estimated from the contents in carbohydrates (1.19 g COD·g-1), proteins (1.42 g COD·g-1) 7 [SOURCE: Page_9]

176   and lipids (2.90 g COD·g-1), assuming a 10% of inert COD (based on Batstone et al., (2002), [SOURCE: Page_10]

177   and from the FW biodegradability estimated from the BMPs, of 92%). [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   2.5. Model calibration and evaluation [SOURCE: Page_12]

231   To compare the models (i.e. default vs. modified ADM1s) and to evaluate the effects of GAC [SOURCE: Page_12]

232   on the AD performance (see Section 2.2), a systematic approach was followed. First, a global [SOURCE: Page_12]

233   sensitivity analysis (GSA) was carried out for each model to identify influential parameters on [SOURCE: Page_12]

234   the model outputs. Afterwards, these parameters were dynamically calibrated to improve the [SOURCE: Page_12]

235   prediction capabilities of the models and to compare between the different experimental [SOURCE: Page_12]

236   conditions (i.e. control vs. GAC-dosed reactors). The required stoichiometric parameters, [SOURCE: Page_12]

237   biomass compositions, biomass yields, and physicochemical parameters were all obtained from [SOURCE: Page_12]

238   the literature, as well as the initial values of the kinetic parameters (Batstone et al., 2002; [SOURCE: Page_12]

239   Capson-Tojo et al., 2020; Rivera-Salvador et al., 2014; Rosen and Jeppsson, 2006). We are [SOURCE: Page_12]

240   willing to share the code files corresponding to both the default and the modified ADM1s [SOURCE: Page_12]

241   (implemented in MATLAB® (MATLAB R2021a, The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA)). [SOURCE: Page_12]

242   2.5.1. Sensitivity analysis [SOURCE: Page_12]

243   The GSA methodology implemented was similar to the one described in Robles et al. (2014), [SOURCE: Page_12]

244   based on the Morris screening Method (Morris, 1991). This approach consists in a one-factor- [SOURCE: Page_12]

245   at-a-time method of GSA, which evaluates the distribution of the scaled elementary effects of [SOURCE: Page_12]

246   each input factor (model parameters) upon model outputs (methane production rates and VFAs [SOURCE: Page_12]

247   concentration), which is afterwards used to calculate the statistical parameters that provide 10 [SOURCE: Page_12]

248   sensitivity data. The variation for each input factor was set to ±20% of the default value, through [SOURCE: Page_13]

249   a resolution of 4 p levels. The number of evaluated trajectories was 100. The absolute mean [SOURCE: Page_13]

250   (μ∗) and the standard deviation (σ) of the scaled elementary effects of each distribution were [SOURCE: Page_13]

251   used as sensitivity measures (Campolongo et al., 2007). The graphical Morris approach was [SOURCE: Page_13]

252   used to systematically differentiate between input factors that could significantly influence the [SOURCE: Page_13]

253   model. The μ∗ and σ obtained for all the scaled elementary effects of each distribution were [SOURCE: Page_13]

254   plotted. Factors with high μ∗ and (relatively) small σ were considered to be influential, with [SOURCE: Page_13]

255   linear and additive effects on the outputs. Factors with small μ∗ but high σ were considered to [SOURCE: Page_13]

256   be influential, with non-linear or interactive effects on the outputs. Factors with low μ and σ [SOURCE: Page_13]

257   were considered as non-influential (Morris, 1991). [SOURCE: Page_13]

295   3.1.2. Model calibration and comparison of prediction capabilities: default vs. modified ADM$_1$ [SOURCE: Page_14]

296   The parameters deemed as relevant according to the GSA were calibrated using both models 12 [SOURCE: Page_14]

297   and the control reactor dataset (as in Section 3.1.1). The GSA allowed to reduce the number of [SOURCE: Page_15]

298   parameters to calibrate from an initial set of 31 in the default ADM$_1$ and 41 in the modified [SOURCE: Page_15]

299   ADM$_1$, to 13 and 16, respectively. The calibration results are shown in Table 2. [SOURCE: Page_15]

300   The parameters deemed as relevant for methane production and/or VFA uptake were selected [SOURCE: Page_15]

301   for calibration. Despite the influence of pHUL,ac (pH upper limit for acetotrophs) on the resulting [SOURCE: Page_15]

302   methane flow rates and acetate uptake rates (see Fig. 1), pHUL,ac and pHLL,ac (pH lower limit for [SOURCE: Page_15]

303   acetotrophs) were excluded from the calibration of the modified ADM$_1$. pH-related inhibition [SOURCE: Page_15]

304   parameters were deemed as relevant by the GSA because, mathematically, AM can be inhibited [SOURCE: Page_15]

305   by modifying the pH inhibition limits. Nevertheless, the resulting calibrated values leading to [SOURCE: Page_15]

306   AM inhibition by pH were 8.0-8.5 for pHLL,ac and over 9.0 for pHUL,ac, which biologically do [SOURCE: Page_15]

307   not make sense (Batstone et al., 2002). Therefore, including these parameters in the calibration [SOURCE: Page_15]

308   procedure resulted in illogical inhibition limits, affecting the values of other parameters and [SOURCE: Page_15]

309   leading to inaccurate results. Furthermore, using the default ADM$_1$, the calibrated values for [SOURCE: Page_15]

310   pHUL,ac were 6.4-7.1, which agree with values reported in the literature (Batstone et al., 2002). [SOURCE: Page_15]

311   The calibration results showed that FW has a relatively fast disintegration kinetics (>0.6 d-1) [SOURCE: Page_15]

312   compared to other solid substrates, with values of kdis higher than those reported in the literature [SOURCE: Page_15]

313   (e.g. 0.24 d-1 for cattle manure or 0.10 d-1 for pig manure (Batstone et al., 2002)). This result is [SOURCE: Page_15]

314   in agreement with the well-known faster disintegration and hydrolysis of FW (Koch et al., [SOURCE: Page_15]

315   2015). The much lower values of kLa when compared to the literature are related to the lack of [SOURCE: Page_15]

316   water and the inherent difficult mixing at high solid contents. [SOURCE: Page_15]

317   To understand the resulting values of the kinetic parameters for each model, their prediction [SOURCE: Page_15]

318   performances and the predicted dominant pathways must be analysed in detail. The modelling [SOURCE: Page_15]

319   results are presented in Fig. 2 (methane flow rates and VFA profiles) and Fig. 3 (biomass [SOURCE: Page_15]

320   concentrations, and pH and TAN/FAN concentrations). As shown in Fig. 2, while both models [SOURCE: Page_15]

321   represented accurately the acetate and total VFA profiles (parity plots with R2 of 0.98-0.99), 13 [SOURCE: Page_15]

322   the calibrated default ADM$_1$ was not able to represent the methane production rate (R$_2$ of 0.61) [SOURCE: Page_16]

323   nor the final consumption of propionate (see Fig. 2). In contrast, the modified ADM$_1$ provided [SOURCE: Page_16]

324   more accurate predictions in both cases (R$_2$ of 0.94 and 0.99, respectively). [SOURCE: Page_16]

325   The improved predicting capabilities of the modified ADM$_1$ are related to the underlying [SOURCE: Page_16]

326   processes governing AD in each model. In the default ADM$_1$, AM is the only acetate- [SOURCE: Page_16]

327   consuming pathway available. Therefore, to fit the experimental methane production rates and [SOURCE: Page_16]

328   the total VFA and acetate profiles, the FAN inhibitory concentrations for acetoclastic archaea [SOURCE: Page_16]

329   (KI,NH$_3$) need to be far above commonly applied inhibitory concentrations (e.g. KI,NH$_3$ values of [SOURCE: Page_16]

330   at 0.0030 vs. 0.0018 M (Batstone et al., 2002)). In contrast, the calibration results with the [SOURCE: Page_16]

331   modified ADM$_1$ showed a realistic FAN inhibitory limit for acetoclastic archaea (i.e. a [SOURCE: Page_16]

332   KI,NH$_3$,max,acet of 0.011 M, as in Capson-Tojo et al. (2020)). This value led to inhibition of AM, [SOURCE: Page_16]

333   which can be confirmed when looking at the predicted concentrations of methanogenic archaea [SOURCE: Page_16]

334   (Fig. 3). Therefore, SAO was the main acetate-consuming pathway, and HM the main methane- [SOURCE: Page_16]

335   producing one, which is in agreement with the experimental results (where the presence of [SOURCE: Page_16]

336   acetoclastic archaea at the end of the batch tests was negligible, see Capson-Tojo et al. (2018a) [SOURCE: Page_16]

337   for a detailed discussion on the microbial communities in the reactors). The accurate [SOURCE: Page_16]

338   representation of the underlying microbial processes by the modified ADM$_1$ was facilitated by [SOURCE: Page_16]

339   including SAO as metabolic pathway, and by using realistic FAN inhibitory limits for [SOURCE: Page_16]

340   acetoclastic archaea. These allowed to account for the observed AM inhibition, resulting in the [SOURCE: Page_16]

341   dominance of SAO (and HM) despite their slower overall kinetics. The low concentrations of [SOURCE: Page_16]

342   syntrophic bacteria predicted by the modified ADM$_1$ are caused by their slow growth, generally [SOURCE: Page_16]

343   representing a minor part of the total microbial community in digesters (Hao et al., 2020). These [SOURCE: Page_16]

344   results agree with previous studies dealing with SAO during AD at high N concentrations. [SOURCE: Page_16]

345   Hydrogenotrophic methanogens were also dominant in thermophilic AD of poultry litter [SOURCE: Page_16]

346   (Rivera-Salvador et al., 2014), and Montecchio et al. (2017) did not detect any acetoclastic 14 [SOURCE: Page_16]

347   archaea in their reactors treating sludge at 0.3 g FAN·L-1. [SOURCE: Page_17]

348   The different predominant methanogenic pathways between both models explain the inability [SOURCE: Page_17]

349   of the default ADM$_1$ to predict the methane production rates. The high H$_2$ concentrations [SOURCE: Page_17]

350   occurring during FW AD can potentially make thermodynamically unfavourable the processes [SOURCE: Page_17]

351   in which H$_2$ was produced, such as SAO, propionate oxidation, butyrate oxidation, and valerate [SOURCE: Page_17]

352   oxidation (accounted for in both models by the 50% inhibitory concentrations of H$_2$, KI,h^2,j). [SOURCE: Page_17]

353   This can lead to the accumulation of VFAs often seen in full-scale FW digesters (Banks et al., [SOURCE: Page_17]

354   2012; Capson-Tojo et al., 2017). In the case of propionate, butyrate, and valerate, high acetate [SOURCE: Page_17]

355   concentrations might further inhibit their consumption (see Batstone et al. (2002) and Capson- [SOURCE: Page_17]

356   Tojo et al. (2017) for a deeper discussion on AD thermodynamics). As the modified ADM$_1$ [SOURCE: Page_17]

357   included SAO and HM, it was able to predict high H$_2$ concentrations and partial pressures in [SOURCE: Page_17]

358   the reactor, thus accurately predicting VFA accumulation. The default ADM$_1$ could not predict [SOURCE: Page_17]

359   an AD system dominated via SAO and HM, and thus could not predict the consequent high H$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_17]

360   concentrations and the resulting VFA accumulation. Therefore, to represent the VFA profiles, [SOURCE: Page_17]

361   the calibration procedure decreased the kLa value in the default ADM$_1$ to values allowing the [SOURCE: Page_17]

362   high H$_2$ concentrations required. The kLa estimated by the default ADM$_1$ was much lower than [SOURCE: Page_17]

363   the one obtained with the modified ADM$_1$ (0.087 and 0.390 d-1, respectively). The low kLa [SOURCE: Page_17]

364   value resulted in the accumulation of, not only H$_2$, but also CH$_4$, reason why the methane [SOURCE: Page_17]

365   production rates could not be predicted by the default ADM$_1$. [SOURCE: Page_17]

366   The phenomena described above can also explain the resulting km values. Regarding acetate, [SOURCE: Page_17]

367   the default ADM$_1$ needed a high km,ac value of 10.7 g COD·g COD-1·d-1, while the value in the [SOURCE: Page_17]

368   modified ADM$_1$ was 1.30 g COD·g COD-1·d-1, as SAO was the dominant acetate-consuming [SOURCE: Page_17]

369   pathway. AM was irrelevant in the modified model (see Fig. 3B), leading to biased values of [SOURCE: Page_17]

370   km,ac. Similarly, the less pronounced H$_2$-induced inhibition predicted by the default model (due [SOURCE: Page_17]

371   to lower H2 concentrations as HM was marginal) resulted in a very low value of km for 15 [SOURCE: Page_17]

372   propionate uptake (2.93 g COD·g COD-1·d-1, far from literature values) to reproduce the [SOURCE: Page_18]

373   propionate accumulation observed experimentally. In contrast, the km for propionate uptake in [SOURCE: Page_18]

374   the modified ADM$_1$ (19.2 g COD·g COD-1·d-1) was within ranges commonly reported in the [SOURCE: Page_18]

375   literature (Batstone et al., 2002). Regarding the H$_2$ uptake rates, the values in the modified [SOURCE: Page_18]

376   ADM$_1$ allowed a simultaneous, syntrophic growth of hydrogenotrophic archaea and SAO (Fig. [SOURCE: Page_18]

377   3B). The higher values of km in the default ADM$_1$ resulted in an initial fast H$_2$ consumption, [SOURCE: Page_18]

378   followed by the death of hydrogenotrophic archaea (Fig. 3A). This allowed to reduce the initial [SOURCE: Page_18]

379   H$_2$ concentrations to values where there was no VFA accumulation. Nevertheless, as in the [SOURCE: Page_18]

380   default ADM$_1$ SAO did not occur, less H$_2$ was predicted than in the modified ADM$_1$, which [SOURCE: Page_18]

381   jeopardised the simultaneous representation of methane production rates and VFA profiles. [SOURCE: Page_18]

382   Butyrate and valerate uptake were separated into two different processes in the modified [SOURCE: Page_18]

383   ADM$_1$, aiming at accurately representing their profiles. This strategy allowed setting different [SOURCE: Page_18]

384   uptake dynamics for each clade, as it was obvious from the experimental data that they had [SOURCE: Page_18]

385   different dynamics (more butyrate was initially generated than valerate, butyrate was produced [SOURCE: Page_18]

386   faster, and valerate consumption was slower). Despite these efforts, both models failed to [SOURCE: Page_18]

387   accurately predict the valerate profile (R$_2$ of 0.74-0.82). The most plausible explanation is that [SOURCE: Page_18]

388   other processes were taking place, affecting both butyrate and valerate concentrations. Several [SOURCE: Page_18]

389   biological reactions involve these compounds as substrate or products, and relevant processes [SOURCE: Page_18]

390   such as chain elongation are known to occur during fermentation or AD of FW (Capson-Tojo [SOURCE: Page_18]

391   et al., 2018b). Nonetheless, the low concentrations of valerate during FW AD (< 5% of the total [SOURCE: Page_18]

392   COD as products) justify the omission of other involved processes. If an accurate prediction of [SOURCE: Page_18]

393   valerate concentrations in the future is needed, further research should be carried out. [SOURCE: Page_18]

394   Another difference between the default and the modified ADM$_1$ is the method used for FAN [SOURCE: Page_18]

395   quantification and FAN-related inhibition. These differences affected the predicted pH profiles [SOURCE: Page_18]

403   3.2. Model application: using the modified ADM$_1$ to explain the effect of AD additives [SOURCE: Page_19]

404   Carbon conductive materials have been reported to enhance the performance of AD reactors, [SOURCE: Page_19]

405   particularly in FAN-rich digesters (Barua and Dhar, 2017). Improvements due to GAC addition [SOURCE: Page_19]

406   have been related to: (i) allowing direct interspecies electron transfer (DIET) (Barua and Dhar, [SOURCE: Page_19]

407   2017); (ii) the formation of biofilms on its surface (Fagbohungbe et al., 2017); (iii) the sorption [SOURCE: Page_19]

408   of inhibitors onto its surfaces (Fagbohungbe et al., 2017); and (iv) an increased buffering [SOURCE: Page_19]

409   capacity (Barua and Dhar, 2017). Bioprocess modelling has never been used to increase our [SOURCE: Page_19]

410   understanding on this topic, likely because available models did not include some of the [SOURCE: Page_19]

411   relevant metabolic pathways occurring in the reactors. [SOURCE: Page_19]

412   The modified and default ADM1s were calibrated over experiments supplemented with GAC [SOURCE: Page_19]

413   (after inoculum adaptation in sequential batch reactors). The calibration and modelling results [SOURCE: Page_19]

414   are shown in Table 3 and Fig. 4, respectively. The modified ADM$_1$ was able to represent the [SOURCE: Page_19]

415   total VFA, acetate, and propionate profiles, and the methane production rates (R$_2$ values from [SOURCE: Page_19]

416   parity plots of 0.93-0.99). As previously, butyrate and valerate concentrations were predicted [SOURCE: Page_19]

417   less accurately. The default ADM$_1$ showed the same limitations found with the control reactor, [SOURCE: Page_19]

418   with barely any methane production (R$_2$ of 0.16) due to an extremely low kLa value. For both [SOURCE: Page_19]

419   models, the corresponding FAN inhibition constants and predicted biomass concentrations (not [SOURCE: Page_19]

420   shown, similar to those in Fig. 3) confirmed the predominant pathways described for the control [SOURCE: Page_19]

421   reactor, i.e. SAO and HM being dominant in the modified ADM1 and AM in the default ADM1 17 [SOURCE: Page_19]

422   (see Table 3 for inhibitory constants). [SOURCE: Page_20]

423   The calibration results (Table 3) show that GAC addition significantly enhanced the H$_2$ uptake [SOURCE: Page_20]

424   kinetics (km,H$_2$ of 4.7 g COD·g COD-1·d-1 in the control reactor and of 24 g COD·g COD-1·d-1 [SOURCE: Page_20]

425   in the GAC dosed reactor), which resulted in a faster uptake of the other VFAs due to a lower [SOURCE: Page_20]

426   H$_2$ partial pressure. The kinetics of SAO, propionate, butyrate, or valerate uptake were not [SOURCE: Page_20]

427   directly enhanced by GAC addition. These results suggest that the improvement observed in [SOURCE: Page_20]

428   AD performance after GAC addition is mainly due to a faster HM kinetics. This can be a [SOURCE: Page_20]

429   consequence of biofilm formation onto the GAC particles, thus favouring syntrophic [SOURCE: Page_20]

430   interactions. Another explanation could be the occurrence of DIET, which is a faster electron [SOURCE: Page_20]

431   transfer mechanism than mediated transport. As single electrons are not a state variable in the [SOURCE: Page_20]

432   model, DIET would simply be translated in the model as a faster HM process. These [SOURCE: Page_20]

433   mechanisms have been further discussed in Capson-Tojo et al. (2018a). Opposed to these [SOURCE: Page_20]

434   findings, the calibration results using the default ADM$_1$ (Table 3) would explain these [SOURCE: Page_20]

435   enhancements via increasing the AM and SPO rates. As microbial community analyses showed [SOURCE: Page_20]

436   that the relative abundance of AM in the reactors was negligible, the default ADM$_1$ would have [SOURCE: Page_20]

437   led to misleading conclusions. [SOURCE: Page_20]

438   These results show that the modified ADM$_1$ can be applied to further understand the underlying [SOURCE: Page_20]

439   processes governing FW AD. The application shown here indicates that the modified ADM$_1$ [SOURCE: Page_20]

440   can be used to explain the positive effects that AD additives have on the process kinetics, [SOURCE: Page_20]

441   allowing to identify the processes that are more significantly affected. [SOURCE: Page_20]

455   limit applied in the default ADM$_1$ (0.0018 M). However, it must be considered that the default [SOURCE: Page_21]

456   ADM$_1$ was designed for modelling AD of dilute sewage sludge (TS <5%) from wastewater [SOURCE: Page_21]

457   treatment plants, with lower FAN concentrations, thus representing microbial communities [SOURCE: Page_21]

458   unadapted to high FAN concentrations. The corresponding inhibitory limit to be used for FAN- [SOURCE: Page_21]

459   adapted processes (applied in our modified model) has been estimated around 0.0057 M [SOURCE: Page_21]

460   (corresponding to values of 4.3·10-4 M and 0.0109 M for KI,NH$_3$,min,acet and KI,NH$_3$,max,acet in the [SOURCE: Page_21]

461   threshold function) (Capson-Tojo et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_21]

462   The results from sensitivity analyses and model calibrations carried out in previous publications [SOURCE: Page_21]

463   including SAO also agree with those presented in Tables 2 and 3 for the modified ADM$_1$ [SOURCE: Page_21]

464   (Montecchio et al., 2017; Rivera-Salvador et al., 2014). In previous publications, the kinetic [SOURCE: Page_21]

465   parameters (e.g. uptake rates) related to SAO and HM were found to be relevant (Montecchio [SOURCE: Page_21]

466   et al., 2017; Rivera-Salvador et al., 2014). For comparison purposes, Table 4 shows the values [SOURCE: Page_21]

467   of the uptake rates for acetate-uptake related processes (i.e. AM and SAO) and for HM, from [SOURCE: Page_21]

468   the literature and from this study. It must be considered that the data used in this work (and in [SOURCE: Page_21]

469   most of the studies presented in Table 4) was obtained from batch reactors. Therefore, the initial [SOURCE: Page_21]

470   biomass concentrations influenced to some extent the values of the obtained kinetic parameters. [SOURCE: Page_21]

471   It is important to consider that most previous AD models including SAO omitted AM, thus 19 [SOURCE: Page_21]

472   excluding potential interactions between competing pathways (Montecchio et al., 2017; Rivera- [SOURCE: Page_22]

473   Salvador et al., 2014). The modified ADM$_1$ presented here considers both AM and SAO, which [SOURCE: Page_22]

474   means that microbial competitions and shifts can be modelled by considering environmental [SOURCE: Page_22]

475   factors (e.g. FAN concentration). To the best of our knowledge, only Wett et al. (2014) [SOURCE: Page_22]

476   implemented both AM and SAO simultaneously, but they did not discuss competitions between [SOURCE: Page_22]

477   them, neither their inhibition under different conditions. In practice, Wett et al. (2014) virtually [SOURCE: Page_22]

478   omitted AM, since the km values were extremely low (0.3 kg COD·kg COD-1·d-1, see Table 4). [SOURCE: Page_22]

479   3.4. Implications for industrial application and further model development [SOURCE: Page_22]

480   This work shows that to properly model FW AD, key modifications must be made to the default [SOURCE: Page_22]

481   ADM$_1$ (i.e. including SAO and the impact of ionic strength on ion speciation). These [SOURCE: Page_22]

482   modifications are important for the accurate prediction of the performances of digesters treating [SOURCE: Page_22]

483   FW, which otherwise could not be achieved (e.g. inaccurate biogas production rates and/or [SOURCE: Page_22]

484   acetate and propionate concentrations in the digesters by the default ADM$_1$). In FW AD, VFAs [SOURCE: Page_22]

485   accumulation is responsible for low performance, or even reactor failure. Their accurate [SOURCE: Page_22]

486   prediction is crucial to understand the behaviour of these systems, to improve digester design, [SOURCE: Page_22]

487   and to better assess mitigation strategies. [SOURCE: Page_22]

488   The accurate representation of methane and the VFA profiles has direct implications for [SOURCE: Page_22]

489   optimisation of operational parameters (e.g. loading rates and retention times), for simulating [SOURCE: Page_22]

490   scenarios with different co-substrates (e.g. predicting the impact of introducing a new waste [SOURCE: Page_22]

491   stream into a territorial digester), for predicting AD inhibition scenarios, and for optimising the [SOURCE: Page_22]

492   co-substrate proportions. These improvements will result in an enhanced waste valorisation. [SOURCE: Page_22]

493   Including competing pathways (e.g. AM or SAO as dominant acetate-consuming pathway) has [SOURCE: Page_22]

494   further practical benefits, since it allows: (i) to account for microbial adaptation without the [SOURCE: Page_22]

495   need of continuous model recalibration; and (ii) to model microbial shifts (e.g. from dominant [SOURCE: Page_22]

496   AM to HM), which could potentially be used to move away from the traditional operational 20 [SOURCE: Page_22]

497   approach of stopping the reactor feed at minimal VFA increases. We consider that the benefits [SOURCE: Page_23]

498   of implementing the modified ADM$_1$ presented here outweigh the minor increase in model [SOURCE: Page_23]

499   complexity. We recommend the application of the modified ADM$_1$ for any AD system where [SOURCE: Page_23]

500   it is suspected that AM might be inhibited due to high FAN concentrations (i.e. over 340 mg [SOURCE: Page_23]

501   FAN-N·L-1, based on Capson-Tojo et al. (2020)). The application of this model is not only [SOURCE: Page_23]

502   restricted to FW AD, but can also be extended to any FAN-rich reactor, such as manure [SOURCE: Page_23]

503   digesters. Further work should focus on calibration and validation of the modified ADM$_1$ with [SOURCE: Page_23]

504   continuous experiments, testing microbial acclimation to FAN and microbial shifts (e.g. from [SOURCE: Page_23]

505   AM to HM). [SOURCE: Page_23]

506   AD models (high-solids models in particular) should account for the non-ideal behaviour of the [SOURCE: Page_23]

507   solution. Further modifications to include activity corrections for chemical species other than [SOURCE: Page_23]

508   FAN, or to consider ion pairing, would allow to: (i) improve the pH and model performance [SOURCE: Page_23]

509   predictions (Solon et al., 2015); (ii) to accurately predict inhibition by other compounds (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_23]

510   H2S) (Durán et al., 2020; Patón et al., 2018); and (iii) to model the precise chemical speciation [SOURCE: Page_23]

511   and complexation of relevant elements (e.g. P, S or Fe) (Flores-Alsina et al., 2016). Although [SOURCE: Page_23]

512   non-ideality considerations are commonly considered by using the Debye-Hückel equation, [SOURCE: Page_23]

513   fully defined comprehensive chemistry engines (e.g. PHREEQC or MINTEQA$_2$) could also be [SOURCE: Page_23]

514   integrated with AD models (Durán et al., 2020). Ion pairing and activity corrections could be [SOURCE: Page_23]

515   coupled to a model considering TE complexation and precipitation, which would be particularly [SOURCE: Page_23]

516   relevant if TEs are dosed in the digesters (Frunzo et al., 2019; Maharaj et al., 2019). Another [SOURCE: Page_23]

517   potential modification could be to consider the variable TS contents in the reactors. As [SOURCE: Page_23]

518   explained in Pastor-Poquet et al. (2018), the TS content can change in high-solids AD reactors [SOURCE: Page_23]

519   due to the conversion of solid organics into biogas (up to around 10% with municipal solid [SOURCE: Page_23]

520   waste as substrate). Consequently, the concentrations of soluble compounds and solids in the [SOURCE: Page_23]

521   reactors can be affected. This effect was not considered in this work because the change in 21 [SOURCE: Page_23]

522   volume from the start to the end of the experiments was considered negligible under the [SOURCE: Page_24]

523   working conditions (estimated at 3-5% reactor volume loss). [SOURCE: Page_24]

524   The accurate prediction of FAN inhibitory limits is relevant, as it allows the compare the [SOURCE: Page_24]

525   obtained values with those from the literature, and to obtain accurate limits for different [SOURCE: Page_24]

526   predominant microbial communities. Furthermore, applying a more realistic model can help to [SOURCE: Page_24]

527   provide a better understanding of FAN inhibition in anaerobic systems and to better predict [SOURCE: Page_24]

528   microbial community shifts due to inhibition. Proper modelling of FAN-rich systems (including [SOURCE: Page_24]

529   accurate inhibition limits) would improve the predictions of acetic and propionic acid profiles, [SOURCE: Page_24]

530   which in turn could be used to better understand the impact of additives (e.g. GAC) on AD. [SOURCE: Page_24]

531   This will not only assist in optimising the dosage and characteristics of these additives but will [SOURCE: Page_24]

532   also aid to find other alternatives. [SOURCE: Page_24]

533 [SOURCE: Page_24]

534   4. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_24]

535   Results showed that the modified ADM$_1$ is a suitable approach to model FW AD. The modified [SOURCE: Page_24]

536   ADM$_1$ was able to represent the methane production rates and the VFA profiles simultaneously, [SOURCE: Page_24]

537   which could not be achieved with the default ADM$_1$. The modified model also predicted the [SOURCE: Page_24]

538   predominant acetate-consuming and methane-producing microbial clades, with SAO and HM [SOURCE: Page_24]

539   being dominant. A modified Davies equation accurately estimated FAN concentrations, which [SOURCE: Page_24]

540   improved pH predictions and provided better estimates for inhibition limits. Finally, the [SOURCE: Page_24]

541   modified model showed that the addition of GAC enhances FW AD by improving the HM [SOURCE: Page_24]

542   kinetics. [SOURCE: Page_24]
