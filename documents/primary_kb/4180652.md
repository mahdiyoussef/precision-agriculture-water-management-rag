Abstract:                                 Purple phototrophic bacteria (PPB) technology for resource recovery is still in its infancy, but progress is occurring fast, and developments are accelerating. The generally higher photobioreactor costs have to be balanced with product revenue. The PPB biomass can be used as single cell protein where high protein contents can be complemented by value add components ( e.g. pigments and ployhydroxyalkanoates), merging functionalities within a single product. This has the potential to increase the product value and impact the economic feasibility, likely justifying higher capital costs for PPB photobioreactors for real life applications, with high future growth demand potential of the PPB product. 38   1       Introduction [SOURCE: Page_5]

39   Purple phototrophic bacteria (PPB) are a ubiquitous group of photosynthetic microbes with [SOURCE: Page_5]

40   versatile metabolic capabilities. PPB perform anoxygenic photosynthesis, which does not [SOURCE: Page_5]

41   produce oxygen because water does not serve as electron donor [1]. Instead, PPB use a diversity [SOURCE: Page_5]

42   of organic donors, such as acetate and succinate, that serve as both carbon and electron source, [SOURCE: Page_5]

43   and inorganic donors such as H2, H2S, or Fe2+, for anaerobic photoheterotrophic and [SOURCE: Page_5]

44   photoautotrophic growth respectively [2]. Phototrophic growth is driven by light harvesting [SOURCE: Page_6]

45   (LH) complexes, (LHI and LHII) containing a range of carotenoids and bacteriochlorophylls [SOURCE: Page_6]

46   (BChls), namely BChl a and/or BChl b. BChls absorb wavelengths in the near infra-red (NIR) [SOURCE: Page_6]

47   spectrum, >850 and >1000 nm [3], while carotenoids have accessory light harvesting functions [SOURCE: Page_6]

48   and also serve for photo-inhibition protection in the visible range (400-600 nm) [4]. The [SOURCE: Page_6]

49   utilisation of light as energy source enables biomass yields close to unity on chemical oxygen [SOURCE: Page_6]

50   demand (COD) basis during photoheterotrophic growth, and the exclusive capability of [SOURCE: Page_6]

51   absorbing NIR wavelengths allows effective selection of PPB in non-sterile environments, such [SOURCE: Page_6]

52   as wastewater [5]. [SOURCE: Page_6]

53   The combination of effective NIR selection and enrichment with anaerobic photoheterotrophy [SOURCE: Page_6]

54   and the consequent high biomass yields, have led to the re-emergence of PPB for environmental [SOURCE: Page_6]

55   biotechnological applications, with a focus on resource recovery. Specifically, PPB have been [SOURCE: Page_6]

56   applied for secondary/tertiary wastewater treatment, achieving the simultaneous removal of [SOURCE: Page_6]

57   organics, nitrogen, and phosphorus from various wastewaters [2, 6]. Non-oxidative removal [SOURCE: Page_6]

58   via biological assimilation enables the partitioning of soluble components (e.g. soluble COD, [SOURCE: Page_6]

59   NH$_4$+-N and PO43--P) into PPB biomass, a solid which can be recovered. This biomass is [SOURCE: Page_6]

60   characterised by high crude protein (CP) contents at around 60% dry weight (DW), and varying [SOURCE: Page_6]

61   amounts of carotenoids, BChls, vitamins and polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) (Figure 1). [SOURCE: Page_6]

62   Consequently, the generated biomass and its components are potential products, depending on [SOURCE: Page_6]

63   quality which is mostly determined by the substrate used. Indeed, biomass has been tested as [SOURCE: Page_6]

64   single cell protein (SCP) source, for example, as a feed additive and bulk ingredient in [SOURCE: Page_6]

65   aquaculture [7, 8]. [SOURCE: Page_6]

66   Photobioreactor (PBR) systems for biomass production and wastewater treatment have been [SOURCE: Page_6]

67   researched extensively in the algae field [9]. It has been well established that the critical barriers [SOURCE: Page_6]

68   are largely economic rather than technical. This is due to the increased capital and operational [SOURCE: Page_6]

69   costs of PBRs compared to conventional treatment processes such as activated sludge systems [SOURCE: Page_7]

70   [10, 11]. In fact, the monetisation of the biomass or related products is a prerequisite to balance [SOURCE: Page_7]

71   the costs and to enable reasonable amortisation rates. Compared to microalgae, PPB products [SOURCE: Page_7]

72   have received little attention and there are no commercial product. Nevertheless, if PPB are to [SOURCE: Page_7]

73   be applied for wastewater treatment and resource recovery, PPB products have to generate [SOURCE: Page_7]

74   revenue, necessitating the type of commercially focused research realised for microalgae [SOURCE: Page_7]

75   products [12]. [SOURCE: Page_7]

76   While it is an advantage for resource recovery to have biomass yields close to unity, it is a [SOURCE: Page_7]

77   costly problem if this biomass cannot be valorised. The application as SCP has great potential [SOURCE: Page_7]

78   due to the high protein content in PPB biomass, without requirement for extensive post- [SOURCE: Page_7]

79   processing, as bulk biomass can be used directly. There is also potential value addition from [SOURCE: Page_7]

80   the combination of PPB cell components. Other than proteins, PPB contain pigments (i.e. [SOURCE: Page_7]

81   BChls and carotenoids) and PHA with potential additional functional benefits such as meat [SOURCE: Page_7]

82   coloration and immuno-nutritional properties in, for example, aquaculture feeds [13, 14]. [SOURCE: Page_7]

83   Whole cell PPB biomass with added functionalities will likely increase product value, [SOURCE: Page_7]

84   improving the economic feasibility of the PPB technology. [SOURCE: Page_7]

85   Here, we summarise the most recent advances in the application of PPB biomass as SCP and [SOURCE: Page_7]

86   its value-added potential due to the presence of other compounds, such as pigments or PHA. [SOURCE: Page_7]

87   This review also addresses recent developments in cultivation of PPB in different [SOURCE: Page_7]

88   configurations, as they are key to determine the production costs. Finally, recommendations [SOURCE: Page_7]

89   for a successful implementation of PPB technology are given. [SOURCE: Page_7]

95   2 PPB as single cell protein and added-value components for feeds [SOURCE: Page_8]

96   Production of fish and shellfish from aquaculture grew from around 10 million metric tonnes [SOURCE: Page_8]

97   in 1987 to more than 80 million tonnes in 2017 [15]. Critically, the production of fed-fish, those [SOURCE: Page_8]

98   fed on formulated compound diets, tripled between 2000 and 2017, while harvest of forage fish [SOURCE: Page_8]

99   for fishmeal, the major protein source in aquaculture feeds, declined over the same period [15, [SOURCE: Page_8]

100   16]. This has resulted in a doubling of fishmeal price since 2020 [15] that, along with questions [SOURCE: Page_8]

101   over sustainability, has driven demand for alternative protein ingredients for fish feeds from [SOURCE: Page_8]

102   the manufacturers. Most plant or algae-based substitutes lack the correct balance of amino [SOURCE: Page_8]

103   acids, contain anti-nutritional factors [17], and have insufficient protein content to formulate [SOURCE: Page_8]

104   feeds for the most valuable farmed fish and shrimp that require >40% dietary protein . In [SOURCE: Page_8]

105   contrast, PPB biomass is generally characterised by high crude protein contents (~60% DW), [SOURCE: Page_8]

106   with a balanced amino acid profile, and including elevated levels of the essential amino acids [SOURCE: Page_8]

107   cysteine and methionine [18], which are often added to aquaculture feed [19]. High protein [SOURCE: Page_8]

108   contents and adequate amino acid profiles enable feed protein substitution for high value [SOURCE: Page_8]

109   aquaculture species, such as salmonids carnivorous marine fish and shrimp that require up to [SOURCE: Page_8]

110   60% of protein in their diets [20]. In fact, PPB can substitute a major fraction of fishmeal in [SOURCE: Page_8]

111   diets for Asian sea bass [7]. Fishmeal substitution with PPB was also tested in prawn feed trials, [SOURCE: Page_8]

112   underlining the application of PPB biomass across diverse valuable taxa [8]. At lower inclusion [SOURCE: Page_9]

113   rates, PPB inclusion in commercial fish (Nile tilapia, marble goby, barramundi and Tor [SOURCE: Page_9]

114   tambroides [21, 22] and shrimp feed (Litopenaeus vannamei) increased survival, growth and [SOURCE: Page_9]

115   resistance to challenges, compared with a control diet [23]. Theoretically, this makes PPB [SOURCE: Page_9]

116   biomass as valuable as fishmeal (~1,500 USD·tonne-1), although the critical price point to [SOURCE: Page_9]

117   compete is expected to be lower than that of fishmeal. Here, immuno-nutritional components [SOURCE: Page_9]

118   such as carotenoids, BChls, vitamins and PHA provide additional points-of-value (Figure 1). [SOURCE: Page_9]

119   This is particularly the case for whole cell PPB products, which avoid costly substitution of [SOURCE: Page_9]

120   extracted pure compounds to enhance vitamin, amino acid, and pigment levels. The value [SOURCE: Page_9]

121   proposition for animals other than fish differs drastically, especially when low value protein [SOURCE: Page_9]

122   sources such as soybean meal are substituted (470 USD·tonne-1), which would hardly cover [SOURCE: Page_9]

123   the production costs [24]. We therefore focus on PPB as source of SCP for aquaculture, with [SOURCE: Page_9]

124   the substitution of fishmeal as primary target. [SOURCE: Page_9]

125   2.1   Secondary points-of-value [SOURCE: Page_9]

126   2.1.1 PPB pigments: carotenoids and bacteriochlorophylls [SOURCE: Page_9]

127   Carotenoids, especially xanthophylls, such as astaxanthin (ASX), are used as antioxidants, and [SOURCE: Page_9]

128   as pigments for meat and skin coloration in commercial aqua feed. for shrimps and salmonids [SOURCE: Page_9]

129   [25]. Incorporation of ASX in feed is recommended at levels of 50–100 mg·kgfeed-1 in [SOURCE: Page_9]

130   aquaculture, which represents between 10-18% of the total feed costs [26]. [SOURCE: Page_9]

131   While PPB do not produce ASX, they do contain a range of other xanthophylls such as [SOURCE: Page_9]

132   spirilloxanthin, lutein, okenone, spheroidene, rhodopin, etc., besides a wide range of carotenes, [SOURCE: Page_9]

133   including lycopene, neurosporene, etc. [27]. These might be relevant as meat colorants (more [SOURCE: Page_9]

134   relevant for shrimps and to a lesser extent for fish) and/or as antioxidants and vitamin A [SOURCE: Page_9]

135   precursors, which can improve the immune functions, liver structure and reproduction [SOURCE: Page_9]

136   performance in fish and crustaceans [28]. Understanding the value of specific PPB pigments, [SOURCE: Page_9]

137   other than lutein, is hampered by the fact that only a handful of analytical standards are [SOURCE: Page_10]

138   available for chemical analysis. Consequently, development of methods for accurate carotenoid [SOURCE: Page_10]

139   quantification, and evaluation of their effects as feed components is an essential prerequisite to [SOURCE: Page_10]

140   extend the application spectrum and value of PPB biomass as a feed component. A similar [SOURCE: Page_10]

141   problem applies to BChls in PPB, noting that chlorophyll a from cyanobacteria is a bioactive [SOURCE: Page_10]

142   component, and part of the diet in herbivorous fish that comprise the majority of fish farmed [SOURCE: Page_10]

143   for food [29]. Consequently, the potential value of pigments in PPB biomass has not yet been [SOURCE: Page_10]

144   determined, a serious omission, as whole cell PPB products contain around 5-10 mg·g-1 total [SOURCE: Page_10]

145   carotenoids and ~10-20 mg·g-1 BChls [2]. [SOURCE: Page_10]

146   Intracellular carotenoids in whole cell products have substantial benefits over synthetic [SOURCE: Page_10]

147   products, with the latter being more easily degraded by light, oxygen, acidity and temperature [SOURCE: Page_10]

148   [26]. This stability may enable lower carotenoid inclusion rates in feed. Pigment content of PPB can be manipulated [SOURCE: Page_10]

149   to a degree, for example via light intensity, but the natural selection of specific carotenoids in [SOURCE: Page_10]

150   PPB biomass has not been reported [30]. Establishing how well the various PPB carotenoids [SOURCE: Page_10]

151   are utilised by fish and shrimp, and potential impacts on animal growth performance are clear [SOURCE: Page_10]

152   avenues for future research to enable full appraisal of benefits and potential revenues. [SOURCE: Page_10]

153   2.1.2 Polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA) [SOURCE: Page_10]

154   Diseases remain a major constraint to growth and profitability in global aquaculture [15, 31]. [SOURCE: Page_10]

155   While significant advances in disease control have been made, antibiotic use in some sectors [SOURCE: Page_10]

156   of the industry remains high [32]. Set against a global "one health" initiative to reduce [SOURCE: Page_10]

157   antimicrobial resistance in the environment and food supply to reduce the public health threat [SOURCE: Page_10]

158   [31], alternative means of reducing or controlling diseases in aquaculture have high potential [SOURCE: Page_10]

159   value. PHAs have been intensively studied for application as biodegradable polymers or [SOURCE: Page_10]

160   bioplastics for packaging. In addition, PHA may have antimicrobial properties and [SOURCE: Page_10]

161   immunomodulatory effects in fish and shrimps [13, 33]. In fact, PHA-rich additives in feed [SOURCE: Page_10]

162   might increase resistance against common pathogens in aquaculture [34], a topic that has [SOURCE: Page_11]

163   recently been reviewed [13]. Inclusion rates between 0.2-5%wt (weight) PHA generally [SOURCE: Page_11]

164   increase the average weight gains, growth rates and survival rates of fish and shrimps in various [SOURCE: Page_11]

165   growth stages, with species dependant effects [35]. These effects are at least partly caused by [SOURCE: Page_11]

166   the microbial PHA degradation to short chain fatty acids (SCFA) in the gastrointestinal tract [SOURCE: Page_11]

167   of the fish or shrimp. SCFA are commercially used as antimicrobial bio-agents in commercial [SOURCE: Page_11]

168   animal feed [36]. [SOURCE: Page_11]

169   Mixed and pure PPB cultures readily produce PHA [37] and can accumulate up to 82%wt under [SOURCE: Page_11]

170   nutrient limiting conditions in dedicated processes [38]. For feed applications, high PHA [SOURCE: Page_11]

171   contents are not required to reach the desired PHA inclusion rates for feed applications (usually [SOURCE: Page_11]

172   around 0.2-5%wt). Non-PHA directed PPB growth resulted in around 2.5%wt of PHA [7], [SOURCE: Page_11]

173   which may be sufficient to add value to the PPB biomass. With limited extra effort, small [SOURCE: Page_11]

174   amounts of PHA can be generated in a PPB process. Intracellular PHA is naturally bio- [SOURCE: Page_11]

175   encapsulated, which enables effective delivery to the gastrointestinal tract of the target species, [SOURCE: Page_11]

176   improving feed palatability (no PHA odour), while minimising losses through leaching during [SOURCE: Page_11]

177   feeding. The amorphous character of intracellular PHA also improves the biotic and abiotic [SOURCE: Page_11]

178   degradability compared to crystalline PHA [39]. This might be a more realistic application of [SOURCE: Page_11]

179   PPB-generated PHA, compared to pure PHA production from PPB, which incurs substantial [SOURCE: Page_11]

180   extraction and purification costs and has production rates that are orders of magnitude lower [SOURCE: Page_11]

181   (~0.5 g PHA·L-1·d -1) [2] compared to aerobic platforms (>10 g PHA·L-1·d-1) [40]. Instead, [SOURCE: Page_11]

182   PHA within the PPB biomass, together with the protein and pigment contents, can substantially [SOURCE: Page_11]

183   increase the biomass values, thanks to the potential benefits of combined functionalities within [SOURCE: Page_11]

184   a single product. There is currently no research about the combined effects on performance of [SOURCE: Page_11]

185   aquatic species fed intracellular PHA from PPB. [SOURCE: Page_11]

186   3 PPB cultivation systems [SOURCE: Page_12]

187   For microalgae cultivation, the most commonly applied systems are high rate algae ponds [SOURCE: Page_12]

188   (HRAPs), with some niche applications of closed PBRs for high value products from sterile [SOURCE: Page_12]

189   substrates [41]. The costs and life cycle analyses for microalgae systems have been detailed in [SOURCE: Page_12]

190   several reviews, e.g. [42]. [SOURCE: Page_12]

191   Economic analyses for PPB mediated systems are basic, and usually rely on artificially [SOURCE: Page_12]

192   illuminated, small-scale lab research, which hardly captures the true picture of a scaled, [SOURCE: Page_12]

193   outdoors PPB wastewater treatment plant [24, 43]. The main problem at the moment is that [SOURCE: Page_12]

194   scaled capital and operational costs have not been detailed, as studies in outdoors, scalable [SOURCE: Page_12]

195   systems are absent from the literature. Therefore, accurate datasets evaluating annual [SOURCE: Page_12]

196   performances, including areal productivities, removal efficiencies and rates, as well as the [SOURCE: Page_12]

197   quality and consistency of the produced biomass and its applicability as product, are missing. [SOURCE: Page_12]

198   PPB reactor technology is still in its infancy. Recent progress is limited to medium-scale [SOURCE: Page_12]

199   laboratory and field reactors (<100 L) employing artificial illumination in controlled [SOURCE: Page_12]

200   environments. There is no consensus on the reactor design, and several configurations have [SOURCE: Page_12]

201   been proposed. These include closed PBRs (flat plate, tubular, cylindrical, sequencing batch [SOURCE: Page_12]

202   reactors, gas-lift, etc.) and reconfigured HRAP [2]. [SOURCE: Page_12]

203   One might think that 60 years of algal research would have paved the way for PPB, but results [SOURCE: Page_12]

204   from algal research cannot directly be translated to PPB systems. This is a consequence of the [SOURCE: Page_12]

205   profound differences among these two microbial groups and include; (i) NIR vs. visible (VIS) [SOURCE: Page_12]

206   light uptake (ii) anaerobic vs. aerobic phototrophic growth (iii) photoheterotrophic vs. [SOURCE: Page_12]

207   photoautotrophic growth which necessitate organic carbon vs. CO$_2$ supply. Due to the COD [SOURCE: Page_12]

208   requirements, PPB are fundamentally more suited to secondary treatment, whereas algae [SOURCE: Page_12]

209   systems are well suited to tertiary applications. In addition, higher biomass concentrations can [SOURCE: Page_12]

210   be achieved in PPB reactors, and PPB grow faster than algae [2]. These aspects affect the [SOURCE: Page_12]

211   process fundamentally, modifying the light distribution through the reactor, potential microbial [SOURCE: Page_13]

212   synergies and competitions, the growth kinetics, reactor design and mixing, biomass contents, [SOURCE: Page_13]

213   and various design parameters, such as hydraulic retention time (HRT), sludge retention time [SOURCE: Page_13]

214   (SRT) and organic loading rate (OLR). Open ponds have lower volumetric cost per unit than [SOURCE: Page_13]

215   closed PBRs, but the combination of poor NIR penetration, high PPB biomass concentrations, [SOURCE: Page_13]

216   and the requirement to limit oxygen input (see below) complicate their use. Recent studies [SOURCE: Page_13]

217   confirm the issue of light limitation in ponds, with light-limited processes at light paths (pond [SOURCE: Page_13]

218   depth) of 10, 15 and 20 cm (surface to volume ratio (S/V) of 5-10 m^2·m-3) [44, 45]. Sepúlveda- [SOURCE: Page_13]

219   Muñoz, Ángeles [45] found an improved performance with a light path of 7.5 cm when [SOURCE: Page_13]

220   combined with a light intensity increase from 100–200 W·m-2, confirming that light was [SOURCE: Page_13]

221   limiting. A 15 cm deep horizontal PBR (simulating a pond) required HRTs of 4-11 d, which [SOURCE: Page_13]

222   are much higher than those commonly applied (PPB have phototrophic growth rates of 2-4 d-1 [SOURCE: Page_13]

223   [46-48]). This further confirm light limitation at these conditions. Such short light transfer [SOURCE: Page_13]

224   distances imply large footprints (land areas), which increases cost and oxygen transfer into the [SOURCE: Page_13]

225   liquid. Excessive oxygen transfer (i) inhibits PPB pigment synthesis [49] and allows the growth [SOURCE: Page_13]

226   of aerobic heterotrophs. This has the potential to result in complete out-competition of PPB [SOURCE: Page_13]

227   [50]. This will most certainly result in mixed cultures, inconsistent product contents, and [SOURCE: Page_13]

228   reduced value and may be referred to as PPBALBAZOD (Purple Phototrophic Bacteria, Algae, [SOURCE: Page_13]

229   Bacteria, Zooplankton and Detritus). [SOURCE: Page_13]

230   Flat plate PBRs might be an elegant alternative to ponds, reducing the required land area and [SOURCE: Page_13]

231   the oxygen transfer, while effectively doubling the light path (i.e. at the same pond depth and [SOURCE: Page_13]

232   PBR thickness, the light path is double in the PBR due to illumination from both sides) and [SOURCE: Page_13]

233   increasing the illuminated surface to volume ratio, albeit at higher capital cost (Figure 2). Costs [SOURCE: Page_13]

234   can be further decreased by low energy mixing options, no need for CO$_2$ supply or O$_2$ stripping, [SOURCE: Page_13]

235   and no cooling requirements. PPB tolerate up to 55°C and daily temperature fluctuations [SOURCE: Page_13]

236   between 20-30°C [51]. Any viable PBR must be run outdoors unless energy for artificial [SOURCE: Page_14]

237   illumination is free (or exceptionally cheap) [2]. Mixing, harvesting, up-concentration, drying [SOURCE: Page_14]

238   and sterilisation costs have to be added. To date, there is no realistic value assessing these costs [SOURCE: Page_14]

239   as long term outdoor data are missing, and economic studies and life cycle analysis are based [SOURCE: Page_14]

240   on lab results [24, 43, 46]. The maximum allowable production costs cannot exceed the sum [SOURCE: Page_14]

241   of wastewater treatment (i.e. discharge savings) and the product revenue to enable a reasonable [SOURCE: Page_14]

242   payback time for the PPB technology. Mixing is an issue still to be addressed. Inert gas mixing [SOURCE: Page_14]

243   and return liquid mixing is energetically expensive, and paddle wheels increase oxygen transfer [SOURCE: Page_14]

244   [52]. PPB can be harvested as biofilms from illuminated submerged surfaces at >10% dry solids [SOURCE: Page_14]

245   [5, 7]. Formation of flocculant or granular biomass also has the potential to reduce harvesting [SOURCE: Page_14]

246   costs [53, 54], and might improve the product consistency over time. [SOURCE: Page_14]

251   4 Further research [SOURCE: Page_14]

252   In order to advance and finally implement the PPB technology in the real world, research needs [SOURCE: Page_14]

253   to move to relevant-scale outdoor units. These units will enable the determination of the impact [SOURCE: Page_15]

254   that biotic and abiotic factors have on the wastewater treatment performance, as well as on the [SOURCE: Page_15]

255   PPB cultivation and the product quality on an annual basis. This will enable an economic [SOURCE: Page_15]

256   evaluation, while producing large quantities of PPB biomass that can be used for feed trials. [SOURCE: Page_15]

257   Further fundamental research is needed on the manipulation of pigment and PHA contents, [SOURCE: Page_15]

258   including validating their effect via dedicated feed trials. This should include feed [SOURCE: Page_15]

259   manufacturing as well as nutritional aspects, and requires a multidisciplinary team of [SOURCE: Page_15]

260   researchers. High value fish and shrimp is a clear initial target to maximise the value of the [SOURCE: Page_15]

261   product. However, use as feed is overall the most economically viable use of PPB biomass, [SOURCE: Page_15]

262   and has already been validated in the more expensive algae area . [SOURCE: Page_15]

264   5 Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_15]

265   PPB technology is still in its infancy, but progress is occurring fast, and developments are [SOURCE: Page_15]

266   accelerating. The economics of different PPB cultivation systems have to be evaluated and the [SOURCE: Page_15]

267   value of PPB biomass as product will play a major role in the overall feasibility and subsequent [SOURCE: Page_15]

268   realisation of a PPB platform. The potential value add of the product will impact the overall [SOURCE: Page_15]

269   feasibility, likely justifying higher capital costs for closed photo bioreactors for real life [SOURCE: Page_15]

270   applications, with high future growth demand potential of the PPB product. [SOURCE: Page_15]
