integral part of modern wastewater treatment. This work presents the potential to [SOURCE: Page_4]

### 1. Introduction Intensive animal farming and processing generates large volumes of wastewater with

very high nitrogen, phosphorus, and organic carbon loads, requiring intensive treatment. [SOURCE: Page_5]

For example, in Australia up to 550 million chicken are processed in abattoirs annually, [SOURCE: Page_5]

producing on average 12L wastewater per bird (Chainetr et al. 2020). This offers a huge [SOURCE: Page_5]

recovery potential of resources (e.g. around 20000, 1650, 200 tonnes of COD, TN and [SOURCE: Page_5]

TP from chicken processing in Australia alone), even greater considering that there is a [SOURCE: Page_5]

general trend of centralisation of animal processing activities. In addition to abattoirs [SOURCE: Page_5]

(processing), intensive (centralised) animal rearing also generates large amounts of [SOURCE: Page_5]

manure, which also contains substantial amounts of resources that can potentially be [SOURCE: Page_5]

recovered (Orner et al. 2021, Potter et al. 2010). [SOURCE: Page_5]

The recovery of organics, nitrogen, and phosphorus from these waste streams via [SOURCE: Page_5]

established technologies such as anaerobic digestion, ammonia stripping and struvite [SOURCE: Page_5]

precipitation is a common practice, applied worldwide (Mehta et al. 2015). However, [SOURCE: Page_5]

these technologies generate low-value products (e.g., biogas and fertilisers such as [SOURCE: Page_5]

ammonium sulphate and struvite) and are hence challenged economically. In this [SOURCE: Page_5]

context, the biological up-concentration of soluble organics and nutrients (e.g. nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_5]

3 [SOURCE: Page_5]

and phosphorous) via assimilative and/or accumulative partitioning is receiving [SOURCE: Page_6]

increased attention (Batstone et al. 2015). [SOURCE: Page_6]

The transformation of resources in the wastewater into single-cell protein (SCP), and/or [SOURCE: Page_6]

biological fertiliser, could be a major step towards minimising resource losses in a [SOURCE: Page_6]

circular economy. When grown in wastewater, microorganisms can partition up to 100% [SOURCE: Page_6]

of organics and nutrients into microbial, protein-rich biomass. This is particularly [SOURCE: Page_6]

applicable for phototrophic mediators, such as microalgae or purple phototrophic [SOURCE: Page_6]

bacteria (PPB) (Hülsen et al. 2016b, Matassa et al. 2015, Shi et al. 2007). Phototrophic [SOURCE: Page_6]

organisms can assimilate organics, ammonia and phosphate using light as energy [SOURCE: Page_6]

source, rather than organics, which minimises carbon and nutrient dissipation (e.g., as [SOURCE: Page_6]

CO$_2$ or N$_2$). When SCP is generated in a closed intensive animal production facility [SOURCE: Page_6]

(e.g., pig farms) or an abattoir, it can be used to effectively recycle nutrients in the form [SOURCE: Page_6]

of protein-rich biomass. This can substantially increase whole system feed conversion [SOURCE: Page_6]

ratios while enhancing on-farm profitability through reduced discharge costs and the [SOURCE: Page_6]

creation of additional revenue streams (e.g., fishmeal substitution in aquaculture feeds [SOURCE: Page_6]

(Alloul et al. 2021b, Delamare-Deboutteville et al. 2019)). [SOURCE: Page_6]

However, besides obvious regulatory, health, and safety concerns (largely depending [SOURCE: Page_6]

on the wastewater source and the production process) there are several other barriers [SOURCE: Page_6]

preventing widespread SCP generation from wastewater. For phototrophs, these [SOURCE: Page_6]

include economic constraints, high total ammonia-N concentrations (>1.0 g·L-1) (with [SOURCE: Page_6]

high pH resulting in high free ammonia concentrations (Ayre et al. 2017)), high turbidity [SOURCE: Page_6]

(limiting light penetration (Barlow et al. 1975)), and long microbial adaptation times [SOURCE: Page_6]

(Ayre et al. 2017). In outdoor phototrophic systems, the operational and economic [SOURCE: Page_6]

4 [SOURCE: Page_6]

feasibility is further impacted by: (i) seasonal weather variations (e.g. dilution during rain [SOURCE: Page_7]

period, evaporation during hot weather resulting in increased salinity, high or low [SOURCE: Page_7]

temperatures, and illumination); (ii) by natural day/night cycles (Kumar et al. 2015); (iii) [SOURCE: Page_7]

by microbial contamination (e.g. grazers); and (iv) by high biomass harvesting costs [SOURCE: Page_7]

(Alabi et al. 2009, Molina Grima et al. 2003). The latter is partly caused by low biomass [SOURCE: Page_7]

concentrations (e.g. 0.35 and 2 g·L-1 in open and closed photobioreactors (PBRs), [SOURCE: Page_7]

respectively (Jorquera et al. 2010)). Especially in open ponds, these challenges reduce [SOURCE: Page_7]

the volumetric and areal productivities (Brennan and Owende 2010, Davis et al. 2011, [SOURCE: Page_7]

Jorquera et al. 2010). [SOURCE: Page_7]

Recent developments in the field of mixed culture PPB might offer solutions for some of [SOURCE: Page_7]

the barriers encountered in phototrophic systems. PPB are photoheterotrophs, utilising [SOURCE: Page_7]

light in the infra-red (IR) spectrum for ATP generation, and organic carbon as anabolic [SOURCE: Page_7]

substrate. They offer the potential for shortened hydraulic retention times (HRT), due to [SOURCE: Page_7]

faster growth rates than microalgae, and for combined secondary and tertiary treatment, [SOURCE: Page_7]

due to the simultaneous removal of organics and nutrients (Hülsen et al. 2018b). The [SOURCE: Page_7]

technological feasibility of this approach has been proven at laboratory scale under [SOURCE: Page_7]

artificial illumination, both using batch reactors (Hülsen et al. 2018a, Marín et al. 2019), [SOURCE: Page_7]

continuous PBRs (Sepúlveda-Muñoz et al. 2020) and raceways/ponds (Alloul et al. [SOURCE: Page_7]

2021a). The utilisation of native organics as carbon source avoids issues with CO$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_7]

addition, which is limiting for microalgae and is commonly added (Park and Craggs [SOURCE: Page_7]

2010). PPB have also been reported to grow at low light intensities (Dalaei et al. 2020), [SOURCE: Page_7]

adapt to low temperatures (Hülsen et al. 2016a), and maintain dominance under non- [SOURCE: Page_7]

axenic conditions (i.e., no sterile influent/media) (Hülsen et al. 2016a). Altogether, these [SOURCE: Page_7]

5 [SOURCE: Page_7]

capabilities can provide some independence from varying environmental conditions [SOURCE: Page_8]

(e.g., light intensity and availability, and temperature). Infra-red (IR) driven anoxygenic [SOURCE: Page_8]

photosynthesis by PPB (as opposed to oxygenic photosynthesis driven by ultraviolet- [SOURCE: Page_8]

visible (UV-VIS) light) under anaerobic conditions allows effective enrichment and [SOURCE: Page_8]

further eliminates common aerobic grazers. The high organic content of industrial [SOURCE: Page_8]

wastewater, tolerance of PPB towards ammonia (Puyol et al. 2020), and ability to use [SOURCE: Page_8]

outdoor systems make PPB a good potential match for agri-industrial wastewaters. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Illumination has been previously identified as the critical cost factor, effectively [SOURCE: Page_8]

prohibiting economic PPB wastewater treatment systems (Capson-Tojo et al. 2020), [SOURCE: Page_8]

particularly for industrial systems (with higher organic loads). Using sunlight (filtered or [SOURCE: Page_8]

natural) as energy source appears as a straight-forward solution to reduce production [SOURCE: Page_8]

costs. However, outdoor studies, utilising sunlight are scarce and none of the research [SOURCE: Page_8]

performed to date has dealt with real wastewater, mixed PPB cultures, or biofilms [SOURCE: Page_8]

(Adessi et al. 2012, Carlozzi et al. 2010, Carlozzi et al. 2006, Carlozzi and Sacchi [SOURCE: Page_8]

2001). Consequently, the results from these studies cannot be directly extrapolated to [SOURCE: Page_8]

real wastewater treatment systems, which are non-sterile and require an enriched, [SOURCE: Page_8]

robust, non-axenic PPB culture, and sufficient biomass retention (Capson-Tojo et al. [SOURCE: Page_8]

2020). The use of non-sterile inputs, and the requirement to use natural light, introduce [SOURCE: Page_8]

the need to consider interactions and competitions between PPB and other microbes, [SOURCE: Page_8]

including other phototrophs (e.g., microalgae and cyanobacteria). Using real wastewater [SOURCE: Page_8]

as substrate will also impact the harvested biomass quality and its application as feed [SOURCE: Page_8]

(e.g. as supplement/additive for aquaculture), where the potential applicability of PPB [SOURCE: Page_8]

6 [SOURCE: Page_8]

biomass as aqua feed has already been demonstrated (Banerjee et al., 2000; [SOURCE: Page_9]

Chowdhury et al., 2016; Delamare-Deboutteville et al., 2019; Loo et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_9]

To retain and concentrate biomass, photo-anaerobic membrane bioreactors have been [SOURCE: Page_9]

effectively used for both microalgae and PPB-based processes (Hülsen et al., 2016; [SOURCE: Page_9]

Viruela et al., 2018). However, this is expensive, and adds additional capital (e.g. 183 – [SOURCE: Page_9]

645 USD m-3 d-1) and operational costs (e.g. 0.13 – 0.27 USD m-3treated (Gao et al. [SOURCE: Page_9]

2021)). A lower cost option might be to use biofilm-based reactors, where phototrophic [SOURCE: Page_9]

biomass is retained as biofilm, attached onto an illuminated surface (e.g., reactor walls). [SOURCE: Page_9]

Biofilm-based systems also enable the exclusion of undesired wastewater components [SOURCE: Page_9]

(e.g. inert compounds), which enhances the quality of the produced biomass (Hülsen et [SOURCE: Page_9]

al. 2020). This approach can also facilitate biomass harvesting. This step, known to be [SOURCE: Page_9]

particularly challenging in microalgae-based systems (accounting for over 30% of the [SOURCE: Page_9]

total production costs), is often done via centrifugation, flocculation or filtration (Kadir et [SOURCE: Page_9]

al., 2018). In a biofilm reactor, biomass can be directly harvested from the walls, [SOURCE: Page_9]

potentially reducing costs and achieving biomass concentrations of >10% DM (Hülsen [SOURCE: Page_9]

et al. 2020). [SOURCE: Page_9]

Here, we applied mixed PPB cultures in outdoor flat plate photobioreactors (FPPBRs) [SOURCE: Page_9]

for growth and resource recovery from piggery (PWW) and chicken processing [SOURCE: Page_9]

wastewaters (CWW), in two separate tests. The experiments using piggery wastewater [SOURCE: Page_9]

were followed by those using chicken processing wastewater. Over a period of 1.5 [SOURCE: Page_9]

years, the systems were run in sequential batch mode at two different locations, to [SOURCE: Page_9]

assess the impacts of varying outdoor conditions (e.g., day/night cycles of sunlight and [SOURCE: Page_9]

temperature) on the wastewater treatment and resource recovery performance, biofilm [SOURCE: Page_9]

7 [SOURCE: Page_9]

formation, and the microbial community. The effects of filtered (IR) vs. non-filtered [SOURCE: Page_10]

sunlight (full spectrum) were also studied and compared. The attached and suspended [SOURCE: Page_10]

biomass characteristics and its consistency were assessed in view of their potential [SOURCE: Page_10]

application as SCP or fertilisers. [SOURCE: Page_10]
