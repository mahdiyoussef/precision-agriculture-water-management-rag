### 1. Introduction

Purple phototrophic bacteria (PPB) have reappeared recently as a potential mediator for [SOURCE: Page_7]

resource recovery from wastewater, transforming its constituents into single cell proteins [SOURCE: Page_7]

(SCP) and several other products, such as polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA), hydrogen, [SOURCE: Page_7]

carotenoids, or fertilizers (Capson-Tojo et al., 2020). Recent literature has exploited their [SOURCE: Page_7]

anaerobic photoheterotrophic metabolism, which enables simultaneous removal and [SOURCE: Page_7]

recovery of organics, nitrogen and phosphorus at biomass yields close to 1.0 g CODbiomass·g [SOURCE: Page_7]

CODremoved-1 (COD being chemical oxygen demand) (Capson-Tojo et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_7]

High biomass yields during photoheterotrophic growth are enabled by the utilisation of light [SOURCE: Page_7]

as energy source (i.e., photophosphorylation, and to a lesser extent phosphorylation). PPB [SOURCE: Page_7]

use mostly near infrared (NIR) light to drive photoheterotrophic metabolism under anaerobic [SOURCE: Page_7]

conditions, a capability almost exclusive to PPB (Saer and Blankenship, 2017). NIR light is [SOURCE: Page_7]

harvested via bacteriochlorophylls (BChls) a and b, with maximum absorption peaks between 5 [SOURCE: Page_7]

780 and 1040 nm (Saer and Blankenship, 2017). PPB dominance in photoheterotrophic [SOURCE: Page_8]

cultures, artificially illuminated with NIR light for resource recovery, has been proven at lab [SOURCE: Page_8]

scale in various reactors for a range of municipal and industrial wastewaters (Hülsen et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

2018a, 2018b; López-Serna et al., 2019). The incident illumination intensities in these [SOURCE: Page_8]

experiments varied between 2.0 and 270 W·m-2 (Chitapornpan et al., 2013; Dalaei et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

2020), with even the lowest intensities being prohibitively expensive when considering a PPB [SOURCE: Page_8]

process at scale (Capson-Tojo et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_8]

The most logical solution for this challenge is outdoors operation, reducing the capital (for [SOURCE: Page_8]

lamps and frames) and operational costs of illumination to zero (due to natural light). Non- [SOURCE: Page_8]

NIR wavelengths (i.e., visible or ultraviolet (UV-VIS)) can be eliminated with a low-cost filter. [SOURCE: Page_8]

To date, there are only a handful of articles reporting outdoors PPB reactor operation, and [SOURCE: Page_8]

most of them did not focus on wastewater treatment or nutrient recovery, but on the [SOURCE: Page_8]

generation of hydrogen and PHA from artificial media (Adessi et al., 2012; Carlozzi et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

2006; Carlozzi and Sacchi, 2001). While these studies exposed the PPB reactors to [SOURCE: Page_8]

changing environmental conditions, they are not relevant to wastewater treatment [SOURCE: Page_8]

applications, as artificial media and pure cultures were used. In particular, in wastewater [SOURCE: Page_8]

applications, influent contamination results in competition with non-phototrophic heterotrophic [SOURCE: Page_8]

microbes. Changing irradiances and temperatures, including day and night fluctuations [SOURCE: Page_8]

create very dynamic conditions that are expected to affect the wastewater treatment [SOURCE: Page_8]

performance via kinetic, microbial, and metabolic changes as well as chemical and [SOURCE: Page_8]

physiological changes due to varying wastewater characteristics. So far, only one study has [SOURCE: Page_8]

assessed these factors in PPB outdoors reactors, confirming the feasibility of this approach [SOURCE: Page_8]

and the resilience of PPB to changing environmental conditions (Hülsen et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_8]

For PPB technology to emerge as a realistic resource recovery alternative, outdoors [SOURCE: Page_8]

implementation, at scale, and using real wastewater needs to be tested. A general sense of [SOURCE: Page_8]

design parameters including hydraulic retention time (HRT), sludge retention time (SRT), [SOURCE: Page_8]

organic loading rate (OLR), as well as influent loads removal rates, removal efficiencies, and [SOURCE: Page_8]

6 [SOURCE: Page_8]

biomass productivities are also required. This is crucial to advance the current infancy of [SOURCE: Page_9]

PPB technology to the next level, but also to avoid extrapolating unrealistically high [SOURCE: Page_9]

performances from small lab-scale reactors to scaled outdoor photobioreactors (PBRs), as [SOURCE: Page_9]

previously experienced with microalgae (Richardson et al., 2014). Sensationalised [SOURCE: Page_9]

productivities from the lab in algae systems (e.g., 60 g·m-2·d-1 or 220 tonnes·ha-1·d-1 [SOURCE: Page_9]

(National-Research-Council, 2012)) attracted major interest and investment that could not be [SOURCE: Page_9]

translated into the field, at least not as annual daily average performance. This is because [SOURCE: Page_9]

photosynthetic efficiencies were lower and culture instabilities and grazers impacted [SOURCE: Page_9]

productivities, among several other issues (Day et al., 2017; Taiganides, 1992). These [SOURCE: Page_9]

problems apply mainly to open ponds, which have lower cost compared with closed PBRs [SOURCE: Page_9]

(Benemann et al., 2018). Closed PBRs for microalgae production were deemed too [SOURCE: Page_9]

expensive (capital and operational) for wastewater treatment, as production costs generally [SOURCE: Page_9]

exceed the relatively low value of the product (Acién et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_9]

The main challenge in PPB reactor research is to identify an economic reactor design which [SOURCE: Page_9]

allows reasonable translation of laboratory research, and hence avoids the issues which [SOURCE: Page_9]

have hindered uptake of algae systems. This requires testing of PBRs in field-relevant [SOURCE: Page_9]

conditions early in the technology cycle, preferably using natural light. The metabolic [SOURCE: Page_9]

diversity of PPB (and mixed culture communities) in addition offers the opportunity to operate [SOURCE: Page_9]

photoheterotrophically at anaerobic, illuminated conditions during the day (PhAnI), and [SOURCE: Page_9]

chemoheterotrophically under aerobic, dark conditions during the night (ChAerD). To the [SOURCE: Page_9]

best of our knowledge, this mixed metabolic mode (3M) concept with PPB, named 3MPPB, [SOURCE: Page_9]

has not been tested to date. Both metabolic modes (PhAnI and ChAerD) have been studied [SOURCE: Page_9]

separately, with PPB rapidly outcompeted by non-phototrophic aerobes under prolonged [SOURCE: Page_9]

continuous aeration (Capson-Tojo et al., 2021). However, regular metabolic mode changes [SOURCE: Page_9]

in day/night coupled to anaerobic/aerobic interval changes have not been studied, and might [SOURCE: Page_9]

offer the potential to polish or even treat wastewater at night, at the expense of lower nutrient [SOURCE: Page_9]

recovery efficiencies and biomass productivities (due to oxidative losses). [SOURCE: Page_9]

7 [SOURCE: Page_9]

This study aims to evaluate the performance of a 10 m long, 950 L, outdoor PPB flat plate [SOURCE: Page_10]

photobioreactor (FPPBR) for resource recovery from poultry processing wastewater. For this [SOURCE: Page_10]

purpose, a scalable, modular FPPBR was operated for 253 days, exposed to naturally [SOURCE: Page_10]

varying environmental conditions. Various design and operational aspects were tested (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_10]

HRT, OLR, feeding regimes, etc.) to optimise the treatment performance and biomass [SOURCE: Page_10]

productivities. Large quantities of PPB biomass were harvested to enable the value [SOURCE: Page_10]

evaluation of the SCP product. Finally, the possibility of aerating at night in a mixed [SOURCE: Page_10]

metabolic mode system (3MPPB) was assessed. [SOURCE: Page_10]

### 3.1.1. Treatment performance

The characteristics of the FPPBR effluent and the removal efficiencies (only based on [SOURCE: Page_17]

FPPBR input and output) during the whole operational period are presented in Figure 2 (A - [SOURCE: Page_17]

D). The reactor achieved high VFA removal efficiencies in most of the phases (>90%), [SOURCE: Page_17]

showing that PPB were able to photoheterotrophically assimilate the VFAs in the wastewater, [SOURCE: Page_17]

at rates over 0.5 g SCOD·L-1·d-1 (mainly VFA-COD), with peaks up to 1.0 g SCOD·L-1·d-1 [SOURCE: Page_17]

(Table 3), noting a general VFA-COD limitation. This also indicates that light was not limiting. [SOURCE: Page_17]

The process was able to remove all the biodegradable SCOD, leaving a baseline of around [SOURCE: Page_17]

500-700 mg COD·L-1, which was non-degradable (see Figure S$_3$ for the difference between [SOURCE: Page_17]

SCOD and VFA-COD in the fermenter) in the given time. This residual, slowly or hardly [SOURCE: Page_17]

biodegradable SCOD, was likely composed of complex proteins (keratin residues from [SOURCE: Page_17]

feather stream) and lipids (see Section 3.2.2 for a detailed discussion), and will be [SOURCE: Page_17]

considered as non-degradable under the given conditions. [SOURCE: Page_17]

The availability of degradable COD in the reactor also determined the N and P removals. [SOURCE: Page_17]

PPB have an uptake ratio of around 100:6-10:1-2 COD:N:P (Capson-Tojo et al., 2020; Puyol [SOURCE: Page_17]

et al., 2017) and, although the reactor influent had a ratio of 100:6.7:1.5 (see Table 1, [SOURCE: Page_17]

15 [SOURCE: Page_17]

fermenter effluent), the non-degradable COD in the influent led to a lack of organic matter to [SOURCE: Page_18]

support complete nutrient uptake. Therefore, the concentrations of N and P in the reactor [SOURCE: Page_18]

effluent exceeded common surface water discharge limits (e.g., TN and TP concentrations of [SOURCE: Page_18]

10 and 1.0 mg·L-1). The final effluent quality ultimately depends on the wastewater, its [SOURCE: Page_18]

COD:N:P ratios, as well as potential pretreatment to adjust the ratios. Additional COD, via [SOURCE: Page_18]

dosing (not desired economically) or combining COD rich streams (usually availabe in agri- [SOURCE: Page_18]

industrial processes) would improve the treatment performance, removing more of the [SOURCE: Page_18]

available N and P (e.g., as N-NH$_4$+ and P-PO43-). For comparison with other treatment [SOURCE: Page_18]

processes, the SCOD, TN and TP removal efficiencies corresponding to the overall plant [SOURCE: Page_18]

performance (i.e., including the DAF unit, the fermenter, and the FPPBR) were around 70%, [SOURCE: Page_18]

70% and 50%, respectively (Figure S$_4$). Note, we are not aware of a single biological step [SOURCE: Page_18]

that would be able to treat this wastewater as described here. Generally, conventional [SOURCE: Page_18]

anaerobic technologies (e.g. Internal ciculation (IC) or upflow anaerobic sludge blanket [SOURCE: Page_18]

nitricfication/denitrification,   nitritation/denitritation   or   anaerobic   ammonium   oxidation [SOURCE: Page_18]

nutrients compared to PPB mediated systems (e.g. high rate A-stage) where conventional [SOURCE: Page_18]

### 3.3. Implications for industrial application

This work describes a modular, scalable FPPBR for PPB mediated wastewater treatment [SOURCE: Page_36]

and resource recovery in the form of PPB biomass. The reactor was functional, removing [SOURCE: Page_36]

almost all the VFA in the influent at rates around 0.5 g SCOD·L-1·d-1, with peaks up to 1.0 g [SOURCE: Page_36]

SCOD ·L-1·d-1. This was achieved without biomass retention in a flowthrough system at total [SOURCE: Page_36]

HRTs (equal to SRT) of 1.6-2.4 d (or illuminated HRTs of 0.8-1.2 d). The OLR can [SOURCE: Page_36]

theoretically be higher, but the effects on the pH have to be considered, particularly if feeding [SOURCE: Page_36]

fermented wastewater (i.e., reduced pH due to high VFA concentrations will inhibit PPB). In [SOURCE: Page_36]

subtropical regions, at a light path of 40 mm (80 mm reactor width), the PBR effluent had TS [SOURCE: Page_36]

34 [SOURCE: Page_36]

concentrations of around 1.5-2.0 g·L-1 (with up to 50% of native solids from the wastewater). [SOURCE: Page_37]

This resulted in estimated (conservative estimates, not measured values) biomass [SOURCE: Page_37]

productivities between 6.0-24 g VS·m-2·d-1, where 24 g VS·m-2·d-1 were achieved during [SOURCE: Page_37]

stable operation in the Australian autumn, at a low light intensity and low temperature period. [SOURCE: Page_37]

In consideration of the underestimation of this productivity (as explained above), we take this [SOURCE: Page_37]

value as a conservative estimate of realisable biomass production throughout the year [SOURCE: Page_37]

(corresponding to 504 g VS·m-3·d-1). For poultry processing wastewater, the corresponding [SOURCE: Page_37]

COD, VFA-COD, TN and TP removal efficiencies were around 67±12, 87±7.5, 53±0 and [SOURCE: Page_37]

45±6.0, respectively (e.g., Phase VII). For mixed anaerobic (day) and aerobic (night) [SOURCE: Page_37]

operation in a 3MPPB system, additional aeration costs will occur (e.g., to realise 0.1 mg [SOURCE: Page_37]

DO·L-1 during the night). The latter depends on the standard oxygen transfer efficiency of the [SOURCE: Page_37]

aerators and the height of the water column, here around 900 mm. A detailed economic [SOURCE: Page_37]

analysis including the capital costs of a scaled plant must be done in a follow up study. [SOURCE: Page_37]

This study has shown that the reactor operation is robust and that it can handle temperature [SOURCE: Page_37]

and irradiance fluctuations throughout the year. This includes day and night temperature [SOURCE: Page_37]

fluctuations of up to 24°C, with peak lows of 6.0°C and peak highs over 45°C, confirming [SOURCE: Page_37]

results from lab studies (Hülsen et al., 2016a) and a recent outdoor study (Hülsen et al., [SOURCE: Page_37]

2022). The system was operated without a PLC and did not require detailed controls (expect [SOURCE: Page_37]

for the latter phases where DO concentration was controlled). However, for prefermented [SOURCE: Page_37]

wastewater as feed, pH control is desirable, and for aeration at night, a DO sensor would [SOURCE: Page_37]

optimise the oxygen supply. Without the aerobic step at night, the PPB plant performance is [SOURCE: Page_37]

limited to a semi-continuous feed regime during daytime hours. The 3MPPB concept might [SOURCE: Page_37]

enable continuous day and night operation at higher OLR and higher volumetric conversions [SOURCE: Page_37]

but the microbial competition has to be determined. The overall resource recovery would [SOURCE: Page_37]

likely be reduced due to lower biomass yields under predominant chemoheterotrophic growth [SOURCE: Page_37]

(Tchobanoglous et al., 2003). The latter would depend on the volumetric conversion at night [SOURCE: Page_37]

relative to the conversion during the day. More research is required to assess this option and [SOURCE: Page_37]

35 [SOURCE: Page_37]

its effects on the microbial community. However, we showed that effluent polishing is [SOURCE: Page_38]

feasible. Nevertheless, this approach requires an aeration system and strict microbial [SOURCE: Page_38]

community control. The cost-benefit comparison has to be made to evaluate this concept. [SOURCE: Page_38]

One major option to enhance the reactor performance in strict anaerobic or in mixed [SOURCE: Page_38]

metabolic mode, is to provide biomass retention via membranes or cloth filtration, which has [SOURCE: Page_38]

the potential to increase the PPB biomass in the reactor and increase the volumetric and [SOURCE: Page_38]

areal removal efficiencies and productivities, while shortening the HRTs. This has to be [SOURCE: Page_38]

combined with continuous harvesting of biomass from the reactor to maintain a SRT of 2-4 d [SOURCE: Page_38]

(Hülsen et al., 2018b, 2016b). This will also impact the light supply, via increased shading [SOURCE: Page_38]

and light attenuation of the infrared light, which requires detailed analysis. The options of [SOURCE: Page_38]

attached biofilm or even the use of PPB granules (Stegman et al., 2021) are perceivable, but [SOURCE: Page_38]

require more research. [SOURCE: Page_38]

From this work, we can further outline some major differences of FPPBRs for algal and PPB [SOURCE: Page_38]

biomass production. For strictly anaerobic operation (photoheterotrophy), a PPB FPPBR [SOURCE: Page_38]

does not require a distribution system for CO$_2$ supply and O$_2$ stripping (no product inhibition). [SOURCE: Page_38]

A PPB FPPBR does likely not require cooling or heating (subject to local conditions) and the [SOURCE: Page_38]

impact of (aerobic) grazers can be neglected. Another argument is the doubling of the light [SOURCE: Page_38]

path (relative to a pond), due to two sided light supply, which should enable less shading, [SOURCE: Page_38]

higher biomass concentrations, higher volumetric removal and productivities. Biomass [SOURCE: Page_38]

productivity is further independent from CO$_2$ supply, as COD in a secondary treatment step is [SOURCE: Page_38]

the main driver for N and P removal. In combination with lower light requirements of PPB, [SOURCE: Page_38]

this might result in higher annual productivities, especially on VFA rich wastewaters (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_38]

after prefermentation). [SOURCE: Page_38]

Theoretically, PPB can be used to treat any wastewater with sufficient bioavailable COD, as [SOURCE: Page_38]

shown in several publications (Capson-Tojo et al., 2020). As presented here, this includes [SOURCE: Page_38]

wastewater with fats, proteins, or other (inert) solids, which, however, might affect the [SOURCE: Page_38]

biomass quality and its use as valuable product (e.g., SCP as feed). The here achieved [SOURCE: Page_38]

e.g., from food processing, which would likely result in improved performances. Finally, the [SOURCE: Page_39]

PPB biomass was tested as fish and prawn feed, where up to 66% and 100% of fishmeal in [SOURCE: Page_39]

commercial feed have been successfully substituted with PPB biomass (Delamare- [SOURCE: Page_39]

were also performed on various crops (data not shown). The product testing further enables [SOURCE: Page_39]

addition of a potential price tag on the biomass, which can be used for economic feasibility [SOURCE: Page_39]

studies, which will be key to evaluate the viability of this technology. [SOURCE: Page_39]

### 4. Conclusions

The PPB demonstration plant provided consistent performance (i.e., removal efficiencies of [SOURCE: Page_39]

VFAs, TKN, and TP of >90%, 34-77%, and 28-45%) and estimated (conservative) biomass [SOURCE: Page_39]

productivities of 6-24 g VS·m-2·d-1, under varying wastewater characteristics and [SOURCE: Page_39]

environmental conditions. The removal of N and P was limited by the availability of [SOURCE: Page_39]

biodegradable COD, a property of the feed wastewater. The harvested biomass had a VS/TS [SOURCE: Page_39]

ratio over 90%, a CP content of 58%, and a compatible amino acid profile. On average, the [SOURCE: Page_39]

FPPBR showed average biomass productivities of ~15 g VS·m-2·d-1 and SCOD removal rates [SOURCE: Page_39]

>500 mg·L-1·d-1, working at an HRT of 2.4 d and with semicontinuous/day-only feeding. [SOURCE: Page_39]

Under these conditions, the relative abundance of PPB was 0.56. During continuous (day- [SOURCE: Page_39]

night) feeding, VFA peaked during the night, particularly at low retention times. A minimum [SOURCE: Page_39]

overall HRT of 1.6-2.4 d is recommended to avoid PPB washout. Finally, a combined daily- [SOURCE: Page_39]

illuminated-anaerobic/night-aerobic operation (supplying air during night-time), called the [SOURCE: Page_39]

3MPPB concept, improved the removal performances, avoiding VFA accumulation (and a [SOURCE: Page_39]

concomitant pH drop) during the night. Nevertheless, this approach resulted in lower PPB [SOURCE: Page_39]

abundances and biomass productivities and would add extra infrastructure and operational [SOURCE: Page_39]

costs. [SOURCE: Page_39]
