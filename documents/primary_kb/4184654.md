15   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

16   The implementation of the new Water Reuse regulation in the European Union brings to the [SOURCE: Page_3]

17   forefront the need to evaluate the risks of using wastewater for crop irrigation. Here, a two- [SOURCE: Page_3]

18   tier ecotoxicological risk assessment was performed to evaluate the fate of wastewater-borne [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   micropollutants in soil and their ecotoxicological impact on plants and soil microorganisms. [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   To this end, two successive cultivation campaigns of lettuces were irrigated with wastewater [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   (at agronomical dose (not spiked) and spiked with a mixture of 14 pharmaceuticals at 10 and [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   100 µg/L each) in a controlled greenhouse experiment. Over the two cultivation campaigns, [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   an accumulation of PPCPs was observed in soil microcosms irrigated with wastewater spiked [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   with 100 μg/L of PPCPs with the highest concentrations detected for clarithromycin, [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   hydrochlorothiazide, citalopram, climbazole and carbamazepine. The abundance of bacterial [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   and fungal communities remained stable over the two cultivation campaigns and was not [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   affected by any of the irrigation regimes applied. Similarly, no changes were observed in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   abundance of ammonium oxidizing archaea (AOA) and bacteria (AOB), nor in clade A of [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   commamox no matter the cultivation campaign or the irrigation regime considered. Only a [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   slight increase was detected in clade B of commamox bacteria after the second cultivation [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   campaign. Sulfamethoxazole-resistant and -degrading bacteria were not impacted either. The [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   irrigation regimes had only a limited effect on the bacterial evenness. However, in response to [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   wastewater irrigation the structure of soil bacterial community significantly changed the [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   relative abundance of Acidobacteria, Chloroflexi, Verrucomicrobia, Beta-, Gamma- and [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   Deltaprotebacteria. Twenty-eight operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were identified as [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   responsible for the changes observed within the bacterial communities of soils irrigated with [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   wastewater or with water. Interestingly, the relative abundance of these OTUs was similar in [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   soils irrigated with either spiked or non-spiked irrigation solutions. This indicates that under [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   both agronomical and worst-case scenario the mixture of fourteen PPCPs had no effect on soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

40   bacterial community. [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   Water is a finite resource crucial for livestock and agricultural crop production. Agricultural [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   practices alone account for up to 70% of water withdrawals. The effects of global change and [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   water scarcity constitutes a major concern for the agricultural sector, especially in arid and [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   semiarid regions and countries with poor water management practices. Within this context, [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   the use of wastewater can overcome the shortage of freshwater resources for crop irrigation [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   (Garcia and Pargament, 2015; Petrie et al., 2015). Recently, the European Commission [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   launched the Water Reuse regulation that among others, defined the minimum quality [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   requirements of wastewater for crop irrigation (European Comission, 2020). Although the [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   environmental risks caused by water reuse in agriculture are on the top priority, [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   environmental fate of wastewater-borne biotic and chemical contaminants and their possible [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   ecotoxicological effects on soil living organisms and supported ecological functions are still [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   missing. [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   In spite of the important reduction of pollutants in the treatment plants, most of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   wastewater discharges remain contaminated with pharmaceutical active compounds (PhACs) [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   and personal care products (PPCPs). Irrigation of crops with wastewater therefore brings a [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   mixture of micropollutants to agricultural soils. In soils, the fate of these contaminants is [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   governed by a series of abiotic and biotic processes interacting together and varying according [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   to the physico-chemical properties of each pollutant and to the edaphoclimatic conditions [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   specific for each soil (Hiller and Šebesta, 2017). While sorption results in the stabilisation of [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   micropollutants in the soil where they persist and accumulate (Tolls, 2001; Xu et al., 2021), [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   abiotic and biotic transformations contribute to their dissipation (Grossberger et al., 2014; [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   Thiele-Bruhn, 2003; Thiele-Bruhn and Peters, 2007). Nevertheless, these transformation are [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   often partial and lead to transformation products that can be more toxic, persistent or [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   bioavailable than the parent compounds from which they originate (Celiz et al., 2009; Pérez [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   and Barceló, 2007). Micropollutants and their transformation products in the soil water can [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   further be transferred to connected water resources via leaching or runoff as well as to soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   living organisms including plants, macro-and mesofauna (Bigott et al., 2020; Carter et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   2020; Gallego et al., 2021). Uptake of residues of PhACs and PPCPs by plants can result in [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   the contamination of the food chain in a variety of ways with possible consequences on [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   human health (Aryal and Reinhold, 2011; Kalaji and Rastogi, 2017). [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   Microorganisms are able to degrade a wide range of organic micropollutants including PhACs [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   and PPCPs. In soils microbial degradation of the anti-inflammatory drugs naproxen (Topp et [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   al., 2008), ibuprofen (Girardi et al., 2013) and diclofenac (Facey et al., 2018), the analgesic [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   paracetamol (Li et al., 2014), the antiseptics triclosan and triclocarban (Al-Rajab et al., 2015), [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   and several antibiotics and antifungals (J. Chen et al., 2019; Sabourin et al., 2011) was [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   observed. Degrading microorganisms use PhACs and PPCPs as carbon source or energy to [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   fuel their growth (Moreira et al., 2014). In some cases, their degrading capacities are [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   improved in response to repeated exposure to micropollutants (Hirth et al., 2016). However, [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   other microorganisms can be affected in response to their exposure to these micropollutants. [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   Certain PhACs and PPCPs can be toxic to specific microbial guilds with great repercussion on [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   the accomplishment of key microbial functions with consequences on the numerous [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   ecosystem services supported by soil microorganisms (Cavicchioli et al., 2019; Delgado- [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   Baquerizo et al., 2020; Domeignoz-Horta et al., 2020; Wagg et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   Despite the important role of soil microorganisms for soil health, studies assessing the [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   ecotoxicological effects of PhACs on soil microorganisms are scarce (Barra Caracciolo et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   2015; Gallego and Martin-Laurent, 2020). In addition, most of them are focused on the [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   estimation of the effects of single active compounds on the abundance of bacteria and fungi [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   with classical Pasteurian approaches and on enzyme activities (Butler et al., 2011; Cycon et [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   al., 2016; Cycoń et al., 2019; Park et al., 2013; Waller and Kookana, 2009). Only a few [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   studies measured the ecotoxicological effects of PhACs applied alone to agricultural soils [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   using the latest -omic methodologies available (Gallego et al., 2021; Thelusmond et al., 2019, [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   2018, 2016) and to our best knowledge, no studies assess the effects of PhAC mixtures on soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   microorganisms. [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   Within this context, this study aims to investigate the impact of crop irrigation with [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   wastewater contaminated with a complex mixture of PPCPs on soil microorganisms. A two- [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   tier experiment to test both agronomical (treated wastewater) and worst-case (treated [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   wastewater spiked with a complex mixture of PPCPs (at 10 µg/L and 100 µg/L each)) [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   scenario of exposure was conducted in a greenhouse under controlled conditions over two [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   successive culture cycle of lettuces. The fate of the 14 different pollutants and the presence of [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   transformation products spiked to treated wastewater in soil was determined by liquid [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS). The ecotoxicological [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   effects of the irrigation with treated wastewater spiked or not with a mixture of PPCPs on soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   microorganisms were evaluated using an array of approaches including the estimation of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   abundances of the total fungal and bacterial communities, component of specific microbial [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   guilds involved in the ammonium oxidation and in the antibiotic resistance and degradation, [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   as well as the bacterial community composition and diversity assessed by 16S rRNA [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   amplicons sequencing. [SOURCE: Page_6]

462   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_21]

463   The advantages of treated wastewater reuse in agriculture have been widely reported (Meli et [SOURCE: Page_21]

464   al., 2002; Mohammad Rusan et al., 2007). Nutrients brought by wastewater effluents can [SOURCE: Page_21]

465   reduce the need for supplementary mineral compounds (such as N, P, and K) and increase the [SOURCE: Page_21]

466   concentration of some elements (Ca, B, Fe, Cu, Zn, Mn and Mb), essential for the growth and [SOURCE: Page_21]

467   development of crops (Gatta et al., 2016; Urbano et al., 2017; Vivaldi et al., 2019). In our [SOURCE: Page_21]

468   study, the fresh weight of lettuces irrigated with treated domestic wastewater (spiked with [SOURCE: Page_21]

469   PPCPs or not) was significantly higher than that of lettuces irrigated with clean water (spiked [SOURCE: Page_21]

470   with PPCPs or not) at the end of the second campaign. The high amounts of N-NH$_4$+ (29 [SOURCE: Page_21]

471   mg/L) and phosphorous (8 mg/L) supplied by the wastewater could explain the observed [SOURCE: Page_21]

472   difference. [SOURCE: Page_21]

473   Although the supply of mineral nutrients brought by wastewater is valuable to crops, [SOURCE: Page_21]

474   continuous and/or repeated irrigation of crops with wastewater may lead to the accumulation [SOURCE: Page_21]

475   of wastewater-borne micropollutants into the soil. Numerous studies have addressed this [SOURCE: Page_21]

476   environmental key question by monitoring the fate of micro-pollutants in crops irrigated with [SOURCE: Page_21]

477   wastewater. However only a very few have assessed the ecotoxicological effects of PhACs on [SOURCE: Page_21]

478   soil-living organisms, such as microorganisms (Barra Caracciolo et al., 2015; Gallego and [SOURCE: Page_21]

479   Martin-Laurent, 2020). These are generally experiments performed under controlled or [SOURCE: Page_21]

480   laboratory conditions within a limited period of time, considering the effect of contaminants 20 [SOURCE: Page_21]

481   one by one and only a few of them report the fate and the effect of complex mixtures of [SOURCE: Page_22]

482   contaminants in the natural environment or field conditions (Manasfi et al., 2020). In an [SOURCE: Page_22]

483   attempt to evaluate under a worst-case scenario, the fate and ecotoxicological effects of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

484   most relevant wastewater-borne organic contaminants in soil, the accumulation of fourteen [SOURCE: Page_22]

485   chemicals and major transformation products was assessed over two successive lettuce [SOURCE: Page_22]

486   campaigns planted on the same soil in a controlled greenhouse experiment. Water and treated [SOURCE: Page_22]

487   wastewater spiked with a mixture of PPCPs (at 100 µg/L for each compound) were used to [SOURCE: Page_22]

488   mimic the worst-case scenario defined by repeated irrigation of crops with wastewater. [SOURCE: Page_22]

489   Although wastewater generally results in soil alkalinisation, the soil pH remained stable [SOURCE: Page_22]

490   during the two campaigns of productions no matter the solution used for watering of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

491   lettuces, (data not shown). This is worth noticing since changes in soil pH have been reported [SOURCE: Page_22]

492   to modify the sorption of several pharmaceuticals active compounds, their bioavailability and [SOURCE: Page_22]

493   consequently their ultimate distribution into the different environmental compartments [SOURCE: Page_22]

494   (Brienza et al., 2020; Hiller and Šebesta, 2017; Y. L. Zhang et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_22]

495   Residues of all 14 spiked compounds were detected in soil samples collected after the first [SOURCE: Page_22]

496   and/or    second      cultivation   campaign.        The     antibiotic   clarithromycin,      diuretic [SOURCE: Page_22]

497   hydrochlorothiazide, antidepressant citalopram, antifungal climbazole and antiepileptic [SOURCE: Page_22]

498   carbamazepine were detected at the highest concentrations ranging from few ng up to several [SOURCE: Page_22]

499   hundred ng per g of soil. These observations are in line with Manasfi et al. (2020), who [SOURCE: Page_22]

500   similarly found the highest concentration for these compounds in a parallel study conducted [SOURCE: Page_22]

501   under realistic agronomical conditions using the same soil and treated wastewater fortified at [SOURCE: Page_22]

502   10 µg/L over five cultivation campaigns. Their relatively high Kd values (Klement et al., [SOURCE: Page_22]

503   2018; Manasfi et al., 2020; Sibley and Pedersen, 2008) and recalcitrance (Chen et al., 2013; [SOURCE: Page_22]

504   Li et al., 2013; Styrishave et al., 2011) could account for the observed results. The occurrence [SOURCE: Page_22]

505   of   clarithromycin     (Corada-Fernández       et    al.,   2015;    Dalkmann     et   al.,    2012), [SOURCE: Page_22]

21 [SOURCE: Page_22]

506   hydrochlorothiazide and carbamazepine (Biel-Maeso et al., 2018; Corada-Fernández et al., [SOURCE: Page_23]

507   2015; Dalkmann et al., 2012; Gibson et al., 2010; Grossberger et al., 2014; Kinney et al., [SOURCE: Page_23]

508   2006) has been previously documented in soils irrigated with wastewater. However, contrary [SOURCE: Page_23]

509   to our findings, clarithromycin was detected at very low concentrations (<3ng/g), probably as [SOURCE: Page_23]

510   consequence of its enhanced microbial dissipation after years of exposure as reported by Topp [SOURCE: Page_23]

511   et al., 2016. Interestingly, carbamazepine epoxide, a degradation intermediate of [SOURCE: Page_23]

512   carbamazepine was also detected at few ng per g of soil, which evidences the presence of [SOURCE: Page_23]

513   carbamazepine degrading microorganisms in the soil (Golan-Rozen et al., 2015; Kittelmann et [SOURCE: Page_23]

514   al., 1993; Li et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_23]

515   A few ng per g of soil of irbesartan, ciprofloxacin and benzotriazole were detected, which is [SOURCE: Page_23]

516   consistent with observations made by Manasfi et al (2020). The angiotensin receptor blocker [SOURCE: Page_23]

517   irbesartan, poorly removed in wastewater treatment plant (Bayer et al., 2014), showed a [SOURCE: Page_23]

518   relatively low sorption to soil (Klement et al., 2018) but is known to be degraded by various [SOURCE: Page_23]

519   soil microorganisms (Frková et al., 2020), which could explain the low concentrations found [SOURCE: Page_23]

520   in our study. The antibiotic ciprofloxacin and the corrosion inhibitor benzotriazole are hardly [SOURCE: Page_23]

521   biodegradable (Girardi et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2011; Walters et al., 2010) but they are known [SOURCE: Page_23]

522   to photodegrade under UV irradiation and simulated solar radiation respectively (Batchu et [SOURCE: Page_23]

523   al., 2014; Hem et al., 2003; Serdechnova et al., 2014), and to be assimilated by plants (Castro [SOURCE: Page_23]

524   et al., 2003; Eggen et al., 2011; LeFevre et al., 2015; Riemenschneider et al., 2016; Sabourin [SOURCE: Page_23]

525   et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2018), which might explain why they were detected in our [SOURCE: Page_23]

526   experiment at low concentrations. The food additive sucralose was also detected at few ng per [SOURCE: Page_23]

527   g of soil in microcosms irrigated with both spiked water and wastewater, presumably due to [SOURCE: Page_23]

528   its limited biodegradation (Biel-Maeso et al., 2019; Buerge et al., 2011). However, it was [SOURCE: Page_23]

529   hardly detected after the first cultivation campaign, probably because of its high water [SOURCE: Page_23]

530   solubility, lack of sorption to soil (Kd <10) or even plant uptake (Manasfi et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_23]

22 [SOURCE: Page_23]

531   Concentrations lower than 1 ng per g of soil were detected for the artificial sweetener [SOURCE: Page_24]

532   acesulfame, the antibiotic sulfamethoxazole and the anti-inflammatory diclofenac. This [SOURCE: Page_24]

533   observation is in agreement with their high mobility (Belton et al., 2020; Drillia et al., 2005; [SOURCE: Page_24]

534   Lin and Gan, 2011; Storck et al., 2016) and fast microbial degradation in soils (Al-Rajab et [SOURCE: Page_24]

535   al., 2010; Buerge et al., 2011; Reis et al., 2014). Additionally, recent studies have shown that [SOURCE: Page_24]

536   they can be transferred from soil to plants (Bartha et al., 2014; González García et al., 2018; [SOURCE: Page_24]

537   Kodešová et al., 2019; Manasfi et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2017). Diclofenac was only detected [SOURCE: Page_24]

538   in soil microcosms irrigated with spiked wastewater. Given that 4’-hydroxydiclofenac, a [SOURCE: Page_24]

539   biotransformation product from diclofenac (Prior et al., 2010) was never detected, we could [SOURCE: Page_24]

540   hypothesize that the degradation of the diclofenac molecule was complete. Interestingly, [SOURCE: Page_24]

541   mineralization kinetics of 14C-sulfamethoxazole recorded in soil samples collected at the end [SOURCE: Page_24]

542   of each campaign of production showed that less than 10% of 14C-sulfamethoxazole initially [SOURCE: Page_24]

543   applied was mineralized by an indigenous soil microbial community and that more than half [SOURCE: Page_24]

544   of the radioactivity was recovered as NER. Additionally, 4-nitro-sulfamethoxazole, a photo- [SOURCE: Page_24]

545   transformation product of sulfamethoxazole (Su et al., 2016) was only detected at BLOQ [SOURCE: Page_24]

546   concentrations, which led us to conclude that contrary to previous studies this compound was [SOURCE: Page_24]

547   hardly transformed and remained adsorbed to soil particles. [SOURCE: Page_24]

548   The anti-hypertensive metoprolol was also found at very low concentrations. Manasfi et al. [SOURCE: Page_24]

549   (2020) using chiral analysis showed that enantiomer profiles did not changed during the time [SOURCE: Page_24]

550   course of the five lettuce growing campaigns as compared to the standard, suggesting that [SOURCE: Page_24]

551   microbial biodegradation did not occur. Given its moderate photostability (Neamţu et al., [SOURCE: Page_24]

552   2014) and its variable sorption behavior (Kodešová et al., 2015), further studies are needed to [SOURCE: Page_24]

553   elucidate the processes governing its dissipation in soil. [SOURCE: Page_24]

554   While Manasfi et al. (2020) detected the antihypertensive valsartan at concentrations around 2 [SOURCE: Page_24]

555   ng per g of soil, in our study the antihypertensive valsartan was always detected in soil [SOURCE: Page_24]

23 [SOURCE: Page_24]

556   microcosms at BLOQ concentrations no matter the production campaign considered. [SOURCE: Page_25]

557   Valsartan acid, its major transformation product was detected at few ng per g of soil in [SOURCE: Page_25]

558   microcosms irrigated with spiked treated wastewater only after the second cultivation [SOURCE: Page_25]

559   campaign. This is in accordance with Gallego et al. (2021) suggesting that valsartan [SOURCE: Page_25]

560   dissipation is accompanied by the formation of valsartan acid. [SOURCE: Page_25]

561   As a general trend, a significant carryover of contaminants was observed over the two [SOURCE: Page_25]

562   cultivation campaigns. The accumulation and persistence of PhACs in soils has been [SOURCE: Page_25]

563   previously reported (Chen et al., 2013; Dalkmann et al., 2012; Williams and McLain, 2012) [SOURCE: Page_25]

564   because of the associated human and environmental risks (Aryal and Reinhold, 2011; Fatta- [SOURCE: Page_25]

565   Kassinos et al., 2011; Pérez et al., 2020). Similar concentrations of spiked PCPPs and [SOURCE: Page_25]

566   metabolites were observed in soil microcosms irrigated with spiked wastewater or water [SOURCE: Page_25]

567   irrigated. For climbazole, carbamazepine and irbesartan significantly higher values were [SOURCE: Page_25]

568   observed in spiked wastewater-irrigated than in spiked water-irrigated microcosms. This [SOURCE: Page_25]

569   difference might be explained by the interaction of these compounds with different [SOURCE: Page_25]

570   components brought by wastewater influencing their fate in soil (Goldstein et al., 2018; [SOURCE: Page_25]

571   Katsoyiannis and Samara, 2007; Müller et al., 2007; Nason et al., 2019). It might also be [SOURCE: Page_25]

572   explained by an inhibitory or toxic effect of other PPCPs brought by wastewater, in addition [SOURCE: Page_25]

573   to the 14 micropollutants spiked, on specific microbial guilds involved in their degradation, [SOURCE: Page_25]

574   which might hinder their elimination and increase their persistence in soil. This hypothesis is [SOURCE: Page_25]

575   supported by the fact that several antibiotics (Cycoń et al., 2019), the non-steroidal anti- [SOURCE: Page_25]

576   inflammatory drugs naproxen and diclofenac (Cycon et al., 2016) and the antiseptic triclosan [SOURCE: Page_25]

577   (Butler et al., 2011; Waller and Kookana, 2009) have previously been shown to induce [SOURCE: Page_25]

578   changes in the abundance and enzymatic activities of soil microorganisms. [SOURCE: Page_25]

579   The ecotoxicological effects of wastewater irrigation on the abundance and the composition [SOURCE: Page_25]

580   of soil microbial communities were assessed. The abundance of bacterial and fungal [SOURCE: Page_25]

24 [SOURCE: Page_25]

581   communities remained stable no-matter the irrigation regime and cultivation campaign [SOURCE: Page_26]

582   considered. This trend was also observed for the abundance of microbial guilds (AOA, AOB [SOURCE: Page_26]

583   and clade A commamox) involved in ammonia oxidation processes. Only the abundance of [SOURCE: Page_26]

584   the clade B commamox bacteria was slightly increased in water and 10 μg/L spiked water [SOURCE: Page_26]

585   irrigated microcosms after the second cultivation campaign. Altogether one can conclude that [SOURCE: Page_26]

586   none of the soil microbial communities targeted in our study were affected in their abundance [SOURCE: Page_26]

587   in response to the nutrients and PhACs brought by spiked wastewater or to the PhACs [SOURCE: Page_26]

588   brought by spiked water used to irrigate the microcosms planted with lettuces. These [SOURCE: Page_26]

589   observations are in agreement with previous studies showing that below 5 µg per g various [SOURCE: Page_26]

590   pharmaceutical and pollutant applied had no effect on the abundance and activities of N- [SOURCE: Page_26]

591   cycling microbial communities (Crouzet et al., 2016; Rosendahl et al., 2012; Wang and [SOURCE: Page_26]

592   Gunsch, 2011). Given these results, it could be hypothesized that none of the irrigation [SOURCE: Page_26]

593   regimes had an effect on the total respiration and nitrification processes. To further test this [SOURCE: Page_26]

594   hypothesis normalized OECD and ISO tests (such as luminescent bacteria tests ISO 11348- [SOURCE: Page_26]

595   3:2007, respiration and nitrification inhibition tests OECD 209 and ISO 9509:2006, [SOURCE: Page_26]

596   respectively) might be applied. [SOURCE: Page_26]

597   Similarly, the abundance of sulfamethoxazole-resistant bacteria remained stable no matter the [SOURCE: Page_26]

598   irrigation regime, probably because this antibiotic was dissipated in soil. Only 9% of 14C- [SOURCE: Page_26]

599   sulfamethoxazole was mineralized over the incubation period, which is consistent with the [SOURCE: Page_26]

600   fact that sadA carrying microorganisms involved in the biodegradation of sulfamethoxazole [SOURCE: Page_26]

601   were not detected. These results indicate that contrary to previous studies (Topp et al., 2016, [SOURCE: Page_26]

602   2013), an enhanced biodegradation of sulfamethoxazole did not occur despite the repeated [SOURCE: Page_26]

603   exposure to this antibiotic. The simultaneous application of a complex mixture of PhACs [SOURCE: Page_26]

604   might have caused a detrimental effect on both sulfamethoxazole-resistant bacteria and [SOURCE: Page_26]

25 [SOURCE: Page_26]

605   sulfamethoxazole-degrading microorganisms, hindering their growth in response to repeated [SOURCE: Page_27]

606   exposure to sulfamethoxazole. [SOURCE: Page_27]

607   α- diversity analyses showed that none of the irrigation regimes had a significant effect on the [SOURCE: Page_27]

608   richness and phylogenetic diversity of the bacterial community. Only a limited effect for soils [SOURCE: Page_27]

609   irrigated with 100 µg/L spiked wastewater on the evenness of the bacterial community was [SOURCE: Page_27]

610   observed. Our findings are in line with other studies (Broszat et al., 2014; Frenk et al., 2014; [SOURCE: Page_27]

611   Ibekwe et al., 2018) but partly in accordance with previous studies performed in agricultural [SOURCE: Page_27]

612   soils receiving swine and dairy manures (Z. Chen et al., 2019) or soil microcosms treated with [SOURCE: Page_27]

613   valsartan, carbamazepine, or tetracycline (Gallego et al., 2021; Thelusmond et al., 2016; [SOURCE: Page_27]

614   Zheng et al., 2020). This discrepancy might be explained by the high doses applied in those [SOURCE: Page_27]

615   studies and shorter incubation times which combined led to the observation of a marked [SOURCE: Page_27]

616   ecotoxicological impact on both diversity and composition of soil bacterial communities. [SOURCE: Page_27]

617   However, the irrigation with wastewater significantly modified the structure of soil bacterial [SOURCE: Page_27]

618   communities, which resulted in changes in its composition. In-depth analysis of the OTUs [SOURCE: Page_27]

619   responsible for the significant differences found between the six irrigation regimes led to the [SOURCE: Page_27]

620   identification of 28 OTUs affiliated to Betaproteobacteria (21), Deltaproteobacteria (2), [SOURCE: Page_27]

621   Chloroflexi (1), Gammaproteobacteria (1), Actinobacteria (1), Cytophagia (1) and [SOURCE: Page_27]

622   Alphaproteobacteria (1). With the exception of two OTUs belonging to Betaproteobacteria [SOURCE: Page_27]

623   and one OTU closely related to Herpetosiphonales, the relative abundance of the rest of them [SOURCE: Page_27]

624   significantly increased in the presence of wastewater, suggesting that those OTUs may benefit [SOURCE: Page_27]

625   from it by using it as carbon source or energy for their growth. Interestingly, similar OTU [SOURCE: Page_27]

626   profiles were found in soils irrigated with either spiked or non-spiked irrigation regimes [SOURCE: Page_27]

627   indicating that in our experimental conditions, the PPCPs had no effect on soil bacterial [SOURCE: Page_27]

628   community. [SOURCE: Page_27]

629   5. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_27]

26 [SOURCE: Page_27]

630   Lab-to-field tiered exposure scenarios are recommended to accurately assess the fate and the [SOURCE: Page_28]

631   assessment of environmental risks for in soil living organisms within the pesticide [SOURCE: Page_28]

632   authorization processes of the active ingredient. This approach was applied in this study to [SOURCE: Page_28]

633   assess the fate and ecotoxicological effects of a mixture of PPCPs frequently found in treated [SOURCE: Page_28]

634   wastewater. This is the first report on the evaluation of the ecotoxicological effects on soil [SOURCE: Page_28]

635   microorganisms of a complex mixture of PPCPs brought by wastewater used to irrigate [SOURCE: Page_28]

636   lettuces. Our results showed that under the worst-case scenario of exposure (irrigation with [SOURCE: Page_28]

637   water or wastewater spiked with a mixture of 14 PPCPs at 100 μg/L each) several PPCPs [SOURCE: Page_28]

638   accumulated in lettuce planted soil. Irrigation of lettuces with wastewater (spiked or not) had [SOURCE: Page_28]

639   limited or no effects on the abundance, diversity and functions of soil microbial populations, [SOURCE: Page_28]

640   but induced significant changes in the structure of the soil bacterial community. PPCPs [SOURCE: Page_28]

641   accumulated in the lettuce planted soil had no effect on soil microorganisms. However, they [SOURCE: Page_28]

642   may transfer from the soil to the plant and enter the food chain. [SOURCE: Page_28]
