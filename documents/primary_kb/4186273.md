32   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_5]

33 [SOURCE: Page_5]

34   Plants are the primary producers in most terrestrial ecosystems, where they embody [SOURCE: Page_5]

35   the basis of the food webs, insects being a dominant group among plant consumers. [SOURCE: Page_5]

36   Thus, plant-insect interactions have long been the central subject for understanding [SOURCE: Page_5]

37   insect and plant community structure and dynamics (Bruce 2015). Herbivorous insects [SOURCE: Page_5]

38   rely on the consumption of plant materials to obtain water and nutrients for their [SOURCE: Page_5]

39   survival and development, but in the meantime they have to cope with plant chemical [SOURCE: Page_5]

40   and physical defenses (Schoonhoven et al. 2005). Furthermore, changing abiotic [SOURCE: Page_5]

41   conditions alter the plant nutritional and defensive profiles, which may trigger cascade [SOURCE: Page_5]

42   effects on organisms from higher trophic levels, including herbivorous insects and [SOURCE: Page_5]

43   their natural enemies: the so-called “bottom-up” effects (Hunter and Price 1992; [SOURCE: Page_5]

44   Denno et al. 2002). Over the last decade, recent knowledge has been gained on this [SOURCE: Page_5]

45   subject, indicating a potentially high application value of such effects for managing [SOURCE: Page_5]

46   insect pests in agro-ecosystems (Chen et al. 2010; Han et al. 2014; Becker et al. 2015; [SOURCE: Page_5]

47   Han et al. 2015; Zhong et al. 2017). Invasive alien insect pests are often armed with [SOURCE: Page_5]

48   competitive biological traits such as a high growth rate and reproduction potential that [SOURCE: Page_5]

49   makes them especially difficult to manage (Hufbauer and Torchin 2007). We believe [SOURCE: Page_5]

50   that bottom-up effects are an essential and effective component of successful [SOURCE: Page_5]

51   Integrated Pest Management (IPM) programs against invasive pests. [SOURCE: Page_5]

52   The South American tomato pinworm, Tuta absoluta (Meyrick, 1917) (Lepidoptera: [SOURCE: Page_5]

53   Gelechiidae), originated from South America, is a devastating pest on tomato and [SOURCE: Page_5]

54   other Solanaceous plants (Desneux et al. 2010). Since its first detection outside its [SOURCE: Page_6]

55   native areas, in Spain in 2006, T. absoluta has rapidly spread across Europe, Africa, [SOURCE: Page_6]

56   middle-East and Central Asia in the following years, considerably threatening the [SOURCE: Page_6]

57   tomato industry worldwide (Desneux et al. 2011; Campos et al. 2017; Xian et al. [SOURCE: Page_6]

58   2017). Many attempts have been made to combat this pest. Chemical control was the [SOURCE: Page_6]

59   first option. It proved efficient, but high resistance to various insecticide classes in this [SOURCE: Page_6]

60   moth was recently emerged (Campos et al. 2014, 2015; Silva et al. 2016; Roditakis et [SOURCE: Page_6]

61   al. 2017; 2018). The methods preventing pest mating, including male annihilation [SOURCE: Page_6]

62   and/or mating disruption by using synthetic pheromones, have been shown effective [SOURCE: Page_6]

63   (Vacas et al. 2011; Cocco et al. 2013), but the emerging evidence of deuterotokous [SOURCE: Page_6]

64   parthenogenesis in T. absoluta might compromise their efficacy (Caparros Megido et [SOURCE: Page_6]

65   al. 2012). Biological control has been implemented widely using indigenous natural [SOURCE: Page_6]

66   enemies (Zappala et al. 2013) and is currently the most promising component of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

67   IPM package. However, the efficacy of natural enemies in suppressing T. absoluta [SOURCE: Page_6]

68   populations may be altered by environmental abiotic factors through bottom-up [SOURCE: Page_6]

69   effects triggered by agronomic practices such as irrigation and fertilization. Moreover, [SOURCE: Page_6]

70   plant constitutive and/or induced resistance traits against T. absoluta are another [SOURCE: Page_6]

71   source of bottom-up effects, which may interact with irrigation and fertilization and [SOURCE: Page_6]

72   jointly affect the performance of T. absoluta, counterpart natural enemies and their [SOURCE: Page_6]

73   interactions. [SOURCE: Page_6]

74   In recent years, there has been a growing body of literatures reporting on how bottom- [SOURCE: Page_6]

75   up effects take place and how the association of diverse fields - plant ecophysiology - [SOURCE: Page_6]

76   chemical ecology and behavioral ecology- may help to understand the underlying [SOURCE: Page_7]

77   mechanisms (Chen et al. 2010; Han et al. 2014, 2015, 2016; Becker et al. 2015; [SOURCE: Page_7]

78   Larbat et al. 2016; Coqueret et al. 2017; Dong et al. 2017). The knowledge gained [SOURCE: Page_7]

79   from those studies is a good starting point and should encourage future research [SOURCE: Page_7]

80   efforts. Therefore, we provide a timely review on the empirical studies addressing [SOURCE: Page_7]

81   bottom-up effects of fertilization, irrigation and plant resistant traits on T. absoluta. [SOURCE: Page_7]

82   The implications of bottom-up effects for the integrated pest management and future [SOURCE: Page_7]

83   research directions are discussed. [SOURCE: Page_7]

87   Irrigation 88 [SOURCE: Page_7]

89   Water, in terms of quantity as well as quality, is an important abiotic factor able to [SOURCE: Page_7]

90   influence the interactions between plants and herbivorous insects. In the global [SOURCE: Page_7]

91   change context, water consumption by agricultural systems has to be optimized. [SOURCE: Page_7]

92   Especially dry regions like the Mediterranean Basin face the challenge of reducing the [SOURCE: Page_7]

93   quantity of water used for food production while, at the same time, maintaining [SOURCE: Page_7]

94   economically sustainable yields. Salinity is another major environmental issue and a [SOURCE: Page_7]

95   substantial constraint to plant growth (Debouba et al 2006). Sodium chloride (NaCl) is [SOURCE: Page_7]

96   one of the most common components in soil or irrigation water, causing salinity stress [SOURCE: Page_7]

97   in plants (Mahajan & Tuteja 2005). Unlike in natural ecosystems such as salt marshes [SOURCE: Page_7]

98   where many species have evolved to tolerate high salinity (Parida & Das 2005), crops [SOURCE: Page_7]

99   and particularly tomatoes    are not very tolerant to salinity (Greenway & Munns [SOURCE: Page_8]

100   1980). They embody the important greenhouse crops in many semi-arid regions where [SOURCE: Page_8]

101   salinity in soil and groundwater constitutes a major issue in crop production (Cuartero [SOURCE: Page_8]

102   & Fernandez-Munoz, 1999). It has been shown that the quantity and the quality of [SOURCE: Page_8]

103   water inputs are able to impact the second trophic level, including the herbivorous T. [SOURCE: Page_8]

104   absoluta, through plant-mediated effects as well as the third trophic level, including T. [SOURCE: Page_8]

105   absoluta natural enemies (Han et al 2014, 2015b, 2016a). [SOURCE: Page_8]

106 [SOURCE: Page_8]

107   Leaf miners develop very intimate relationships with their host plants since the larvae [SOURCE: Page_8]

108   penetrate and feed within the tissues and are therefore considered highly relevant to [SOURCE: Page_8]

109   test plant-mediated bottom-up effect of water inputs (Inbar 2001). Recent studies have [SOURCE: Page_8]

110   demonstrated that water input variations can significantly affect T. absoluta biological [SOURCE: Page_8]

111   traits (Han et al 2014, 2016a): water limitation reduced the survival as well as the [SOURCE: Page_8]

112   pupal weight and slowed down the larval development (Han et al 2014, 2016a) (See [SOURCE: Page_8]

113   Figure 1). This supports the “plant vigor hypothesis” (Price 1991), since T. absoluta [SOURCE: Page_8]

114   survived better and developed faster on tomato plants with a better growth status (Han [SOURCE: Page_8]

115   et al 2014). Yet, the net effect of water-limitation on the host plant and thus the [SOURCE: Page_8]

116   herbivore strongly depends on the frequency and severity of water limitation (Huberty [SOURCE: Page_8]

117   & Denno, 2004). [SOURCE: Page_8]

118   Which physiological changes in the host plants could explain the effects on [SOURCE: Page_8]

119   T. absoluta biological traits? Varying water availability may change plant nutritional [SOURCE: Page_8]

120   quality and chemical defenses both of which can affect the performance of insect [SOURCE: Page_8]

121   herbivores (Gutbrodt et al 2011, Tariq et al 2012) (Fig 1). (i) Plant water content is [SOURCE: Page_9]

122   generally a useful index of its nutritional value for many lepidopterous larvae [SOURCE: Page_9]

123   (Schoonhoven 2005). Han et al (2014) suggested that T. absoluta larvae’s difficulties [SOURCE: Page_9]

124   to obtain enough water explain their decelerated development. Water not only acts as [SOURCE: Page_9]

125   an indispensable prerequisite for herbivore metabolism, facilitating efficient nutrient [SOURCE: Page_9]

126   utilization (Scriber, 1977),   its limitation furthermore decreases the nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_9]

127   availability for herbivores by decreasing plant nitrogen and protein contents [SOURCE: Page_9]

128   (Ximénez-Embún et al., 2016). Accordingly, there are reports showing that water- [SOURCE: Page_9]

129   limited and/or wilted foliage is more difficult to digest (Slansky & Scriber 1985, [SOURCE: Page_9]

130   Gutbrodt 2011) than turgid foliage. (ii) Water limitation can alter the regular level of [SOURCE: Page_9]

131   plant chemical defense, leading to either enhanced or reduced resistance to herbivores [SOURCE: Page_9]

132   (Inbar 2001, Gutbrodt 2011). Han et al (2016a) observed that the concentration of leaf [SOURCE: Page_9]

133   glycoalkaloids increased in tomato plants subjected to water limitation, accompanied [SOURCE: Page_9]

134   by negative indirect effects on T. absoluta survival and development. So far, however, [SOURCE: Page_9]

135   the toxicity of these compounds is not known for T. absoluta. [SOURCE: Page_9]

136 [SOURCE: Page_9]

137   Salinity can also trigger bottom-up effects on T. absoluta (Han et al 2016b): adding [SOURCE: Page_9]

138   salt to the plant’s nutrient solution shortened the pest development time but did not [SOURCE: Page_9]

139   reduce the pupal mass although insects have the faculty to pupate with a lower body [SOURCE: Page_9]

140   mass under adverse conditions like reduced host plant quality. At least three different [SOURCE: Page_9]

141   mechanisms could explain that salinity has deleterious effects on herbivorous insects: [SOURCE: Page_9]

142   (i) Increasing salt concentration in the irrigation water raises the osmotic potential [SOURCE: Page_9]

143   and, thus, decreases plant water availability (Soria & Cuartero 1997, Romero-Aranda [SOURCE: Page_10]

144   & Cuartero 2001). Similar to drought, salinity stress can thus affect T. absoluta [SOURCE: Page_10]

145   development by reducing larva water availability ; (ii) salinity stress can also lead to [SOURCE: Page_10]

146   excessive accumulation of Na+ and Cl- ions in leaves (Manaa et al 2011), which [SOURCE: Page_10]

147   lowers leaf dietary quality for T. absoluta larvae ; (iii) furthermore, elevated soil [SOURCE: Page_10]

148   salinity may induce changes in plant secondary metabolism, i.e. plant chemical [SOURCE: Page_10]

149   defense (Ballhorn & Elias, 2014). Although Han et al (2016b) did not observe any [SOURCE: Page_10]

150   correlations between the concentration of leaf glycoalkaloids and the development of [SOURCE: Page_10]

151   T. absoluta, other defense compounds in tomato leaves may increase in response to [SOURCE: Page_10]

152   high salinity: phenolic compounds, for instance, have been observed to accumulate in [SOURCE: Page_10]

153   tomato fruits (Flores et al 2015) and might respond similarly in leaves. The possible [SOURCE: Page_10]

154   negative effects of these three mechanisms on insect development may, to some [SOURCE: Page_10]

155   degree, be counterbalanced by higher concentrations of amino acids due to higher [SOURCE: Page_10]

156   proteolytic activities in salt-stressed tomato leaves (Debouba et al 2016). This may [SOURCE: Page_10]

157   explain why T. absoluta managed to reach a normal pupal mass after a reduced larval [SOURCE: Page_10]

158   development time on NaCl stressed plants (Han et al 2016b). It is assumed that larvae [SOURCE: Page_10]

159   have the potential to adapt their feeding strategy, spending less time for larval [SOURCE: Page_10]

160   development to cope with salinity stress. [SOURCE: Page_10]

162   Fertilization [SOURCE: Page_10]

163 [SOURCE: Page_10]

164   Fertilization is required to preserve soil fertility of cultivated fields and to maintain [SOURCE: Page_10]

165   crop productivity. Nitrogen is one of the key macro-elements that impacts plant [SOURCE: Page_11]

166   quality, both in terms of nutritional value and defensive profile and thus triggers [SOURCE: Page_11]

167   bottom-up effects on herbivore insect performances. Many studies have shown that T. [SOURCE: Page_11]

168   absoluta larvae feeding on tomato plants grown with low nitrogen inputs, had reduced [SOURCE: Page_11]

169   survival rates and delayed development (Han et al. 2014; Han et al. 2016; Larbat et al. [SOURCE: Page_11]

170   2016; Coqueret et al. 2017; Dong et al. 2017; Blazhevski et al. 2018). This has been [SOURCE: Page_11]

171   ascribed to the low protein content (higher foliar C/N ratio as well) and enhanced [SOURCE: Page_11]

172   accumulation of constitutive phenolics and glyco-alkaloids in the leaves (Han et al. [SOURCE: Page_11]

173   2016; Larbat et al. 2016). Such alterations in plant chemical profile are consistent with [SOURCE: Page_11]

174   previous studies (Inbar et al. 2001; Le Bot et al. 2009; Royer et al. 2013). All these [SOURCE: Page_11]

175   findings support the nitrogen limitation hypothesis (White 1993) (See figure 1) [SOURCE: Page_11]

176   especially in the case of Lepidopteran insects. [SOURCE: Page_11]

177   Potassium is another macro nutrient able to mediate bottom-up effects on T. absoluta. [SOURCE: Page_11]

178   Sung et al (2015) found that the metabolite profile of K-deficient tomato leaves was [SOURCE: Page_11]

179   characterized by an increased concentration of soluble sugars and a general decrease [SOURCE: Page_11]

180   in most amino acids, except for arginine, glutamine, proline and citrulline. Whether or [SOURCE: Page_11]

181   not K-limitation affects tomato defense compounds is not well studied: to our [SOURCE: Page_11]

182   knowledge, there is only one publication so far, investigating the effecton fruit [SOURCE: Page_11]

183   composition (Flores et al 2015). The authors found that a low K-level caused variable [SOURCE: Page_11]

184   but strong cultivar-dependent effects, increasing chlorogenic acid concentration while [SOURCE: Page_11]

185   decreasing or unaffecting that of rutin and other phenolic compounds in one of two [SOURCE: Page_11]

186   studied cultivars. In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana potassium-limitation led to [SOURCE: Page_11]

187   the accumulation of plant defense compounds like glucosinolates and an overall [SOURCE: Page_12]

188   increased defense against leaf chewers by interfering with the jasmonic acid-mediated [SOURCE: Page_12]

189   signaling pathway (Amtmann et al 2008). The consequently possible bottom-up [SOURCE: Page_12]

190   effects on T. absoluta have received very little attention so far. Leite et al (2004) [SOURCE: Page_12]

191   found no effect of tomato leaf K content on T. absoluta preference or performance, [SOURCE: Page_12]

192   measured as oviposition events and number of mines. Similarly, Blazhevski et al [SOURCE: Page_12]

193   (2018) detected almost no effect of reduced tomato leaf K on T. absoluta life history [SOURCE: Page_12]

194   traits. The only significant effects were that reduced K retarded larval development [SOURCE: Page_12]

195   and slightly decreased the intrinsic rate of population development. Yet, the [SOURCE: Page_12]

196   mechanisms are unclear. [SOURCE: Page_12]

198   Bottom-up effects of plant resistant traits [SOURCE: Page_12]

199 [SOURCE: Page_12]

200   Tuta absoluta has reached the economic pest status and is now considered as a key [SOURCE: Page_12]

201   pest on tomato crops (Desneux et al 2011; Campos et al 2017). This status explains [SOURCE: Page_12]

202   why the main research on plant resistant traits against T. absoluta has focused on [SOURCE: Page_12]

203   tomato. However, we must keep in mind that the plant host range of T. absoluta is [SOURCE: Page_12]

204   broader as it is able to develop on many Solanaceous species, tomato, potato and [SOURCE: Page_12]

205   European black nightshade (Solanum nigrum) being the most suitable (Abbes et al [SOURCE: Page_12]

206   2016, Desneux et al 2010, Biondi et al 2018). It can also oviposit and develop on [SOURCE: Page_12]

207   several plants belonging to the Amaranthaceae, Convolvulaceae, Fabaceae and [SOURCE: Page_12]

208   Malvaceae families (Bawin et al 2016). Studying this broader plant host range might [SOURCE: Page_12]

209   bring innovations in the search of resistant plants against T. absoluta. [SOURCE: Page_13]

210   Cultivated tomatoes (S. lycopersicum) are generally highly susceptible to T. absoluta, [SOURCE: Page_13]

211   and recent tomato population screenings identified only moderately resistant cultivars [SOURCE: Page_13]

212   (Gharekhani and Salek-Ebrahimi, 2014; Rostami et al., 2017; Sohrabi et al., 2016, [SOURCE: Page_13]

213   2017). Most plant resistant traits to T. absoluta have been identified in wild tomato [SOURCE: Page_13]

214   relatives. Resistant traits may be multiple, integrating resistance by antixenosis and [SOURCE: Page_13]

215   antibiosis (Gharekhani and Salek-Ebrahimi, 2014). They are categorized as either [SOURCE: Page_13]

216   constitutive resistance (naturally present in the plant before infestation), induced [SOURCE: Page_13]

217   resistance by the presence of T. absoluta on the plant or introduced resistance via [SOURCE: Page_13]

218   genetic modification. [SOURCE: Page_13]

219 [SOURCE: Page_13]

220   Constitutive resistance traits - In wild tomato species, plant resistant traits to T. [SOURCE: Page_13]

221   absoluta are mainly related to the type and density of leaf trichomes (Fernandes et al. [SOURCE: Page_13]

222   2012; Sohrabi et al. 2016, 2017) and the compounds produced and stored inside. Both [SOURCE: Page_13]

223   provide a constitutive protection against the leafminer since contact with the leaf is a [SOURCE: Page_13]

224   key component for inducing T. absoluta oviposition (Proffit et al 2011). Three main [SOURCE: Page_13]

225   chemical classes have been identified, methyl-ketones (notably 2-tridecanone; Maluf [SOURCE: Page_13]

226   et al., 1997), sesquiterpenes (notably zingiberene; Azevedo et al., 2003, Bleeker et al [SOURCE: Page_13]

227   2012) and acyl sugars (Resende et al., 2006). The former two are abundant in S. [SOURCE: Page_13]

228   habrochaites (previously referred as L. hirsutum) whereas the latter is present in S. [SOURCE: Page_13]

229   pennellii. The resistance assessment of descendants from interspecific crossing [SOURCE: Page_13]

230   between S. lycopersicum and S. habrochaites on the one hand and S. lycopersicum and [SOURCE: Page_13]

231   S. pennellii on the other hand, led to the conclusion that each chemical class mediates [SOURCE: Page_14]

232   T. absoluta resistance through both antixenosis and antibiosis: the compounds [SOURCE: Page_14]

233   mentioned above impair egg laying and larval feeding, leading to antixenosis or larval [SOURCE: Page_14]

234   toxicity, leading to antibiosis (Bleeker et al 2012; de Azevedo et al 2003; Resende et [SOURCE: Page_14]

235   al 2006). In addition, genotypes exhibiting high contents in both zingiberene and acyl [SOURCE: Page_14]

236   sugars, showed greater resistance than genotypes accumulating only one of these [SOURCE: Page_14]

237   chemical, indicating a synergistic effect between zingiberene and acyl sugars (Maluf [SOURCE: Page_14]

238   et al., 2010). Despite the demonstration of higher resistance of these interspecific [SOURCE: Page_14]

239   hybrids, no commercial line has been released, yet. This is probablydue to both the [SOURCE: Page_14]

240   distance separating S. lycopersicum from those two wild tomato species and the fact [SOURCE: Page_14]

241   that resistance transfer from these species is accompanied by other gene introgression [SOURCE: Page_14]

242   mediating poor horticultural value (Rakha Mohamed et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_14]

243   Studies on wild tomato close to S. lycopersicum, i.e. S. galapagense, S. cheesmaniae and S. [SOURCE: Page_14]

244   pimpinellifolium highlighted also resistant traits to T. absoluta (Rakha Mohamed et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_14]

245   These species as well have a high density of glandular trichomes secreting acyl sugars and [SOURCE: Page_14]

246   sesquiterpenes and it may be expected that their resistance to T. absoluta is linked to these traits. [SOURCE: Page_14]

247   Due to ashorter distance between these species and the domesticated tomato, the objective of [SOURCE: Page_14]

248   breeders to obtain S. lycopersicum lines resistant to T. absoluta may be easier to achieve. Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_14]

249   a first patent for commercial tomato lines resistant to arthropods including the leaf [SOURCE: Page_14]

250   miner has alreadybeen published (Snoeren et al. 2014, US Patent US 20140283191 [SOURCE: Page_14]

251   A$_1$ US2017/0240910 A$_1$). These lines have been obtained through introgressed DNA [SOURCE: Page_14]

252   sequences from S. galapagense. The patent encompasses 12 single nucleotide [SOURCE: Page_14]

253   polymorphisms (SNP) displayed on several chromosomes of the S. lycopersicum [SOURCE: Page_15]

254   genome that confer resistance and or tolerance to T. absoluta, white fly (Bemisia [SOURCE: Page_15]

255   tabaci) and spider mite (Tetranychus urticae). This indicates that the commercial [SOURCE: Page_15]

256   development of T. absoluta resistant tomato cultivars is underway. [SOURCE: Page_15]

258   Induced resistant traits - Upon infestation by T. absoluta, tomato plants are able to [SOURCE: Page_15]

259   develop inducible resistance mechanisms that are mainly produced through the [SOURCE: Page_15]

260   jasmonic acid pathway. These resistance mechanisms involve indirect defense via [SOURCE: Page_15]

261   modified volatile organic compound (VOC) blends emitted by infested plants [SOURCE: Page_15]

262   (Strapasson et al., 2014; Backer et al., 2015; Silva et al., 2017). Some of these VOCs [SOURCE: Page_15]

263   are known to attract T. absoluta natural enemies belonging to the Miridae family, like [SOURCE: Page_15]

264   Macrolophus pygmaeus (De Backer et al 2015), M. basicornis, Engytatus varians and [SOURCE: Page_15]

265   Campyloneuropsis infumatus (Naselli et al 2017; Backer et al., 2017; Silva et al., [SOURCE: Page_15]

266   2018). Modification of the VOC blend might also alter host-plant attractiveness as [SOURCE: Page_15]

267   plant volatiles are involved in T. absoluta orientation toward its host plants (Proffit et [SOURCE: Page_15]

268   al 2011). In addition, plant resistance induction could promote the direct expression of [SOURCE: Page_15]

269   defensive allelochemicals. Indeed, Coqueret et al. (2017, supplementary data) [SOURCE: Page_15]

270   highlighted recently that a few hours after the onset of larval feeding, tomato plants [SOURCE: Page_15]

271   induce the accumulation of defensive enzymes (polyphenol oxidase, PPO; Peroxidase, [SOURCE: Page_15]

272   POD) at the feeding site and in distant organs. Such inducible defense provides good [SOURCE: Page_15]

273   opportunities for new breeding strategies to obtain tomato lines (i) emitting higher [SOURCE: Page_15]

274   amounts of selected VOCs able to increase plant attractiveness to pest natural enemies [SOURCE: Page_15]

275   or (ii) inducing a strong enzymatic defense. [SOURCE: Page_16]

276 [SOURCE: Page_16]

277   Introduced plant resistance - Another aspect of resistance can be gained by genetic [SOURCE: Page_16]

278   modifications. For instance, expression of insecticidal proteins like Bt (Bacillus [SOURCE: Page_16]

279   thuringiensis) can be incorporated into the tomato genome (Bergougnoux 2014; [SOURCE: Page_16]

280   Selale et al. 2017). In the latter study, introduction of the cry^1Ac gene to the tomato [SOURCE: Page_16]

281   genome was achieved and a single copy of the gene in the hemizygous condition was [SOURCE: Page_16]

282   sufficient to confer tolerance to T. absoluta. Development of transgenic Bt tomato [SOURCE: Page_16]

283   lines may have great potential for area-wide adoption in both greenhouses and open [SOURCE: Page_16]

284   fields. Such technology may promote pest management in agro-ecosystems, as [SOURCE: Page_16]

285   documented in the case of Bt cotton (Lu et al. 2012). [SOURCE: Page_16]

286   RNA interference (RNAi) technology may also play an important role in tomato [SOURCE: Page_16]

287   genetic transformation aimed at resistance to T. absoluta. RNAi is a mechanism for [SOURCE: Page_16]

288   post-transcriptional gene silencing conserved in most eukaryotes and has proven to be [SOURCE: Page_16]

289   a powerful tool for gene function studies. RNAi can also be applied for pest control: if [SOURCE: Page_16]

290   the sequences of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), the trigger of RNAi, target essential [SOURCE: Page_16]

291   genes of insects, it provokes the endogenous RNAi machinery of pest, thus result in [SOURCE: Page_16]

292   the silencing of these genes which leads to impaired growth and even death of pest. [SOURCE: Page_16]

293   Over the past decade, RNAi-mediated pest control has emerged as an important [SOURCE: Page_16]

294   alternative alongside Bt-based strategies (Zhang et al. 2017). Although transgenic [SOURCE: Page_16]

295   plants expressing dsRNA targeted against insect genes can impair pest growth and [SOURCE: Page_16]

296   development, efficient control of insects and complete protection of plants were only [SOURCE: Page_16]

297   recently achieved by plastid-mediated RNAi technology (Zhang et al. 2015). Because [SOURCE: Page_17]

298   plastids of plant cells do not have an RNAi pathway and because the number of copies [SOURCE: Page_17]

299   of plastid genome is extremely high,1,000 - 2,000 copies of the plastid genome in a [SOURCE: Page_17]

300   typical leaf mesophyll cell (Bock 2014), long dsRNAs in transplastomic (plastid [SOURCE: Page_17]

301   genome transformed) potato plants can be stably accumulated, reaching up to 0.4% of [SOURCE: Page_17]

302   total cellular RNA, being at least two orders of magnitude higher than that of nuclear- [SOURCE: Page_17]

303   transformed plants. [SOURCE: Page_17]

304   Whether or not T. absoluta can be controlled by this approach remains to be [SOURCE: Page_17]

305   investigated. Recently, the transcriptome data showed that most of the core genes of [SOURCE: Page_17]

306   RNAi pathway such as Dicer-like and Argonaute and putative orthologous Sid-1 [SOURCE: Page_17]

307   genes are present in T. absoluta, suggesting the feasibility of RNAi for controlling this [SOURCE: Page_17]

308   pest (Camargo et al. 2015). The application of RNAi has recently been shown as a [SOURCE: Page_17]

309   promising approach for controlling T.absoluta: by targeting against Vacuolar ATPase- [SOURCE: Page_17]

310   A and Arginine Kinase genes of T.absoluta, feeding tomato leaflet uptaking in vitro [SOURCE: Page_17]

311   synthetic dsRNA or transgenic plants expressing dsRNA to 1st instar larvae led to [SOURCE: Page_17]

312   significant knock-down of targeted genes and reduced growth of T. absoluta. [SOURCE: Page_17]

313   However, full plant protection and high larval mortality of T. absoluta have not been [SOURCE: Page_17]

314   achieved probably due to a low expression of dsRNA in transgenic plants (Camargo et [SOURCE: Page_17]

315   al. 2016). As a plastid transformation protocol for tomato plants has been well [SOURCE: Page_17]

316   established (Ruf et al. 2001), it would be workable to express long dsRNA in plastids [SOURCE: Page_17]

317   targeting T. absoluta to enhance the resistant levels of tomato plants. [SOURCE: Page_17]

318 [SOURCE: Page_17]

319   Implications for IPM [SOURCE: Page_18]

320 [SOURCE: Page_18]

321   Manipulating irrigation and fertilization inputs seems to offer promising agronomic [SOURCE: Page_18]

322   leverage for IPM packages against T. absoluta that are based on bottom-up effects [SOURCE: Page_18]

323   (Han et al. 2014; 2016). In these studies, however, the impacts on fruit yield and [SOURCE: Page_18]

324   quality have not been considered, which refrains us from drawing the definite [SOURCE: Page_18]

325   conclusion that low nitrogen and water input are valuable to improve the management [SOURCE: Page_18]

326   of T. absoluta. We propose, therefore, that future research efforts should attempt to [SOURCE: Page_18]

327   trade off a part of crop yield for a gain in pest management. There are great potentials [SOURCE: Page_18]

328   for reducing fertilizers and water inputs in greenhouse productions (Le Bot et al. [SOURCE: Page_18]

329   1998; Le Bot and Adamowicz, 2005) suggesting that we may be on the way to [SOURCE: Page_18]

330   develop a win-win situation, strengthening pest management while gaining important [SOURCE: Page_18]

331   ecological and economic benefits. [SOURCE: Page_18]

332 [SOURCE: Page_18]

333   Modern crop cultivars often display a diversity of tolerance to sub-optimal abiotic [SOURCE: Page_18]

334   environments (Ballhorn et al. 2011). For instance, some crop cultivars have been [SOURCE: Page_18]

335   deliberately selected to withstand various environmental stresses, such as nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_18]

336   deficiency (Feng et al. 2010) or drought (Cattivelli et al. 2008). One of our recent [SOURCE: Page_18]

337   studies show, surprisingly, that the negative bottom-up effects of limited nitrogen and [SOURCE: Page_18]

338   water inputs can cascade up to T. absoluta even though the tomato cultivars exhibited [SOURCE: Page_18]

339   various adaptive or tolerance traits to those resource limitations (Han et al. 2016). In [SOURCE: Page_18]

340   other words, the use of resistant cultivars may “kill two birds with one stone”, i.e. by [SOURCE: Page_18]

341   preserving the negative bottom-up effects of limited resources on the herbivorous [SOURCE: Page_19]

342   insects while allowing the cultivars to cope with sub-optimal conditions. This finding [SOURCE: Page_19]

343   would not only apply to low-input farming systems, but also benefit pest control on [SOURCE: Page_19]

344   cultivars grown in arid areas or semi-barren lands around the world, e.g., drought- [SOURCE: Page_19]

345   prone environments (Farooq et al. 2009). [SOURCE: Page_19]

346 [SOURCE: Page_19]

347   Bottom-up effects of irrigation, fertilization and/or plant resistance traits may cascade [SOURCE: Page_19]

348   up onto the organisms from higher trophic levels (e.g. arthropod natural enemies), [SOURCE: Page_19]

349   thus favoring or compromising the pest control efficacy (e.g. “nitrogen” reviewed by [SOURCE: Page_19]

350   Chen et al. 2010). In general, abiotic factors and plant resistance traits can trigger [SOURCE: Page_19]

351   bottom-up effects on natural enemies either (a) directly via impacts on natural [SOURCE: Page_19]

352   enemies foraging behavior or (b) indirectly via trophic cascade through their [SOURCE: Page_19]

353   prey/host. A small number of studies investigated bottom-up effects reaching the third [SOURCE: Page_19]

354   trophic level (Han et al 2015a, b; Dong et al 2017, Becker et al in prep). So far, [SOURCE: Page_19]

355   however, the fourth trophic level has not been investigated (e.g. intraguild predation [SOURCE: Page_19]

356   among natural enemies or hyperparasitism). Omnivorous predators, mainly [SOURCE: Page_19]

357   Heteroptera species, e.g. M. pygmaeus and Nesidicoris tenuis are the most commonly [SOURCE: Page_19]

358   used predators in biological control programs against T. absoluta (Zappala et al 2013; [SOURCE: Page_19]

359   Biondi et al 2018). Similarly, the larval-parasitoid Necremnus tutae (Hymenoptera: [SOURCE: Page_19]

360   Eulophidae) can be used in biocontrol strategies against T. absoluta (Calvo et al 2016, [SOURCE: Page_19]

361   Naselli et al 2017). Both are often applied in greenhouses where resources availability [SOURCE: Page_19]

362   can be easily manipulated. [SOURCE: Page_19]

363 [SOURCE: Page_20]

364   Bottom-up effects on predators – Omnivorous predators acquire nutrients and energy [SOURCE: Page_20]

365   from both host plants and insect prey (Janssen et al 2003). Their efficacy as biocontrol [SOURCE: Page_20]

366   agents may vary with biotic and abiotic variables (Symondson et al 2002). Yet, little is [SOURCE: Page_20]

367   known about possible plant-mediated bottom-up effects that can be triggered by [SOURCE: Page_20]

368   manipulated resource availability and their impact on predators. Han et al (2015a) [SOURCE: Page_20]

369   demonstrated the importance of water supply: highly irrigated plants attracted more [SOURCE: Page_20]

370   M. pygmaeus adults than those receiving low water input. In another study, the same [SOURCE: Page_20]

371   authors showed that water limitation decreased the feeding activity of M. pygmaeus, [SOURCE: Page_20]

372   on prey as well as on host plants, reducing the predator’s longevity by nearly 30% [SOURCE: Page_20]

373   (Han et al 2015b). The positive correlation between the two feeding activities [SOURCE: Page_20]

374   indicates a physiological link (Facilitation hypothesis, Gillepsie & McGregor 2000) [SOURCE: Page_20]

375   (See Figure 1): omnivorous predators must feed on plant tissue to obtain the water [SOURCE: Page_20]

376   required for key biological processes, for instance producing saliva needed for extra- [SOURCE: Page_20]

377   oral digestion of prey (Cohen 1998, Sinia et al 2004). Han et al (2015b) assumed that [SOURCE: Page_20]

378   water deficit in the plant tissue restricted M. pygmaeus ability to digest preyed eggs, [SOURCE: Page_20]

379   which in turn decreased predator longevity. However, it is also possible that the [SOURCE: Page_20]

380   simultaneous decrease in plant and prey feeding arose from elevated concentrations of [SOURCE: Page_20]

381   defense compounds in plant tissues (Inbar et al 2001, Kaplan and Thaler 2011). [SOURCE: Page_20]

382   According to the “mother knows best” hypothesis (Kohandani et al. 2017), mated [SOURCE: Page_20]

383   females should avoid oviposition on water-stressed plants. However, another [SOURCE: Page_20]

384   omnivorous predator species preferred to lay eggs on water-stressed plants but on [SOURCE: Page_20]

385   which nymphs failed to perform better (Seagraves et al. 2011). Regarding plant [SOURCE: Page_21]

386   resistance, tomato cultivars with higher trichome densities were shown to reduce the [SOURCE: Page_21]

387   predation rate of Hemipteran predatory bugs on T. absoluta, at odds with biological [SOURCE: Page_21]

388   control (Salehi et al. 2016; Bottega et al. 2017). [SOURCE: Page_21]

389 [SOURCE: Page_21]

390   Bottom-up effects on parasitoids - Plant water and nitrogen availability could trigger [SOURCE: Page_21]

391   bottom-up effects on parasitic wasps and thereby alter the top-down control forces [SOURCE: Page_21]

392   provided by parasitoids. The only two studies conducted on this topic, however, did [SOURCE: Page_21]

393   not find strong effects of plant water or nitrogen supply on N. tutae (Becker et al in [SOURCE: Page_21]

394   prep, Dong et al 2017), suggesting that this biocontrol agent would not suffer negative [SOURCE: Page_21]

395   ecological consequences. Yet, in both studies, the number of parasitoid offspring [SOURCE: Page_21]

396   showed a tendency to be sensitive to plant water limitation depending on plant [SOURCE: Page_21]

397   nitrogen status, which should be taken into account when designing IPM packages [SOURCE: Page_21]

398   comprising this biocontrol agent. Additionally, the slower development of T. absoluta [SOURCE: Page_21]

399   on water-limited leaves (Han et al 2014) might result in higher parasitism rates [SOURCE: Page_21]

400   because larvae remain for a longer time in the developmental stages preferred by the [SOURCE: Page_21]

401   parasitoids, i.e. the second instar regarding N. tutae (Dong et al 2017). Large-scale [SOURCE: Page_21]

402   long-term experiments should be interesting to investigate if the slow-growth-high- [SOURCE: Page_21]

403   mortality hypothesis (Benrey & Denno 1997) applies in this scenario. In another [SOURCE: Page_21]

404   experiment, humic fertilizer and vermicompost applied to the soil enhanced [SOURCE: Page_21]

405   population growth parameters of a Trichogramma parasitoid species on eggs of T. [SOURCE: Page_21]

406   absoluta (Mohamadi et al. 2017). This is puzzling as it is unlikely that the quality of [SOURCE: Page_21]

407   T. absoluta eggs be affected by tomato fertilization, and we may suggest that such [SOURCE: Page_22]

408   experiment should be repeated while checking fertilization effects on tomato VOC [SOURCE: Page_22]

409   emission and their effects on parasitoids. [SOURCE: Page_22]

410 [SOURCE: Page_22]

411   Both parasitoids and predators use herbivory-induced plant volatiles (HIPV) to locate [SOURCE: Page_22]

412   their host/prey – this interaction has become famous as the plants’ “cry for help” [SOURCE: Page_22]

413   (Dicke et al 1989, Turlings et al 1990, Dicke and Baldwin 2010). Abiotic factors have [SOURCE: Page_22]

414   the potential to change the quality and quantity of these HIPV blends (See Becker et [SOURCE: Page_22]

415   al 2015 for a review). As a result, the “chemical communication” between plants and [SOURCE: Page_22]

416   natural enemies might be disturbed if such changes render the “chemical messages” [SOURCE: Page_22]

417   incomprehensible to the natural enemies. Alternatively, these changes might be of no [SOURCE: Page_22]

418   consequence or even improve the communication, e.g. by adding information on the [SOURCE: Page_22]

419   plant’s status. Yet, the consequences for biological control services remain heavily [SOURCE: Page_22]

420   understudied. HIPV blends associated to feeding T. absoluta larvae contain higher [SOURCE: Page_22]

421   proportions of δ-selinene when emitted from water-limited compared to well- [SOURCE: Page_22]

422   watered tomato leaves and higher proportions of the monoterpene β-myrcene when [SOURCE: Page_22]

423   emitted from tomato leaves containing elevated compared to low leaf N (Becker et al [SOURCE: Page_22]

424   in prep). These are very small changes and it is rather unlikely that it would affect [SOURCE: Page_22]

425   communication with parasitoids or predators. Still, a negative impact on attraction and [SOURCE: Page_22]

426   arrest cannot be excluded without behavioral assays on commercially important [SOURCE: Page_22]

427   biocontrol agents. The manipulation of HIPV blends in order to optimize host/prey [SOURCE: Page_22]

428   detection by natural enemies harbors a great potential to be exploited in future IPM [SOURCE: Page_22]

429   packages. [SOURCE: Page_23]

431   Conclusions and future directions [SOURCE: Page_23]

432 [SOURCE: Page_23]

433   The evidence of bottom-up effects of fertilization, irrigation and plant resistant traits [SOURCE: Page_23]

434   on T. absoluta has been shown. In practice, bottom-up effects might become an [SOURCE: Page_23]

435   effective component of the IPM program against this pest. In order to verify this [SOURCE: Page_23]

436   prognosis, knowledge about      the ecological consequences of bottom-up effects on T. [SOURCE: Page_23]

437   absoluta has to be expanded. Further studies considering tritrophic interactions are [SOURCE: Page_23]

438   needed to draw convincing conclusions. Future investigations on the bottom-up [SOURCE: Page_23]

439   effects would benefit from the following strategies. [SOURCE: Page_23]

440   a) Assessing the bottom-up effects on T. absoluta at larger-scale [SOURCE: Page_23]

441   Most findings about plant-mediated bottom-up effects on T. absoluta have been [SOURCE: Page_23]

442   obtained at an individual level using microcosms. Only a few studies focused on T. [SOURCE: Page_23]

443   absoluta populations in greenhouses or in the open field (e.g. plant cultivar resistance: [SOURCE: Page_23]

444   Sohrabi et al. 2016) whereas monitoring of population dynamics has been done on [SOURCE: Page_23]

445   other insect pests such as whiteflies and beet armyworm (Bentz et al. 1995; Bi et al. [SOURCE: Page_23]

446   2003; Chen et al. 2008). Experiments conducted on other insects clearly show that [SOURCE: Page_23]

447   bottom-up effects studied on individuals do not consistently translate into population [SOURCE: Page_23]

448   effects, suggesting that it is crucial to separate the effects acting on individuals   from [SOURCE: Page_23]

449   those acting on populations (Zaugg et al. 2013). Therefore, more research efforts are [SOURCE: Page_23]

450   required on T. absoluta under greenhouse and/or field conditions in order to get [SOURCE: Page_23]

451   convincing conclusions (Han et al. in prep.). One should note that assessing the [SOURCE: Page_24]

452   bottom-up effects of fertilization, irrigation and plant resistance in large-scale [SOURCE: Page_24]

453   conditions is straightforward, but costly and labor consuming. [SOURCE: Page_24]

454 [SOURCE: Page_24]

455   b) Disentangling the role of plant phenotypic variations from the observed bottom-up [SOURCE: Page_24]

456   effects [SOURCE: Page_24]

457   Two aspects of plant phenotypic plasticity, i.e., plant resistance to T. absoluta and [SOURCE: Page_24]

458   plant tolerance and/or adaptation to abiotic stress, should be taken into account when [SOURCE: Page_24]

459   designing experiments. Tomato cultivars adapted to resource limitation maintain their [SOURCE: Page_24]

460   ability to mediate the effects of resource limitation on the pest (Han et al. 2016 a or [SOURCE: Page_24]

461   b???) and this gives good grounds for studying further this interaction. Future [SOURCE: Page_24]

462   experiments should thus be designed to include a wide range of tomato cultivars [SOURCE: Page_24]

463   showing either adaption or resistance traits to allow for a concrete assessment on the [SOURCE: Page_24]

464   role of those factors in bottom-up effects. This calls, therefore, for renewed [SOURCE: Page_24]

465   collaborative interdisciplinary research bridging entomology, plant physiology and [SOURCE: Page_24]

466   crop breeding. [SOURCE: Page_24]

467 [SOURCE: Page_24]

468   c) Identifying how the bottom-up effects influence the interspecific interactions [SOURCE: Page_24]

469   Surprisingly little is known about how the invasive species T. absoluta interacts with [SOURCE: Page_24]

470   endemic pest species that share the same micro-habitat and/or arthropod natural [SOURCE: Page_24]

471   enemies. It remains unknown for instance, how T. absoluta initial presence affects the [SOURCE: Page_24]

472   oviposition preference of the subsequent attacker Helicoverpa armigera on tomato [SOURCE: Page_24]

473   and its subsequent larval performance. Do the effects vary across contrasting levels of [SOURCE: Page_25]

474   water/nitrogen inputs (i.e. bottom-up effects)? Answering these questions is required [SOURCE: Page_25]

475   to predict the spatio-temporal pattern of field damages, where multiple pests coexist. [SOURCE: Page_25]

476   Various chemical mechanisms are involved in species interactions, including [SOURCE: Page_25]

477   herbivory-induced VOCs (Becker et al. 2015; De Backer et al. 2015; Silva et al. 2017) [SOURCE: Page_25]

478   and/or plant defensive secondary compounds (Tan et al. 2012). Moreover, it is [SOURCE: Page_25]

479   important to consider their common enemies among the arthropod community in [SOURCE: Page_25]

480   order to clarify how bottom-up effects influence the indirect interactions among insect [SOURCE: Page_25]

481   herbivores (Poelman and Dicke 2014; Stam et al. 2014). [SOURCE: Page_25]

482 [SOURCE: Page_25]

483   d) Breeding novel cultivars [SOURCE: Page_25]

484   With the emergence of chemical insecticide and Bt resistances       among insects, it [SOURCE: Page_25]

485   becomes very urgent to develop new and alternative approaches for efficient pest [SOURCE: Page_25]

486   control. Over the past decade, RNAi-mediated pest control has emerged as an [SOURCE: Page_25]

487   important alternative alongside the Bt-based strategy (Zhang et al. 2017). Expression [SOURCE: Page_25]

488   of long double-stranded RNAs against pest essential genes in plastids can reach a very [SOURCE: Page_25]

489   high level and thus offer full crop protection that is both highly specific to target pests [SOURCE: Page_25]

490   and environmentally-safe (Zhang et al. 2015). It would be an interesting challenge to [SOURCE: Page_25]

491   investigate whether or not T. absoluta can be controlled by this promising approach. 492 [SOURCE: Page_25]
