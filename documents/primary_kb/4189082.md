216   2.1 Observed data [SOURCE: Page_10]

217   2.1.1 University of Nebraska, Mead, Nebraska, USA [SOURCE: Page_10]

218   One set of field data came from the University of Nebraska Agricultural Research and [SOURCE: Page_10]

219   Development Center near Mead, Nebraska, USA (http://csp.unl.edu/public/). The soils were deep [SOURCE: Page_10]

220   silty clay loams of Yutan (fine-silty, mixed, superactive, mesic Mollic Hapludalfs), Tomek (fine, [SOURCE: Page_10]

221   smectitic, mesic Pachic Argialbolls), Filbert (fine, smectitic, mesic Vertic Argialbolls), and [SOURCE: Page_10]

222   Filmore (fine, smectitic, mesic Vertic Argialbolls). The eddy covariance technique was used to [SOURCE: Page_10]

223   determine ET of maize and soybean (Glycine max) in alternate years, as well as fluxes of [SOURCE: Page_10]

224   sensible heat and CO$_2$. Additional details can be found in Suyker and Verma (2008, 2009) and [SOURCE: Page_10]

225   Suyker et al. (2004, 2005). Briefly, fluxes of latent heat, sensible heat, and momentum were [SOURCE: Page_10]

226   determined using data from the following sensors at each site: an omnidirectional 3D sonic [SOURCE: Page_10]

227   anemometer (Model R$_3$: Gill Instruments Ltd., Lymington, UK) and an open-path infrared [SOURCE: Page_10]

228   CO$_2$/H2O gas analyzing system (Model LI7500: Li-Cor Inc., Lincoln, NE). [SOURCE: Page_10]

229 [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   The instruments were deployed near the centers of the fields, and the fetch was about 400 m in [SOURCE: Page_10]

231   all directions. The eddy covariance sensors were mounted 5.5 m above the ground. Fluxes were [SOURCE: Page_10]

232   corrected for inadequate sensor frequency, and they were also adjusted for the variation in air [SOURCE: Page_10]

233   density due to the transfer of water vapor and sensible heat. Air temperature and relative [SOURCE: Page_10]

10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

234   humidity (Humitter^50Y, Vaisala, Helsinki, FIN), soil heat flux at 0.06m (Radiation and Energy [SOURCE: Page_11]

235   Balance Systems, Inc., Seattle, WA), and net radiation at 5.5m (CNR$_1$, Kipp and Zonen Ltd., [SOURCE: Page_11]

236   Delft, NLD) were also measured. Missing data due to sensor malfunction, power outages, [SOURCE: Page_11]

237   unfavorable weather, etc. (approximately 15 20% per year), were estimated using an approach [SOURCE: Page_11]

238   that combined measurement, interpolation, and empirical data synthesis. When hourly values [SOURCE: Page_11]

239   were missing (day or night), the latent heat values were estimated as a function of available [SOURCE: Page_11]

240   energy. Linear regressions between latent heat and available energy were determined (separately [SOURCE: Page_11]

241   for dry and wet conditions) for sliding 3-day intervals, and these estimates were used to fill in [SOURCE: Page_11]

242   missing flux values. [SOURCE: Page_11]

243 [SOURCE: Page_11]

244   To check closure of the energy balance, the sum of latent and sensible heat fluxes ( E + H) [SOURCE: Page_11]

245   measured by eddy covariance were plotted against the sum of Rn (net radiation) + four storage [SOURCE: Page_11]

246   terms, determined by other methods (e.g., Suyker and Verma, 2008). Linear regressions were [SOURCE: Page_11]

247   calculated between the hourly values of H + E and Rn + G at the study sites (excluding winter [SOURCE: Page_11]

248   months and periods with rain and irrigation). Here G = Gs (soil heat storage) + Gc (canopy heat [SOURCE: Page_11]

249   storage) + Gm (heat stored in the mulch) + Gp (energy used in photosynthesis). The regression [SOURCE: Page_11]

250   slopes averaged 0.89 ± 0.08, implying a fairly good closure of the energy balance. [SOURCE: Page_11]

251 [SOURCE: Page_11]

252   We used values of daily ET flux, called observed-ET for 2003, 2005, 2007, 2009, 2011, and [SOURCE: Page_11]

253   2013 from the US-Ne$_2$ (41.165 N, 96.470 W, 362 m; http://ameriflux.lbl.gov/sites/siteinfo/US- [SOURCE: Page_11]

254   Ne$_2$) irrigated maize-soybean rotation field and from the US-Ne$_3$ (41.180 N, 96.440 W, 363 [SOURCE: Page_11]

255   m; http://ameriflux.lbl.gov/sites/siteinfo/US-Ne$_3$) rainfed maize-soybean rotation field. [SOURCE: Page_11]

256   Conservation tillage practices were used, so plant residues were not ploughed into the soil, and [SOURCE: Page_11]

11 [SOURCE: Page_11]

257   the soil surface was generally partially covered with prior soybean crop residue. Both sites are [SOURCE: Page_12]

258   part of the Ameriflux (https://ameriflux.lbl.gov/sites) U.S. surface gas flux observation system, [SOURCE: Page_12]

259   and the two sites are within 1.6 km of each other. The cultivars were Pn^33B51, Pn^33G66, [SOURCE: Page_12]

260   Pn^33H26, Pn^33T57, DK_61-72, and DK_62-98 used in 2003, 2005, 2007, 2009, 2011, and 2013, [SOURCE: Page_12]

261   respectively The irrigated crops were planted on 14 May, 2 May, 1 May, 21 April, 17 May, and [SOURCE: Page_12]

262   30 May, and the rainfed crops on 13 May, 26 April, 2 May, 22 April, 2 May, and 13 May in [SOURCE: Page_12]

263   2003, 2005, 2007, 2009, 2011, and 2013, respectively. Destructive measurements of green leaf [SOURCE: Page_12]

264   area index (LAI) and biomass were made approximately bi-monthly during the growing season. [SOURCE: Page_12]

276   al., 1988). The fields and their associated lysimeters were designated NE, SE, NW, and SW [SOURCE: Page_12]

287   Maize harvests were on 15 October 2013 and on 13 and 17 October 2016. [SOURCE: Page_13]

411                                                                     s fell within the short (1-3 mm/d) [SOURCE: Page_20]

412   green boxes much of the time, which indicates many of the models produced respectable [SOURCE: Page_20]

413   simulations. There was only a slight (< 1 mm/d) improvement in model performance going from [SOURCE: Page_20]

414   Phase 1 to Phase 4. It appears that the greatest variability and uncertainty among the models [SOURCE: Page_20]

415   occurred from about 10 days before planting to 10 DAP (soil E dominated) and from about 130 [SOURCE: Page_20]

416   to 160 DAP (after the crop matured). A likely cause of the latter issue is that many models [SOURCE: Page_20]

419   adjustments or calibrations going from Phase 1 to Phase 4 as more information was provided [SOURCE: Page_20]

422   needed to improve the simulation of bare soil ET. [SOURCE: Page_21]

1009        The models were CS, AMSW, ECOS, XNSM, AHC, DIFR, DCFR, and DIGR. Six of them [SOURCE: Page_61]

1010        follow the general approach of using FAO-56/Penman-Monteith (Allen et al., 1998, 2005) [SOURCE: Page_61]

1011        to simulate ETs, while AMSW uses a transpiration efficiency approach (Probert et al., 1998; [SOURCE: Page_61]

1012        Keating et al., 2003), and ECOS uses an energy balance approach (Grant et al., 2007; Grant [SOURCE: Page_61]

1013        and Flanagan, 2007). All of these models or their ancestors have been in existence and have [SOURCE: Page_61]

1014        been widely used for a long time. Thus, there has been time for improvement over a wide [SOURCE: Page_61]

1015        range of climatic and soil conditions. Unlike the previous inter-comparison (Kimball et al., [SOURCE: Page_61]

1016        2019), none of the simpler models were among the best at simulating all four variables for [SOURCE: Page_61]

1017        this study involving a wider range of environmental conditions from two locations. [SOURCE: Page_61]

1018   4.4 Although the ensemble median was not among the best estimates of soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_61]

1019        (Supplementary), it was at the top or close to the top for all other categories. That the [SOURCE: Page_61]

61 [SOURCE: Page_61]

1020       ensemble median generally outperforms any individual model is consistent with previous [SOURCE: Page_62]

1021       intercomparisons, e.g., Asseng et al. (2015). [SOURCE: Page_62]

1022   4.5 Within the DSSAT family, the older Ritchie (1972) approach for simulating soil E was [SOURCE: Page_62]

1023       markedly better than the newer Suleiman and Ritchie (2003, 2004) approach, which [SOURCE: Page_62]

1024       appeared to overestimate upward movement of soil moisture. [SOURCE: Page_62]

1025   4.6 Further, within the DSSAT family, the FAO-56 (Allen et al., 1998) method for calculating [SOURCE: Page_62]

1026       potential ETp was best for simulating ETs from 40 to 100 DAP (mostly canopy T) and [SOURCE: Page_62]

1027       worse for -10 to +20 DAP (mostly soil E). The Priestly and Taylor (1972) method was best [SOURCE: Page_62]

1028       for soil E and worse for canopy T. The ASCE Standardized Equation approach with short or [SOURCE: Page_62]

1029       tall crop coefficients (Allen et al., 2005) was intermediate for canopy T and worst for soil E, [SOURCE: Page_62]

1030       although this result might be somewhat biased because they were not independently [SOURCE: Page_62]

1031       calibrated. [SOURCE: Page_62]

1032   4.7 DSSAT CSM-IXIM tended to be slightly better than DSSAT CSM-CERES for simulating [SOURCE: Page_62]

1033       canopy T, probably because IXIM simulated leaf area progression better. [SOURCE: Page_62]

1034   4.8 Both STCK (which considers one surface to compute ETp) and STSW (which considers [SOURCE: Page_62]

1035       both soil and canopy surfaces to compute ETp) were among the best models to simulate ETs [SOURCE: Page_62]

1036       at the beginning of the seasons, with slightly better results for STSW. During the mid- [SOURCE: Page_62]

1037       season periods, STCK globally performed better than STSW, but both performed poorly [SOURCE: Page_62]

1038       with SDI in 2013, which distorted results. [SOURCE: Page_62]

1039   4.9 XNSM and XNGM appeared to do equally well at simulating both soil E and canopy T, [SOURCE: Page_62]

1040       with XNGM following the more detailed Farquhar modeling approach in calculating [SOURCE: Page_62]

1041       photosynthesis and leafT, but greatly simplifying vertical root distribution. However, [SOURCE: Page_62]

62 [SOURCE: Page_62]

1042       XNSM did better than XNGM at simulating grain yield, possibly due to its simpler but more [SOURCE: Page_63]

1043       robust approach in simulating assimilate distribution among plant organs. [SOURCE: Page_63]

1044   4.9 MZD and MZH both have hourly time steps, yet MZD which uses daily weather data did [SOURCE: Page_63]

1045      slightly better than MZH which uses hourly weather data at simulating soil E, but there was [SOURCE: Page_63]

1046      no significant difference between them at simulating canopy T. This is somewhat surprising, [SOURCE: Page_63]

1047      but nevertheless shows that simulated diurnal patterns of hourly weather can be as accurate [SOURCE: Page_63]
