13   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

14   Cover crops have multiple benefits, such as improving water quality, providing a green manure effect, [SOURCE: Page_1]

15   and storing carbon in the soil. They can, however, reduce drainage significantly during key periods of [SOURCE: Page_1]

16   hydrosystem recharge, especially in winter. The objective of this study was to evaluate the influence [SOURCE: Page_1]

17   of cover crops and/or crop diversification at the watershed scale on water in the downstream [SOURCE: Page_1]

18   watershed of the Aveyron River, based on three scenarios with different management practices. It is [SOURCE: Page_1]

19   an illustrative case study of situations of water imbalance involving 1150 farms, with agricultural fields [SOURCE: Page_1]

20   covering 40,000 ha, of which ca. 40% may be irrigated. The MAELIA model was used to simulate 10 [SOURCE: Page_1]

21   years (2007-2016) of dynamics to estimate the influence of cover crops on water flows. Simulations [SOURCE: Page_1]

22   showed that short-duration cover crops terminated in autumn generally had little influence on water: [SOURCE: Page_1]

23   they decreased drainage slightly in autumn, but the recharge in winter compensated for this decrease [SOURCE: Page_1]

24   and thus did not influence the water dynamics or yields of the succeeding cash crops. Although long- [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   duration cover crops grow for a longer period and are sown more frequently in fields, they also had [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   relatively little influence on water in the region, except for decreasing drainage. A scenario with long- [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   duration cover crops and diversification of rotations was a good compromise for quantitative water [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   management. Diversifying rotations, notably by replacing maize with crops that required less water, [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   compensated for potential negative effects of long-duration cover crops. Although this scenario [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   increased variability depending on the weather year and reduced autumn drainage, it influenced [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   irrigation withdrawals and river flows little over the 10-year period. However, greater variability [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   occurred at the field scale, where cover crops can have more influence. Thus, it is important to adapt [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   the management practices for cover crops in rotations to decrease negative effects, particularly on [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   water availability, which could increase withdrawals in an area that already has a water deficit, and [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   not to decrease yields and thus farmers' profits. Our results are valid for the study area, but these [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   scenarios should be extrapolated to other soil and climate conditions and other rotations and [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   management systems. [SOURCE: Page_2]

39       1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   Cover crops are commonly grown during the fallow period between two main cash crops to provide [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   ecosystem services, such as i) water quality regulation by reducing nitrate leaching (Abdalla et al., [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   2019; Ascott et al., 2017; Justes et al., 2012), ii) nitrogen supply through the “green manure” effect [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   (Thorup-Kristensen et al., 2003; Tonitto et al., 2006), iii) climate change regulation by storing soil [SOURCE: Page_2]

44   carbon (Launay et al., 2021; Poeplau and Don, 2015) and reducing greenhouse gas emissions (Kaye and [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   Quemada, 2017; Plaza-Bonilla et al., 2017; Tribouillois et al., 2018a), v) erosion regulation due to [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   continuous protection of soil in winter (Dabney et al., 2001; Langdale et al., 1991; Ryder and Fares, [SOURCE: Page_2]

47   2008), and vi) pest, disease, and weed regulation and biodiversity maintenance (Schipanski et al., [SOURCE: Page_2]

48   2014). However, Meyer et al. (2019) highlighted that the influence of these multiple services of cover [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   crops on water-balance components remains little studied. [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   Water resources in ecosystems are usually classified into two categories: blue water (from lakes, rivers, [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   oceans, and groundwater) and green water (precipitation stored in the soil and returned to the [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   atmosphere through evapotranspiration) (Falkenmark and Rockström, 2006). Rainfed agriculture uses [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   only green water, while irrigated agriculture uses both green and blue water (Liu et al., 2009). The [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   review of Meyer et al. (2019) highlighted that cover crops may have reduced drainage compared to [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   that under bare soil in 90% of the cases studied (by a mean of 27 mm), but with high variability among [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   soils, climates, and cropping systems. Accordingly, introducing cover crops during the fallow period [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   can create an antagonism between the increase in evapotranspiration (green water) due to the cover [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   crops and a decrease in drainage and groundwater recharge (blue water). An intrinsic biophysical [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   relation exists between evapotranspiration and drainage: when the amount of water evapotranspired [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   by plants increases, the amount of water drained decreases. Cover crops can also reduce the water [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   content of the soil and thus the water available for the succeeding crop (Meyer et al., 2019). Thus, [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   even though cover crops provide multiple services, introducing them in a watershed that has a strong [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   water deficit could negatively impact water resources and the water available for succeeding cash [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   crops, and potentially increase irrigation withdrawals, thus placing more strain on water management [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   in the watershed. Thus, introducing cover crops could yield serious disservices for water cycles at the [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   watershed scale, particularly if applied to a large area. To our knowledge, no study has focused on the [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   influence of cover crops on water cycles at the watershed scale, even though it is the relevant scale at [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   which to assess their interactions with water resources. Due to the difficulty in performing experiments [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   at such a large scale, model-based assessment is the main way to investigate the complexity of these [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   interactions (e.g. Alcamo et al., 2003; Letcher et al., 2006; Murgue et al., 2014b; Zhang and Guo, 2016). [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   The present study combined scenarios and an integrated modelling approach to evaluate the influence [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   of introducing cover crops on drainage, irrigation of cash crops, river water flows, and cash crop yields [SOURCE: Page_3]

73   at the watershed scale. Our case study was the downstream Aveyron watershed, located in south- [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   western France. The watershed experiences strong, recurring water deficits (Martin et al. 2016) that [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   are due in particular to large withdrawals to irrigate maize, a predominant crop in the watershed. We [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   used MAELIA (Therond et al., 2014), an integrated assessment and modelling platform that can [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   represent fine-scale spatiotemporal interactions among crop management, the hydrology of different [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   water resources, and water-resource management (i.e. dam releases and regulations) within [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   watersheds (Gaudou et al., 2014; Murgue et al., 2014). The model represents daily water withdrawals [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   and crop management at the field scale. We used MAELIA to assess three contrasting scenarios: [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   introducing short-duration cover crops, long-duration cover cops, or long-duration cover crops [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   combined with diversified rotations. [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   The overall objective of this work is to assess the impact of the introduction of cover crops on the main [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   water flows in the investigated watershed: water withdrawals for irrigation, drainage and river flows. [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   Our hypothesis is that the introduction of long-cover crops with spring termination should have a [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   greater effect on these variables than the scenario with short-cover crop and autumn termination. [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   Another objective of this study is to assess effects of a scenario integrating more agroecological [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   practices i.e. the combination of long-cover crops and diversified rotations. [SOURCE: Page_4]

91       2. Materials and methods [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   2.1 Case study overview [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   The downstream Aveyron watershed is an illustrative case study of situations of water imbalance in [SOURCE: Page_4]

94   which low water inputs combine with high agricultural demand for irrigation water. The watershed is [SOURCE: Page_4]

95   located in the Adour-Garonne basin, which has the largest structural deficit of water in France (7 [SOURCE: Page_4]

96   hm^3.yr-1, on average). The study watershed area is part of this basin; it covers ca. 800 km^2 and contains [SOURCE: Page_4]

97   many irrigated farming systems, which account for up to 80% of total water consumption during the [SOURCE: Page_4]

98   low-flow period (June-September) (Mazzega et al., 2014). For Aveyron withdrawal, annual irrigation [SOURCE: Page_4]

99   withdrawals are observed in the order of 13 hm^3.yr-1. The watershed contains 1150 farms, with [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   agricultural fields covering 40,000 ha, of which ca. 40% is irrigated (according to the French Land Parcel [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   Identification System, 2014). The main cash crops are maize (Zea mays L.) (six cultivars, from very early [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   to very late precocity), wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), oilseeds with mainly sunflower (Helianthus [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   annuus L.), and a few protein crops as pea (Pisum sativum L.). The watershed also contains fields of [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   fruit crops and seed maize, which has a high added value. Two main rivers cross the watershed: the [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   Aveyron and Lère. They have a measured annual flow of 35 m^3.s-1 and 2 m^3.s-1 respectively, falling to [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   around 7 m^3.s-1 and 0.7 m^3.s-1 in average during the low-flow period. Moreover, their flow rates [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   frequently drop below the target regulatory thresholds (4.0 and 0.1 m^3.s-1, respectively) established to [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   ensure aquatic ecosystem health. Irrigation demand and the resulting withdrawals strongly influence [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   river flow dynamics. When a flow rate drops below the threshold, water managers use two main [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   mechanisms: restrictions on irrigation withdrawals and flow-support releases from large collective [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   reservoirs (Murgue et al., 2015). The number of days with flow rates below the low-flow target thus [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   indicates the degree of potential restriction of irrigation. [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   We studied a 10-year period (2007-2016) using daily SAFRAN climate data (8  8 km grid) (Vidal et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   2010) that covered the entire watershed. Over the study period, mean cumulative annual precipitation [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   and potential evapotranspiration were 757 and 849 mm, respectively, with a mean annual water deficit [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   of 91 mm. The daily mean minimum and maximum temperatures were 9.0 and 17.2°C, respectively. [SOURCE: Page_5]

117   The years 2011 and 2015 were the driest (547 and 673 mm of precipitation, respectively), with an [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   annual water deficit of more than 300 mm. For the low-flow period from June to September, 2009, [SOURCE: Page_5]

119   2012, and 2016 were the driest, with a water deficit of more than 300 mm. [SOURCE: Page_5]

121   2.2 MAELIA model overview [SOURCE: Page_5]

122   MAELIA was developed to study environmental and socioeconomic impacts of water-management [SOURCE: Page_5]

123   strategies (Martin et al., 2016; Murgue et al., 2015, 2014). It was developed in an open-source generic [SOURCE: Page_5]

124   modelling and simulation environment: GAMA® (Grignard et al., 2013). MAELIA simulates, at a daily [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   time step, multi-scale interactions among the following: [SOURCE: Page_6]

126       1) agriculture, i.e. soil-crop dynamics (crop growth, yield, irrigation, and soil water dynamics), at [SOURCE: Page_6]

127            the field scale using the AqYield model (Constantin et al., 2015; Tribouillois et al., 2018b), and [SOURCE: Page_6]

128            farmer behaviour using a farmer-as-agent model to represent crop management (Murgue et [SOURCE: Page_6]

129            al., 2014) [SOURCE: Page_6]

130       2)   hydrology of rivers, reservoirs, and groundwater using SWAT (Arnold et al., 2010) algorithms [SOURCE: Page_6]

131            (Martin et al., 2016) [SOURCE: Page_6]

132       3) domestic and industrial water uses using econometric equations (Therond et al., 2014) [SOURCE: Page_6]

133       4) water-management practices such as withdrawal restrictions and water-release decisions [SOURCE: Page_6]

134            (Mazzega et al., 2014; Olivier Therond et al., 2014) [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   In the agriculture module, AqYield simulates crop growth based on cumulative mean daily effective [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   temperature, and soil water content is predicted daily as the balance of water inputs and outputs. All [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   cash crops currently in the study watershed have been calibrated in AqYield, which is considered [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   sufficiently accurate to represent the dynamics of soil water content and evapotranspiration well for [SOURCE: Page_6]

139   a wide range of situations (Constantin et al., 2015; Tribouillois et al., 2018b). Detailed description and [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   equations of AqYield can be found in previous studies (Constantin et al., 2015; Tribouillois et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

141   2018b). Five cover crop species were simulated in MAELIA to construct alternative scenarios: vetch [SOURCE: Page_6]

142   (Vicia sativa L.), faba bean (Vicia faba L.), rapeseed (Brassica napus L.), white mustard (Sinapis alba L.), [SOURCE: Page_6]

143   and Italian ryegrass (Lolium multiflorum Lam.). These cover crop species were calibrated in AqYield [SOURCE: Page_6]

144   based on several experimental databases for sites with contrasting soil and climate across France. The [SOURCE: Page_6]

145   calibration ensured that AqYield accurately predicted soil water dynamics under the five cover crops [SOURCE: Page_6]

146   for different soils and climates. [SOURCE: Page_6]

147   In MAELIA, AqYield simulates interactions between soil water content and crop growth in each field by [SOURCE: Page_6]

148   considering the crop rotation identified in the French Land Parcel Identification System (Murgue et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

149   2016; Martin et al., 2016). The farmer’s behaviour for each field of each farm is represented through [SOURCE: Page_6]

150   IF-THEN decision rules that describe the conditions that trigger tillage, seeding, irrigation, and harvest. [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   In addition, a temporal window that defines early and late dates for triggers is defined for each [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   technical operation. Irrigation decision rules, based on the climate, soil type, irrigation equipment, and [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   available workforce, determine the amounts and dates of water supplied to each irrigated field on [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   each irrigated farm. The decision rules for this case study were collected in a previous study using a [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   dedicated farm survey (Murgue et al., 2015). Based on local databases, expert knowledge, and [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   geographic information system analysis, each irrigated field is connected to one or more water sources [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   (i.e. river(s) or reservoir(s)) (Murgue et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   The hydrology of the watershed’s surface water and groundwater was simulated using the soil and [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   routing phases of SWAT (Arnold et al., 2010), which have been recoded in MAELIA. In the water- [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   management module, IF-THEN decision rules are used to simulate daily management of dam releases [SOURCE: Page_7]

161   and withdrawal restrictions (Mayor et al., 2012; Mazzega et al., 2014). These decision rules were [SOURCE: Page_7]

162   determined using information from regional authorities about current practices. Four dams are [SOURCE: Page_7]

163   available in the watershed to increase river flow rates during the low-flow period. More information [SOURCE: Page_7]

164   about MAELIA can be found in previous studies (Martin et al., 2016; Murgue et al., 2016; Therond et [SOURCE: Page_7]

165   al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_7]

167   2.3 The reference scenario [SOURCE: Page_7]

168   The current situation was considered the reference scenario. Specific modules of MAELIA, as well as [SOURCE: Page_7]

169   the entire model, were calibrated and validated in previous studies of the Aveyron watershed, using [SOURCE: Page_7]

170   quantitative and qualitative expert-based approaches (Constantin et al., 2015; Martin et al., 2016; [SOURCE: Page_7]

171   Murgue et al., 2016; Tribouillois et al., 2018b; Tribouillois et al., 2022). Results of these studies [SOURCE: Page_7]

172   highlighted the model’s ability to simulate water-related variables accurately at the field scale (i.e. soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

173   water content, evapotranspiration, and drainage) and the watershed scale (i.e. irrigation withdrawals [SOURCE: Page_7]

174   and river flows) as a function of the climate. More specifically, the predictive quality of MAELIA was [SOURCE: Page_7]

175   previously assessed from measured data as satisfactory for estimating the volume of irrigation [SOURCE: Page_7]

176   withdrawals and the flow of the two rivers of the same watershed (Tribouillois et al., 2022). Local [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   experts also validated the results of simulated crop-management practices (e.g. sowing dates, first and [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   last dates of irrigation) (Murgue et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_8]

180   2.4 Alternative scenarios [SOURCE: Page_8]

181   We developed three alternative scenarios at the watershed scale to investigate the influence of cover [SOURCE: Page_8]

182   crops on water cycles (Fig. 1). Some management rules for cover crops were applied to all three [SOURCE: Page_8]

183   scenarios: [SOURCE: Page_8]

184       1) Cover crops were sown as early as possible beginning from 1 August to promote emergence [SOURCE: Page_8]

185            and development (due to the potential for thunderstorms in August) or from 7 days after [SOURCE: Page_8]

186            harvesting the previous crop. [SOURCE: Page_8]

187       2) Cover crops were terminated three days before beginning tillage or, at the latest, 14 days [SOURCE: Page_8]

188            before beginning sowing of the succeeding cash crop in order to avoid a pre-emptive effect on [SOURCE: Page_8]

189            water, as recommended by Justes et al. (2012) and Meyer et al. (2022). [SOURCE: Page_8]

190       3) Cover crops were not irrigated or fertilised. [SOURCE: Page_8]

198   2.4.1    Short duration cover crop scenario [SOURCE: Page_9]

199   In the short-duration cover crop scenario (Short-CC), all cover crops grew for at least two months and [SOURCE: Page_9]

200   were terminated before winter. The objective was to reproduce the current regulatory situation in [SOURCE: Page_9]

201   nitrate vulnerable zones, where the main objective is to reduce nitrate leaching to aquifers. Like under [SOURCE: Page_9]

202   current management practices, only non-leguminous cover crops were sown in order to maximise [SOURCE: Page_9]

203   nitrate capture before winter and reduce the risk of leaching during the drainage period (especially in [SOURCE: Page_9]

204   winter). Nearly 90% of the cover crops sown were white mustard, as it develops rapidly, which makes [SOURCE: Page_9]

205   it suitable for a short growing period. The other ca. 10% of cover crops were Italian ryegrass, which [SOURCE: Page_9]

206   was sown after rapeseed cash crops to avoid a cruciferous cover crop and its associated risk of diseases [SOURCE: Page_9]

207   or pests. Cover crops were sown only after rapeseed, wheat, and winter pea, and only before spring [SOURCE: Page_9]

208   cash crops and winter pea. Cover crops were not sown after cash crops with a late harvest (i.e. [SOURCE: Page_9]

209   sunflower, maize, or soya bean) because the interval between this harvest and the tillage and/or [SOURCE: Page_9]

210   sowing of the succeeding cash crop (< 2 months) was too short. In this scenario, 8000 ha of the 19,300 [SOURCE: Page_9]

211   ha of agricultural land in the watershed (i.e. ca. 41%) was sown with a cover crop each year. [SOURCE: Page_9]

212 [SOURCE: Page_9]

213   2.4.2    Long-duration cover crop scenario [SOURCE: Page_9]

214   In the long-duration cover crop scenario (Long-CC), the objective was to maximise the influence of the [SOURCE: Page_9]

215   multiple services provided by cover crops. Accordingly, cover crops were sown in every fallow period [SOURCE: Page_9]

216   possible, with termination at the end of winter or in spring, as close as possible to the sowing of the [SOURCE: Page_9]

217   succeeding cash crop to provide the cover crops with a longer growing period and promote their [SOURCE: Page_9]

218   development. At the field scale, leguminous cover crops were introduced before non-leguminous cash [SOURCE: Page_9]

219   crops, and vice-versa. For non-leguminous cover crops, rapeseed was sown instead of white mustard [SOURCE: Page_9]

220   when possible because it maintains its cover during the winter under the study conditions and [SOURCE: Page_9]

221   continues to grow in spring before termination. Due to these allocation rules, at the watershed scale [SOURCE: Page_10]

222   the main cover crops were rapeseed, faba bean, and vetch (ca. 30% each), followed by white mustard [SOURCE: Page_10]

223   and Italian ryegrass. In total, 13,300 ha of agricultural land in the watershed (i.e. ca. 69%) was sown [SOURCE: Page_10]

224   with a cover crop each year. [SOURCE: Page_10]

225 [SOURCE: Page_10]

226   2.4.3   Scenario with long-duration cover crops and diversified rotations [SOURCE: Page_10]

227   Cover crops and diversification of rotations are two key pillars of an agroecological transition (Duru et [SOURCE: Page_10]

228   al., 2015), and the latter has been studied as one way to limit the water deficit in the Aveyron [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   watershed (Allain et al., 2018). Consequently, we developed a third alternative scenario (Long-CC&div) [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   to explore potential effects of combining long-duration cover crops with diversification of the main [SOURCE: Page_10]

231   crop rotations in the watershed (Figure 1a). It was designed in two steps: 1) legumes were introduced [SOURCE: Page_10]

232   to diversify the crop rotations, and then 2) cover crops were introduced. We chose diversified crop [SOURCE: Page_10]

233   rotations from those recommended by Tribouillois et al. (2018b) to diversify the main cropping systems [SOURCE: Page_10]

234   in south-western France, developed in consultation with experts in the region. Because rotations that [SOURCE: Page_10]

235   already included a legume (soya bean) every two years (wheat/soya bean or maize/soya bean) [SOURCE: Page_10]

236   benefitted from its presence, they were not modified. Seed maize monocultures were not modified [SOURCE: Page_10]

237   either due to their high added value for the region. The other rotations were modified as follows [SOURCE: Page_10]

238   (Figure 1 b-c): [SOURCE: Page_10]

239       -   Maize monocultures and maize/wheat rotations were replaced with a soya bean/maize/wheat [SOURCE: Page_10]

240           rotation. [SOURCE: Page_10]

241       -   Cereal monocultures, wheat/rapeseed, and wheat/sunflower rotations were replaced with a [SOURCE: Page_10]

242           pea/wheat/sorghum/sunflower/wheat rotation. [SOURCE: Page_10]

243   The beginning of the new rotations was established in a spatially homogeneous way between the [SOURCE: Page_10]

244   different crops. This means that for a given year, there was a homogeneous distribution between the [SOURCE: Page_10]

245   different crops, and not a single crop. In the new rotations, maize was always a mid-late cultivar to [SOURCE: Page_10]

246   ensure that it could be harvested before sowing the succeeding crop. The rotations were modified by [SOURCE: Page_10]

247   alternating the first species in the rotation in order to distribute all species evenly over the watershed [SOURCE: Page_11]

248   each year. Thus, diversification of the rotations reduced the area of maize by ca. 2500 ha (from 6000 [SOURCE: Page_11]

249   to 3500 ha), wheat by ca. 1200 ha (from 9000 to 7800 ha), sunflower by ca. 700 ha (from 3100 to 2400 [SOURCE: Page_11]

250   ha), rapeseed by ca. 500 ha (from 560 to 60 ha), temporary grassland by ca. 400 ha (from 7600 to 7200 [SOURCE: Page_11]

251   ha), and fallow land by ca. 300 ha (from 2300 to 2000 ha). Conversely, it increased the area of sorghum [SOURCE: Page_11]

252   by ca. 1900 ha (from 0 to 1900 ha), pea by ca. 1900 ha (from 100 to 2,000 ha), and soya bean by ca. [SOURCE: Page_11]

253   1700 ha (700 to 2400 ha). Thus, the area of leguminous crops in the watershed increased greatly, which [SOURCE: Page_11]

254   resulted in more equal crop areas in the watershed. [SOURCE: Page_11]

255   Next, cover crops were introduced into the new rotations, following the rules applied in the Long-CC [SOURCE: Page_11]

256   scenario. The cover crop species were rapeseed (35%), faba bean (30%), Italian ryegrass (16%), white [SOURCE: Page_11]

257   mustard (14%), and vetch (5%). Similar to the Long-CC scenario, 13,400 ha of agricultural land in the [SOURCE: Page_11]

258   watershed (ca. 70%) was sown with a cover crop each year. [SOURCE: Page_11]

260   2.4.4   Modifying crop-management practices to introduce cover crops [SOURCE: Page_11]

261   To attempt to maximise the development of the new cover crops as a farmer might, the management [SOURCE: Page_11]

262   practices used in the reference scenario were modified in the alternative scenarios. The latest possible [SOURCE: Page_11]

263   maize harvest date was advanced by 10 days so that the succeeding cover crop could be sown earlier. [SOURCE: Page_11]

264   In the Long-CC and Long-CC&div scenarios, tillage on clay soils (hillsides) was moved from autumn to [SOURCE: Page_11]

265   spring to terminate the cover crop later. This spring tillage often delayed the sowing of spring crops by [SOURCE: Page_11]

266   a few days to a few weeks. [SOURCE: Page_11]

268   2.5 Data analysis [SOURCE: Page_11]

269   As MAELIA had already been largely validated for this study area (Allain et al., 2018; Martin et al., 2016; [SOURCE: Page_11]

270   Murgue et al., 2016; Tribouillois et al., 2018b), we calculated most results as a difference of the [SOURCE: Page_11]

271   alternative scenarios from the reference scenario in order to highlight the influence of cover crops. We [SOURCE: Page_11]

272   focused on irrigation withdrawals, drainage, river flow, and yields at the watershed scale. For irrigation [SOURCE: Page_11]

273   withdrawals and drainage, we calculated totals for each of the 10 years simulated, as well as mean [SOURCE: Page_12]

416   The simulations of the downstream Aveyron watershed indicated that widespread introduction of [SOURCE: Page_19]

417   cover crops with autumn termination would have little influence on water withdrawals, river flows, or [SOURCE: Page_19]

418   water-resource management. Even though cover crops decrease autumn drainage, terminating them [SOURCE: Page_20]

419   before winter provides sufficient time for soil water to recharge during winter and thus has no [SOURCE: Page_20]

420   influence on water availability for the succeeding cash crop or its yield, and thus little influence on [SOURCE: Page_20]

421   irrigation withdrawals. [SOURCE: Page_20]

422   The simulations indicated slightly more influence when cover crops are sown over a larger area and [SOURCE: Page_20]

423   for longer durations to maximise their production of ecosystem services. Mean irrigation withdrawals [SOURCE: Page_20]

424   over the 10 years increased by only 2%. The influence on decreasing drainage was larger, however, [SOURCE: Page_20]

425   which is consistent with many studies (Meyer et al., 2019, 2022). Although river flows decreased [SOURCE: Page_20]

426   sharply during the summer in the reference scenario, cover crops in the Short-CC and Long-CC [SOURCE: Page_20]

427   scenarios did not decrease river flows because they had little influence on withdrawals, and they [SOURCE: Page_20]

428   influenced drainage especially in winter. [SOURCE: Page_20]

429   Finally, combining long-duration cover crops with crop diversification seems to be a good compromise [SOURCE: Page_20]

430   for quantitative water management. Diversifying crops, notably by replacing maize with crops that [SOURCE: Page_20]

431   require less water, reduces the total water demand and compensates for potential negative effects of [SOURCE: Page_20]

432   cover crops with late termination. Diversifying the crop rotation increased withdrawals in early [SOURCE: Page_20]

433   summer due to more crops with greater water requirements in early summer (especially wheat), which [SOURCE: Page_20]

434   increased irrigation. However, irrigation withdrawals decreased in July, early August, and the autumn, [SOURCE: Page_20]

435   due to less area with maize, which requires more water than the crops introduced in the diversified [SOURCE: Page_20]

436   rotations (i.e. wheat, soya bean, and sorghum). Cover crops had little influence on mean withdrawals, [SOURCE: Page_20]

437   river flows, or the number of days with flow below the low-flow target over the 10 years simulated. [SOURCE: Page_20]

438   However, the Long-CC&div scenario increased variability among years, with some dry years having a [SOURCE: Page_20]

439   stronger influence, which can become a problem for water management in these years. Finally, [SOURCE: Page_20]

440   drainage decreased mainly in autumn because irrigation of the crops introduced into the rotations [SOURCE: Page_20]

441   stopped earlier in the summer that it had for maize, resulting in drier soil at the end of summer and [SOURCE: Page_20]

442   thus less drainage in autumn. [SOURCE: Page_20]

443 [SOURCE: Page_20]

444   4.2 Adapting management to limit the negative influence of cover crops [SOURCE: Page_21]

445   Although cover crops have little influence at the watershed scale, their influence does depend on the [SOURCE: Page_21]

446   production situation (i.e. soil, climate, and cropping system). At the field scale, cover crops before non- [SOURCE: Page_21]

447   irrigated maize on shallow soils (hillsides) tends to have more influence on maize’s yield. In the Aveyron [SOURCE: Page_21]

448   watershed, these earlier maize cultivars are sown in shallow soils on hillsides and are irrigated less [SOURCE: Page_21]

449   than later maize cultivars sown along the rivers because irrigation water is more difficult to obtain. In [SOURCE: Page_21]

450   the Aveyron watershed, approximately 55% of fields of earlier maize cultivars (1-3) are irrigated, [SOURCE: Page_21]

451   compared to 70-80% of those of later maize cultivars (4-6). However, the early cultivars cover less area [SOURCE: Page_21]

452   in the watershed: 50, 170, and 300 ha for maize 1, 2, and 3, respectively, compared to 1400, 1000, and [SOURCE: Page_21]

453   100 ha for maize 4, 5, and 6, respectively. Thus, for most of the years simulated, introducing cover [SOURCE: Page_21]

454   crops during fallow periods decreased yields only slightly. Thus, the negative influence on yields [SOURCE: Page_21]

455   occurred mainly in particularly dry years for non-irrigated maize on shallow soils, which covers a [SOURCE: Page_21]

456   relatively small area of the watershed. From an economic viewpoint, as shown by Bergtold et al. (2019) [SOURCE: Page_21]

457   and Cai et al. (2019), the costs of cover crops need to be balanced against the benefits to properly [SOURCE: Page_21]

458   assess their economic interest. Accordingly, the benefits (e.g. green manure effect) and costs of [SOURCE: Page_21]

459   introducing cover crops (e.g. purchase of seeds, additional tillage) must be considered for complete [SOURCE: Page_21]

460   evaluation of the influence of cover crops at the regional scale. Also, for more integrated assessment [SOURCE: Page_21]

461   of the influence of cover crops, indicators related to nitrogen cycles, carbon cycles, and greenhouse [SOURCE: Page_21]

462   gas emissions should also be assessed. A version of MAELIA that simulates water, nitrogen, carbon, [SOURCE: Page_21]

463   and economic indicators at the watershed scale is under development to perform this kind of multi- [SOURCE: Page_21]

464   criteria evaluation. [SOURCE: Page_21]

465   A previous study highlighted that cover crops with late termination are strongly discouraged in dry [SOURCE: Page_21]

466   regions to avoid the risk of water (and nitrogen) pre-emption, which could decrease the yield of the [SOURCE: Page_21]

467   succeeding cash crop (Alonso-Ayuso et al., 2014). As recommended in the literature (Ewing et al., 1991; [SOURCE: Page_21]

468   Justes et al., 2012), care was taken in the Long-CC scenario to select a termination date long enough [SOURCE: Page_21]

469   before the sowing date of the succeeding cash crop to avoid influencing soil water availability at [SOURCE: Page_21]

470   sowing. Analysis at the watershed scale indicated that, given the trend for rainy springs in this region, [SOURCE: Page_22]

471   the mean termination date was early enough to allow soil water to recharge before sowing the spring [SOURCE: Page_22]

472   crop. However, for non-irrigated maize on shallow soils, the termination dates chosen did not seem [SOURCE: Page_22]

473   conservative enough to ensure adequate yields. Shorter-duration cover crops should be planted in [SOURCE: Page_22]

474   these production situations. [SOURCE: Page_22]

475   In the Long-CC scenario, late termination of cover crops required changing management practices, [SOURCE: Page_22]

476   notably by delaying tillage (i.e. autumn ploughing postponed to spring) for clay soils. Doing so can be [SOURCE: Page_22]

477   difficult due to the positive influence of frost on soil structure after tillage in some soils (Leuther and [SOURCE: Page_22]

478   Schlüter, 2021), however the beneficial effects of cover crops on the soil and the reduction of erosion [SOURCE: Page_22]

479   (Blanco-Canqui and Ruis, 2020) can counterbalance this effect. More generally, terminating cover [SOURCE: Page_22]

480   crops in late winter delayed spring tillage dates slightly, which then delayed the sowing of spring crops, [SOURCE: Page_22]

481   sometimes by several weeks, particularly on shallow soils. Indeed, in MAELIA, the termination of cover [SOURCE: Page_22]

482   crops in late winter implies additional technical management to be carried out at this time, and which [SOURCE: Page_22]

483   are also conditioned by a sufficient bearing capacity of the ground (IF-THEN decision rules). Thus, some [SOURCE: Page_22]

484   years, the bearing capacity may not be sufficient and thus delay the technical managements (cover [SOURCE: Page_22]

485   crop termination, soil tillage and/or sowing of spring crop), in addition to the time also necessary for [SOURCE: Page_22]

486   their realization. This later sowing of spring crops often shifted crop growth and its associated water [SOURCE: Page_22]

487   requirements to a period with less precipitation (e.g. August), which explains the increased irrigation [SOURCE: Page_22]

488   withdrawals, slightly decreased river flows, and decreased yields, especially for early cultivars in dry [SOURCE: Page_22]

489   years. [SOURCE: Page_22]

490   Long-duration cover crops tend to influence management practices by delaying sowing of spring crops [SOURCE: Page_22]

491   and decreasing yields locally. In our scenarios, management practices were assumed to be the same [SOURCE: Page_22]

492   for the entire watershed, but they should be adapted for each farm to limit potential negative effects [SOURCE: Page_22]

493   of long-duration cover crops on irrigation withdrawals and yield for succeeding cash crops. Delayed [SOURCE: Page_22]

494   sowing is common in conservation agriculture and remains compatible with crop temperature [SOURCE: Page_22]

495   requirements. A compromise exists to be found among a cover crop’s duration, the ecosystem services [SOURCE: Page_22]

496   it provides (e.g. carbon storage), and optimal dates for the succeeding cash crop, knowing that the [SOURCE: Page_23]

497   final weeks of a cover crop strongly influence the amount of biomass it produces. [SOURCE: Page_23]

498 [SOURCE: Page_23]

499   4.3 Integrated assessment of the influence of cover crops [SOURCE: Page_23]

500   Well-managed cover crops (i.e. with the species and sowing and termination dates adapted to the [SOURCE: Page_23]

501   location) could be an effective way to provide many ecosystem services such as reducing nitrate [SOURCE: Page_23]

502   leaching, green manure effect, increasing carbon storage in soil or decreasing erosion risks... Our [SOURCE: Page_23]

503   results show that long-duration cover crops have little influence on water at the watershed scale, but [SOURCE: Page_23]

504   a more negative influence in certain production situations. However, given the many services that [SOURCE: Page_23]

505   cover crops can provide in these situations, multi-criteria assessment could be useful for assessing [SOURCE: Page_23]

506   costs and benefits of introducing them. From an economic viewpoint, the benefits of ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_23]

507   services for farmers (e.g. green manure effect) could outweigh potential negative effects. Accordingly, [SOURCE: Page_23]

508   economic impacts on cash crop yields and the additional costs of introducing cover crops (e.g. purchase [SOURCE: Page_23]

509   of seeds, additional tractor passes) must be considered for complete evaluation of the influence of [SOURCE: Page_23]

510   cover crops at the regional scale. Also, for more integrated assessment of the influence of cover crops, [SOURCE: Page_23]

511   indicators related to nitrogen cycles, carbon cycles, and greenhouse gas emissions should also be [SOURCE: Page_23]

512   assessed. A version of MAELIA that simulate water, nitrogen, carbon, and economic indicators at the [SOURCE: Page_23]

513   watershed scale is under development to perform this kind of multi-criteria evaluation. [SOURCE: Page_23]

514 [SOURCE: Page_23]

515   4.4 Additional scenarios [SOURCE: Page_23]

516   Allain et al. (2018) and Tribouillois et al. (in revision) highlight that optimising irrigation has the most [SOURCE: Page_23]

517   influence on decreasing irrigation withdrawals. Accordingly, simultaneously introducing long-duration [SOURCE: Page_23]

518   cover crops, diversifying rotations, and optimising irrigation could be a promising way to meet [SOURCE: Page_23]

519   objectives for ecosystem services, yields, and conservation of water resources. As Obiang Ndong et al. [SOURCE: Page_23]

520   (2020) suggested, it would be interesting to define and simulate new scenarios of cover crop [SOURCE: Page_23]

521   management that could reduce their negative impacts on water drainage (blue water) and available [SOURCE: Page_23]

522   water for succeeding crops, such as harvesting cover crop biomass before the termination date. As a [SOURCE: Page_24]

523   step further, Therond et al. (2017) recommended designing scenarios that combine optimisation, [SOURCE: Page_24]

524   diversification, and management-based mechanisms to explore the ability to maximise the supply a [SOURCE: Page_24]

525   balanced bundle of ecosystem services under current and future climate conditions. [SOURCE: Page_24]

526   Finally, our results and conclusions are valid under the specific conditions of the downstream Aveyron [SOURCE: Page_24]

527   watershed: climate (e.g. precipitation, evapotranspiration), soil types, cropping systems, and [SOURCE: Page_24]

528   management practices. The conclusions could differ for regions with different soils and climate, [SOURCE: Page_24]

529   farming systems, and/or specific constraints (e.g. little or no access to water). It would be interesting [SOURCE: Page_24]

530   to investigate these scenarios for contrasting sites to see how the influence of cover crops, diversified [SOURCE: Page_24]

531   rotations, and other key mechanisms differ among regions. [SOURCE: Page_24]

532 [SOURCE: Page_24]

533       5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_24]

534   The MAELIA model showed that cover crops, even with late termination, generally had relatively few [SOURCE: Page_24]

535   negative effects on water at the watershed scale. Even introducing long-duration cover crops to [SOURCE: Page_24]

536   maximise ecosystem services had little influence on mean irrigation withdrawals (+2%) or irrigation [SOURCE: Page_24]

537   restrictions, and decreases in yields were generally small and occurred only in dry years. However, soil [SOURCE: Page_24]

538   water availability for non-irrigated maize on shallow soils can be more sensitive to cover crops. In these [SOURCE: Page_24]

539   situations, it is important to adapt the management practices of long-duration cover crops to avoid [SOURCE: Page_24]

540   impacting succeeding cash crops. [SOURCE: Page_24]

541   Finally, simultaneously introducing long-duration cover crops and diversifying crop rotations, as a [SOURCE: Page_24]

542   stronger pathway of agroecological transition, can compensate for the potentially negative influence [SOURCE: Page_24]

543   of long-duration cover crops on water flows. The results of the associated scenario illustrate that an [SOURCE: Page_24]

544   agroecological transition can generate solutions that improve water resource conservation in regions [SOURCE: Page_24]

545   with a strong water imbalance due to irrigation. This integrated assessment and modelling study can [SOURCE: Page_24]

546   be extended by exploring more ambitious scenarios based on combining optimisation, management, [SOURCE: Page_25]

547   and agroecological changes to develop a balanced bundle of ecosystem services to farmers and society. [SOURCE: Page_25]
