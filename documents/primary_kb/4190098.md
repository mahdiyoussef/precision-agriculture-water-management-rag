27   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   Due to the more frequent use of crop models at regional and national scale, the effects of spatial data input [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   resolution have gained increased attention. However, little is known about the influence of variability in crop [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   management on model outputs. A constant and uniform crop management is often considered over the simulated [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   area and period. This study determines the influence of crop management adapted to climatic conditions and input [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   data resolution on regional-scale outputs of crop models. For this purpose, winter wheat and maize were simulated [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   over 30 years with spatially and temporally uniform management or adaptive management for North Rhine- [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   Westphalia (~34 083 km²), Germany. Adaptive management to local climatic conditions was used for 1) sowing [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   date, 2) N fertilization dates, 3) N amounts, and 4) crop cycle length. Therefore, the models were applied with four [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   different management sets for each crop. Input data for climate, soil and management were selected at five [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   resolutions, from 1×1 km to 100×100 km grid size. Overall, 11 crop models were used to predict regional mean [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   crop yield, actual evapotranspiration, and drainage. Adaptive management had little effect (<10 % difference) on [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   the 30-year mean of the three output variables for most models and did not depend on soil, climate, and [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   management resolution. Nevertheless, the effect was substantial for certain models, up to 31 % on yield, 27 % on [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   evapotranspiration, and 12 % on drainage compared to the uniform management reference. In general, effects [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   were stronger on yield than on evapotranspiration and drainage, which had little sensitivity to changes in [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   management. Scaling effects were generally lower than management effects on yield and evapotranspiration as [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   opposed to drainage. Despite this trend, sensitivity to management and scaling varied greatly among the models. [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   At the annual scale, effects were stronger in certain years, particularly the management effect on yield. These [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   results imply that depending on the model, the representation of management should be carefully chosen, [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   particularly when simulating yields and for predictions on annual scale. [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   Large-scale assessment studies based on simulations by crop models are frequently used to evaluate the impacts [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   of agriculture. These studies usually focus on predictions of crop production in different contexts, such as climate [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   change, its inter-annual variability, or trends over time (Gaiser et al., 2010; Nendel et al., 2013). Crop models are [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   also used to study carbon sequestration or the greenhouse gas balance at regional or national scale (Gaiser et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   2009, 2008; Tornquist et al., 2009). Other studies focus on the water balance and its dynamics at the watershed [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   scale. For the latter, crop models are combined with other models (e.g., hydrological) and applied to quantitative [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   water management and irrigation issues (Noory et al., 2011; Robert et al., 2018; Therond et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   Crop models are useful tools for large-scale assessment since exhaustive measurements are not feasible or [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   available. However, they were developed to simulate homogeneous fields, each represented by a combination of [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   one soil and one climate. Some of these models were designed to simulate only one season, e.g. one crop and its [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   management, while others are capable of simulating different crops in sequence, mimicking a crop rotation over a [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   longer time period (Kollas et al., 2015). When applied at a larger scale, these models are usually applied in a [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   gridded approach, simulating each grid cell independently, while assuming homogeneity within each grid cell (De [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   Wit et al., 2012; Huang et al., 2015; Mo et al., 2005; van Ittersum et al., 2013). For such approach, it is necessary [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   to provide input data for soil, climate, and management for each simulated unit. Depending on the study and the [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   systems’ heterogeneity, the number of homogeneous units can range from a few to millions. Such data, especially [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   management data, are not easily available at large scales and at high spatial or temporal resolution. Several [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   methods exist to scale-up the data over the whole study area, such as sampling, aggregation from fine to coarser [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   resolution, extrapolation or interpolation of the available data (Ewert et al., 2011). As an alternative, management [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   information can also be simulated for large-scale studies (Hutchings et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   Nowadays, it is possible to obtain soil and climate data at a relatively high resolution and at a large or even global [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   scale from databases such as those in the Global Soil Map project (http://globalsoilmap.net/), the European soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   portal for soil, the SoilGrids project (soilgrids.org) and the international CORDEX initiative for climate projection [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   (https://www.euro-cordex.net/). On the other hand, the available databases on crop management data are at [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   coarser resolutions such as those reported by Portmann et al. (2010) and Sacks et al. (2010) for crop growing [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   periods or earthstat.org for fertilizer inputs. Usually, the few data available on crop management come from [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   interviews with farmers, local experts, or observation networks. It provides an average date of sowing, harvest, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   fertilization for instance or fertilizer input amounts for a given region for different crops and generally concern only [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   one or a few years. Some initiatives such as the observation network of the German weather service DWD [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   documenting key phenological stages as well as sowing and harvest could provide useful data for regional [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   modelling (Kersebaum and Nendel, 2014) but do not cover the wide range of cultivation operations such as nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   fertilization for instance. As a result, large-scale studies usually consider management as uniform across the region [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   and fixed over multiple years. However, it is well known that crop management, such as sowing, varies over space [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   and time (Leenhardt and Lemaire, 2002). Additionally, the sowing date significantly impacts crop development and [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   yield (Bonelli et al., 2016), and influences subsequent management actions during season. [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   To address the scarcity of the data and to adapt the management to the local and annual conditions, some authors [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   suggested using management rules. Such management rules aim at reproducing the behavior of farmers and their [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   crop management strategies (Maton et al., 2005; Nendel, 2009; Senthilkumar et al., 2015). In addition, these rules [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   would help identify better management strategies. For example, suitable climate and soil conditions could be [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   identified to perform cultivation operations (e.g., avoiding soil compaction by triggering an operation when the soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   is not too wet or avoiding the risk of frost for spring crops). This adaptive management, based on management [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   decision rules, could have a strong impact on model outputs but is rarely investigated at a large scale. Since the [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   impact of input data aggregation and adaptive management can differ according to the output variables and crop [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   models, these effects should be investigated with respect to a range of different crop models, output variables, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   cultivation operations (i.e. sowing, soil tillage, irrigation…). [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   The objective of this study was to analyze the effect of adaptive management and spatial resolution on regional [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   yields, evapotranspiration, and drainage predicted by a set of crop models. The main issues addressed were (1) [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   whether adaptive management and/or input resolution influence the crop models’ outputs at the regional scale, in [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   which way and how much and (2) whether the scaling effect varies when management changes over time and [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   space. [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   To meet this goal, we quantified the impact of adaptive management and input resolution on the regional mean of [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   simulated yield, evapotranspiration, and drainage for each individual year as well as for the 30-year average. We [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   further analyzed whether the impact of management or spatial resolution depended on the crop model, output of [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   interest, crop, or cultivation operation. To do so, we introduced adaptive management for sowing dates, fertilization [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   dates, and crop maturity classes based on decision rules and variable amounts of nitrogen fertilization. [SOURCE: Page_6]

384   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_18]

385   4.1. Management and scaling effect on the 30-year regional mean [SOURCE: Page_18]

386   At the multi-year scale over 30 years, the scaling and management effects were weak for most models, crops and [SOURCE: Page_18]

387   outputs, even if significant. The scaling effect results confirm the results of previous studies on the impact of soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

388   and climate aggregation on yield and net primary productivity (NPP) for the same study site and simulation period [SOURCE: Page_18]

389   (Hoffmann et al., 2016b; Kuhnert et al., 2016). Further, our results indicate that varying management options over [SOURCE: Page_18]

390   space and time in the region did not change the overall findings made when assuming constant management. [SOURCE: Page_18]

391   Nevertheless, the scaling effect depended on the output variable, being larger for drainage than for yield or [SOURCE: Page_18]

392   evapotranspiration when compared between the five models simulating the three output variables. The impact of [SOURCE: Page_18]

393   the choice of the crop (winter or spring crop) on the other hand was negligible. The stronger scaling effect on [SOURCE: Page_18]

394   drainage (observed for models providing the three outputs) and the direction of its difference was probably due to [SOURCE: Page_18]

395   the choice of the dominant soil when moving from high to lower resolution. Lowering the resolution of soil input data [SOURCE: Page_18]

396   resulted in an increase in the total soil water storage because deep soils were dominant in the region, which induced [SOURCE: Page_18]

397   lower drainage. Grosz et al. (2017) also observed the scaling effect on predictions of change in soil organic carbon [SOURCE: Page_18]

398   over time, which depend greatly on soil input data. In the same way, Coucheney et al. (2018) showed that the [SOURCE: Page_18]

17 [SOURCE: Page_18]

399   sensitivity to scaling was output-dependent with a greater effect of soil aggregation on soil C mineralization and N [SOURCE: Page_19]

400   leaching than on yield and drainage for the CoupModel. [SOURCE: Page_19]

401   The maximum management effect tended to be higher than the maximum scaling effect, with 42 vs. 10 % of the [SOURCE: Page_19]

402   cases in which differences compared to the reference were greater than 10 %, respectively. The management [SOURCE: Page_19]

403   effect varied among models, with most 30-year regional mean outputs being slightly sensitive to management [SOURCE: Page_19]

404   (absolute difference below 10%). This was particularly true for evapotranspiration of both crops, drainage and wheat [SOURCE: Page_19]

405   yield, regardless of the input resolution. The stronger effect on yield could be partly due to the use of percentage [SOURCE: Page_19]

406   to quantify the effect. Since average yields are much lower than evapotranspiration and drainage, a small variation [SOURCE: Page_19]

407   lead to a higher percentage for this output. However, for the scaling effect the effect was strongest on drainage. [SOURCE: Page_19]

408   The management effect tended to be higher on maize than on winter wheat yield for most models, suggesting a [SOURCE: Page_19]

409   greater impact of management on spring crops than on winter crops. This result seems consistent with the shorter [SOURCE: Page_19]

410   growing season of spring crops, leaving less time to compensate a late sowing for instance. The hypothesis of a [SOURCE: Page_19]

411   higher sensitivity of spring crops should be tested with other crops such as sunflower or soybean. For some models [SOURCE: Page_19]

412   (2-4 models), different representation of management changed the 30-year regional mean substantially (by more [SOURCE: Page_19]

413   than 15% for yield and for evapotranspiration depending on the resolution and crop), indicating the need to carefully [SOURCE: Page_19]

414   choose how to represent management in these crop models to obtain relevant multi-year regional means. Contrary, [SOURCE: Page_19]

415   management choices seemed less important for the 30-year regional drainage, (showing less than 13 % difference [SOURCE: Page_19]

416   in all management sets). [SOURCE: Page_19]

417   4.2. Stronger effects at the annual scale [SOURCE: Page_19]

418   The same trend occurred at the annual scale as for the 30-year regional mean: the management effect was usually [SOURCE: Page_19]

419   higher than the scaling effect, with large differences among models. The management effect as well as the scaling [SOURCE: Page_19]

420   effect on the regional mean were stronger for certain years than for others. This indicates that the choices made to [SOURCE: Page_19]

421   represent management are more important when studies focus on annual regional outputs than on multi-year [SOURCE: Page_19]

422   average regional outputs. This importance varied among models and, depending on the model, the cultivation [SOURCE: Page_19]

423   operation considered. Hereby, it is crucial to ensure that the chosen model is able to predict effects of a given [SOURCE: Page_19]

424   management strategy, such as sowing date, to accurately predict variability in the outputs caused by the [SOURCE: Page_19]

18 [SOURCE: Page_19]

425   management changes. If the management strategy has a substantial effect on the output variable of interest, the [SOURCE: Page_20]

426   uncertainty due to the choice of management option in the simulation should be estimated. [SOURCE: Page_20]

427   Since the years with large effects on management options or scaling differed among models, it is difficult to identify [SOURCE: Page_20]

428   which characteristics of the years that interact with the models to generate the more or less strong effects. No effect [SOURCE: Page_20]

429   of climate characteristics such as a dry or hot year effects was found in the analyses. The effect were strongly [SOURCE: Page_20]

430   model-dependent, the same year predictions being sensitive to scaling or management effect for some models but [SOURCE: Page_20]

431   not for others. No generic characteristics of the input data could be identified; the effect being probably due to a [SOURCE: Page_20]

432   model-soil-climate interaction. This difference between crop model outputs behavior is probably partly due to model [SOURCE: Page_20]

433   structures as well as their parametrization, their the relative contribution being unclear. Hereby, sensitivity analysis [SOURCE: Page_20]

434   performed in individual studies of each model could be helpful to understand model behavior and to determine [SOURCE: Page_20]

435   characteristic input-output relationships (Specka et al., 2015; Varella et al., 2012). It could then clarify the major [SOURCE: Page_20]

436   factors behind model differences with respect to the occurrence of strong effects of management strategies in [SOURCE: Page_20]

437   specific years. [SOURCE: Page_20]

438   4.3. Representation of management strategies in large-scale studies [SOURCE: Page_20]

439   We used decision rules to generate management options based on climatic conditions. We then compared [SOURCE: Page_20]

440   simulations based on these management options with those of uniform and fixed sowing, harvest, and fertilization [SOURCE: Page_20]

441   dates over one region over multiple years. In general, uniform sowing, harvest, and fertilization dates as well the [SOURCE: Page_20]

442   use of a single cultivar are an unrealistic representation of common management at the regional scale. Folberth et [SOURCE: Page_20]

443   al. (2016) showed that in model-based global scale assessments, absolute yield levels depend on the [SOURCE: Page_20]

444   parameterization and distribution of crop cultivars. However, it is still commonly applied in large-scale modelling [SOURCE: Page_20]

445   studies since real data are often scarce (Faivre et al., 2004). The advantage of using decision rules is that it provide [SOURCE: Page_20]

446   a management, which is consistent with local climate and soil as compared to fixed assumptions. These can also [SOURCE: Page_20]

447   be used to simulate changes in management over time due to climate change (Senthilkumar et al., 2015). One [SOURCE: Page_20]

448   limitation is that the same decision rules are used for all grid cells, while different farmers apply different rules for [SOURCE: Page_20]

449   crop management (Maton et al., 2005) depending on their social, economic, and pedoclimatic conditions. Decisions [SOURCE: Page_20]

450   rules based on an optimal strategy according to climatic conditions could lead to overestimated yields. Moreover, [SOURCE: Page_20]

19 [SOURCE: Page_20]

451   not taking into account soil characteristics could also lead to unrealistic management in some cases. Since the [SOURCE: Page_21]

452   purpose of this study was to evaluate if management choices had an impact on regional output variables, these [SOURCE: Page_21]

453   concerns were not of critical importance. To get more realistic data on management at large scale, remote sensing [SOURCE: Page_21]

454   could add useful information on crop type (Griffiths et al., 2019), sowing and harvest dates, or irrigation schedules [SOURCE: Page_21]

455   (Battude et al., 2017). Here, we analyzed the potential impact of choosing a variable management to predict the [SOURCE: Page_21]

456   difference in crop model outputs compared to a reference based on a spatially uniform management fixed in time. [SOURCE: Page_21]

457   The access and use of observed management data for the entire region to validate the relevance and accuracy of [SOURCE: Page_21]

458   the decision rules, would improve assessments of the role and effect of management input data and resolution for [SOURCE: Page_21]

459   simulations at regional scale. It could be relevant to include other cultivation operations, such as soil tillage or [SOURCE: Page_21]

460   irrigation, depending on the outputs of interest. For instance, irrigation is important when water balance is the focus [SOURCE: Page_21]

461   of the simulation study, particularly in southern Europe. [SOURCE: Page_21]

462   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_21]

463   In our regional-scale study, we showed that the management effect was generally stronger than the scaling effect. [SOURCE: Page_21]

464   The strength of the effects depended on the crop model and the output variable of interest, with some models and [SOURCE: Page_21]

465   output variables being much more sensitive to management options than others. Scaling and management effects [SOURCE: Page_21]

466   were also stronger when evaluated on individual years than on the 30-year mean, for which these effects were [SOURCE: Page_21]

467   usually weak. The effects varied both between models and among years. Strong impacts occurred but not [SOURCE: Page_21]

468   necessarily during the same years for all models, which indicates a need for further analysis with respect to each [SOURCE: Page_21]
