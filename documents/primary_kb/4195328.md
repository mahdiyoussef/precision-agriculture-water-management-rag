1   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

2   Cover crops harvested at a low maturity stage generally have a high moisture content, which [SOURCE: Page_2]

3   may generate energy losses during silage storage via effluent production and undesirable [SOURCE: Page_2]

4   fermentations. This paper investigates the use of different waste types as absorbent co- [SOURCE: Page_2]

5   substrates to be added before ensiling. The relation between the absorbent water holding [SOURCE: Page_2]

6   capacity and silage effluent volume was first studied to find an effective parameter to prevent [SOURCE: Page_2]

7   effluent production. Effluent retention was found to be proportional to the absorbent loading [SOURCE: Page_2]

8   and water holding capacity (r^2 = 0.98) and up to 90% of effluent production was avoided when [SOURCE: Page_2]

9   compared to control (295 L.t-1). The impact of different co-substrates (including bio-waste and [SOURCE: Page_2]

10   manures) on overall ensiling performances was then investigated at an optimized absorbent [SOURCE: Page_2]

11   loading. All co-substrates allowed a total effluent retention while a 76 L.t-1 effluent volume was [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   reported for the control. The silage fermentation was modified or mostly unchanged depending [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   on the co-substrate chemical and microbial properties and different metabolic pathways were [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   observed (e.g. homolactic or butyric fermentation). In most conditions, the methane potential [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   of the crop was efficiently preserved over a storage of 60 days. Co-ensiling was shown to be a [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   relevant silage preparation method for biogas production. [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   1   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   Cover crops, also called catch crops or intermediary crops, are receiving an increasing interest to [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   be used as substrates for anaerobic digestion. These crops are cultivated during the intercultural [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   period of main crops and bring agro-ecological services by limiting soil erosion, nitrate leaching [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   and the need for mineral fertilizers (Molinuevo-Salces et al., 2013). In agricultural biogas sector, 2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   cover crops allow to produce a large amount of biomass for methane production, without [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   competing with the production of food or feed crops. Due to the management of crop rotations, [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   cover crops are usually harvested once or twice a year. Since the biogas digesters are fed [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   continuously, an efficient storage of the crops from their harvest to their final use in anaerobic [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   digesters is mandatory. The storage of fresh fodder or energy crops is commonly performed by [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   ensiling (Teixeira Franco et al., 2016). This storage process relies on the fermentation of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   substrate, mostly by lactic acid bacteria. In the anaerobic environment of the storage silos, the [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   water soluble carbohydrates (WSC) are fermented into lactic acid and other metabolites, [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   causing a pH drop to about 4 (Woolford and Pahlow, 1998). The combination of acidic pH and [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   anaerobic environment then ensures the inhibition of microbial activity over the whole storage [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   duration. [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   The performance of ensiling greatly depends on silo confection practices and crop [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   characteristics (Teixeira Franco et al., 2016). Among the key parameters, the total solids (TS) [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   content of the crop has a major influence on storage performances. For biogas production, a TS [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   range from 26 to 36% has been reported to be optimal (Villa et al., 2020). Silage production [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   from lower TS crops may lead to fermentation pathways dominated by Clostridia, characterized [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   by butyric acid and H$_2$ production (Woolford and Pahlow, 1998). In silage production for animal [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   feeding, clostridial fermentations are highly undesirable because of the possible development of [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   pathogenic microorganisms, poor palatability and decrease in feed value (Woolford and Pahlow, [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   1998). In silage production for biogas production, such issues are less relevant, but clostridial [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   fermentation is reported to induce energy losses due to the production of H$_2$ (Kreuger et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   2011). However, the overall effect of clostridial development in silage for biogas remains to be [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   investigated, since energy losses may be compensated by an increase in lignocellulose [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   hydrolysis and biomass degradability (Cui et al., 2020). Another issue with the silage of low TS [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   crops is the high production of liquid effluents (Gebrehanna et al., 2014). For TS values lower [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   than 25%, hundreds of liters of effluent can seep per ton of ensiled crop (Bastiman and Altman, [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   1986; Sutter, 1957). If not collected and valorized properly, silage effluents may cause methane [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   potential losses (Teixeira Franco et al., 2016) and surface water pollution (Holly et al., 2018). In [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   addition, silage effluents can generate significant odor nuisance (Keck et al., 2018) that greatly [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   threatens the local acceptance of biogas plants (Schumacher and Schultmann, 2017). They can [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   also cause corrosion of steel and concrete (Koenig and Dehn, 2016). As a consequence, silage [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   effluent production is reported to be highly undesirable in biogas plants. [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   Because they are grown during short periods and/or with unfavorable weather conditions, [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   cover crops are often harvested at a low maturity stage which is associated to a low TS. TS of [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   less than 20% are commonly met in cover crops (Molinuevo-Salces et al., 2013) and significant [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   volumes of effluents are expected during the storage of such crops. In order to avoid this issue, [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   various practices can be adopted, such as field wilting or the addition of an absorbent co- [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   substrate. Field wilting consists in a partial open-air drying of the freshly mowed crop prior to its [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   storage and can result in an efficient increase in TS when proper conditions are met (Borreani et [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   al., 2009). This method is however weather-dependent and unsuitable conditions may make [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   wilting inappropriate (Teixeira Franco et al., 2017). The addition of a dry co-substrate presents [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   the advantage of being independent from weather conditions and co-ensiling a wet organic [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   matter with a substrate having absorbent properties allows to immediately increase the TS of [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   the pre-silage mixture and thus avoid the effluent production (Jones and Jones, 1996). In the [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   literature, diverse co-substrates have been studied like the straw and stalks of different crop [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   species (Haigh, 1998; Razak et al., 2012), rice, barley and wheat bran (Haigh, 1999; Razak et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   2012), dried beet pulp (Fransen and Strubi, 1998; Haigh, 1999; Razak et al., 2012), ground barley [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   (Khorvash et al., 2006), dry bean hulls (Razak et al., 2012), newspaper (Fransen and Strubi, [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   1998), sodium bentonite (Fransen and Strubi, 1998; Khorvash et al., 2006; Woolford et al., 1983) [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   and various polymers (Fransen and Strubi, 1998; Healy et al., 1997), with different liquid holding [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   efficiencies. These co-ensiling experiments were mainly applied for silage production for animal [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   feeding and thus most of the tested co-substrates are edible. In the context of anaerobic [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   digestion, the strategy for selecting co-substrates is different, since as an example, high value [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   edible substrates like ground barley or dried beet pulp should be avoided. On the other hand, [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   non-edible substrates compatible with anaerobic digestion can be considered. [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   This study focuses on the use of waste-like and/or agricultural byproducts as absorbent [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   substrates in ensiling prior to anaerobic digestion. The first objective was to evaluate the [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   behavior of a well-studied absorbent substrate (wheat straw) as a co-ensiling substrate with [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   sunflower, in order to define the adequate co-substrate loading. In a second part, the influence [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   of the addition of various co-substrates (soiled paper and cardboard, wood chips, miscanthus [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   straw, chicken manure and horse manure) on the ensiling of rye was investigated. Production of [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   effluent, metabolites and gas as well as the influence on microbial community structure and the [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   preservation of methane potential were analyzed during two months of storage, in order to [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   propose a global understanding of the addition of co-substrate on silage storage. [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   2.2   Silage storage experiments [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   2.2.1 Experimental setups [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   Two different storage protocols were used, depending on the objective. [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   For the monitoring of silage effluent production, the experiments were conducted in mini-silos [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   made of PVC pipe of 100 mm inner diameter and 800 mm height (6.3 L volume), in a similar [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   configuration to that described by Savoie et al (2002). The filling was carried out by adding, in a [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   successive way, layers of approximately 200 mm of loose catch crop which were compacted [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   using a hydraulic press (20-ton capacity, KS Tools GmbH) until the targeted packing density. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   packing density was applied as recommended by Latsch and Sauter (2014) (Fresh matter basis – [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   FM): 800 kg.m-3 for 20% TS forage ; 700 kg.m-3 for 25% TS forage ; 650 kg.m-3 for 30% TS forage. [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   Packing density of intermediate levels of TS were calculated by linear interpolation. The silos [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   were closed, and two tamps allowed to measure the gas volume and composition and to collect [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   the liquid effluent at the bottom. In order to avoid air penetration, liquid effluent drainage was [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   performed at most once a day. The effluent was characterized as described in section 2.2.4. All [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   storage experiments were started on the same day and operated for 2 months. For each storage [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   condition, two replicates were prepared in independent silos. The silos and their content were [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   weighted before and after storage. [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   For the dynamic monitoring of silage fermentation, 2.6L glass bottles were used, as previously [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   described (Van Vlierberghe et al., 2021). This storage method was used in addition to storage in [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   PVC silos for the co-ensiling of rye, in order to allow a higher number of replicates for dynamic [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   monitoring of storage. For each storage condition, several replicates were prepared to be [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   sacrificed after 2, 7, 21 and 60 days of storage in order to carry out a complete physico-chemical [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   and microbial analysis of the samples during the ensiling process as described in section 2.2.5. [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   A 60-day storage period was chosen as sufficient for fine effluent and fermentation [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   characterization. Previous studies of the literature reported that the peak flow of effluent [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   production usually occurs during the first days of storage, with 90% of the total volume being [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   usually released during the 20 to 26 first days of storage (Mayne and Gordon, 1986). Concerning [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   fermentation, most of the transformations that may occur usually happen during the first weeks [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   of storage (Woolford and Pahlow, 1998). [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   2.2.2 Co-ensiling of sunflower with straw with variable absorbent loading [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   A first round of experiments was only conducted in tubular PVC silos. Five different ratios of [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   wheat straw were tested: 0, 40, 80, 150 and 250 g·kgsunflower-1, on a fresh matter basis. In each [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   reactor, 3 kgFM of sunflower were added, and the amount of wheat straw was adapted in order [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   to match with the desired loading. A first layer of wheat straw was added and packed, and [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   sunflower was then added on top, and packed (protocol called “bottom”). A second protocol, [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   named “mixed”, was also carried out for the ratio of wheat straw of 40 g·kg-1; sunflower and [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   straw were homogenized before filling into the silos. 6 different storage conditions were thus [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   tested. [SOURCE: Page_8]

227   3.1    Sample characterization [SOURCE: Page_13]

228   The main characteristics of the crops and co-substrates are presented in Table 1. Sunflower and [SOURCE: Page_13]

229   rye were characterized by a low TS content of 14.6 and 21.1%, respectively. The expected [SOURCE: Page_13]

230   volume of effluent produced according to Bastiman and Altman (1986) and Sutter (1957) [SOURCE: Page_13]

231   estimations were 187 and 345 mL·kg-1 for sunflower and 57 and 199 mL·kg-1 for rye, [SOURCE: Page_13]

232   respectively. The average estimated volume were calculated and corresponded to 138 and 266 [SOURCE: Page_13]

233   mL·kg-1 for oat and rye, respectively. Co-ensiling substrates were characterized by higher TS [SOURCE: Page_13]

234   contents, with value ranging from 53 (horse manure) to 92.7 % (miscanthus stalks). The WHC of [SOURCE: Page_13]

235   the different co-substrates ranged from 0.8 (chicken manure) to 2.1 mL.g-1 (soiled paper and [SOURCE: Page_13]

236   cardboard), which confirmed their ability to be used as absorbent materials. These values are [SOURCE: Page_13]

237   lower than other found in the literature. For example, WHC of 2.3 – 2.8 mL.g-1 were reported for [SOURCE: Page_13]

238   miscanthus stalks (Dennery G., Dezat E., 2012) whereas they were around 3 for wheat straw [SOURCE: Page_13]

239   (Razak et al., 2012). This difference may be explained by the protocol used in the present work [SOURCE: Page_13]

240   (centrifugation with a draining material), ensuring that no water remained in the interstitial [SOURCE: Page_13]

241   spaces of the substrate. The two crops had C/N ratios of 22.2 and 22.1 g.g-1 for rye and [SOURCE: Page_13]
