11   Abstract (400 words max) [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   The future climate is projected increasingly rougher for the European forest ecosystems, likely leading [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   to decline episodes for many tree species. Forest management has to be adapted to cope with the [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   negative effect of these changes. Despite the close interactions between nutrients and water in plant [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   physiology, very few studies have investigated the link between soil fertility and tree response to soil [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   water availability and vapour pressure deficit. This study’s goal was to test whether fertilization might [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   modify the forest trees resilience to soil water deficit. [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   The potential impact of fertilization on trees during soil water deficit was assessed from a fertilization [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   experiment carried on sessile oaks. Three fertilization treatments (NCa, NPKCaMg and control) were [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   applied on 40-year-old oaks that were cored almost 50 years later. The drought response of fertilized [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   and control trees were compared by analysing the radial growth chronologies according to two [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   complementary approaches. Firstly, the relationships between radial growth and climate interannual [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   variations were investigated through dendroclimatic modelling including water deficit indices [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   computed with the daily soil water balance model Biljou©. Secondly, resistance, recovery and [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   resilience to drought-induced crises were evaluated from tree-ring width and compared between [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   treatments. In addition, the carbon isotope composition (13C) of tree rings was measured to evaluate [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   potential differential gas-exchange processes between fertilized and control trees. [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   Fertilization had an immediate and strong positive effect on oak growth that rapidly faded. NCa and [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   NPKCaMg fertilizations had similar effects suggesting that adding P, K and Mg had no more impact on [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   radial growth. Whatever the treatment, ring width variations appeared to be identically constrained [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   by climate, especially by summer water deficit of the current and previous years. The tree resistance [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   to soil water deficit was negatively affected by the severity of the drought event but did not [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   significantly differ between control and fertilized trees. The tree resilience was not affected by [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   fertilization and remained stable and close to 1 across crises. The tree-ring δ13C was clearly increased [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   by the severe 1976 water deficit but did not vary between treatments. The possible mechanisms [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   involved in growth change and the lack of significant effect on resilience to drought events observed [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   after fertilization are discussed. [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

43 [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   Most scenarios for future climate project that the increase in global mean surface temperature due to [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   anthropogenic emissions will be accompanied in Europe by an augmentation in intensity and [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   frequency of severe climate events like drought episodes (IPCC 2013). Intense or recurrent drought [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   events have long been identified as a major contributing factor of tree decline and mortality (Bréda et [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   al. 2006, Carnicer et al. 2011, Sergent et al. 2014). To avoid widespread forest declines, and their [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   catastrophic ecological and economic consequences, forest management practices have to adapt the [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   modern forest to the ongoing environmental changes. Many studies had already focused on forest [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   management showing that thinning is efficient to mitigate soil water deficit (Bréda et al. 1995, Misson [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   et al. 2003, Sohn et al. 2013). Mixing species in forest stands also appeared to be beneficial to limit [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   growth reduction under drought event, yet depending on the species combination (Pretzsch et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   2013, Thurm et al. 2016). Lately, a few observations mentioned a lower drought resistance for trees [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   growing on poor soils (Royo & Knight 2012, Sergent et al. 2014) putting in question the interest of soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   fertilization as a management option alleviating drought impact on forest trees. [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   As recently pointed out by Gessler et al. (2017), the role of soil fertility in drought survival of forest [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   trees has been surprisingly unstudied. Several nutrients have been identified to be involved in plant [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   drought response, generally from studies on herbaceous species or on tree species at sapling stages. [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   For instance, K is known as an important actor of stomatal control (Sardans et al. 2015) and Ca was [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   found to play a major role in cavitation resistance (Herbette & Cochard 2010). However, because of [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   numerous interactions between functions, or organs, these specific pieces of knowledge cannot be [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   readily assembled to forecast the overall effect of soil nutrients fertility on mature tree response to [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   drought. For example, higher N availability was associated with higher resistance to cavitation but this [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   could not compensate for concomitant lower root-to-leaf area ratio, and resulted in overall higher [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   probabilities of hydraulic failure in drought conditions (Ewers et al. 2000, Bucci et al. 2006). [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   Radial growth variations have long been considered as an integrative proxy of tree functioning and [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   were frequently studied in a context of soil water deficit (Becker et al. 1994, Bréda & Granier 1996). [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   Many studies also reported the effect of nutrient additions on radial growth (Spiecker 1991, Sikstrom [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   1997, Long et al. 2011) and at least one of them evaluated the influence of climate variations on [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   fertilization effect (Van der Perre et al. 2012). However, to our knowledge, no study tried yet to [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   determine whether fertilization could help to mitigate drought negative impacts on forest trees. In [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   addition to ring width, the carbon isotopes composition (13C) of tree rings, which integrates the [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   variations in gas exchange, provides a valuable indicator of tree water status (Brooks & Mitchell 2011) [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   and has already been used to monitor the tree physiological response to fertilization (Balster et al. [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   2009, Brooks & Coulombe 2009). Here again, no study related yet the effect of drought × fertilization [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   interaction on tree-ring 13C, but when 13C measurements were performed in the leaves, the [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   differences between N-fertilized and control trees did not appear to be weather dependant (Betson et [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   al. 2007). [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   Sessile oak (Q. petraea (Matt.) Liebl.) is a major species of the Western Europe forests, usually [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   considered as relatively drought tolerant (Friedrichs et al. 2009b). However, projections of its future [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   potential range under moderate warming push it away from its current distribution area by the end of [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   the century (Hanewinkel et al. 2013). In the past 50 years, several fertilization experiments were [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   conducted in France to increase its productivity. The current study took advantage of one of the oldest [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   trials, making retrospectively available 50-year long radial growth chronologies where both fertilization [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   and water deficit events took place. [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   The objectives of this study were to determine whether soil fertilization (NCa or NPKCaMg) could: i) [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   improve oak radial growth, ii) modify the relationship between growth and climate and iii) change [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   negatively or positively the resistance, recovery and resilience of oak trees to drought. To address [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   these questions, drought events were quantified by daily soil water balance modelling and relate to [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   interannual ring width variations. Growth chronologies were also used to calculate resistance, recovery [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   and resilience (Lloret et al. 2011) to drought-induced crises. In addition, the tree-ring 13C was [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   measured to evaluate potential differential gas-exchange processes between fertilized and control [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   trees. [SOURCE: Page_5]

99    2.1. Site and experimental design [SOURCE: Page_6]

100 [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   The study was carried in an experimental site set up in 1969 in a sessile oak stand located in the forest [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   of Bercé, France (N 47° 48’ 23.19’’, E 0° 23’ 17.63’’; alt. 153 m asl). The trial was composed of nine 0.33- [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   ha square plots, distributed in three blocks (see Garbaye et al. 1974 for a complete description). Each [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   block included three modalities of fertilization: 1) NCa, 2) NPKCaMg and 3) control. In August 1969, [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   1500 kg.ha-1 of CaO from slaked lime, 200 kg.ha-1 of P2O$_5$ from scories, and 34 kg.ha-1 and 120 kg.ha-1 [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   of MgO and K2O, respectively, from Patentkali® were spread by hand. Nitrogen was supplied as [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   ammonitrate in May 1970 (120 kg N.ha-1) and May 1971 (100 kg N.ha-1). The stand was about 40 years [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   old at the year of the fertilization. [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   The soil is a stagnic luvisol, with a relatively low pH (3.7<pH$_0$.5cm<4.0), a high C/N (23.2 at 0-5cm depth) [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   and a low Ca content (<0.10 mmolc.l-1) (Garbaye et al. 1974, Bakker 1998). [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   2.2. Sampling and measurements [SOURCE: Page_6]

113 [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   In each of the nine plots, five dominant trees were cored to the pith at breast height in April 2008. Two [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   cores were sampled from each tree: one intended for ring width measurements, the other one for [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   isotopes measurements. Tree-ring width was measured from the bark to the pith to the nearest 1/100 [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   mm. Ring-reading mistakes were checked and corrected by crossdating the cores according to the [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   procedure described in Becker et al. (1994). During the crossdating step, each individual chronology [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   was compared to the average chronology (N=44) where 27 pointer years were identified from 1933 to [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   2007. [SOURCE: Page_6]

121 [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   The 13C measurements were performed on four trees of each of the three blocks of the NCa and [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   control treatments. However, because of a poor quality core, only 11 trees were considered in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   control treatment instead of 12 in the NCa treatment. The assessment was limited to a period starting [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   five years before the fertilization event and ending five years after the drought event of 1976. Thus, [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   on each of the available 23 cores, all the rings corresponding to the years 1965 to 1980 were separated [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   with a scalpel under a microscope. From each ring, only the latewood was kept and placed for 48 hours [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   in a 50°C oven. The dried samples were finely ground with a ball mill (MM200, Retsch GmbH, Haan, [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   Germany) before a 1±0.1 mg aliquot was weighted in a 4x^6 tin capsule (Elemental Microanalysis [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   Limited, Devon, UK). The samples were analysed with a continuous-flow elemental analyzer (vario [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   ISOTOPE cube, Elementar, Hanau, Germany) coupled with an isotope mass spectrometer (IsoPrime [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   100, Isoprime Ltd, Cheadle,UK). Measurements were performed at the INRA facility in Functional [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   Ecology (SILVATECH OC 081). Stable carbon isotope ratio was expressed as the 13C/12C ratio relative to [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   the Pee Dee Belemnite standard (δ13C; ‰) The precision of the δ13C measurements, calculated as the [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   standard deviation of 48 repetitions of a lab working standard, was 0.04‰. [SOURCE: Page_7]

186   2.4. Resistance, Recovery and Resilience calculations [SOURCE: Page_10]

192   the capacity of trees to grow after a drought event. It is calculated as the ratio of growth after crisis to [SOURCE: Page_10]

194   calculated as the ratio of post- to pre-crisis growth. Ring widths during, before and after drought were [SOURCE: Page_10]

195   averaged over one to ten years to calculate the per-, pre- and post-crisis of growth, depending on the [SOURCE: Page_10]

196   duration of the drought event and of the periods between two successive drought events; Table 2 [SOURCE: Page_10]

207 [SOURCE: Page_11]

208   As no age trend was observed on ring width over the studied period and in order to prevent any [SOURCE: Page_11]

232   3.1. Radial growth differences between fertilized and control trees [SOURCE: Page_12]

233 [SOURCE: Page_12]

234   Compared to the control trees, the fertilized trees showed an increase in radial growth that started [SOURCE: Page_12]

347   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_20]

348 [SOURCE: Page_20]

349   4.1 The fertilization had an immediate positive effect on radial growth that rapidly attenuated [SOURCE: Page_20]

350 [SOURCE: Page_20]

351   The supply of fertilizers dramatically increased the radial growth of the oak trees. Contrary to other [SOURCE: Page_20]

352   forest trials that often displayed a slightly delayed and gradual growth enhancement (Lebourgeois et [SOURCE: Page_20]

353   al. 1993, Picard et al. 1999), the growth increase started the growing season that immediately followed [SOURCE: Page_20]

354   the fertilization. Nitrogen fertilization generally has a positive and rapid effect in stimulating tree [SOURCE: Page_20]

355   growth (Spiecker 1991, Becker 1992, Sikstrom 1997, Brooks & Coulombe 2009) but depressive effect [SOURCE: Page_20]

356   were also observed (Picard et al. 1999). This enhancing effect is usually short-termed (Sikstrom 1997) [SOURCE: Page_20]

357   likely because new limiting factors (e.g. nutrients, light) rapidly appear (Elvir et al. 2006). On the [SOURCE: Page_20]

358   contrary, the improving effect of liming (e.g. Ca addition) in deficient soils classically occurs after [SOURCE: Page_20]

359   several years and lasts a longer time (Spiecker 1991, Long et al. 2011). In the slightly Ca-deficient soil [SOURCE: Page_20]

360   we studied, it then appeared that the fertilization effect on radial growth was dominated by the [SOURCE: Page_20]

361   relatively important quantity of supplied N (i.e. 220 Kg N.ha-1). The Ca supply may have prevented the [SOURCE: Page_20]

362   acidifying effect of N addition although the slake lime form was likely not the most efficient buffer. The [SOURCE: Page_20]

363   lack of significant differences in growth between treatments in 1976 and 1977 was also consistent with [SOURCE: Page_20]

364   observations that NPP response to N-fertilization nullified in dry years (Lim et al. 2015). The addition [SOURCE: Page_20]

365   of P+K+Mg to the NCa dose (similar in both fertilization treatments) had no additional effect on radial [SOURCE: Page_20]

366   growth, likely because the availability of these elements was optimal in the original soil and remained [SOURCE: Page_20]

367   optimal after the addition of the NCa dose. [SOURCE: Page_20]

368 [SOURCE: Page_20]

369   4.2 The fertilization did not change the growth response to drought [SOURCE: Page_20]

370 [SOURCE: Page_20]

371   One of the most severe water deficit observed in Europe in the late decades (i.e. the 1976 drought) [SOURCE: Page_21]

372   occurred only six years after the fertilization application, while its effect was still clearly visible on the [SOURCE: Page_21]

373   radial growth. Yet, the drought response of fertilized and control trees, assessed from two different [SOURCE: Page_21]

374   and complementary approaches, appeared identical. [SOURCE: Page_21]

375   By relating the ring width variations to the climatic observations, the first approach (cf. 3.2) led to the [SOURCE: Page_21]

376   identification of the principal variables constraining the radial growth. Summer soil water deficits of [SOURCE: Page_21]

377   the current and previous years were the main factors controlling the ring width of the studied trees, [SOURCE: Page_21]

378   as it is commonly observed on sessile oaks (Lebourgeois et al. 2004; Friedrichs et al. 2009a, 2009b; [SOURCE: Page_21]

379   Michelot et al. 2012; Trouvé et al. 2014). In addition to climate variables, the between-year variations [SOURCE: Page_21]

380   in ring width also appeared to be influenced by the previous-year growth, confirming the importance [SOURCE: Page_21]

381   of lag effects on oak radial growth (Lebourgeois et al. 2004, Misson et al. 2004) that cannot be [SOURCE: Page_21]

382   summarized by the only water deficit. [SOURCE: Page_21]

383   The relative contributions of these different variables to the dendroclimatic model applied to the three [SOURCE: Page_21]

384   treatments were rather similar (Tab. 3, Fig. 4). The slightly better goodness of fit observed for the [SOURCE: Page_21]

385   control trees was expected, since the variable selection was performed on this population only. The [SOURCE: Page_21]

386   real duration of the fertilization effect on the tree physiology was difficult to evaluate. Its legacy on [SOURCE: Page_21]

387   radial growth ceased before the second climatic crisis occurred, in 1989. However, the leaf chemical [SOURCE: Page_21]

388   contents of the NPKCaMg trees were still different from the control ones in 2014 (Durand et al. [SOURCE: Page_21]

389   submitted). Depending on the effective durability of the fertilization, considering the whole 1970-2007 [SOURCE: Page_21]

390   period in the dendroclimatic analysis could have dampened the potential treatment effects. [SOURCE: Page_21]

391   In the second approach (cf. 3.3), each climatic crisis was considered independently. However, even if [SOURCE: Page_21]

392   a gradual and significant increase in resistance was observed over the four crises that followed the [SOURCE: Page_21]

393   fertilization event, fertilized and control trees displayed equivalent performances (Fig. 5). This [SOURCE: Page_21]

394   resistance increase from crisis n°3 to crisis n°6 was likely mainly due to a concurrent decrease in [SOURCE: Page_21]

395   drought intensity (characterized by the variable SWD678max in this study). The expected negative [SOURCE: Page_21]

396   relationship observed between resistance and drought intensity (also seen in Zang et al. 2014 on other [SOURCE: Page_22]

397   tree species) appeared weaker for the control than the fertilized trees but the identification of these [SOURCE: Page_22]

398   differences was based on a very restricted number of observations (n=6 and 4, respectively). The [SOURCE: Page_22]

399   recovery indexes mirrored the resistance ones, as already observed (Zang et al. 2014; Gazol et al. [SOURCE: Page_22]

400   2017), by showing a positive significant relationship with SWD678max but no differences between [SOURCE: Page_22]

401   fertilized and control trees. Because of the way the indexes are calculated, the fact that recovery and [SOURCE: Page_22]

402   resistance behave oppositely, logically involves that the growth levels were the same before and after [SOURCE: Page_22]

403   the crisis, thus that the resilience value is 1. Thus, consistently with the observations made on [SOURCE: Page_22]

404   resistance and recovery, the average resilience appeared remarkably stable over the whole studied [SOURCE: Page_22]

405   period for the three treatments. This is coherent with the observations of Merlin et al. (2015) or Trouvé [SOURCE: Page_22]

406   et al. (2017), the latter observing that the dominant trees of Q. petraea stands resumed their pre-1976 [SOURCE: Page_22]

407   growth level between 1978 and 1980. Such a stable resilience was generally mentioned for species [SOURCE: Page_22]

408   growing in their optimal ecological conditions (Abies alba and/or Fagus sylvatica in Zang et al. 2014; [SOURCE: Page_22]

409   Metz et al.. 2016; Diaconu et al. 2017, Vitali et al. 2017) and contrasted with those observed for species [SOURCE: Page_22]

410   growing at the edge of their distribution range (Picea abies in Zang et al. 2014; Lloret et al. 2011). [SOURCE: Page_22]

411 [SOURCE: Page_22]

412   4.3 Which processes led to the increase of radial growth without changing the responses to drought [SOURCE: Page_22]

413   and climate? [SOURCE: Page_22]

414 [SOURCE: Page_22]

415   Based on the short lasting effect on radial growth and on the absence of differences between the two [SOURCE: Page_22]

416   fertilization modalities (NCa and NPKCaMg), we hypothesized that growth stimulation resulted mainly [SOURCE: Page_22]

417   from nitrogen inputs in this study. The simultaneous input of Ca was planned to prevent any [SOURCE: Page_22]

418   acidification processes and associated nutrients loss to occur. As N is paradoxically both a common [SOURCE: Page_22]

419   limiting factor for plant productivity and a pollutant deposited worldwide, its effect on plant physiology [SOURCE: Page_22]

420   have been largely studied. Yet, increasing the N availability affects many different plant processes that [SOURCE: Page_22]

421   can compensate each other, especially on large-size and long-lived plants as forest trees. Identifying [SOURCE: Page_23]

422   the processes that ended to increase the radial growth of the oak trees without changing their [SOURCE: Page_23]

423   response to drought is thus not straightforward. Water relations, water-use efficiency then carbon [SOURCE: Page_23]

424   allocation are successively discussed below. [SOURCE: Page_23]

425   In the current study, the 13C of the rings built after the fertilization event were not significantly [SOURCE: Page_23]

426   different between the NCa-fertilized and the control trees (although slightly higher in fertilized trees). [SOURCE: Page_23]

427   It suggested that (i) the fertilization did not change the leaf gas exchanges or (i) that stomatal [SOURCE: Page_23]

428   conductance and photosynthesis rate changed in parallel. Such a lack of significant effect of N [SOURCE: Page_23]

429   fertilization on tree-rings 13C was already observed on Pseudotsuga menziesii (Balster et al. 2009) or [SOURCE: Page_23]

430   Picea abies (Krause et al. 2012) although, in most cases, N-fertilization was found to induce a short- [SOURCE: Page_23]

431   time increase in 13C, being interpreted as an increase in photosynthesis (Brooks & Mitchell 2011) or a [SOURCE: Page_23]

432   reduction in stomatal conductance (Brooks & Coulombe 2009). As a decrease in C assimilation [SOURCE: Page_23]

433   (required if a decrease in stomatal conductance is considered) is not likely to occur in fertilized trees, [SOURCE: Page_23]

434   we hypothesize that the fertilization, in the current study, either did not change the leaf gas exchange, [SOURCE: Page_23]

435   or led to equivalent increases in C assimilation and stomatal conductance. When water availability is [SOURCE: Page_23]

436   not limiting, the increase in leaf area index (LAI) generally observed in response to N inputs could be [SOURCE: Page_23]

437   accompanied with (Green & Mitchell 1992, Clearwater & Meinzer 2001) or without (Samuelson et al. [SOURCE: Page_23]

438   2014; Krause et al. 2012) an increase of the photosynthetic rate (resulting from an increase of the total [SOURCE: Page_23]

439   amount of Rubisco). Both changes would lead to an increase in the tree-level C assimilation that can [SOURCE: Page_23]

440   explain the observed increase in radial growth. As a matter of fact, several studies tend to show that [SOURCE: Page_23]

441   the stimulated growth is the result of a two-phases response, with an initial increase in photosynthetic [SOURCE: Page_23]

442   rate and growth efficiency followed by a longer phase of elevated LAI and C allocation changes (Brooks [SOURCE: Page_23]

443   & Coulombe 2009; Lim et al. 2015). When water availability is limited, a higher (N-induced) LAI, by [SOURCE: Page_23]

444   increasing rainfall interception and quantity of transpired water, tends to accentuate drought intensity [SOURCE: Page_23]

445   and cause hydraulic imbalance. N-fertilized trees are thus expected to decrease their stomatal [SOURCE: Page_23]

446   conductance, which turns into a 13C increase, in a first step (Brooks & Coulombe 2009, Samuelson et [SOURCE: Page_23]

447   al. 2018). After a few years, trees eventually recovered their hydraulic balance by adjusting leaf and [SOURCE: Page_24]

448   sapwood area to the available water supply. In the current study, it is noticeable that the slight (but [SOURCE: Page_24]

449   not significant) increase in 13C occurred on fertilized oaks during years without water deficit and [SOURCE: Page_24]

450   disappeared in 1976. [SOURCE: Page_24]

451   Coming to carbon allocation, Gessler et al. (2017) suggested that a high nutrient availability might have [SOURCE: Page_24]

452   a detrimental effect on tree drought recovery because of preferential biomass allocation aboveground. [SOURCE: Page_24]

453   Indeed, increasing the quantity of available Ca has been shown to decrease the C allocation to the root [SOURCE: Page_24]

454   system, thus favouring the allocation to aboveground biomass (Fahey et al. 2016). Regarding N [SOURCE: Page_24]

455   availability, its effect on fine root dynamics is still controversial with some results showing an increase [SOURCE: Page_24]

456   in root production (Shi et al. 2017) and turn-over with N availability and others showing no systematic [SOURCE: Page_24]

457   changes (Beets & Whitehead 1996, Dziedek et al. 2016) or even decrease (Nadelhoffer 2000). Even if [SOURCE: Page_24]

458   fertilization does not change root production, the N-induced stimulation of the aboveground [SOURCE: Page_24]

459   production generally leads to a decrease of the C fraction allocated to the roots (Beets & Whitehead [SOURCE: Page_24]

460   1996; Ewers et al. 2000; Lim et al. 2015). [SOURCE: Page_24]

461   In addition to these possible changes in the distribution of growth between above- and below-ground [SOURCE: Page_24]

462   compartment, fertilization could also change C allocation between growth and other functions, [SOURCE: Page_24]

463   particularly respiration, storage or reproduction. Indeed, rather than an augmentation of carbon [SOURCE: Page_24]

464   assimilation, the increase of growth could be explained by a diminution of carbon loss by respiration. [SOURCE: Page_24]

465   However, as the fertilization is known to improve leaf production and increase N storage in tissues, [SOURCE: Page_24]

466   both maintenance and growth respirations are also expected to increase (Manter et al. 2005). The [SOURCE: Page_24]

467   fertilization could also decrease the C amount dedicated to storage (Ludovici et al. 2002; Goodsman et [SOURCE: Page_24]

468   al. 2010), reproduction and defence functions (Hoft et al. 1996) for the benefit of the radial growth. In [SOURCE: Page_24]

469   the experiment carried at Bercé, though, the fertilization had enhanced the acorn production (Garbaye [SOURCE: Page_24]

470   & Leroy 1974). It can therefore be concluded that under the conditions of the Bercé site, fertilization [SOURCE: Page_24]

471   temporarily stimulates aerial growth without inducing imbalances that could modify resilience and [SOURCE: Page_25]

472   resistance to severe drought episodes. [SOURCE: Page_25]

475   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_25]

476 [SOURCE: Page_25]

477   The objectives of the study were to determine if soil fertilization (NCa or NPKCaMg) can i) improve [SOURCE: Page_25]

478   trees growth, ii) modify the relationship between growth and climate and iii) change negatively or [SOURCE: Page_25]

479   positively the resistance, recovery and resilience of trees to drought. The radial growth of the sessile [SOURCE: Page_25]

480   oaks increased dramatically the first year following fertilization, peaked the year after then declined [SOURCE: Page_25]

481   progressively for the next six years when fertilized and control trees eventually converged. These [SOURCE: Page_25]

482   results suggested that fertilization led to a temporary increase in carbon acquisition (or aboveground [SOURCE: Page_25]

483   production) and that the involved morphological and physiological changes did not alter the capacity [SOURCE: Page_25]

484   of trees to cope with drought. The resilience of the sessile oaks in Bercé, a site with high soil available [SOURCE: Page_25]

485   water, fertilized and control alike, was high and rapid confirming the drought tolerant behaviour of [SOURCE: Page_25]

486   this species (Bréda et al. 1993). From this study, it is not possible to conclude that the fertilization could [SOURCE: Page_25]

487   not improve the oak resilience. Such a conclusion could only be drawn from experimental designs [SOURCE: Page_25]

488   where the resilience of control trees is lessened, for instance because of drier conditions (i.e. lower [SOURCE: Page_25]

489   available water) or poorer soil fertility than those encountered in Bercé. It is however noteworthy here [SOURCE: Page_25]

490   that fertilization increased wood production without weakening trees in their response to drought. [SOURCE: Page_25]
