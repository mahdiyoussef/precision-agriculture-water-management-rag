34   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   Rainfed Mediterranean agriculture must adapt to water scarcity due to climate change and pressures [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   on water resources. According to recent literature, two adaptation solutions based on the concept of [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   diversification can be explored. The first solution is crop diversification at the field level. Three [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   main cropping systems, namely agroforestry, intercropping and service crops, have been shown to [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   increase soil water availability and to improve crop water use. The second solution is to consider [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   diversification at the landscape level by diversifying crops and associated agricultural management [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   practices (both in number, abundance and spatial organization) and building small-scale water [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   harvesting infrastructures. In order to move towards a sustainable Mediterranean agriculture, one of [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   the main scientific challenges ahead is to provide knowledge and tools, such as integrated agro- [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   hydrological models, useful to evaluate several spatiotemporal combinations of these solutions in [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   order to optimize soil water availability and crop water use. [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   1.     Introduction [SOURCE: Page_5]

49   Mediterranean agriculture (MA) has developed over the millennia by benefiting from mild to hot [SOURCE: Page_5]

50   temperatures and adequate insolation. The population has shaped a very heterogeneous biophysical [SOURCE: Page_5]

51   environment (soil, topography, biodiversity) by designing landscapes and agricultural practices [SOURCE: Page_5]

52   adapted to successive periods of drought and heavy rainfall. The Mediterranean diet is recognized [SOURCE: Page_5]

53   worldwide, based on emblematic crops such as olives, grapes, fruits, cereals and legumes (as a [SOURCE: Page_5]

54   substitute for animal protein) ([1], Figure 1). MA is an essential economic sector and employs a [SOURCE: Page_5]

55   large part of the population. For instance, agriculture in Maghreb contributes 10 - 20% of Gross [SOURCE: Page_5]

56   domestic product (GDP) and employs 13 - 20% of the working population [2]. Agriculture is the [SOURCE: Page_5]

57   primary user of water in the Mediterranean region. Crop irrigation is for instance responsible for up [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   to 79% of the total water withdrawals in Eastern Mediterranean countries [3]. [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   Climate change accentuates for MA the challenge of maintaining or even increasing production to [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   feed a growing population, while simultaneously preserving natural resources such as soil, water, [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   and biodiversity that are vulnerable to climate hazards and anthropogenic pressures. This also [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   requires addressing potential yield gaps that may exist. Climate projections show that average [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   temperatures will increase from 0.5° to 5.6°C, depending on the scenario, and that rainfall will [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   continue to decrease from April to September, by about 4% per °C of global warming [4]. [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   Sustainable management of the scarce water resource in a warmer and drier climate is one of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   most crucial issues facing MA. Based on climate projections and crop modeling, water and heat [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   waves are, and will continue to be, key limiting factors for crop production [4]. The sustainability of [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   the Mediterranean food system will therefore depend on the sustainability of MA in a context of [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   water scarcity. [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   Adapting MA to water scarcity has already been extensively studied in the field of irrigation, [SOURCE: Page_6]

72   including new irrigation practices and technologies. Alternative water resources such as the reuse of [SOURCE: Page_6]

73   treated wastewater and desalinated water have also been increasingly used [4]. However, new [SOURCE: Page_6]

74   margins of progress also exist for Mediterranean rainfed agriculture in the framework of the agro- [SOURCE: Page_6]

75   ecological transition, to optimize the use of rainfall for agricultural production. Based on recent [SOURCE: Page_6]

76   literature, we aim to demonstrate the potential of diversification, considered at two distinct spatial [SOURCE: Page_6]

77   levels, in facilitating the adaptation of MA to water scarcity. Firstly, at the field level, [SOURCE: Page_6]

78   diversification can be achieved through practices such as intercropping, agroforestry, and service [SOURCE: Page_6]

79   crops. Secondly, at the landscape level, diversification involves diversifying crops and agricultural [SOURCE: Page_6]

80   management practices both in terms of their number, abundance and spatial organization, as well as [SOURCE: Page_6]

81   building small-scale water harvesting infrastructures with optimal density and topology. [SOURCE: Page_6]

87    2.       Field diversification [SOURCE: Page_7]

88    Field diversification has received increasing attention in recent years to build resilience to climate [SOURCE: Page_7]

89    change and to improve resource-use efficiency and ecological performances of agrosystems in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

90    Mediterranean [6]. Field diversification is a process that transforms simplified agrosystems into a [SOURCE: Page_7]

91    more biologically and technically diverse state [7]. It consists of enhancing plant genetic, functional [SOURCE: Page_7]

92    and/or structural diversity within cultivated fields to regulate plant competition and promote [SOURCE: Page_7]

93    complementarity and cooperation effects resulting from ecological differences among plants, [SOURCE: Page_7]

94    usually leading to overperformance (e.g., overyielding) compared to sole crops [8]. Plant diversity [SOURCE: Page_7]

95    is also targeted to reduce risks regarding environmental adversity through compensation effects [SOURCE: Page_7]

96    among plants with different environmental sensitivities. It can be achieved by multiple means (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_7]

97    mixing varieties, species, growth forms, functional types etc.) and supported by appropriate [SOURCE: Page_7]

98    management practices (e.g., plantation design, sowing dates, tree pruning, reduced tillage, etc.) [9]. [SOURCE: Page_7]

99    Mediterranean regions benefit from an extremely rich heritage of diversity of crops and trees, [SOURCE: Page_7]

100   including under-utilized or associated non-cultivated plant species, representing a unique [SOURCE: Page_7]

101   opportunity to support field diversification [10]. In the context of water scarcity, we present here [SOURCE: Page_7]

102   three main approaches for field diversification, namely (1) intercropping, (2) agroforestry, and (3) [SOURCE: Page_7]

103   service crops, that would be promising for the future of MA although they would need pursuing [SOURCE: Page_7]

104   research and engineering efforts in specifically addressing plant diversity-water relations at the field [SOURCE: Page_7]

105   level. [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   Intercropping [SOURCE: Page_8]

108   Intercropping or polyculture is defined as the cultivation of two or more different crop species [SOURCE: Page_8]

109   simultaneously in the same field during a growing season. In the Mediterranean, intercropping is an [SOURCE: Page_8]

110   ancient practice consisting of mixtures of grain or fodder cereals (e.g., barley, durum wheat) and [SOURCE: Page_8]

111   legumes (e.g., chickpea, fava bean), and to a lesser extent, of other arable crops. Intercropping has [SOURCE: Page_8]

112   recently gained increased interest due to its potential to increase climate resilience of agriculture [SOURCE: Page_8]

113   [11], and its well-demonstrated advantage over monocultures in terms of resource efficiency, crop [SOURCE: Page_8]

114   yield [12], quality [13], stability [14], and provision of ecosystem services [15]. [SOURCE: Page_8]

115          Several mechanisms are involved in regulating water balance in intercrops as compared to [SOURCE: Page_8]

116   sole crops. When water is a limiting factor to crop production, intercropping can increase crop [SOURCE: Page_8]

117   water uptake and water use efficiency through physiological and ecological mechanisms and [SOURCE: Page_8]

118   optimize the overall soil moisture environment for crops [14]. Better water use efficiency is [SOURCE: Page_8]

119   generally attributed to better water uptake in the soil profile, resulting from the exploitation of [SOURCE: Page_8]

120   denser root systems and complementary spatial root distribution of both intercrop components [SOURCE: Page_8]

121   species [17, 18], and to the delay between species in reaching their maximum water requirement. [SOURCE: Page_8]

122   For instance, in cereal-legume mixtures, the capacity of cereals to use water from deep soil layers [SOURCE: Page_8]

123   when they reach their flowering stage allows associated legumes to use water from shallow layers [SOURCE: Page_8]

124   while they are at their grain-filling stage, limiting water competition during sensitive stages for [SOURCE: Page_8]

125   grain production of both species [19]. Moreover, increased dry matter accumulation rate and [SOURCE: Page_8]

126   reduced water loss through soil evaporation and transpiration and runoff are conducive to better [SOURCE: Page_8]

127   water utilization [20]. [SOURCE: Page_8]

128          However, the choice of crop species and varieties, sowing densities and spatial arrangements [SOURCE: Page_8]

129   are critical to finding the right mix achieving the expected positive outcomes from intercropping. [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   For instance, the lack of complementarity for water can strongly limit yield advantages [21]. A [SOURCE: Page_8]

7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   better understanding of processes underlying crop competition, complementarity, cooperation and [SOURCE: Page_9]

132   compensation regarding water use under future dry conditions will be essential to manage and [SOURCE: Page_9]

133   predict crop growth, water use efficiency, and yield formation in Mediterranean intercropping [SOURCE: Page_9]

134   systems. Crop modeling has the potential to address such issues, but would require further [SOURCE: Page_9]

135   development before being used for intercropping design purposes, mainly because they have been [SOURCE: Page_9]

136   used for sole crops and therefore processes relevant to intercropping like plant competition and [SOURCE: Page_9]

137   plasticity have been only rarely coded so far which could promote complementarity among [SOURCE: Page_9]

138   associated crops [22]. [SOURCE: Page_9]

139 [SOURCE: Page_9]

140   Agroforestry [SOURCE: Page_9]

141   Agroforestry is a type of land use that combines trees and/or shrubs with crops and/or livestock in [SOURCE: Page_9]

142   the same field for forestry, agricultural and pastoral purposes. In the Mediterranean region, [SOURCE: Page_9]

143   agroforestry systems (AFS) have been part of the traditional forms of agriculture for millennia, and [SOURCE: Page_9]

144   consist mainly of silvopasture and fruit tree-based silvoarable agroforestry [23, 24]. AFS are now of [SOURCE: Page_9]

145   increasing interest because planting trees has a series of beneficial outcomes on ecological functions [SOURCE: Page_9]

146   and services within the agricultural field and contributes to strengthening resilience to climate [SOURCE: Page_9]

147   change [25]. [SOURCE: Page_9]

148          Regarding the water cycle, trees act positively on multiple above- and below- ground [SOURCE: Page_9]

149   ecohydrological processes, and they offer the opportunity to preserve water and improve the water [SOURCE: Page_9]

150   balance at the field level. Trees can regulate the local microclimate by providing shade, thus [SOURCE: Page_9]

151   reducing evapotranspiration [10], limiting water loss through soil evaporation, and preventing crops [SOURCE: Page_9]

152   from severe water stress under drought [26]. By digging deep, trees can use water from deep soil [SOURCE: Page_9]

153   reserves usually not accessible to arable crops, making the overall water harvesting more complete, [SOURCE: Page_9]

154   and limiting water and nutrient losses through deep drainage. In addition, trees with deep roots can [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

155   activate several water redistribution mechanisms. They can lift water from deep soil layers up to the [SOURCE: Page_10]

156   upper layers through a process known as hydraulic lift and potentially act as ‘bio-irrigators’ for the [SOURCE: Page_10]

157   underlying crops [27]. In semiarid areas with less than 450 mm of rainfall, hydraulic lift was [SOURCE: Page_10]

158   estimated to provide up to an additional 35-47 mm of water annually, representing around 10% of [SOURCE: Page_10]

159   the total precipitation [28]. By creating alliances with soil microorganisms, trees can facilitate the [SOURCE: Page_10]

160   use of water and nutrients under dry conditions [29]. As a result, AFS were shown to support higher [SOURCE: Page_10]

161   land- and water productivities than monocultures under Mediterranean or semiarid conditions, as [SOURCE: Page_10]

162   recently illustrated by an increase in total productivity of 10-44% in olive- [30], walnut- [31], apple- [SOURCE: Page_10]

163   [32], apricot- [33], jujube- [34] based agroforestry. They also help mitigate the effect of increasing [SOURCE: Page_10]

164   drought on ecosystem services in more extensive pastures [35]. [SOURCE: Page_10]

165          However, the relevance of AFS for a drier future remains controversial in the drylands. [SOURCE: Page_10]

166   Despite promising evidence, the positive outcomes of AFS may be counterbalanced by increased [SOURCE: Page_10]

167   negative interactions between trees and crops with increasing aridity, which are still insufficiently [SOURCE: Page_10]

168   understood and poorly predicted by the modeling tools available to date [36]. Indeed, most of the [SOURCE: Page_10]

169   aforementioned facilitative mechanisms are strongly context-dependent and rely on tree species [SOURCE: Page_10]

170   choices and management. Investigating tree species diversity, especially of native or under-utilized [SOURCE: Page_10]

171   tree species, is needed to explore new species choices and rationalize the selection of trees with the [SOURCE: Page_10]

172   most adapted physiological, phenological and architectural traits to agroforestry under the future [SOURCE: Page_10]

173   drought conditions in the Mediterranean. Management practices like crown and root pruning are [SOURCE: Page_10]

174   additional possibilities to shape tree forms, drive their functioning and optimize tree-crop [SOURCE: Page_10]

175   interactions towards beneficial outcomes, e.g., by regulating shade or root overlap, but such [SOURCE: Page_10]

176   practices are still in their infancy in the agroforestry context [37]. Finally, plantation design and [SOURCE: Page_10]

177   density should also be revisited with possibly unconventional spatial patterns, i.e., not necessarily in [SOURCE: Page_10]

178   rows as in most alley-cropping systems, and with more consideration of environmental [SOURCE: Page_10]

9 [SOURCE: Page_10]

179   heterogeneity within fields to promote the overall complementarity and facilitative effects of trees [SOURCE: Page_11]

180   in AFS. [SOURCE: Page_11]

181 [SOURCE: Page_11]

182   Service crops [SOURCE: Page_11]

183   Service crops, also known as cover crops, are plant species grown in cultivated fields to provide [SOURCE: Page_11]

184   supporting and regulating ecosystem services [38]. In arable agrosystems, services crops are usually [SOURCE: Page_11]

185   sown after cash crop harvest while they are increasingly grown as intercrops in perennial [SOURCE: Page_11]

186   agrosystems, like vineyards, olive groves, and fruit orchards. They have recognized benefits for [SOURCE: Page_11]

187   protecting soils against erosion, nutrient leaching, and loss of organic matter, regulating weeds and [SOURCE: Page_11]

188   pests, and preserving habitats and biodiversity. They are key components of conservation and [SOURCE: Page_11]

189   regenerative agriculture that are developing in Mediterranean countries to face climate change [39]. [SOURCE: Page_11]

190          Recent syntheses have highlighted positive outcomes of service crops regarding the water [SOURCE: Page_11]

191   cycle in the fields. Compared to bare soils, service crops were shown to reduce runoff by 27% [40], [SOURCE: Page_11]

192   deep drainage by 27-32 mm [41], and leaching by 2.5-fold, especially those caused by heavy rain [SOURCE: Page_11]

193   events, which are increasingly frequent in the Mediterranean [42]. They also contribute to reducing [SOURCE: Page_11]

194   soil albedo and evaporation, thus potentially increasing soil water content, mainly through mulching [SOURCE: Page_11]

195   effects, either as live plants or residues left after destruction. In the long term, the improvement of [SOURCE: Page_11]

196   soil organic matter content and soil aggregation promoted by service crops may lead to enhanced [SOURCE: Page_11]

197   rain harvest through improved soil hydraulic properties (e.g., better infiltration) and soil water [SOURCE: Page_11]

198   storage capacity. They have indirect effects on crop water productivity by limiting weed [SOURCE: Page_11]

199   colonization and therefore water competition. [SOURCE: Page_11]

200          However, drawbacks specific to water-limited regions need to be accounted for when [SOURCE: Page_11]

10 [SOURCE: Page_11]

201   introducing service crops under climate change. Below 700 mm of annual rainfall, service crops [SOURCE: Page_12]

202   were shown to reduce soil water content by 18% and subsequent crop yield by 7% on average [43] [SOURCE: Page_12]

203   because water supply cannot compensate for the transpiration of water by service crops. Similarly, [SOURCE: Page_12]

204   the reduction in drainage can have detrimental consequences on groundwater recharge because the [SOURCE: Page_12]

205   observed drainage reduction may be close to the annual recharge potential under such climate [SOURCE: Page_12]

206   conditions [44]. The balance between the positive and negative effects of service crops regarding [SOURCE: Page_12]

207   water use in the Mediterranean climate remains therefore to be quantitatively assessed over short- to [SOURCE: Page_12]

208   long-term periods. This would require more field experiments with service crops and water balance [SOURCE: Page_12]

209   measurements, combined with long-term simulation studies with improved crop models able to [SOURCE: Page_12]

210   simulate a range of non-crop species under varying climatic and soil conditions [45]. [SOURCE: Page_12]

211          To take advantage of synergies between service crops and climate change adaptation, [SOURCE: Page_12]

212   management is key. Several authors recommend being agile in terms of sowing and destruction [SOURCE: Page_12]

213   dates to adjust service crops’ water use and reduction of soil evaporation according to the actual [SOURCE: Page_12]

214   drought conditions and cash crop development stages. In this perspective, techniques and tools are [SOURCE: Page_12]

215   to be developed to regulate service crops' biomass [38] and ensure the desired water-use dynamics. [SOURCE: Page_12]

216   Finally, service crop selection is an essential aspect that requires further work. The choice of service [SOURCE: Page_12]

217   crop species or mixtures based on their functional traits, especially belowground traits, appears to [SOURCE: Page_12]

218   be one relevant approach to design eco-hydrologically performant service crops [46] that are both [SOURCE: Page_12]

219   drought-resilient and drought-facilitative for the main crops. As for trees in agroforestry, exploring [SOURCE: Page_12]

220   local plant biodiversity should be promising [47]. [SOURCE: Page_12]

223   3.     Landscape diversification [SOURCE: Page_12]

11 [SOURCE: Page_12]

224   The term landscape is polysemic and can be ambiguous depending on the context of the study and [SOURCE: Page_13]

225   on the scientific domain. We make our own the definition of Marshall [48], that defines the [SOURCE: Page_13]

226   agricultural landscape as ‘a mosaic of farmers’ fields, semi-natural habitats, human infrastructures [SOURCE: Page_13]

227   (e.g., roads) and occasional natural habitats’ (Figure 2). In what follows, we propose that adapting [SOURCE: Page_13]

228   MA to water scarcity can be achieved through two approaches at the landscape level: 1) [SOURCE: Page_13]

229   diversifying crops and agricultural management practices, and 2) implementing small-scales water [SOURCE: Page_13]

230   harvesting infrastructures. These measures come to increase the heterogeneity of two main [SOURCE: Page_13]

231   properties of landscape. Firstly, the composition that refers to the number and proportion of land- [SOURCE: Page_13]

232   use category and, for agricultural lands, to the number and abundance of crops and agricultural [SOURCE: Page_13]

233   practices; secondly the configuration that refers to the physical distribution and spatial [SOURCE: Page_13]

234   characteristics of land uses, crops and agricultural practices [49]. Landscape diversification involves [SOURCE: Page_13]

235   actually complexifying both composition and configuration [50]. [SOURCE: Page_13]

236 [SOURCE: Page_13]

237   Crop and agricultural practices. We argue that considering the composition and configuration of the [SOURCE: Page_13]

238   landscape in terms of crops and agricultural management practices is a means to adapt agricultural [SOURCE: Page_13]

239   systems to water scarcity. Reasoning involves selecting the appropriate crops and agricultural [SOURCE: Page_13]

240   management practices at the right location in the landscape and time, taking into account the spatio- [SOURCE: Page_13]

241   temporal variability of water fluxes and soil water. This reasoning aims to strike a balance between [SOURCE: Page_13]

242   crop water requirements and soil water availability. Nelson and Burchfield [51] did observe that [SOURCE: Page_13]

243   landscapes with increased complexity across various pedo-climatic conditions in the United States [SOURCE: Page_13]

244   are generally more productive. They also found that crop yields were more influenced by [SOURCE: Page_13]

245   compositional complexity rather than configurational complexity. However, there is currently no [SOURCE: Page_13]

246   direct evidence in the literature linking crop production to landscape diversification under water- [SOURCE: Page_13]

247   scarce conditions. Nonetheless, pieces of evidence have to be found in the correlations, reported in [SOURCE: Page_13]

12 [SOURCE: Page_13]

248   many different climatic and agricultural regions, between crop yield and topographic position [SOURCE: Page_14]

249   within agricultural landscapes [52, 53, 54, 55]. For instance, Ajami et al. [54] observed, in a [SOURCE: Page_14]

250   climatic context similar to the Mediterranean one, that wheat yield ranged from 5.7 to 15.8 ton/ha [SOURCE: Page_14]

251   depending on hillslope position (i.e., shoulder, back-, foot-, and toe-slope). The correlations can [SOURCE: Page_14]

252   vary from year to year depending on weather conditions, and from region to region depending on [SOURCE: Page_14]

253   the hydrological, edaphic and climatic conditions [53, 54]. The spatial structure of crop yield in [SOURCE: Page_14]

254   relation to topography reveals that crop water use efficiency varies spatially as a result of i) the [SOURCE: Page_14]

255   spatial heterogeneity of soil properties (texture, depth, organic matter content,…) due partly to long [SOURCE: Page_14]

256   term soil redistribution by water erosion [56] (Lagacherie et al., 2018), ii) the spatial variability of [SOURCE: Page_14]

257   soil water content as a result of hydrological processes (infiltration, runoff, subsurface flow, [SOURCE: Page_14]

258   evapotranspiration) [57, 58] and iii) the local climate conditions controlled by elevation and [SOURCE: Page_14]

259   exposure, among others [59]. Landscape diversification would aim at reaching the optimal [SOURCE: Page_14]

260   combination in space and time of crops, cultivars and agricultural practices regarding crop yields. [SOURCE: Page_14]

261   Landscape diversification is expected to optimize combinations of measures such as for instance [SOURCE: Page_14]

262   reducing plant density, encouraging the cultivation of drought-resistant crops or varieties in [SOURCE: Page_14]

263   landscape areas with soil prone to drier conditions, conducting soil tillage during the rainy season to [SOURCE: Page_14]

264   enhance water infiltration, or selecting water-demanding crops in areas of water flow convergence [SOURCE: Page_14]

265   and deep soil. This requires consequently a sound understanding of the soil variability, the [SOURCE: Page_14]

266   hydrological cycle and the microclimate variability within the landscape. [SOURCE: Page_14]

267 [SOURCE: Page_14]

268   Water Harvesting Infrastructures (WHI) [SOURCE: Page_14]

269   Small-scale WHI encompasses various linear structures, including terraces, hedgerows, earth bunds [SOURCE: Page_14]

270   and stone barriers [60]. These structures are primarily constructed along topographic contour lines [SOURCE: Page_14]

271   within fields or along field borders (Figure 2). Their purpose is to intercept and facilitate the local [SOURCE: Page_14]

13 [SOURCE: Page_14]

272   infiltration of runoff water into the soil. Additionally, WHI also includes small reservoirs such as [SOURCE: Page_15]

273   check dams, farm ponds and hillslope reservoirs, which supply water to irrigated plots [61, 62]. [SOURCE: Page_15]

274   They capture runoff, store water and may refill shallow aquifers, thus increasing water availability [SOURCE: Page_15]

275   for crops. Most of WHI are also referred to as water conservation systems. Implementing WHI [SOURCE: Page_15]

276   substantially increases crop yields. From a meta-analysis of WHI effects [61], Bouma et al. reported [SOURCE: Page_15]

277   an average 78% increase in crop yields, with larger values in low rainfall years, but with a great [SOURCE: Page_15]

278   variability depending on the study. In addition to increasing yields, the WHI can enhance crop [SOURCE: Page_15]

279   production by helping farmers to convert fallow lands into productive agricultural land [63]. The [SOURCE: Page_15]

280   WHI in Mediterranean regions have always been considered as relevant techniques toward resilient [SOURCE: Page_15]

281   agricultural landscapes facing water scarcity. However, the hydrological functioning of WHI at both [SOURCE: Page_15]

282   local and landscape extents, as well as the impacts of WHI on local crop production, still remain [SOURCE: Page_15]

283   major scientific challenges to be tackled, prior to designing efficient networks of infrastructures [SOURCE: Page_15]

284   within landscapes for the management of water scarcity and crop water demand. [SOURCE: Page_15]

285 [SOURCE: Page_15]

286   Designing sustainable Mediterranean landscapes [SOURCE: Page_15]

287   Agroecosystem modeling can complement field experiments (1) by scaling up impacts from local [SOURCE: Page_15]

288   (field biodiversification, water harvesting infrastructures) to landscape levels and (2) by numerically [SOURCE: Page_15]

289   exploring ex-ante landscape scenarios with varying crop diversification and water harvesting [SOURCE: Page_15]

290   infrastructures [64]. Designing resilient and sustainable Mediterranean landscapes requires [SOURCE: Page_15]

291   agroecosystem models that holistically represent at the landscape level the interactions between [SOURCE: Page_15]

292   crop growth and development, management practices and water circulation, in relation to cropping [SOURCE: Page_15]

293   systems and water harvesting infrastructures. Such models have also to consider soil and climate [SOURCE: Page_15]

294   local variability. The primary shortcomings of current models lie in their failure to integrate the [SOURCE: Page_15]

295   relevant spatial resolution and extent that encompass these interactions. Specifically, these include [SOURCE: Page_15]

14 [SOURCE: Page_15]

296   the field scale, where crops and agricultural practices are implemented, the scale of water harvesting [SOURCE: Page_16]

297   infrastructures, and the hydrological compartments (aquifer, hillslope, river) that govern water [SOURCE: Page_16]

298   circulation within the landscape. While certain models strive towards achieving such integrative [SOURCE: Page_16]

299   agroecosystem modeling and addressing these aforementioned shortcomings [65], the design of [SOURCE: Page_16]

300   such models continues to present a scientific challenge in the future and overcoming these [SOURCE: Page_16]

301   shortcomings [SOURCE: Page_16]

331   4.     Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_17]

332   Improving the sustainability of Mediterranean rainfed agriculture in a context of water scarcity and [SOURCE: Page_17]

333   climate change can be achieved through crop diversification at the field level by considering [SOURCE: Page_17]

334   agroforestry, intercropping and service crops. Diversification of landscape by rearranging the [SOURCE: Page_17]

335   composition and configuration of crops, as well as through landscape water harvesting [SOURCE: Page_17]

336   infrastructures, can also help to achieve this goal. Diversification, at the field and landscape level, [SOURCE: Page_17]

337   can provide food and livelihoods, but also ecosystem services (pest control, nutrient provision, [SOURCE: Page_17]

338   carbon sequestration, soil preservation). In this review, we enlighten the scientific issues concerning [SOURCE: Page_17]

339   each of the diversification modes. Nevertheless, the adaptation of Mediterranean Agriculture to [SOURCE: Page_17]

340   water scarcity will not be achieved by mobilizing only one of these solutions, but by combining in [SOURCE: Page_17]

341   space and time these solutions at both levels. New tools that consider the spatial and temporal [SOURCE: Page_17]

16 [SOURCE: Page_17]

342   variability of Mediterranean landscapes, such as agro-hydrological models, are needed to evaluate [SOURCE: Page_18]

343   such combinations. Landscape management will also require innovative governance based on new [SOURCE: Page_18]

344   institutions and organizations involving the different stakeholders (farmers, water managers, land [SOURCE: Page_18]

345   managers...) who are central actors for acceptability. Finally, the ability of Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_18]

346   agrosystems to evolve towards biodiversification is also contingent upon the social, cultural, and [SOURCE: Page_18]

347   environmental contexts. Indeed, these agrosystems are typified by potential obstacles to achieving [SOURCE: Page_18]

348   successful transition towards biodiversification, including limited market opportunities for some [SOURCE: Page_18]

349   crops, insufficient availability of new crop varieties, equipment constraints and gaps in farmers' [SOURCE: Page_18]

350   knowledge regarding cultivation of new varieties. Although the impact of such contextual factors on [SOURCE: Page_18]

351   transition towards biodiversification has been explored in European and North American countries, [SOURCE: Page_18]

352   it remains an issue for Mediterranean regions. [SOURCE: Page_18]
