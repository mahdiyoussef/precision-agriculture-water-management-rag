13   Explaining changes in waterfowl distribution and abundance is requested by wetland managers 14   for a better understanding of their population dynamics and habitat use. The objective of this 15   study was to assess the influence of interannual changes in wetland management, both through 16   direct data and proxies, on the distribution dynamics of Teal day-roosts in the Camargue, a large 17   wetland complex in southern France. We constructed a state-space model accounting for a 18   conditional detection probability by aerial observers during duck counts, since changes in 19   observers have a strong influence on variations in detection probability. First, we showed that 20   the distribution of Teal day-roosts within the Camargue delta has changed over the last 35 years. 21   Second, on a sub-sample of 18 years, we showed that annual changes in Teal abundance 22   depended on salinity and open water area at the day-roost, and on the availability of potential 23   feeding grounds surrounding the day-roost (available wetland area within 5 km). No association 24   was detected between changes in Teal abundance and changes in the typology of wetland 25   hydrology, or with changes in site protection status (i.e. hunted to protected). Our results 26   reinforce the importance of considering management at the scale of functional units, by 27   considering the complementarity of nocturnal feeding areas (mainly hunted areas) specifically 28   managed for waterfowl, and diurnal roosts (mainly nature reserves, which have high 29   conservation value for other animal and plant species). A good understanding of the factors 30   affecting the localisation of waterfowl day-roosts is becoming more important in the context of 31   climate change, which is likely to redistribute local birds with rising sea levels and increasing 32   salinity in wetlands. 34   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

35          From a sustainable management perspective, explaining changes in waterfowl [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   distribution and abundance is requested for a better understanding of their population dynamics [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   and habitat use in a context of global change (Williams et al. 2002). Many wildlife species are [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   exploited by humans, especially waterfowl which have been used for millennia (Broughton et [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   al. 2007; Kear 2010). Harvest and disturbances are the two hunting effects on exploited [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   populations (Madsen and Fox 1995; Vercauteren and Hygnstrom 1998; Tamisier et al. 2003; [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   Frank et al. 2017). Although harvest is the most visible effect because it results in the removal [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   of individuals, its consequences on population size are sometimes difficult to evaluate due to [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   individual turn-over (Gourlay‐Larour et al. 2013), additive/compensatory processes to natural [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   mortality (Anderson and Burnham 1976), and crippling loss or restocking operations (Henny [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   1967; Champagnon et al. 2012). Disturbance can also have an impact on local population size [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   and distribution (Gill et al. 2001a; Beauchamp 2003; Béchet et al. 2004), because waterfowl [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   generally avoid areas disturbed by human activities (Sutherland and Crockford 1993; Livezey [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   et al. 2016). European and North American studies often showed an increase in local waterfowl [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   population size after the creation of reserves or hunting ban (Cox Jr and Afton 1997; Madsen [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   1998; Mathevet and Tamisier 2002; Paillisson et al. 2002). One potential explanation that [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   hunting disturbance would reduce site carrying capacity is consolidated by earlier works [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   (Burton 2007; Nye et al. 2007). Particularly, during the winter period, hunting disturbance has [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   been found to negatively affect the numbers of most dabbling duck species within given areas [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   (Allouche et al. 1989; Madsen and Fox 1995; Bregnballe et al. 2004; Brochet et al. 2009). [SOURCE: Page_3]

55          Human management actions also often impact environmental characteristics that may [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   condition the choice made by waterfowl to establish in a given area (Cross 1988; Williams [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   1997). For example, timing of flooding and intended manipulation of water levels by managers [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   affect wetland food abundance and accessibility to ducks (Allouche et al. 1989; Smith et al. [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   1989; Tamisier and Dehorter 1999; Béchet et al. 2004; Brochet et al. 2009; Davis et al. 2014). [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   In addition, niche segregation between dabbling ducks ultimately leads to very low levels of [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   interspecific competition for food during winter (differences in diet, segregation by body length [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   and therefore foraging depth, by beak lamella spacing and therefore prey size, etc.; see the work [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   by Guillemain et al. 2002c), and therefore is unlikely to be a strong driver in the selection of [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   roosting and feeding grounds, and their dynamics across space and time. The carrying capacity [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   of a wetland for waterfowl is also affected by water area (Leschisin et al. 1992; McKinstry and [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   Anderson 2002). During the winter period, dabbling duck abundance tends to be higher on [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   larger water bodies for safety reasons (earlier and easier detection of approaching predators; [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   Allouche et al. 1989; Tamisier and Dehorter 1999; Brochet et al. 2009). Redistribution of [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   waterfowl is usually an indicator of changing habitat quality and availability (e.g., Guillemain [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   et al. 2000). Therefore, changes in wetland management between two wintering seasons should [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   drive habitat quality for ducks when they arrive at the beginning of the winter, which earlier [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   literature has shown to be driving habitat use for the rest of the season (Tamisier and Tamisier [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   1981; Guillemain et al. 2002a, 2010). [SOURCE: Page_4]

74          Here we developed a Bayesian State-space model to examine the spatio-temporal [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   changes in wintering Common Teal (Anas crecca, hereafter Teal) day-roosts in the Camargue, [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   as measured as growth rate in Teal number from one winter to the next one, with regards to [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   changes in local environmental conditions, that is conditions at the scale of Camargue sites. [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   More specifically, we examined the relative importance of physico-chemical, trophic, [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   landscape and anthropic parameters (salinity, wetland status and type, water and nocturnal [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   foraging area) in determining where the Teal day-roosts are established within the delta. This [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   study did not aim at comparing attractivity of different sites of a given winter quarter for Teals, [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   as already examined in the past (Brochet et al. 2009), with the potential pitfall that sites may [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   differ by more means than just those under study. Instead, the present paper engages in a [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   diachronic analysis, to set out how changes in environmental conditions in a given site from [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   one year to the next may translate into changes in the number of wintering birds it hosts. [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   Because this analysis relied on an exceptional long-term and robust duck census dataset [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   (Vallecillo et al. 2021), we could also describe whether the distribution of the focal Teal day- [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   roosts has changed over an extended period of one third of a century (i.e. 1985-2020). [SOURCE: Page_5]

89          Teal was chosen as a study species to address the issue of this study given its [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   demonstrated interwinter site fidelity in the Camargue (Guillemain et al. 2009). The Camargue [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   is a large complex of wetlands in France involving a large number and a diversity of [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   management actors. It is also one of the major sites for waterfowl hunting in Europe (Mathevet [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   2004; Mondain-Monval et al. 2013), ducks being predominant in waterfowl, and, hence, the [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   main targets for wetland management for private hunting (Tamisier and Dehorter 1999). Ducks [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   form dense flocks of variable sizes diurnally, and mostly engage in comfort activities (preening, [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   resting and swimming; Tamisier et al. 1995). Such day-roosts are where birds are censused. [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   Since day-roosts often do no fully meet the energy needs of ducks, they disperse at night to [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   reach more attractive feeding grounds often located in hunted marshes (shallow waters [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   supporting productive aquatic plants (Tamisier and Dehorter 1999)). Diurnal roosts and [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   nocturnal feeding areas are geographically linked with each other and constitute together a [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   “functional unit” (sensu Tamisier (1978)). [SOURCE: Page_5]

102          We hypothesized that an increase in water area would result in an increase in the number [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   of individuals at a site, owing to safer conditions and potentially greater food availability. [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   Moreover, we also expected that Teal abundance on a day roost would decrease with increasing [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   hunting disturbance. Moreover, a functional unit must be able to provide enough trophic [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   resources for the ducks to meet their nutrient requirements nocturnally on feeding areas, but [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   also on the roost diurnally during periods of the winter when necessary (Arzel et al. 2009). The [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   availability of trophic resources for seed-eating birds, like Teal, depends on several factors, [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   primarily salinity (Teal in Camargue feed mainly on vegetation that grows in fresh water) and [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   wetland typology (temporary, sub-permanent, or permanent periods of flooding) (Bonis et al. [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   1995; Grillas and Battedou 1998). In relation to this, we hypothesized that an increase in diurnal [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   roost and neighbouring nocturnal feeding opportunities would lead to an increase in the number [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   of Teals that could stay on a given diurnal roost. We hence expected that increased salinity and [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   modified flooding status (permanent/sub-permanent to temporary) on day-roosts and a decrease [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   in neighbouring nocturnal feeding areas would result in fewer individuals in a wetland diurnally [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   owing to fewer foraging opportunities. [SOURCE: Page_6]

325   State-space models [SOURCE: Page_15]

326          The count data from the 54 sites and 18 years were analysed with State-space models [SOURCE: Page_15]

327   (Valpine and Hastings 2002; Dennis et al. 2006) using a Bayesian framework (Kery and Schaub [SOURCE: Page_15]

328   2012). State-space models consist of a set of linked models: (1) a model for the state process, [SOURCE: Page_15]

329   (2) a model for the observation process and (3) a model for the initial state. The state process [SOURCE: Page_15]

330   describes the dynamics of the latent state (in our case the bird abundance index) over time and [SOURCE: Page_15]

331   for each site, and the observation model links the observations (counts) to the state. The model [SOURCE: Page_15]

332   takes into account random sampling errors (counting errors) from both sources of data (Kery [SOURCE: Page_15]

333   and Schaub 2012). The model represents environmental stochasticity as log-Student process [SOURCE: Page_15]

334   noise. We assumed a stochastic exponential population growth in an unlimited environment [SOURCE: Page_15]

335   (with no density-dependence) for the dynamic of the state because it is expected that the [SOURCE: Page_15]

336   carrying capacity of the sites is not reached, notably because of a high hunting pressure [SOURCE: Page_15]

337   (Tamisier and Dehorter 1999; Brochet et al. 2009; Guillemain et al. 2021). In our case, the [SOURCE: Page_15]

338   modelling process estimates an abundance index corrected by a conditional probability of [SOURCE: Page_15]

339   detection (see below). Thus, the population size N in a site s in year t is estimated by: [SOURCE: Page_15]

340   Ns,t = Ns,t-1 × exp(rs,t ) [SOURCE: Page_15]

14 [SOURCE: Page_15]

341   where rs,t is the instantaneous growth rate of site s from year t - 1 to t. To define the initial state [SOURCE: Page_16]

342   (population size in the year when the monitoring started) we estimated the size of the population [SOURCE: Page_16]

343   for each site s in the first year as: [SOURCE: Page_16]

344   log(Ns, 1 ) ~ Normal (mu, σ2site ) [SOURCE: Page_16]

345   where mu is the expected abundance during the first year and σ2site is the variability of the size [SOURCE: Page_16]

346   of the population among sites. The growth rate of site s and year t was modelled by a first order [SOURCE: Page_16]

347   random walk given by: [SOURCE: Page_16]

348   rs,t ~ Normal (rs,t-1 + 𝛾s, t , σ2year ) [SOURCE: Page_16]

349   where the growth rate in year t depends on the growth rate in year t-1 plus the gamma parameter [SOURCE: Page_16]

359   growth rate in the current year t. The effect of the explanatory variables was modelled by [SOURCE: Page_16]

502          Predominant role of salinity change [SOURCE: Page_24]

503          Although several studies have shown that saltier sites are less used by Teal (Pirot et al. [SOURCE: Page_24]

504   1984; Allouche et al. 1989), the effect of annual changes in salinity on the attractiveness of a [SOURCE: Page_24]

505   given site to Teal, to our knowledge, had not been evaluated. These results again highlight that [SOURCE: Page_24]

506   rapid changes in hydraulic management, such as pumping freshwater from the Rhone to reduce [SOURCE: Page_24]

507   salinity, can have consequences on Teal abundance at a given site. An important increase in [SOURCE: Page_24]

508   salinity (no Teal was present on Camargue marshes or ponds with more than 100 g/l of salt) is [SOURCE: Page_24]

509   unfavourable as this will decrease growth of individuals and survival of ducks (Moorman et al. [SOURCE: Page_24]

510   1991). Teal are not adapted to consume salt water (as they have no salt gland) and hence need [SOURCE: Page_24]

511   regular access to fresh water to avoid salt poisoning (Barnes and Nudds 1991; Moorman et al. [SOURCE: Page_24]

512   1991). The salinity parameter analysed here covered fresh (0 to 1 g of salt per litre of water), [SOURCE: Page_24]

513   brackish (1 to 5 g/l) and salty (> 5 g/l) sites. The transition from salty to fresh wetlands also [SOURCE: Page_24]

514   influences the abundance and species composition of submerged macrophyte communities [SOURCE: Page_24]

516   of the wintering season (although feeding activity remains low in proportion during the day [SOURCE: Page_24]

517   compared to the night, see Tamisier and Dehorter 1999). In our study, we showed that an annual [SOURCE: Page_24]

518   change in salinity had consequences on Teal relative abundances. Although Teal are present in [SOURCE: Page_24]

519   salty environments during the day (48% of Teal numbers over the entire study period), a [SOURCE: Page_24]

520   sufficient decrease in salinity, as a change from brackish to fresh water, between two water [SOURCE: Page_24]

521   seasons at a given site does increase Teal numbers, likely through greater food availability [SOURCE: Page_24]

522   (Tamisier and Dehorter 1999). Salt marshes, in the southern part of the Camargue, may have [SOURCE: Page_24]

523   very low food resources for Teal (Grillas 1990; Tamisier and Grillas 1994). Freshwater marshes [SOURCE: Page_24]

23 [SOURCE: Page_24]

524   in the north of the delta conversely have lower salinity and hence a greater aquatic plant [SOURCE: Page_25]

525   production (Allouche et al. 1989; Grillas 1990). Despite potentially greater food productivity [SOURCE: Page_25]

526   in wetlands in the North of the delta, we did record major day-roosts in the saltier southern part [SOURCE: Page_25]

527   of the Camargue (Fig.4). Despite the fact that no measurement of food availability could be [SOURCE: Page_25]

537          Influence of the extend of habitat change: role of water area and nocturnal [SOURCE: Page_25]

538   foraging sites [SOURCE: Page_25]

539          While it was known that Teal favour large open areas as day-roosts (Leschisin et al. [SOURCE: Page_25]

540   1992; Tamisier and Dehorter 1999; McKinstry and Anderson 2002), to our knowledge this is [SOURCE: Page_25]

541   the first work of the effects of changes in water area on the attractiveness of a given site to Teal. [SOURCE: Page_25]

542   Previous studies showed that surface area is important in explaining diurnal distribution of [SOURCE: Page_25]

543   ducks in the Camargue (Tamisier and Pradel 1992; Dehorter and Tamisier 1996; Brochet et al. [SOURCE: Page_25]

544   2009) and other wetlands (Tuite et al. 1984; Perry and Deller 1996). However, this previous [SOURCE: Page_25]

545   information was obtained by comparing wetlands of different sizes, which may also differ in [SOURCE: Page_25]

546   other aspects, while in this study we studied the consequences of changes within sites. An [SOURCE: Page_25]

547   increase in open water area likely benefits Teal through an increased protection from predators [SOURCE: Page_25]

548   and a greater food availability (Tamisier and Dehorter 1999). Evans & Day (2001) have shown [SOURCE: Page_25]

549   that during the hunting season, most diving ducks at Toome Bay (Northern Ireland) were [SOURCE: Page_26]

640          Conclusions and management implications [SOURCE: Page_29]

641          Based on long-term data, we demonstrated that rapid changes in local wetland [SOURCE: Page_29]

642   management at a site level influences the distribution of Teal day-roosts during winter, at the [SOURCE: Page_29]

643   scale of an entire winter quarter. We believe the primary strategy to increase the number of Teal [SOURCE: Page_29]

644   on a wetland is hydraulic management in the functional unit, specifically water area and salinity [SOURCE: Page_29]

645   on day-roosts, and feeding ground area around day-roosts. Even if the attractiveness of a site [SOURCE: Page_29]

646   seems more complex than we demonstrated (interaction between environmental variables), our [SOURCE: Page_29]

647   results highlight the importance of predation and human disturbance (effect of water area) and [SOURCE: Page_29]

649   feeding ground area surrounding the roost. It is therefore necessary to consider how to promote [SOURCE: Page_30]

654   and integrating the different landowners through consultation tools instead of mostly promoting [SOURCE: Page_30]

655   the protection of day-roosts as done so far, may be much positive to duck populations. [SOURCE: Page_30]

656           These measures would help maintain waterfowl species in a changing climatic context, [SOURCE: Page_30]

657   where the impact of future changes, such as an increase in salinity at the scale of the delta [SOURCE: Page_30]

658   through intensified periods of drought and a rise in sea level (Pachauri et al. 2014), could not [SOURCE: Page_30]

659   only influence the local distribution but also decrease the attractiveness of the delta and hence [SOURCE: Page_30]

660   Teal population size at the Camargue scale. Furthermore, the species to be conserved in a given [SOURCE: Page_30]

661   wetland may change in the future with climate change (Gaget et al. 2021), with Teal gradually [SOURCE: Page_30]

663   ecological needs of the species currently present on a given wetland, but also to consider the [SOURCE: Page_30]

Prior Predictive Check [SOURCE: Page_45]

What is the Prior Predictive Check? [SOURCE: Page_45]

This is a verification step. It allows us to highlight the adequacy between our model, the priors that we impose and the data on which we are going to work. [SOURCE: Page_45]

How does it work? [SOURCE: Page_45]

- the first step is to create a function that allows us to simulate data according to the model that will be used on our real dataset. - we define the priors we want to use in our analysis. - we draw values for each priors (10 000 simulations) within their distribution laws. - we generate data with our simulation model for each combination of priors values. - we compare the simulated data with the real data set. [SOURCE: Page_45]

If the majority of the simulated data falls within the same range of variation as the real dataset, then the defined priors are suitable for working with our waterbird count data. Care should be taken to ensure that the defined priors are not too informative or too uninformative. [SOURCE: Page_45]

Conditional detection probability [SOURCE: Page_53]

Our model relied on count data of unmarked individuals and accounted for changes in [SOURCE: Page_53]

conditional detection probability to estimate an abundance index. While a true abundance can [SOURCE: Page_53]

only be obtained by Capture-Mark-Recapture (CMR) data, our indices of abundance are [SOURCE: Page_53]

sufficient and adequate to detect spatio-temporal changes in population size. We developed our [SOURCE: Page_53]

model under a Bayesian framework to incorporate knowledge on the conditional detection [SOURCE: Page_53]

probability for each aerial observer a priori (Vallecillo et al. 2022). In this way, the model [SOURCE: Page_53]

allowed us to correct the abundance index by changes in the conditional detection probability. [SOURCE: Page_53]

Specifically, the two changes of observers over the whole study period were accounted for in [SOURCE: Page_53]

the estimation of growth rates at the 54 sites, i.e. 108 growth rate values. As mentioned in [SOURCE: Page_53]

Vallecillo et al. (2022), there is a subtlety to be considered regarding the definition of the term [SOURCE: Page_53]

“conditional detection probability” in our model, which corresponds to both the [SOURCE: Page_54]

underestimation due to the detection error and the group estimation error. However, in the [SOURCE: Page_54]

context of our study, it is difficult to measure the respective influence of these two errors on the [SOURCE: Page_54]

underestimation made by the observers, among other things because they can vary according to [SOURCE: Page_54]

the habitats that are site-dependent (Vallecillo et al. 2022). [SOURCE: Page_54]

This figure shows the estimation of the abundance index (black curve with its 95% credible intervals) over the entire monitoring period for the month of September in the whole Camargue. Aerial counts are represented by blue dots.
