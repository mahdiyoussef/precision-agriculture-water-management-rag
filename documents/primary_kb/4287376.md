## 1   INTRODUCTION

2   In Europe, grasslands cover 22% of the EU-25 land area (FAOSTAT data, [SOURCE: Page_4]

3   http://faostat.fao.org) and provide various ecosystemic services such as forage production, [SOURCE: Page_4]

4   carbon storage, soil protection and biodiversity preservation (Millenium Ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_4]

5   Assessment, 2005). Climate change will likely impact this ecosystem through shifts in [SOURCE: Page_4]

6   grassland composition and production potential (Grime et al., 2000; Gilgen and Buchmann, [SOURCE: Page_4]

7   2009; Craine et al., 2011; Zwicke et al., 2013). In temperate areas, more extreme rainfall [SOURCE: Page_4]

8   regimes are expected to increase the duration and severity of drought (Knapp et al., 2008; [SOURCE: Page_4]

9   Seneviratne et al., 2012). In this context, more knowledge is required to understand the [SOURCE: Page_4]

10   mechanisms of resistance, survival under severe drought and recovery of herbaceous species [SOURCE: Page_4]

11   after drought (Chaves et al., 2003; Volaire et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_4]

12      There is evidence for a persistence-productivity trade-off, species with a high maximum [SOURCE: Page_4]

13   growth rate having a higher mortality under restricted resources (Sibly and Calow, 1989; [SOURCE: Page_4]

14   Wright et al., 2010). In the case of drought tolerance, evidence for this trade-off has been [SOURCE: Page_4]

15   described for woody plants (Reich, 2014), but the relationships between plant growth rate [SOURCE: Page_4]

16   under optimal conditions and subsequent performance under severe drought has been scantly [SOURCE: Page_4]

17   explored in herbaceous species (Pérez-Ramos et al., 2013). Perennial herbaceous species are [SOURCE: Page_4]

18   interesting plant models, since they can resist and survive severe drought through adaptive [SOURCE: Page_4]

19   strategies contributing to dehydration avoidance and dehydration tolerance (Ludlow, 1989; [SOURCE: Page_4]

20   Blum, 1996). Under moderate drought, dehydration avoidance ensures the maintenance of [SOURCE: Page_4]

21   plant tissue hydration and osmotic potential by maximizing water uptake and minimizing [SOURCE: Page_4]

22   water losses (Ludlow, 1989; Volaire et al., 2009). Under severe drought, once complete leaf [SOURCE: Page_4]

23   senescence is reached, dehydration tolerance ensures plant survival by the maintaining cell [SOURCE: Page_4]

24   integrity in meristematic tissues through cell membrane stabilization, accumulation of water [SOURCE: Page_4]

25   soluble carbohydrates (WSC) and dehydrins (Volaire et al., 1998a, b; Verslues et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

1   Grass species of temperate areas accumulate large amounts of reserve carbohydrates mainly [SOURCE: Page_5]

2   as fructans in leaf meristems (Chatterton et al., 1989; Pollock and Cairns, 1991). It has been [SOURCE: Page_5]

3   shown that this accumulation during drought improves plant survival after drought (Volaire et [SOURCE: Page_5]

4   al., 1998a; Clark et al., 2004). Fructans in particular were shown to stabilize membranes in [SOURCE: Page_5]

5   vitro by interaction with lipids under stress (Vereyken et al., 2001, 2003; Hincha et al., 2002, [SOURCE: Page_5]

6   2007) and because they may act as antioxidants (Peshev et al., 2013). These compounds play [SOURCE: Page_5]

7   an indirect role in drought survival when they are hydrolysed to fuel growth after rehydration [SOURCE: Page_5]

8   (Volaire et al., 1998b; Thomas and James, 1999; Amiard et al., 2003). [SOURCE: Page_5]

9      Under progressive soil drying, higher biomass allocation to roots (Poorter et al., 2012) and [SOURCE: Page_5]

10   extensive root system have been commonly observed in drought resistant species (Comas et [SOURCE: Page_5]

11   al., 2013), since deep roots can take up water from moister soil layers. Under severe soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

12   drought conditions root mortality increases due to tissue dehydration (Eissenstat and Yanai, [SOURCE: Page_5]

13   1997; Facette et al., 1999). However, it has been shown in ryegrass that some roots can [SOURCE: Page_5]

14   survive very dry conditions (soil water content < -10 MPa), especially root apices, and that [SOURCE: Page_5]

15   production of lateral roots from existing roots is observed after soil rewetting (Jupp and [SOURCE: Page_5]

16   Newman, 1987). Thus maintaining live roots in severe soil drying conditions should enhance [SOURCE: Page_5]

17   plant recovery (Weaver and Zink, 1946; Huang et al., 1997). However, despite of their crucial [SOURCE: Page_5]

18   role in water uptake under drought, below-ground plant traits remain poorly quantified [SOURCE: Page_5]

19   compared with leaf traits (Reich, 2014). The combination of these plant responses depends on [SOURCE: Page_5]

20   species and genotypes, and can be investigated by assessing leaf and root traits (Pérez-Ramos [SOURCE: Page_5]

21   et al., 2013) associated with water status and WSC metabolism (Volaire, 2008). [SOURCE: Page_5]

22      The present study aimed (i) to characterize the adaptive responses whereby temperate [SOURCE: Page_5]

23   grassland species resist, survive and recover from a severe drought and (ii) to explore the [SOURCE: Page_5]

24   relationships between plant resource use and drought resistance strategies. To this end, we [SOURCE: Page_5]

25   compared six dominant perennial herbaceous species (five grasses and one forb) originating [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

1   from upland temperate grasslands (Louault et al., 2005), according to their resistance to and [SOURCE: Page_6]

2   recovery from summer extreme (Zwicke et al., 2013), with a Mediterranean grass cultivar [SOURCE: Page_6]

3   used as control for its high drought survival. These species were grown in semi-controlled and [SOURCE: Page_6]

4   non-limiting rooting depth conditions to analyse leaf and root traits. Optimum resource-use [SOURCE: Page_6]

5   strategies were identified under irrigation in spring, dehydration avoidance strategy was [SOURCE: Page_6]

6   assessed under moderate drought (20 days of withholding water) and dehydration tolerance [SOURCE: Page_6]

7   was estimated under severe drought (50 days). Plant survival and resilience indices (according [SOURCE: Page_6]

8   to Van Ruijven and Berendse, 2010) were respectively measured two weeks and one year [SOURCE: Page_6]

9   after rehydration. We tested the following hypotheses: (i) the drought survival of temperate [SOURCE: Page_6]

10   species mainly depends on dehydration avoidance through water acquisition strategies, (ii) [SOURCE: Page_6]

11   accumulation of WSC, especially fructans and sucrose in surviving organs including not only [SOURCE: Page_6]

12   leaf meristems, but also roots and root apices, is associated with dehydration tolerance, and [SOURCE: Page_6]

13   (iii) there is a trade-off between high resource-use under non-limiting conditions and high [SOURCE: Page_6]

14   ability to survive and recover from severe drought. [SOURCE: Page_6]

## 6   DISCUSSION

7   Temperate and Mediterranean species display different strategies for dehydration avoidance [SOURCE: Page_18]

8   Against our first hypothesis, the results show a similar contribution of dehydration avoidance [SOURCE: Page_18]

9   and tolerance to drought survival in species from temperate upland grassland. Dehydration [SOURCE: Page_18]

10   avoidance is achieved through a combination of responses favouring maximized water uptake [SOURCE: Page_18]

11   and minimized water loss under drought (Ludlow, 1989). The changes in relative water [SOURCE: Page_18]

12   content in tissues (MWC and LRWC, Table 1) are indicators of the cellular adjustments [SOURCE: Page_18]

13   (accumulation of solutes and/or cell wall hardening) made by the plant to achieve a low water [SOURCE: Page_18]

14   potential while avoiding water loss (Verslues et al., 2006). In this study, LRWC at moderate [SOURCE: Page_18]

15   drought was correlated to RM, WU and plant survival (Fig. 7B, Table 3), which confirms that [SOURCE: Page_18]

16   maximizing water uptake during drought is an important mechanism in maintaining the [SOURCE: Page_18]

17   hydration of lamina (Passioura, 1981; Chaves and Pereira, 1992; Volaire et al., 2009; Lelièvre [SOURCE: Page_18]

18   et al., 2011). As previously shown for other species (Jackson et al., 2000; Pinheiro et al., [SOURCE: Page_18]

19   2005; Volaire, 2008), water uptake is mainly associated with initial root traits (Table 3). The [SOURCE: Page_18]

20   species with higher RM and Dmax, such as T. officinale, D. glomerata ‘Medly’, D. glomerata [SOURCE: Page_18]

21   and F. arundinacea, had a higher water-use before and during moderate drought (Table 1, [SOURCE: Page_18]

22   Table 3, Fig. 7B). In arid and semi-arid environments, relatively small proportions of roots in [SOURCE: Page_18]

23   deeper, moister soil layers may suffice to sustain water absorption of plants (Ehleringer and [SOURCE: Page_18]

24   Dawson, 1992). The maintenance of water uptake by roots during a drought period is mainly [SOURCE: Page_18]

25   driven by hydraulic continuity between soil, roots and leaves, which all depend on plant [SOURCE: Page_18]

17 [SOURCE: Page_18]

1   transpiration, hydraulic properties of roots (Passioura, 1988) and also on root morphology and [SOURCE: Page_19]

2   anatomy (North and Nobel, 1991; Rieger and Litvin, 1999; Huang and Eissenstat, 2000; [SOURCE: Page_19]

3   Steudle, 2000; Hernández et al., 2010). Between-species differences observed on leaf and root [SOURCE: Page_19]

4   traits such as LRWC, MWC and RDMC (Table 1) suggest differences in water acquisition [SOURCE: Page_19]

5   strategy, and therefore in the ability to maintain water uptake, explained by rooting depth [SOURCE: Page_19]

6   (Fig. 7A, 7B; Table 3) and differences in root diameter and tissue density (Picon-Cochard et [SOURCE: Page_19]

7   al., 2012). Overall, root lifespan maintenance during drought could be related to root [SOURCE: Page_19]

8   hydration maintenance by the hydraulic lift mechanism (Bauerle et al., 2008), cell osmotic [SOURCE: Page_19]

9   increase in cortex cells (Sharps and Davies, 1979), and suberization of the endoderm [SOURCE: Page_19]

10   protecting xylem cells to dehydration, which could explain at least part of the increase in [SOURCE: Page_19]

11   RDMC and decrease in plant water content (Table 1). The species able to maintain a root [SOURCE: Page_19]

12   production during moderate drought, such as T. officinale and D. glomerata ‘Medly’ (Table 1, [SOURCE: Page_19]

13   Fig. 2) had higher drought survival rates (Fig. 6B). Root growth maintenance under moderate [SOURCE: Page_19]

14   water stress was also identified as an important mechanism to avoid dehydration by [SOURCE: Page_19]

15   maximizing water uptake through an increase in root absorption area (Passioura, 1981, 1988; [SOURCE: Page_19]

16   Vartanian, 1981; Pérez-Ramos et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_19]

17     Other mechanisms, such as modifications in water-use efficiency and regulation of [SOURCE: Page_19]

18   stomatal conductance are involved in dehydration avoidance (Jones et al., 1981; Chaves and [SOURCE: Page_19]

19   Pereira, 1992; Golluscio and Oesterheld, 2007). In addition, when stress intensifies, increased [SOURCE: Page_19]

20   leaf senescence contributes to the minimization of water loss by reducing leaf area and [SOURCE: Page_19]

21   evaporation (Ludlow, 1989; Gepstein, 2004). In this study, leaf senescence, which was [SOURCE: Page_19]

22   inversely correlated to plant survival, was not adaptive but an indicator of drought [SOURCE: Page_19]

23   vulnerability (Fig. 7C, Table 3). Conversely, gradual foliage senescence was positively [SOURCE: Page_19]

24   correlated with plant survival in a range of Mediterranean grasses (Volaire et al., 1998b; [SOURCE: Page_19]

25   Volaire and Lelièvre, 2001; Pérez-Ramos et al., 2013). This suggests differences in plant [SOURCE: Page_19]
