24   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   In comparison with northern countries, limited data are available on the occurrence [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   and potential toxicity of cyanobacterial blooms in lakes and ponds in Sub-Saharan [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   countries. With the aim of enhancing our knowledge on cyanobacteria and their toxins [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   in Africa, we performed a 17-month monitoring of a freshwater ecosystem, Lagoon [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   Aghien (Ivory Coast), which is used for multiple practices by riverine populations and [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   for drinking water production in Abidjan city. The richness and diversity of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   cyanobacterial community were high and displayed few variations during the entire [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   survey. The monthly average abundances ranged from 4.1x^104 cell mL-1 to 1.8x^105 [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   cell mL-1, with higher abundances recorded during the dry seasons. Among the five [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   cyanotoxin families analyzed (anatoxin-a, cylindrospermopsin, homoanatoxin, [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   microcystins, saxitoxin), only microcystins (MC) were detected with concentrations [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   ranging from 0 to 0.364 µg L-1 in phytoplankton cells, from 32 to 1,092 µg fresh weight [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   (FW) kg-1 in fish intestines and from 33 to 383 µg FW kg-1 in fish livers. Even if the MC [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   concentrations in water and fish are low, usually below the thresholds defined in WHO [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   guidelines, these data raise the issue of the relevance of these WHO guidelines for [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   Sub-Saharan Africa, where local populations are exposed throughout the year to these [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   Eutrophication of freshwater ecosystems due to point and diffuse nutrient pollution is [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   ongoing on the African continent (Jenny et al. 2020). As shown for Lake Victoria (East [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   Africa), this process is associated with the rapid demographic changes taking place on [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   this continent (United Nations 2017) and leads to significant land use changes and [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   increasing pollution pressures on freshwater ecosystems (e.g., Olokotum et al. 2020). [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   Like anywhere else in the world, one would expect that the ongoing eutrophication of [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   African lakes would lead to the development of cyanobacterial blooms in these [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   ecosystems (Nyenje et al. 2010). This has already been well documented in a few [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   large lakes, such as Lake Victoria (e.g., Krienitz et al. 2002; Olokotum et al. 2021), and [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   in lakes and reservoirs located in northern and southern Africa (e.g., Oberholster et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   2010; Matthews and Bernard 2015; Guellati et al. 2017; Hammou et al. 2018). [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   However, in comparison with northern countries, limited data are available on the [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   occurrence of cyanobacterial blooms in lakes and ponds located in intertropical areas [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   (e.g., Merel et al. 2013). In addition, there is also a lack of data on the potential toxicity [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   of these cyanobacterial blooms, with data available from less than half of African [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   countries (e.g., Ndlela et al. 2016; Svircev et al. 2019). In contrast, several reports of [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   freshwater    cyanobacterial     poisoning     in   terrestrial   wildlife   (flamingos   and [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   megaherbivores) have been made, mainly in eastern and southern Africa (Ash and [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   Patterson 2022). [SOURCE: Page_4]

66          This lack of knowledge on the occurrence and potential toxicity of cyanobacterial [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   blooms in freshwater ecosystems of numerous sub-Saharan countries is partly [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   explained by the rarity of institutional monitoring of cyanobacteria in freshwater [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   ecosystems, and vice versa (Codd et al. 2005). In addition, there are also no [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   management plans, including policy tools, legislation and public communication, [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   dedicated to preventing human exposure to cyanotoxins, despite the potentially high [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   exposure of human populations to these toxins in developing countries. Indeed, the [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   majority of populations living around lakes use raw (untreated) water for multiple [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   purposes, including washing dishes and clothes, bathing, cooking and direct [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   consumption during fishing expeditions (e.g., Effebi et al. 2017; Roegner et al. 2020 [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   and 2023; Olokotum et al. 2022). [SOURCE: Page_5]

77          In an attempt to improve our knowledge on threat assessment attributed to [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   cyanobacterial blooms in Sub-Saharan Africa, we performed a 17-month monitoring of [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   a freshwater lagoon, Lagoon Aghien, located in northern Abidjan city (Ivory Coast). [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   This lagoon supplies treated water for the Abidjan district and is used for recreational [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   and multiple domestic activities and for fishing by local populations living in the villages [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   located along its shores. Finally, this assessment led us to discuss the considerations [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   for applying the microcystin guideline values provided by the WHO in African countries [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   and the limits for their use in these countries. [SOURCE: Page_5]

136   cyanobacteria, the cells were directly counted. For Microcystis, two categories of [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   colonies were considered corresponding to their diameters (< or > 200 μm). The mean [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   Cylindrospermopsis/Raphidiopsis, Limnothrix, Lyngbya, Oscillatoria, Planktothrix and [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   Pseudanabaena), filament lengths of 100 μm were considered, and the mean cell [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   number per 100 μm of filament was estimated on 30 filaments per genus according to [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   the method described by Catherine et al. (2017). Finally, for all genera, the results of [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   the cell counts are expressed as numbers of cells per mL. [SOURCE: Page_8]

409   Cyanobacterial community composition and dynamics [SOURCE: Page_25]

410   Fifteen cyanobacterial genera were observed during the survey of Lagoon Aghien, [SOURCE: Page_25]

411   which is within the same range as data obtained in other tropical freshwater [SOURCE: Page_25]

412   ecosystems, such as those reported by Olokotum et al. (2022), who identified 16 [SOURCE: Page_25]

413   cyanobacterial genera in two embayments of Lake Victoria (Uganda), and by Dalu and [SOURCE: Page_25]

451   These data should also be considered in light of the recent study of Vico et al. (2020), [SOURCE: Page_26]

453   original dispersion center of Raphidiopsis (Cylindrospermopsis) raciborskii and that [SOURCE: Page_26]

454   these African populations were not able to produce CYL. [SOURCE: Page_26]

455          The intracellular MC concentrations in the water were quite low, always <0.4 µg [SOURCE: Page_26]

456   equi. MC-LR eq L-1 and were not correlated with the total abundances of [SOURCE: Page_26]

457   cyanobacterial genera known to be able to produce MC or with the abundance of each [SOURCE: Page_26]

464   non-MC-producing (MC-) strains in Microcystis populations (e.g., Briand et al. 2009; [SOURCE: Page_27]

465   Suominen et al. 2017). Their respective proportions were not estimated in our study, [SOURCE: Page_27]

467   four of 24 M. aeruginosa isolates were able to produce MC, and Ballot et al. (2014) [SOURCE: Page_27]

469   data are interesting to consider, but knowing that there is very high variability in [SOURCE: Page_27]
