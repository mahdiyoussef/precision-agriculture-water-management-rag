50   1.0 Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

51          Rivers were long considered as passive pipes for carbon transport between the land [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   and ocean in early conceptual models of the carbon cycle (Wohl et al., 2017). Now, we know [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   that physical, chemical, and biological processes in river networks have an important [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   influence on carbon dynamics (Benstead & Leigh, 2012; Cole et al., 2007; Wohl et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   River networks are areas of disproportionate biogeochemical exchange compared to their [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   spatial extent in the landscape (Allen & Pavelsky, 2018). The river continuum concept [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   describes the longitudinal gradient of physical conditions in unaltered rivers (Vannote et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   1980). It details the fate of organic matter along a river network, with high allochthonous [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   inputs in headwater reaches, and increasing importance of autochthonous primary production [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   and organic matter transport from upstream as stream size increases (Vannote et al., 1980). [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   However, river networks do not always follow this conceptual model, for example, due to [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   alterations as a result of the increasing pressure of fragmentation of river networks in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   Anthropocene era (Fuller et al., 2015). In this review, we define fragmentation at the [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   landscape scale as the temporary (drying) or permanent (damming) change in hydrologic [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   connectivity, disrupting the spatial flows of energy, materials, and organisms within a river [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   network (Fahrig, 2003; Fuller et al., 2015; Loreau et al., 2003). River networks are [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   fragmented by both natural (e.g. drying, waterfalls, beaver dams) and anthropogenic agents [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   (e.g. damming, water withdrawal, pollution) (Fuller et al., 2015). In this review, we will focus [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   on drying and damming (referring to artificial in-stream barriers of all sizes), as they are two [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   of the most widespread causes of fragmentation in river networks (Belletti et al., 2020; [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   Messager et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_3]

72          The occurrence of drying in river networks is increasing due to climate change, [SOURCE: Page_3]

73   damming, and water abstraction (Datry et al., 2023). These waterways that periodically cease [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   to flow, can be defined as intermittent rivers and ephemeral streams (hereafter, IRES) (Datry, [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

75   Larned, & Tockner, 2014). Recent estimates have quantified the extent of IRES which dry for [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   at least one day of the year, as comprising 51-60% of the total global stream network [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   (Messager et al., 2021). Fragmentation by drying can create different states in space and time: [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   dry river beds, isolated pools, and lotic waters. These flow regime states, and the transitions [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   between them (i.e. rewetting and drying), create unique environments that may promote the [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   production and consumption of GHGs. [SOURCE: Page_4]

81          In parallel, a large majority of the earth’s river systems are impacted by damming. A [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   global analysis found that only 37% of rivers longer than 1000 km are free-flowing without [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   barriers over their entire length (Grill et al., 2019). Dams are a disruption to the river [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   continuum, as defined in the serial discontinuity concept (Stanford, 1983), causing alterations [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   in abiotic and biotic processes and patterns through the fragmentation of longitudinal, lateral, [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   and vertical pathways (Wang et al., 2018). Dams also alter river hydrology, sedimentation, [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   nutrient biogeochemical cycling, reducing the connectivity of water, organisms, sediments, [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   organic matter, nutrients and energy (Grill et al., 2019). Damming can promote GHG [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   emissions from both the reservoir surface, as well as degassing emissions at the reservoir [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   outlet (i.e. dam spillways and turbines), downstream river emissions, and drawdown [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   emissions from reservoir sediments that are periodically inundated and exposed to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   atmosphere (Deemer et al., 2016; Maavara et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_4]

93          Fragmentation by drying and damming occur both separately and in interaction, and [SOURCE: Page_4]

94   they may have certain comparable effects, as well as interactive effects, on GHG fluxes from [SOURCE: Page_4]

95   river networks. Reservoirs increase water residence time in rivers and transform lotic reaches [SOURCE: Page_4]

96   into lentic waters (Abril et al., 2015; Sabater, 2008). Below impoundments, flow rates may be [SOURCE: Page_4]

97   reduced or the flow regime may change from perennial to temporary, leading to an increase in [SOURCE: Page_4]

98   dry riverbed surface and modifications in ecosystem processes (Abril et al., 2015; Bianchi et [SOURCE: Page_4]

99   al., 2017; Datry et al., 2023). In many cases, natural and anthropogenic drivers interact to [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

100   cause intermittence (Datry et al., 2023). Alternatively, baseflow augmentation by reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   can create artificial permanent flow regimes downstream of reservoirs in reaches that [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   previously may have been intermittent in nature (Acuña et al., 2017). These impacts are in [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   many ways parallel to those posed by drying, where we observe a spatial and temporal mosaic [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   of dry, lentic, and lotic reaches (Datry, Larned, & Tockner, 2014). Global dry inland waters [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   represent a significant source of CO$_2$ and methane (CH$_4$) emissions (Keller et al., 2020; Marcé [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   et al., 2019; Paranaíba et al., 2021). Additionally, enhanced CO$_2$ emissions have been [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   observed upon the rewetting of riverbed sediments and leaf litter accumulated in dry reaches [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   (Datry et al., 2018; Gallo et al., 2014; von Schiller et al., 2019). In lentic pools and [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   impoundments, organic matter and sediment can also accumulate (Wang et al., 2018). This [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   accumulation coupled with increased water retention time, allows for an augmentation in [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   water temperature and the creation of anaerobic conditions, which promote denitrification and [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   methanogenesis, and the generation of GHGs (Wang et al., 2018). Both drying (Döll & [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   Schmied, 2012) and damming (Zarfl et al., 2015) are occurring with increasing frequencies, [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   therefore, it is important to quantify their impacts on GHG fluxes in order to inform [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   management decisions and improve global GHG budgets. [SOURCE: Page_5]

116          Our objective was to review the literature of the effects of fragmentation by drying and [SOURCE: Page_5]

117   damming on GHG fluxes in river networks. We discuss the factors and processes affecting [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   GHG fluxes at different hydrological states of flow regimes in IRES and dammed rivers: [SOURCE: Page_5]

119   exposed sediments, lentic waters, and lotic waters. Flow regime states are described along a [SOURCE: Page_5]

120   continuum of scale, from the reach, to the network, to the global scale. We then discuss the [SOURCE: Page_5]

121   individual and interactive effects of drying and damming at the network scale. Finally, we [SOURCE: Page_5]

122   propose four actionable future research directions. [SOURCE: Page_5]

156   3.0 Greenhouse gas fluxes in intermittent rivers [SOURCE: Page_7]

157          Recent advances in our knowledge of controls of GHG fluxes along with updated [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   maps of inland waters have allowed for more well-informed estimates of GHG fluxes in [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   freshwaters (Drake et al., 2018; Raymond et al., 2013). Streams and rivers in particular have [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   been identified as global hotspots of CO$_2$ evasion, with recent global CO$_2$ estimates of 2.0 ± [SOURCE: Page_7]

161   0.2 Pg C y-1 (Liu et al., 2022), with an additional 1.02 Pg CO$_2$ eq. y-1 from CH$_4$ and N2O [SOURCE: Page_7]

162   (Zheng et al., 2022). However, research in the field of IRES has historically fallen behind that [SOURCE: Page_7]

163   of perennial systems, particularly with respect to studies examining biogeochemical cycling [SOURCE: Page_7]

164   (Leigh et al., 2016). This lag in research may be due to the unique nature of intermittent [SOURCE: Page_7]

165   ecosystems - not terrestrial, but not always aquatic - making them difficult to fit into the [SOURCE: Page_7]

166   domain of aquatic or terrestrial ecology (Arce et al., 2019; Steward et al., 2012). We found 43 [SOURCE: Page_7]

167   studies examining GHG fluxes in IRES, with a peak of studies published within the last [SOURCE: Page_7]

168   decade (Figure S$_1$). Most of the studies were conducted in arid regions, where IRES are [SOURCE: Page_7]

169   prevalent such as the Mediterranean, Australia, and Arizona, USA (Figure 1). The majority of [SOURCE: Page_7]

170   studies that we identified were metabolism studies, which measure diel oxygen curves as a [SOURCE: Page_7]

171   proxy for metabolism (Hall & Hotchkiss, 2017). These metabolism studies were primarily [SOURCE: Page_7]

172   reach-scale field studies during lotic and dry states, just in isolated pools, or lotic reaches [SOURCE: Page_7]

173   within an intermittent river network. For those studies measuring in situ GHG efflux, most [SOURCE: Page_7]

174   studies measured CO$_2$ alone, some measured N2O alone, and a few measured several or all [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

175   three major GHGs (Figure 2A). Studies most frequently examined all three of the major [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   hydrological states: dry, isolated pools, and lentic waters. Some studies just looked at drying [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   and rewetting, and others just measured fluxes in isolated pools or just sediment rewetting. [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   We found four global efforts examining GHG fluxes in IRES, however, the large majority of [SOURCE: Page_8]

179   identified studies were at the reach scale, with only a few at the network scale (Figure 2B). [SOURCE: Page_8]

200   3.2 Greenhouse gas fluxes from dry sediments can exceed those of inundated ones [SOURCE: Page_9]

201          3.2.1 Carbon dioxide fluxes [SOURCE: Page_9]

202            The CO$_2$ flux from exposed, dry sediments in IRES was consistently higher than [SOURCE: Page_9]

203   fluxes from lotic waters in the same study; on average dry fluxes were nearly double the flux [SOURCE: Page_9]

204   from lotic waters (Figure 3A, Table S$_3$). This suggests a positive effect of drying on CO$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_9]

205   fluxes, possibly due to an increase of oxygen in the sediments promoting organic matter [SOURCE: Page_9]

206   degradation when water is no longer limiting diffusion (Kosten et al., 2018). In a synthesis of [SOURCE: Page_9]

207   CO$_2$ fluxes from dry inland waters, dry sediment fluxes were higher than those from lentic [SOURCE: Page_9]

208   waters, and more similar to fluxes reported from lotic waters (Marcé et al., 2019). In some [SOURCE: Page_9]

209   cases, the CO$_2$ flux from IRES dry sediments is comparable to riparian soil fluxes (Bretz et [SOURCE: Page_9]

210   al., 2021; Gómez-Gener, et al., 2016; von Schiller et al., 2014). Similar to observations in dry [SOURCE: Page_9]

211   riverbeds, CO$_2$ fluxes from reservoir drawdown areas are on average higher than from the [SOURCE: Page_9]

212   reservoir surface (Keller et al., 2020), resulting in a 50% increase in the current global CO$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_9]

213   emission estimate from reservoirs when accounting for drawdown areas (Keller et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_9]

214          The factors that most influenced CO$_2$ fluxes in IRES were sediment moisture, [SOURCE: Page_9]

215   temperature, organic matter content, and texture (Figure 4). Sediment moisture strongly [SOURCE: Page_9]

216   influences CO$_2$ fluxes, as water stress can inhibit microbial activity (Manzoni et al., 2012). In [SOURCE: Page_9]

217   a global analysis of CO$_2$ fluxes in dry inland waters, soil moisture was the strongest predictor [SOURCE: Page_9]

218   of CO$_2$, followed by the interaction of organic matter content and moisture (Keller et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

219   2020). Thus, under moisture-limiting conditions, increasing the organic matter content or [SOURCE: Page_9]

220   temperature of the sediments will not increase the CO$_2$ flux, as the microbial activity is [SOURCE: Page_9]

221   inhibited by the water stress (Keller et al., 2020). However, the relationship of CO$_2$ flux with [SOURCE: Page_9]

222   sediment moisture is not always linear, as significantly higher microbial activity was observed [SOURCE: Page_9]

223   between 20 and 40% water filled pore space at an Australian IRES (McIntyre et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_9]

224   One study even reported a negative relationship between CO$_2$ flux and sediment moisture in a [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

225   Mediterranean IRES (Gómez-Gener et al., 2015). They noted that although higher water [SOURCE: Page_10]

226   content may promote CO$_2$ production by facilitating contact between microorganisms and the [SOURCE: Page_10]

227   available substrate, it may at the same time act as a physical barrier to restrict the evasion of [SOURCE: Page_10]

228   CO$_2$ (Gómez-Gener et al., 2015). High sediment moisture content may also lead to anaerobic [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   conditions, which inhibit the production of CO$_2$ by aerobic respiration due to oxygen [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   limitation (Kosten et al., 2018). Temperature stimulates the microbial activity of autotrophic [SOURCE: Page_10]

231   and heterotrophic respiration in soils (Raich et al., 2002), with a similar effect in inland water [SOURCE: Page_10]

232   sediments (Lü et al., 2023). A diurnal pattern was observed in high frequency CO$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_10]

233   measurements from exposed riverine sediments, suggesting that warming during the day [SOURCE: Page_10]

234   exponentially increased fluxes (Koschorreck et al., 2022). Sediment organic matter content [SOURCE: Page_10]

235   influences CO$_2$ fluxes as organic matter provides a substrate for decomposition. However, the [SOURCE: Page_10]

236   quantity of organic matter may not explain the whole story, since the quality of organic matter [SOURCE: Page_10]

237   can vary widely, with a rapidly decomposable fraction with a turnover rate in days, to a more [SOURCE: Page_10]

238   slowly decomposable fraction with a turnover rate in days to years (Qiu et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_10]

239   Sediment texture also plays an important role in CO$_2$ fluxes, as soils with a finer texture can [SOURCE: Page_10]

240   have higher organic matter content and moisture retention, as well as greater surface area for [SOURCE: Page_10]

241   microbial colonization, creating more favourable conditions for respiration (Gallo et al., [SOURCE: Page_10]

242   2014). For example, higher CO$_2$ fluxes were reported in loam and sandy loam (490 and 333 [SOURCE: Page_10]

243   mmol m-2 d-1, respectively) than in sandy (77 mmol m-2 d-1) channels (Gallo et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_10]

244          There has been some research examining the fluxes of CO$_2$ from both sediments and [SOURCE: Page_10]

245   organic matter in IRES upon rehydration due to a rain event, with rewetting fluxes at least an [SOURCE: Page_10]

246   order of magnitude higher than dry sediment fluxes in the same study (Table S$_4$). In soils, [SOURCE: Page_10]

247   pulses of CO$_2$ after rehydration have been termed the “Birch effect”, wherein the [SOURCE: Page_10]

248   decomposition of labile soil organic matter, nitrogen mineralization, as well as CO$_2$ flux are [SOURCE: Page_10]

249   enhanced upon rewetting (Birch, 1958). Sediment texture can play an important role in [SOURCE: Page_10]

10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

250   rewetting fluxes, CO$_2$ fluxes were significantly higher and more variable in sites with fine [SOURCE: Page_11]

251   sediment texture than more coarse textures (Gallo et al., 2014). Carbon dioxide flux had a [SOURCE: Page_11]

252   significant positive correlation with percent clay content, soil nitrogen, ammonium and nitrate [SOURCE: Page_11]

253   content (Gallo et al., 2014). The rewetting of leaf litter may also contribute to rewetting CO$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_11]

254   pulses, however, sediments emitted CO$_2$ an order of magnitude higher than leaf litter, [SOURCE: Page_11]

255   considering an equivalent surface area (Datry et al., 2018; von Schiller et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_11]

349   Carbon dioxide flux from the downstream river were on average three times higher than from [SOURCE: Page_14]

442   4.0 Spatial and temporal patterns of drying and damming at the network scale [SOURCE: Page_18]

443            River networks are meta-ecosystems made up of an aquatic continuum embedded [SOURCE: Page_18]

444   within a terrestrial matrix (Battin et al., 2008). A meta-ecosystem is defined as a set of [SOURCE: Page_18]

445   ecosystems connected by spatial flows of energy, materials, and organisms across ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_18]

446   boundaries (Loreau et al., 2003). Meta-ecosystem theory, an extension of metapopulation and [SOURCE: Page_18]

447   metacommunity theories, is useful in conceptualizing the effects of river network [SOURCE: Page_18]

448   fragmentation on biogeochemical and ecological processes (Loreau et al., 2003), such as those [SOURCE: Page_18]

449   leading to the production and consumption of GHGs. In river networks, energy, materials, and [SOURCE: Page_18]

18 [SOURCE: Page_18]

450   organisms flow by-way-of hydrological connectivity in three spatial dimensions: longitudinal, [SOURCE: Page_19]

451   lateral, and vertical (Cid et al., 2021). In fragmentation by drying and damming, the [SOURCE: Page_19]

452   interruption of surface flow disrupts hydrological connectivity in one or more of the three [SOURCE: Page_19]

453   dimensions, creating heterogeneous mosaics of habitat patches with varying conditions [SOURCE: Page_19]

454   (Boulton et al., 2017; Datry et al. 2017, Cid et al. 2021). This spatial variability also varies [SOURCE: Page_19]

455   temporally within and among years (Costigan et al., 2015), further contributing to the high [SOURCE: Page_19]

456   habitat heterogeneity in IRES. Compared to the temporary nature of fragmentation by drying, [SOURCE: Page_19]

457   reservoirs typically represent a permanent discontinuity feature within the network altering [SOURCE: Page_19]

458   the fluxes of water, sediment, materials (including organic matter) and organisms (Grill et al., [SOURCE: Page_19]

459   2019). We propose that the spatial distribution of fragmentation at the network scale is a [SOURCE: Page_19]

460   major factor influencing the magnitude and dynamics of GHG fluxes. More specifically, the [SOURCE: Page_19]

461   network location of the fragmentation (i.e. upstream, downstream) and the type of [SOURCE: Page_19]

462   fragmentation (i.e. drying, damming), and their interaction play an important role in GHG [SOURCE: Page_19]

463   dynamics at the river network scale. [SOURCE: Page_19]

464            The location of fragmentation along a river network is an important consideration for [SOURCE: Page_19]

465   determining its ecological impacts (Jacquet et al., 2022; Schmera et al., 2018). Intermittent [SOURCE: Page_19]

466   reaches can occur anywhere in a river network, from headwaters to the mainstem (Datry, [SOURCE: Page_19]

467   Larned, Fritz, et al., 2014). Network scale drying patterns vary due to differences in climate, [SOURCE: Page_19]

468   geology, vegetation, and human interference (Shanafield et al., 2021). For example, in [SOURCE: Page_19]

469   temperate regions intermittent reaches are often in headwaters (Kampf et al., 2021), although [SOURCE: Page_19]

470   central and downstream drying reaches are also common (Arscott et al., 2010; Datry, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_19]

471   In arid regions, intermittent reaches can occur anywhere and often entire river networks are [SOURCE: Page_19]

472   intermittent (Welter & Fisher, 2016). Network scale patterns of drying can have important [SOURCE: Page_19]

473   implications on biogeochemical cycling due to the disparate conditions observed along a river [SOURCE: Page_19]

474   network from the headwaters to the estuarine zone (Allen et al., 2020; Larned et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_19]

19 [SOURCE: Page_19]

475          Within a river network, we expect high GHG fluxes from dry sediments at upstream [SOURCE: Page_20]

476   reaches (Figure 5A), where allochthonous organic matter inputs to the stream bed tend to be [SOURCE: Page_20]

477   greater due to a higher edge to area ratio of riparian tree cover (Richardson & Danehy, 2007). [SOURCE: Page_20]

478   For example, the greatest amount of terrestrial organic matter input in dry channels was [SOURCE: Page_20]

479   reported from first-order, forested, temperate IRES when sampling 212 streams across nearly [SOURCE: Page_20]

480   all continents (Datry et al., 2018). Moreover, the relatively stronger land-water connectivity in [SOURCE: Page_20]

481   headwaters compared to the mainstem can supply terrestrially derived carbon inputs to stream [SOURCE: Page_20]

482   under lotic conditions (Hotchkiss et al., 2015), and it may be an important source of carbon [SOURCE: Page_20]

483   during drying events (Gómez-Gener et al., 2020). The shading effects of riparian cover in [SOURCE: Page_20]

484   headwaters can also buffer against the negative impacts of sediment desiccation on [SOURCE: Page_20]

485   decomposer communities and associated GHG production (Schreckinger et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_20]

486   However, the lower sediment temperature near the headwaters and coarser sediment texture [SOURCE: Page_20]

487   may conversely limit CO$_2$ fluxes. We could expect the highest CH$_4$ and N2O fluxes from dry [SOURCE: Page_20]

488   river reaches in the mainstem, where finer sediments tend to accumulate, retaining moisture [SOURCE: Page_20]

489   and nutrients (Saxton & Rawls, 2006), along with the diminished effect of riparian shading [SOURCE: Page_20]

490   creating warmer sediment temperatures stimulating microbial activity (Raich et al., 2002). [SOURCE: Page_20]

491   Nevertheless, these proposed network scale patterns in dry sediment GHG fluxes still need to [SOURCE: Page_20]

492   be tested. [SOURCE: Page_20]

493          As with drying, the spatial position of a reservoir is important for determining GHG [SOURCE: Page_20]

494   flux magnitudes at the river network scale (Figure 5B). We expect reservoirs located in [SOURCE: Page_20]

495   downstream reaches to have higher CO$_2$ and CH$_4$ fluxes associated with their larger size and [SOURCE: Page_20]

496   the warmer water temperatures when compared to shaded and higher elevation upstream [SOURCE: Page_20]

497   reaches. We expect higher biological activity and chlorophyll a levels in warmer waters [SOURCE: Page_20]

498   (Sarkar et al., 2021), leading to potentially high production and emission of CO$_2$. However, if [SOURCE: Page_20]

499   there are significant reservoirs upstream, this may reduce the amount of organic matter [SOURCE: Page_20]

20 [SOURCE: Page_20]

500   transported downstream (Stanford & Ward, 2001), thereby diminishing the emission potential [SOURCE: Page_21]

501   despite the more favourable environmental conditions for GHG production. At the network [SOURCE: Page_21]

502   scale, CO$_2$ evasion from lakes was controlled by residence time and size, with CO$_2$ fluxes [SOURCE: Page_21]

503   increasing with the stream order of their network position, suggesting that larger lakes [SOURCE: Page_21]

504   downstream are more efficient emitters of terrestrial CO$_2$ (Brinkerhoff et al., 2021). We [SOURCE: Page_21]

505   expect to observe a similar trend in reservoirs, where a reservoir on large order river, with no [SOURCE: Page_21]

506   upstream reservoirs, might have a higher magnitude of GHG fluxes than a reservoir located in [SOURCE: Page_21]

507   the headwaters, considering two reservoirs with similar physical characteristics. [SOURCE: Page_21]

508          We may expect to see higher N2O fluxes at reservoirs in downstream reaches [SOURCE: Page_21]

509   compared to upstream ones. Point sources of nitrogen entering the river may accumulate in [SOURCE: Page_21]

510   downstream reaches, where the often sparse riparian cover has a reduced capacity of nitrate [SOURCE: Page_21]

512   nutrient spiralling concept at the network scale found that nitrate was recycled more [SOURCE: Page_21]

516   we propose that the network position of reservoirs has an important influence on the [SOURCE: Page_21]

517   magnitude of GHG fluxes. [SOURCE: Page_21]

519   5.0 Interactive effects of drying and damming at the network scale [SOURCE: Page_21]

520          Drying and damming present complex and interactive fragmentation effects on a river [SOURCE: Page_21]

521   network. The interactive effect of drying and damming at the network scale, may result in [SOURCE: Page_21]

522   more organic matter processed in dry reaches and reservoirs than in lotic reaches (Acuña & [SOURCE: Page_21]

523   Tockner, 2010). According to our review, dry reaches and reaches downstream of reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_21]

524   have a higher on average GHG flux potential than lotic reaches (Figure 3). Only six studies [SOURCE: Page_21]

21 [SOURCE: Page_21]

525   examining the interactive effects of drying and damming to different degrees were found in [SOURCE: Page_22]

526   our search. The importance of the spatial distribution of drying and damming together along a [SOURCE: Page_22]

527   river network is an important, but heavily understudied research area. In a Mediterranean river [SOURCE: Page_22]

528   network impacted by drying and damming, there was both an increase an decrease in the [SOURCE: Page_22]

529   frequency of low-flow events at two different reaches downstream of reservoirs, but an [SOURCE: Page_22]

530   overall decrease in the frequency of high flow events (Aristi et al., 2014). They further found [SOURCE: Page_22]

531   a consistent increase in all organic matter variables below reservoirs, which was possibly [SOURCE: Page_22]

532   related to materials originating from the reservoir (e.g. re-suspended organic matter or [SOURCE: Page_22]

533   outflow plankton) (Aristi et al., 2014). Similarly, an investigation of small reservoirs along an [SOURCE: Page_22]

534   intermittent Mediterranean river network, found that reservoirs had higher microbial [SOURCE: Page_22]

535   community processing of organic carbon, and they subsidized downstream lotic reaches, [SOURCE: Page_22]

536   thereby increasing production and transformation of organic carbon along the river network [SOURCE: Page_22]

537   (Proia et al., 2016). In disagreement with the aforementioned studies, a simulation of organic [SOURCE: Page_22]

538   carbon dynamics in a Mediterranean river network found lower sediment carbon and [SOURCE: Page_22]

539   respiration rates downstream of reservoirs, due to the accumulation of carbon in reservoirs via [SOURCE: Page_22]

540   sedimentation as a result of high water residence times (Acuña & Tockner, 2010). They [SOURCE: Page_22]

541   further found that increasing flow variability increased the amount of carbon processed within [SOURCE: Page_22]

542   a river network, while reservoirs had a counteractive effect on extreme flow events due to [SOURCE: Page_22]

543   their flow regulation capacity (e.g. reservoir water retention was not markedly reduced during [SOURCE: Page_22]

544   droughts) (Acuña & Tockner, 2010). These differences may be attributed to context- [SOURCE: Page_22]

545   dependent reservoir (e.g. age, depth, water residence time) or river network (e.g. geology) [SOURCE: Page_22]

546   characteristics (Poff & Hart, 2002). For example, if the water is released from the top or the [SOURCE: Page_22]

547   bottom of the reservoir, where the latter is more likely to be enriched in nutrients and organic [SOURCE: Page_22]

548   matter (Nürnberg, 2007). [SOURCE: Page_22]

22 [SOURCE: Page_22]

549          With the interactive effect of drying and damming at the network scale, we expect [SOURCE: Page_23]

550   more organic matter to be processed in dry reaches and reservoirs than in lotic reaches. The [SOURCE: Page_23]

551   potential additive effect would therefore result in more GHGs emitted to the atmosphere [SOURCE: Page_23]

552   compared to an equivalent, unfragmented, river network (Figure 5C). However, upstream [SOURCE: Page_23]

553   drying and reservoirs may also hold back organic matter from downstream reaches (Stanford [SOURCE: Page_23]

554   & Ward, 2001), resulting in lower downstream GHG fluxes. We could also expect the [SOURCE: Page_23]

555   temporal nature of GHG fluxes to be more variable in fragmented river networks, as upon the [SOURCE: Page_23]

556   rewetting of previously dry reaches, the accumulated organic matter can be transported [SOURCE: Page_23]

557   downstream (Datry et al., 2018), where it would then be stored and processed in a reservoir. [SOURCE: Page_23]

558   In addition, there may be a potential synergistic effect as the presence of a reservoir can [SOURCE: Page_23]

559   reduce flow rates, or, particularly if water is abstracted from the reservoir, create new [SOURCE: Page_23]

560   intermittent reaches downstream (Bianchi et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2020). As a result, organic [SOURCE: Page_23]

561   matter processing rates and associated GHG fluxes in the river network are augmented due to [SOURCE: Page_23]

562   the creation of these new dry and lentic states. However, in other cases, the reservoir baseflow [SOURCE: Page_23]

563   may turn previously intermittent reaches perennial (Acuña et al., 2017), resulting in a [SOURCE: Page_23]

564   compensatory effect on GHG fluxes due to the replacement of dry reaches with lotic ones. [SOURCE: Page_23]

565   The interactive effects of drying and damming are complex and context dependant. With [SOURCE: Page_23]

566   much of the current literature on this topic focused on the Mediterranean, we need an [SOURCE: Page_23]

567   increased geographic diversity in studies to test our conceptual models of dry and damming [SOURCE: Page_23]

568   interactive effects. [SOURCE: Page_23]

570   6.0 Future research directions [SOURCE: Page_23]

571          We uncovered several research gaps in geography, spatial scale, type of gas measured, [SOURCE: Page_23]

572   and hydrological state measured. We additionally identified a major knowledge gap on the [SOURCE: Page_23]

573   interactive effects of drying and damming. Here, we propose four actionable future research [SOURCE: Page_23]

23 [SOURCE: Page_23]

574   directions as identified in our review of the literature on the effects of fragmentation by drying [SOURCE: Page_24]

575   and damming on GHG fluxes. [SOURCE: Page_24]

576      1. Firstly, we identified an information gap in terms of the geographic distribution, [SOURCE: Page_24]

577          spatial sampling scale, and GHG measured by studies. Most of the studies about IRES [SOURCE: Page_24]

578          were conducted in the Mediterranean, Australia, and southern United States. This [SOURCE: Page_24]

579          narrow spatial focus omits the world’s temperate regions, which also include IRES [SOURCE: Page_24]

580          (Messager et al., 2021), as well as many other arid landscapes, such as those found in [SOURCE: Page_24]

581          Central and South America, Africa and Asia. The majority of studies about river [SOURCE: Page_24]

582          systems impacted by damming were from China, with some studies in Brazil, French [SOURCE: Page_24]

583          Guiana and the USA. Moreover, most of the studies done on IRES systems were [SOURCE: Page_24]

584          conducted at the reach scale, with few studies at the global or network scale. These [SOURCE: Page_24]

585          scales are important for understanding the ramifications of the highly variable spatial [SOURCE: Page_24]

586          patterns in IRES. Similarly, very few studies examined the impacts of damming on [SOURCE: Page_24]

587          GHG fluxes in rivers at a network scale. The magnitudes and drivers of fluxes may [SOURCE: Page_24]

588          vary across the longitudinal gradient from headwaters to the mainstem. Furthermore, [SOURCE: Page_24]

589          studies that measured GHG fluxes from dry and rewetting IRES most commonly [SOURCE: Page_24]

590          measured CO$_2$, with few studies measuring CH$_4$ and N2O. A similar tendency was [SOURCE: Page_24]

591          observed in studies of river networks fragmented by damming, where fewer studies [SOURCE: Page_24]

592          included measurements of N2O when compared to CH$_4$ and CO$_2$. [SOURCE: Page_24]

593      2. Since our literature review, several notable studies examining GHG fluxes from dry [SOURCE: Page_24]

594          inland waters have been published (e.g. Arce et al., 2023; Bretz et al., 2021; [SOURCE: Page_24]

595          DelVecchia et al., 2021; Koschorreck et al., 2022; Paranaíba et al., 2021; Pinto et al., [SOURCE: Page_24]

596          2021; Schreckinger et al., 2022). However, there is still a marked shortage of studies [SOURCE: Page_24]

597          on GHG fluxes from isolated pools in IRES, with most studies focused on dry [SOURCE: Page_24]

598          sediments (Bonada et al., 2020). For example, we did not find a single study about [SOURCE: Page_24]

24 [SOURCE: Page_24]

599      N2O fluxes from isolated pools. Although isolated pools may occur at a lower [SOURCE: Page_25]

600      frequency and over a smaller area than dry states, they may have a high GHG [SOURCE: Page_25]

601      production potential (Bonada et al., 2020). Isolated pools have lentic waters, warm [SOURCE: Page_25]

602      water temperatures, and high organic matter accumulation, which may result in anoxic [SOURCE: Page_25]

603      conditions which can promote methanogenesis and denitrification (Bonada et al., [SOURCE: Page_25]

604      2020; Day et al., 2019). Thus, isolated pools represent a potential source for CH$_4$ and [SOURCE: Page_25]

605      N2O, particularly under future predictions of increasing freshwater temperatures (van [SOURCE: Page_25]

606      Vliet et al., 2013) and augmented terrestrial subsidies to inland waters (Regnier et al., [SOURCE: Page_25]

607      2022). [SOURCE: Page_25]

608   3. We found that most studies focused on large reservoirs, while small reservoirs (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_25]

609      hydropower plants with < 10 megawatts) were studied to a lesser extent, even though [SOURCE: Page_25]

610      there are significantly more of them (Chanudet et al., 2020; Couto & Olden, 2018). [SOURCE: Page_25]

611      There is currently a boom in small hydropower plants as the hydropower potential of [SOURCE: Page_25]

612      larger rivers has already been exploited, public support for new large reservoirs has [SOURCE: Page_25]

613      diminished, and small reservoirs are perceived to have less of an ecological impact [SOURCE: Page_25]

614      (Couto & Olden, 2018; Lange et al., 2018). Although their surface area and therefore [SOURCE: Page_25]

615      total local GHG flux potential may be smaller, small reservoirs may have important [SOURCE: Page_25]

616      cumulative fragmentation effects on GHG fluxes at the network scale which warrant [SOURCE: Page_25]

617      further investigation. [SOURCE: Page_25]

618   4. Fragmentation by drying and damming do not occur independently. Therefore, it is [SOURCE: Page_25]

619      important to understand how drying and damming interact to influence the dynamics [SOURCE: Page_25]

620      of GHG fluxes. We found six studies that covered this topic in our review of the [SOURCE: Page_25]

621      literature, predominantly conducted in the Mediterranean, with mixed results. [SOURCE: Page_25]

622      Reservoirs may support downstream flow during drought periods, or may exacerbate [SOURCE: Page_25]

623      drying downstream. Given the increasing occurrences of drying and damming (Döll & [SOURCE: Page_25]

25 [SOURCE: Page_25]

624          Schmied, 2012; Zarfl et al., 2015), it is imperative to examine their interactive effects [SOURCE: Page_26]

625          on GHG fluxes in river networks, thereby better integrating global GHG budgets over [SOURCE: Page_26]

626          space and time. [SOURCE: Page_26]

627 [SOURCE: Page_26]

628          In summary, there has been a recent increase in studies examining GHG fluxes in river [SOURCE: Page_26]

629   networks fragmented by drying and damming. We propose that the location and type of [SOURCE: Page_26]

630   fragmentation within a river network are important factors influencing GHG dynamics. There [SOURCE: Page_26]

631   are still many unexplored gaps in the research, which, if addressed, can improve our [SOURCE: Page_26]

632   understanding of these complex and globally important systems and help to inform local [SOURCE: Page_26]

633   management of fragmented river networks as well as climate change mitigation strategies. [SOURCE: Page_26]
