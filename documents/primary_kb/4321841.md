## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The Cato institute, an influential American libertarian think tank, published a piece about glacier change in the High Mountain Asia (False Alarm over the Retreat of the Himalayan Glaciers, 2022) that was relayed in mainstream media (No, Himalayan Glaciers Melting Doesn't Mean Ganga Will Dry Up, 2022). Whereas the article is full of abject climate change denial, it raises a real issue about the contribution of glaciers to water resources. Based on the work of Armstrong et al. (2018), the authors concluded that glacier retreat in the High Mountain Asia does not threaten water supply in the highly populated regions of the Ganges, Indus, Brahmaputra basins. Indeed, the study of Armstrong et al. (2018) showed that annual meltwater produced on glacier areas is very low in com- parison with rainfall runoff or snowmelt from non-glacierized areas in this region (Figure 1). By considering only melt from exposed glacier ice (which is a better measure of glacier contribution as argued below), the glacier contribution to annual runoff in the area of the Ganges and Brahmaputra basin above 2000 m asl is below 1%. These latter values are hydrologic inputs across elevations greater than 2000 m, which means that at lower elevation where most people live, it may be even less. The low contribution of glaciers to runoff in the upper Brahmaputra river basin was also evidenced by Prasch et al. (2013) who also estimated that icemelt represents 2% of the river flow at Lhasa city (3656 m asl). [SOURCE: Page_2]

Glacier melt has a strong seasonal variability and therefore the contribution of icemelt to runoff can be higher at the monthly timestep, especially during the dry season, which is more relevant from a water resource perspective than an annual value. Still, the average icemelt contribution remains low at the monthly time step since it reaches at most 6% in the Indus over the period 2001–2014 (i.e., average contribution in September, Armstrong et al., 2018). Indeed, glacier melt does not necessarily occur during the dry season, in particular, snow and icemelt runoff increases during the monsoon due to the advection of warm air masses in the eastern Himalayas (Fugger et al., 2022; Laha et al., 2023). The Armstrong et al. (2018) study shows however that melt from glacier ice is significant during the dry season in the Amu Darya basin (26% of runoff in September). The same conclusion was drawn by Pritchard (2019). The latter study is often cited to assert that glaciers provide large amounts of freshwater resources to billions of people in Asia (390 citations according to Web of Science on March 31, 2023 by counting both the retracted paper and its updated version (which corrected an error in the glacier mass balance calculation, see Pritchard, 2018). In fact, the Pritchard (2019) study showed that glacier melt is less than 5% of the annual precipitation in all studied basins and acknowledged that it is not a significant input in the Ganges and Brahmaputra basins. This article focused on drought periods and presented glacier melt contribution as a fraction of runoff during the driest month of the driest year (“representative drought year”), that is, the year with the lowest annual precipitation for each basin in the 1951–2007 record (Pritchard, 2019). As a result, the monthly glacier melt contribution highlighted by Pritchard (2019, e.g., fig. 4) is the highest possible monthly value over a 57-year period. In addition, the glacier contribution (“net melt fraction”) was computed as the melt volume divided by the sum of the melt and net precipitation volumes during that month. Hence, as an extreme case, if there is no precipitation at all during this drought month, then the net melt fraction is 100%. Such concept of net melt fraction ignores the fact that baseflow during dry periods is also sustained by groundwater depletion, otherwise, the rivers of many non-glacierized catchments would run dry in summer. The groundwater contribution is difficult to assess since it depends on the geology of the catchments which is highly variable in a region as vast as the High Mountain Asia (Somers & McKenzie, 2020). Yet, Andermann et al. (2012) estimated that in Nepal, the groundwater storage has an approximate response time of 45 days and its capacity is larger than the annual snow and icemelt volume. As a consequence, the least we can say is that runoff sources during a dry month in Nepal are not limited to the surface water inputs like glacier melt and precipitation during that month. ## 2 | DEFINING THE C ONTRIBUTION OF G LACIER TO WATER RESOURCE

First of all, there is a lack of consensus on what is the contribution of glaciers to water resources. Indeed, the very definition of the “glacier contribution” to water resources varies among the studies (Frenierre & Mark, 2014; Radic & Hock, 2014). Glacier contribution is generally defined as a ratio, the numerator being a measure of the glacier runoff and the denominator being a reference water flux. The denominator can be either the river discharge (Cui et al., 2023; Gascoin et al., 2011; Huss, 2011; Huss & Hock, 2018), the precipitation (Kaser et al., 2010), or the net precipitation, that is, precipitation minus evaporation (Pritchard, 2019). The definition of the numerator, that is, the glacier runoff, also varies among the studies. Some authors consider that the glacier runoff is given by the melt of snow, firn, and ice over the glacier surface (Cui et al., 2023; Saks et al., 2022; Soruco et al., 2015), whereas others consider icemelt only (Azam et al., 2019; Burger et al., 2019; Prasch et al., 2013). Some authors include rain on the glacier area to estimate the glacier runoff (Huss, 2011; Huss & Hock, 2018). Others partition glacier runoff between seasonal melt (which includes snow and icemelt from the glacier ablation area) and net glacier mass loss (icemelt reducing the long-term glacier mass due to climate warming) (Bliss et al., 2014). Some authors estimate glacier runoff assuming the glaciers to be in equilibrium with the climate (Kaser et al., 2010), while others include the contribution of annual glacier mass loss to the total runoff (Huss & Hock, 2018; Pritchard, 2019). The inconsistent definitions of glacier contribution to the water resources is likely to have caused confusion not only in the media but also in the scientific literature (Lo   pez-Moreno et al., 2020). For example, Millan et al. (2022) refer to Soruco et al. (2015) to state that “the drainage basin of La Paz, with more than 2.2 M inhabitants, is strongly dependent on the water from ice melt that represents up to one-third of the water resources during the dry season”. In fact, Soruco et al. (2015) defined glacier runoff as the sum of the icemelt and snowmelt. Therefore, their water resources calculation includes the melt of the seasonal snow on the glaciers and the ice melt contribution was actually not estimated. From a water resource perspective, it seems that annual glacier runoff (numerator) would be more adequately rep- resented by the ice (and firn) melt, that is, the release of water that was stored multiple years by the glacier, that is, excluding the melt of the seasonal snowpack. In the case of a mountain glacier in balance with climate and neglecting mass loss by evaporation and sublimation, this meltwater flux is equal to the mass transported from the accumulation area to the ablation area every year. Miles et al. (2021) estimated this flux (referred to as balance ablation) for a large number of glaciers across High Mountain Asia based on remote sensing datasets of glacier velocity and elevation change. The balance ablation is the relevant measure of the sustainable contribution of a glacier to a catchment water balance. As illustrated in Figure 2, this contribution stands out in the Indus, Tarim, and Amu Darya among the major basins in High Mountain Asia. A closer analysis using a large sample of catchments shows that the glacier contribution (expressed as a flux per catchment unit area) decreases exponentially with the area of the glacierized catchment. The influence of glacier on streamflow is therefore expected to be significant in small headwater glacierized catchments. Melt of the seasonal snowpack which accumulates and melt every year on a glacier surface should not be taken into account in glacier water resource assessment, unless it can be demonstrated that the magnitude of the snowfalls on a glacier is influenced by the presence of the glacier itself. Studies have shown that glaciers can be preferential snow accu- mulation areas but this was explained by the influence of the terrain topography on atmospheric conditions or avalanching and not by a positive feedback loop (Dadic et al., 2010; Gascoin et al., 2013; Mott et al., 2019). In other words, according to the current knowledge, the same snowfall amount would be observed if the glacier was to be removed (Figure 3). Regarding the denominator, it is not advisable to use river discharge measurements if the catchment of interest is large. This is because the flow along the course of a river is also influenced by output fluxes of natural and anthropo- genic origins. In particular, river discharge from high alpine catchment contributes to groundwater recharge by stream infiltration (Müller et al., 2023) and is subject to withdrawals for irrigation or seasonal storage in reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_4]

Numerical models could be used to compute the contribution of ice and snow melt in river and groundwater using single particle tracking but this approach is also not feasible at large scale due to its computing cost. In addition, particle tracking methods may not be relevant since they do not represent the propagation of pressures waves which drive the response of hydrologic systems to water input. In the meantime, we as scientists should communicate accurately on what exactly is considered as glacier contribution: does it include snowmelt? Which period of the year is considered? Is it a drought period? Which hydrological components are neglected (groundwater discharge, reservoir management…)? [SOURCE: Page_6]

## 3 | C ON C L U S I ON

We should be careful when presenting the role of glaciers in water resource. As scientists, we have to provide accurate information about scientific research facts in public debate. Current knowledge does not suggest that billions of people will lack water due to glacier shrinkage in contrast to what is written in news articles, reports, and the introduction of many scientific articles. Other factors than glacier shrinkage are more likely to cause water scarcity in Asian countries like excessive groundwater pumping for crop irrigation (Rodell et al., 2009) or surface and groundwater contamination (Ahmed et al., 2006). This does not mean that glaciers are not important water resources. The key message is that glacier runoff can partly and temporarily compensate the lack of water resources during drought periods, especially in headwater catchments. Apart from the water resources perspective, there are other important motives to study glaciers like sea level rise (Gardner et al., 2013), biodiversity (Milner et al., 2017), hazard (Veh et al., 2020), and cultural value (Allison, 2015). In any case, the fragile beauty of glaciers should be a sufficient reason to study them before they vanish. [SOURCE: Page_7]
