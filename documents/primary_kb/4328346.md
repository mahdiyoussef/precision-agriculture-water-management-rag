22   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

23             In Mediterranean areas high precipitation variability and crop dependence on soil water [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   availability make the interaction between climate and agricultural management a key issue for [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   mitigating N2O emissions. In this study we used the STICS model to capture the effect of a [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   water deficit gradient and precipitation variability on N2O emissions and mitigation strategies [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   (i.e. N fertilizer type, grain legumes introduction in crop rotations and crop residues [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   management) in a rainfed Mediterranean transect (HWD-Senés, MWD-Selvanera and LWD- [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   Auzeville, i.e. high, medium and low water deficit, respectively). The model was first tested [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   against a database of daily N2O fluxes measured during twelve growing seasons of winter crops [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   at the LWD site. Several scenarios were then run on each site, always over 9 successive [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   growing seasons to take into account precipitation variability. STICS showed a good ability to [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   simulate the driving variables of N2O fluxes at the daily time scale. The mean observed and [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   simulated cumulative emissions during the growing season were 0.71 and 0.82 kg N2O-N ha- [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   1 , respectively. The simulated N2O emissions (mean of all scenarios) decreased with increasing [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   water deficit being 2.51, 0.65 and 0.26 kg N2O-N ha-1 yr-1 for LWD-Auzeville, MWD- [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   Selvanera and HWD-Senés, respectively, which is consistent with published results. The lower [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   N2O emissions in the driest sites were not only related to lower fertilization rates but also to [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   other factors associated with the Mediterranean characteristics, particularly, the drier water [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   regime. Simulated N2O emissions were highly sensitive to the interannual variability of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   climatic conditions. According to the simulations, urea fertilizer would lead to slightly higher [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   N2O emissions (+6 and +8%) than ammonium- and calcium nitrate, respectively. The [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   incorporation of winter pea in the traditional cereal-based Mediterranean rotations would [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   reduce by ca. 22% the N2O emissions in HWD-Senés without changing wheat yields. [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   Differently, in MWD-Selvanera and LWD-Auzeville, N2O emissions would remain unchanged [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   since the emissions associated to the decomposition of low C:N ratio pea residues would [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   counteract the lower application of N fertilizer. The systematic removal of crop residues at [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   LWD-Auzeville would decrease the N2O emissions by 20%. However, this practice seems not [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   recommendable if tillage is practiced due to the concomitant decrease of soil organic matter, [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   fact that would worsen the C footprint of the system and increase the susceptibility to soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   erosion. Our work highlights the interest of combining experimental and modelling approaches [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   to account for climatic variability and evaluate long-term effects of N2O mitigation practices [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   under Mediterranean conditions. [SOURCE: Page_4]

58      1. Introduction 59 [SOURCE: Page_5]

60          The Mediterranean climate is defined by warm to hot, dry summers and mild to cool, [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   wet winters. It is located between 30 and 45º north and south latitudes. Although it presents a [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   large variability between regions, its common feature is the presence of a severe water deficit [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   during the summer months, while in winter months rainfall usually exceeds evapotranspiration, [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   leading to a soil water recharge (López-Bellido, 1992). Rainfall presents a high intra- and inter- [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   annual variability, with an increasing trend of extreme events in spring and summer months [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   (Ramos and Martínez-Casasnovas, 2006). Crops in the non-irrigated Mediterranean areas [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   strongly depend on the amount of water stored in soil during the recharge period (i.e. September [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   to January) (Cantero-Martínez, et al., 2007), and cropping systems are mainly based on winter [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   cereals, given their cycle synchrony with water availability (López-Bellido, 1992; Álvaro- [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   Fuentes et al., 2009). Crop dependence on soil water availability makes the interaction between [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   climate and agricultural management a key issue both for crop production and N2O emissions [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   mitigation. [SOURCE: Page_5]

73          Nitrous oxide is a powerful greenhouse gas with a global warming potential (GWP) 265 [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   times greater than carbon dioxide (IPCC, 2013). Besides its strong implication in radiative [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   forcing, N2O also influences the depletion of the ozone layer in the stratosphere (Crutzen, [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   1974). Soil N2O emissions are the result of the nitrification-denitrification processes (Bremner, [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   1997). They are known to be highly dependent on mineral nitrogen availability and soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   moisture. The continuous increase in fertilizer use since the invention of the Haber-Bosch [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   process of industrial N$_2$ fixation has exacerbated N2O emissions from soils (Gruver and [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   Galloway, 2008). Nitrogen management has been traditionally oriented towards optimizing the [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   use of N by the crop. In some areas, a significant fraction of N fertilizer is still applied before [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   sowing, which, especially for winter crops, can enhance leaching losses in the rainiest locations [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   and/or lead to an overuse of soil water due to the increase in plant transpiration during the [SOURCE: Page_6]

84   vegetative stages. Because it remains difficult to optimize N fertilization strategies to crop [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   needs under variable water stress conditions, the resulting periods of high mineral N [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   availability can also induce N losses as N2O. Different fertilization strategies have been tested [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   as a means of mitigating N2O emissions in the Mediterranean agroecosystems. Among them, [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   (i) the adaptation of N fertilizer application to crop needs, (ii) the split of N fertilizer at key [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   crop development stages, (iii) the use of organic and synthetic fertilizers, and (iv) the use of [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   nitrification inhibitors (e.g. Aguilera et al., 2013; Meijide et al., 2007, 2009; Plaza-Bonilla et [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   al., 2014a; Sanz-Cobeña et al., 2012; Vallejo et al., 2001). Although there is a growing set of [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   knowledge about the impact of fertilization management on N2O emissions in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   Mediterranean areas, the site and temporal specificity of the studies carried out limit our ability [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   to establish general rules for optimized management practices at the regional level, according [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   to climate idiosyncrasies. In particular it would be useful to better know, among fertilization [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   management strategies, which ones are more strongly affected by a more or less pronounced [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   aridity or precipitation variability. Process based models are complementary to site specific [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   studies in that they have the potential to integrate a range of processes and to study the [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   interactions between pedoclimatic conditions and agricultural management practices (De [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   Antoni-Migliorati et al., 2015; Doltra et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_6]

101          The objectives of this study were to i) compile a dataset of N2O emission obtained in [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   Mediterranean conditions and evaluate the ability of a simulation soil-crop model (STICS) to [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   predict the observed fluxes, ii) evaluate its ability to capture the effect of a gradient in mean [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   precipitation and year to year irregularity on N2O emissions in a Mediterranean transect and [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   iii) to test the efficiency of several agricultural management strategies for mitigating N2O [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   emissions along such a climatic gradient. [SOURCE: Page_6]

108          2.1.    Selection of a representative precipitation transect under Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   conditions [SOURCE: Page_7]

110          Three locations representative of the Mediterranean climate were chosen according to [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   a rainfall gradient: Senés, Selvanera and Auzeville (Table 1). This choice was based on (i) the [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   availability of soil N2O emission data in (or close to) each location to validate the STICS model [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   or check the range of simulated values (Plaza-Bonilla et al., 2014a; Peyrard et al., 2016) and [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   (ii) the presence of cropping systems representative of dryland Mediterranean agriculture. [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   Senés (NE Spain), representative of the Monegros county, was chosen as the lower yield [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   potential threshold given its low annual precipitation (336 mm), large potential [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   evapotranspiration (1250 mm) and long water deficit period (Fig. 1). The upper threshold of [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   the transect was located in Auzeville (SW France) which represents one of the most northern [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   latitude under Mediterranean climate influence and the smallest water deficit (with annual [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   precipitation and potential evapotranspiration values of 685 and 905 mm, respectively) (Fig. [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   1). Finally, Selvanera (NE Spain) was chosen given its intermediate water deficit, annual [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   precipitation (450 mm) and potential evapotranspiration (800 mm) (Fig. 1). Site and soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   characteristics of each location of the transect are shown in Table 1. The average productivity [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   of winter cereal (i.e. wheat or barley) is around 1000, 3200 and 5500 kg ha-1 for Senés, [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   Selvanera and Auzeville, respectively (Plaza-Bonilla et al., 2014b, 2016). [SOURCE: Page_7]

126      Traditional cropping systems consist of barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) monocropping or a [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   barley-wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) rotation in Senés, winter cereal rotations including winter [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   pea (Pisum sativum L.) and/or rapeseed (Brassica napus L.) in Selvanera and wheat – [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   sunflower (Helianthus annuus L.) rotation in Auzeville. Only a small proportion of the farmers [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   includes grain legumes such as vetch (Vicia sativa L.) and winter pea (Pisum sativum L.) in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   rotations in Senés and Selvanera given the high probability of harvest loss due to water stress [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   and doubtful economic benefit (Álvaro-Fuentes et al., 2009). Soil management in the area of [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   Senés and Selvanera is based on reduced tillage (i.e. vertical tillage with chisel ploughs and/or [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   cultivators) and no-tillage (Álvaro-Fuentes et al., 2009; Angás et al., 2006). In turn, in the area [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   surrounding Auzeville, traditional soil management is based on conventional, inversion tillage [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   with moldboard plough (Plaza-Bonilla et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_8]

138          2.2.    Overview and evaluation of the soil-crop model STICS for N2O emissions. [SOURCE: Page_8]

139          The soil and crop model STICS (Brisson et al., 1998, 2002, 2008) is a one-dimensional [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   daily time-step model which simulates plant growth as well as water, C and N cycles over one [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   or several growing seasons. It considers several soil layers with specific properties and uses [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   pedoclimatic characteristics and management practices as inputs for the simulation. The [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   description of the physical and biological processes occurring in the soil-crop system mostly [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   relies on a set of generic parameters which are considered not specific of a context of study and [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   thus are not subject to any calibration. This reduces the risk of introducing bias through specific [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   calibration which often relies on limited or uncertain data when comparing scenarios over a [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   range of contexts. Recently, Coucheney et al. (2015) evaluated the ability of the STICS model [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   to simulate different plant, water and nitrogen outputs over a wide range of pedoclimatic [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   conditions and N fertilization rates in France. Despite the large diversity of conditions [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   considered and the use of the standard set of model parameters, simulated results were shown [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   to be good enough to allow useful predictions of crop and soil variables when the objective is [SOURCE: Page_8]

152   to compare a variety of pedoclimatic and agronomic situations. [SOURCE: Page_8]

153          The simulation of N2O emissions in STICS relies on the concepts described in Bessou [SOURCE: Page_8]

154   et al. (2010). Nitrification and denitrification processes, and the N2O emissions associated to [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   each process, are simulated separately. Coupling of nitrification and denitrification exists [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   through the production of nitrate by nitrification, which then serves as substrate for [SOURCE: Page_8]

7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

157   denitrification and N2O emissions associated to this pathway. Processes are described using a [SOURCE: Page_9]

158   functional approach which is similar to that used in other models like DayCent or Ecosse. [SOURCE: Page_9]

159           Nitrification rate is considered to be proportional to NH$_4$+ content, which proved to be [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   a good approximation over the typical range of ammonium content in soil. It is affected by [SOURCE: Page_9]

161   temperature, increasing until an optimum rate at 32.5°C and then decreasing again (Benoit et [SOURCE: Page_9]

162   al., 2015). Water filled pore space (WFPS) strongly influences the rate of nitrification: [SOURCE: Page_9]

163   nitrification increases until field capacity is reached, then decreases because of the decline of [SOURCE: Page_9]

164   soil aeration (Khalil et al., 2004). In the context of the study, pH does not constrain nitrification [SOURCE: Page_9]

165   rate as pH levels are in the high range. N2O emission associated to the nitrification pathway is [SOURCE: Page_9]

166   calculated as a variable fraction of the nitrification rate. That fraction remains low for WFPS [SOURCE: Page_9]

167   values below 60% (0.16-0.29%) but strongly increase with WFPS afterwards to reach a [SOURCE: Page_9]

168   maximum of 2.56% at 100% WFPS as a consequence of the resulting decline in oxygen [SOURCE: Page_9]

169   availability (Khalil et al., 2004). [SOURCE: Page_9]

170           Denitrification is calculated as the product of a soil dependent potential rate and [SOURCE: Page_9]

171   functions expressing the effects of nitrate concentration, soil temperature and soil water [SOURCE: Page_9]

172   content. Denitrification rate increases with NO$_3$- content following a Michaelis-Menten [SOURCE: Page_9]

173   kinetics with a half maximum constant of 215 mg NO$_3$-N l-1 which, depending on soil water [SOURCE: Page_9]

174   content, corresponds to 20-60 mg NO$_3$-N kg-1 (Bessou et al., 2010). Denitrification rate also [SOURCE: Page_9]

175   increases with temperature over most of the typical range of temperature as the optimum rate [SOURCE: Page_9]

176   is 47°C (Benoit et al., 2015). As in the NEMIS model (Hénault and Germon, 2000), [SOURCE: Page_9]

177   denitrification rate is null when WFPS is below a threshold which default value is 62% and [SOURCE: Page_9]

178   then increases exponentially with WFPS. N2O emission from denitrification is calculated as a [SOURCE: Page_9]

179   variable fraction of the denitrification rate which mainly depends on pH and WFPS. Acid pH [SOURCE: Page_9]

180   strongly inhibits N2O reduction to N$_2$ (Rochester, 2003). In the model, the N2O end-product [SOURCE: Page_9]

181   ratio of denitrification increases with pH decreasing from 9.2 to 5.6. For pH values below 6, [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

182   denitrification has N2O as main end-product. Finally, soil water content values close to [SOURCE: Page_10]

183   saturation favor N2O reduction to N$_2$. This is due to the development of anoxia (Vieten et al., [SOURCE: Page_10]

184   2008) and to the increase in the residence time of N2O into the soil associated to lower gaseous [SOURCE: Page_10]

185   diffusion rates. This effect is taken into account through a linear decrease of the denitrification [SOURCE: Page_10]

186   end-product ratio between the threshold WFPS for the onset of denitrification and maximal soil [SOURCE: Page_10]

187   saturation (Bessou et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_10]

188          Observed data from the multi-year MicMac low input cropping systems experiment [SOURCE: Page_10]

189   located in Auzeville (SW France), which includes intensive (i.e. daily) N2O emissions [SOURCE: Page_10]

190   measurements, were used to evaluate the ability of the STICS model in simulating N2O [SOURCE: Page_10]

191   emissions, in complement to simple comparison of the range of values of emissions for the two [SOURCE: Page_10]

192   other sites. The Auzeville experiment compares three cropping systems which differ in nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_10]

193   rates, pesticide use and cover crops frequency in a randomized design with three blocks. [SOURCE: Page_10]

194   Further details of the experiment can be found in Peyrard et al. (2016) and Plaza-Bonilla et al. [SOURCE: Page_10]

195   (2015). We focused on N2O emissions data obtained during the 2010 to 2014 period under 12 [SOURCE: Page_10]

196   winter wheat and faba bean (Vicia faba L.) phases of the rotation. These crops are [SOURCE: Page_10]

197   representative of the production under Mediterranean dryland conditions (i.e. winter growing [SOURCE: Page_10]

198   cycle) (López-Bellido et al., 1992; Loss and Siddique, 1994). For each crop, the daily N2O [SOURCE: Page_10]

199   emissions observation period covered the entire growing season, from sowing until harvest, [SOURCE: Page_10]

200   except in 2012 when the measurements were stopped after the top-dressing fertilization of [SOURCE: Page_10]

201   wheat (Table 2). Crop management practices carried out during the experimental period during [SOURCE: Page_10]

202   the observed growing seasons are reported in Tables S$_1$ and S$_2$ (Supplementary material). N2O [SOURCE: Page_10]

203   emission data for each crop cycle were obtained from a set of three automatic chambers (0.70 [SOURCE: Page_10]

204   x 0.70 m) inserted into the soil to a depth of 5-10 cm. Four times a day (i.e. each 6-hr) N2O [SOURCE: Page_10]

205   concentration in the headspace of each chamber was quantified over a 20 min period with a 10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

206   s time step. Fluxes at the time of chamber closure were calculated from the initial increase of [SOURCE: Page_10]

9 [SOURCE: Page_10]

207   the concentration vs. time, using a linear or exponential model fit. A linear model was used if [SOURCE: Page_11]

208   the rate constant of the exponential model was lower than 0.01 min-1 or the quality of fit of the [SOURCE: Page_11]

209   exponential was not better than that of the linear (RMSEexp/RMSElin > 0.975) (Peyrard et al. [SOURCE: Page_11]

210   2016). Daily emissions were then calculated from the average of the 4 fluxes measured per [SOURCE: Page_11]

211   day. The soil temperature at 10 cm depth was monitored with the use of two T107 soil probes [SOURCE: Page_11]

212   (Campbell Scientific Inc, Logan, UT, USA). Monthly soil samplings were carried out at the 0- [SOURCE: Page_11]

213   15 and 15-30 cm soil layers to quantify soil water and mineral N contents. A continuous flow [SOURCE: Page_11]

214   autoanalyzer (Skalar 5100, Skalar Analytic, Erkelenz, Germany) was used for mineral N [SOURCE: Page_11]

215   quantification (NH$_4$+ and NO$_3$-) after 1 M KCl extraction. Values were transformed to a [SOURCE: Page_11]

216   gravimetric basis using soil bulk density, which was quantified at the beginning and at the end [SOURCE: Page_11]

217   of each N2O measurement period (i.e. at sowing and crop harvest) by taking two replicate [SOURCE: Page_11]

218   samples of the 0-15 and 15-30 cm soil depths using a 500 cm^3 metal cylinder. [SOURCE: Page_11]

219          Climatic data (i.e. maximum and minimum air temperatures, global radiation, rainfall, [SOURCE: Page_11]

220   wind, vapor pressure and Penman evapotranspiration) and soil characteristics of each plot (i.e. [SOURCE: Page_11]

221   soil depth, clay proportion, organic N, C:N ratio, CaCO$_3$, pH, albedo, bulk density, water [SOURCE: Page_11]

222   content at field capacity and permanent wilting point) were used to run the STICS model. The [SOURCE: Page_11]

223   average of soil bulk density values at sowing and at harvest was used for the simulations. Five [SOURCE: Page_11]

224   soil layers were considered for simulations: 0-15, 15-30, 30-60, 60-90 and 90-120 cm. The [SOURCE: Page_11]

225   ploughed layer was divided into two layers (0-15 and 15-30 cm) given its key importance for [SOURCE: Page_11]

226   the nitrification and denitrification processes and its consistency with soil layers used for [SOURCE: Page_11]

227   measurements. The model was run without changing the standard parameters controlling the [SOURCE: Page_11]

228   soil physical and biological processes, except the potential rate of N2O emission from [SOURCE: Page_11]

229   denitrification and threshold WFPS for the onset of denitrification which were modified to [SOURCE: Page_11]

230   improve the quality of fit at Auzeville. They were set at 2.0 kg NO$_3$--N ha-1 d-1 over 0-20 cm [SOURCE: Page_11]

231   and 50%, respectively. Because of the limited availability of measured data on the two other [SOURCE: Page_11]

10 [SOURCE: Page_11]

232   sites and the proximity of these values to the default ones, these parameters were kept constant [SOURCE: Page_12]

233   for all the three sites considered in the study. Simulated and observed N2O cumulative [SOURCE: Page_12]

234   emissions were compared and the r^2 value, the efficiency of the model (EF), the relative root [SOURCE: Page_12]

235   mean square error (rRMSE) and the mean difference (MD) were calculated according to Eq. [SOURCE: Page_12]

236   S$_1$ (Supplementary material). [SOURCE: Page_12]

237          2.3.    Simulation scenarios [SOURCE: Page_12]

238          The choice of the simulation scenarios aimed at meeting the requirements of the [SOURCE: Page_12]

239   Mediterranean conditions of the transect: (i) the preponderance of cereals in the rotations given [SOURCE: Page_12]

240   their adaptation to water scarcity (López-Bellido, 1992; Álvaro-Fuentes et al., 2009); (ii) the [SOURCE: Page_12]

241   sole use of winter cash crops, since summer crops would rely on irrigation in the sites with [SOURCE: Page_12]

242   highest water deficit (i.e. HWD-Senés and MDW-Selvanera) and cover crops can result in [SOURCE: Page_12]

243   competition for water with cash crops in dryland semiarid areas (Vigil and Nielsen, 1998; [SOURCE: Page_12]

244   Unger and Vigil, 1998); and (iii) the use of tillage, given the low degree of adoption of no-till [SOURCE: Page_12]

245   techniques in the area of the site with the lowest water deficit (LWD-Auzeville) (DRAAF, [SOURCE: Page_12]

246   2014). Soil characteristics (Table 1) and the 2005-2014 climatic data obtained at or close to [SOURCE: Page_12]

247   each site (Fig. 1) were introduced as model inputs. In order to compare between sites, the same [SOURCE: Page_12]

248   values of bulk density were taken for the three sites: 1.4 g cm-3 for the 0-15 and 15-30 cm soil [SOURCE: Page_12]

249   depths and 1.5 g cm-3 for the soil horizons below 30 cm depth, close to actual observed values. [SOURCE: Page_12]

250          The capacity of different management practices to mitigate N2O emissions were tested [SOURCE: Page_12]

251   by simulating scenarios differing in: (i) form of N fertilizer (ammonium nitrate AN, calcium [SOURCE: Page_12]

252   nitrate CN, or urea), (ii) rotation, either three cycles of a 3-yr winter cereal rotation (wheat- [SOURCE: Page_12]

253   wheat-barley, called Cer) or three cycles of a mixed rotation (winter pea-wheat-barley, called [SOURCE: Page_12]

254   CerLeg) reducing synthetic N dependence, and (iii) crop residue management (incorporated or [SOURCE: Page_12]

255   removed residues). [SOURCE: Page_12]

11 [SOURCE: Page_12]

256          A simulation of 9 growing seasons was chosen to (i) represent the climatic variability [SOURCE: Page_13]

257   of the Mediterranean conditions, and (ii) to take into account the carry over effect of the [SOURCE: Page_13]

258   preceding crop of the rotations. Moreover, all the phases of each rotation were simulated each [SOURCE: Page_13]

259   year to take into account the climate x crop interaction. Thus, when not specifically mentioned [SOURCE: Page_13]

260   in the text, annual values correspond to the mean of the three phases. Simulated management [SOURCE: Page_13]

261   practices for the different crops are described in Table 3. For N fertilizer applications, different [SOURCE: Page_13]

262   rates were used on each site according to their yield potential (Table 3). In the rotation including [SOURCE: Page_13]

263   winter pea, N fertilizer applied to the succeeding wheat crop was reduced by 55 kg N ha-1 for [SOURCE: Page_13]

264   LWD-Auzeville, taking into account the values measured in the experiment (Plaza-Bonilla et [SOURCE: Page_13]

265   al., 2015). For MWD-Selvanera and HWD-Senés sites a reduction of 35 and 10 kg N ha-1 was [SOURCE: Page_13]

266   considered according to the site-specific potential yield compared to LWD-Auzeville. The [SOURCE: Page_13]

267   model was run continuously, i.e. without re-initialization, for each scenario (i.e. from 2006 to [SOURCE: Page_13]

268   2014). Simulated wheat yield was used as an agronomical indicator of productivity. [SOURCE: Page_13]

416       4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_19]

417                 4.1.Rainfall variability, crop yields and N2O emissions [SOURCE: Page_19]

418            Tier 1 methodology proposed by the IPCC (2006) does not take into account explicitly [SOURCE: Page_19]

419   pedoclimatic and agricultural management influences on N2O emissions. The results of the [SOURCE: Page_19]

420   present work highlight the potential large variability of N2O emissions under Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_19]

421   conditions, both along an aridity gradient and from year to year. As a consequence, the use of [SOURCE: Page_19]

422   models (i.e. Tier 3) seems especially relevant in the Mediterranean context for a more precise [SOURCE: Page_19]

423   quantification of N2O emissions taking into account its typical strong variability (Conen et al., [SOURCE: Page_19]

424   2000). [SOURCE: Page_19]

425            The ability of the STICS model to simulate N2O emissions was first evaluated using [SOURCE: Page_19]

426   data observed in the LWD-Auzeville for which daily measurements were available. The test [SOURCE: Page_19]

427   indicated a reasonably consistency between observed and simulated cumulative values which [SOURCE: Page_19]

428   varied between 0.28-1.44 and 0.46-1.71 kg N2O-N ha-1 yr-1, respectively. The mean cumulative [SOURCE: Page_19]

18 [SOURCE: Page_19]

429   N2O emissions predicted by the STICS model in the two other sites, HWD-Senés and MWD- [SOURCE: Page_20]

430   Selvanera, were 0.26 and 0.65 kg N2O-N ha-1 yr-1, which falls in the range of published values [SOURCE: Page_20]

431   for semiarid dryland Mediterranean conditions. For instance, Plaza-Bonilla et al. (2014a) [SOURCE: Page_20]

432   measured the effect of different tillage and N fertilizer management practices on N2O emissions [SOURCE: Page_20]

433   in one experimental site close to HWD-Senés and another slightly drier than MWD-Selvanera [SOURCE: Page_20]

434   (i.e. with an annual water deficit of 425 mm compared to the 350 mm of the MWD site), both [SOURCE: Page_20]

435   under winter cereal production in dryland conditions. In the site close to HWD-Senés, they [SOURCE: Page_20]

436   measured an emission of 0.16, 0.21 and 0.58 kg N2O-N ha-1 for three rates of fertilizer (0, 75 [SOURCE: Page_20]

437   and 150 kg N ha-1) as ammonium sulphate and AN under conventional tillage (i.e. disk plough [SOURCE: Page_20]

438   and chisel). In the site drier than MWD-Selvanera, the emission was 0.08, 0.41 and 0.38 kg [SOURCE: Page_20]

439   N2O-N ha-1 yr-1 when applying 0, 60 and 120 kg N ha-1 as ammonium sulphate and AN with a [SOURCE: Page_20]

440   full inversion tillage based on moldboard ploughing. Similar magnitudes were measured in a [SOURCE: Page_20]

441   semiarid area in Central Spain with 315 mm of annual water deficit (calculated as ETo minus [SOURCE: Page_20]

442   precipitation); cumulative N2O emissions of 0.35 kg N ha-1 during a spring barley growing [SOURCE: Page_20]

443   season (i.e. from January to June) fertilized with urea were reported (Meijide et al., 2009). At [SOURCE: Page_20]

444   the same site, Ábalos et al. (2013) measured the N2O emissions under a rainfed barley crop. [SOURCE: Page_20]

445   They found a cumulative emission of 0.67 and 1.30 kg N2O-N ha-1, respectively, after removing [SOURCE: Page_20]

446   or incorporating the previous corn residues, during the period from the incorporation of [SOURCE: Page_20]

447   residues to the harvest (i.e. mid-November to end of June). A similar range of emission (0.32- [SOURCE: Page_20]

448   0.68 kg N2O-N ha-1 yr-1) was reported by Kessavalou et al. (1998) in the semiarid High Plains [SOURCE: Page_20]

449   of the USA in a wheat-fallow rotation. This range is consistent with that obtained in the [SOURCE: Page_20]

450   simulations, which broadens the confidence in simulated results despite the usual difficulties [SOURCE: Page_20]

451   in simulating the dynamics of N2O emissions. [SOURCE: Page_20]

452          As pointed out before, the significant interannual variability of rainfall had a clear [SOURCE: Page_20]

453   influence on cumulative N2O emissions simulated by the model in the three sites analyzed. In [SOURCE: Page_20]

19 [SOURCE: Page_20]

454   temperate semiarid areas and at the landscape scale, fluctuation of N2O emission is mainly [SOURCE: Page_21]

455   influenced by precipitation and irrigation, and when soil moisture is favorable, soil mineral N, [SOURCE: Page_21]

456   temperature and labile C availability become important (Corre et al., 1996; Rowlings et al., [SOURCE: Page_21]

457   2015). The CV of the simulated cumulative N2O emissions reached 52, 49 and 27% for HWD- [SOURCE: Page_21]

458   Senés, MWD-Selvanera and LWD-Auzeville respectively, showing the strong interannual [SOURCE: Page_21]

459   variability of N2O fluxes in the Mediterranean conditions. Similar high interannual variability [SOURCE: Page_21]

460   was reported in the few multiyear greenhouse gas datasets published in the literature. In the [SOURCE: Page_21]

461   site equivalent to HWD-Senés, Plaza-Bonilla et al. (2014a) found a CV of 20% for cumulative [SOURCE: Page_21]

462   emissions measured over two seasons (2011-13) and five fertilization treatments. Du et al. [SOURCE: Page_21]

463   (2006) reported an interannual CV of 71% when measuring N2O emissions in a cold semi-arid [SOURCE: Page_21]

464   grassland in inner Mongolia. [SOURCE: Page_21]

465          The STICS model predicted that water deficit exerted a large impact on crop yields, [SOURCE: Page_21]

466   with a winter cereal yield varying between 0.5 and 9.5 Mg ha-1 along the transect. Such a [SOURCE: Page_21]

467   variation is commonly observed under Mediterranean conditions depending on the amount of [SOURCE: Page_21]

468   water available for the crop (Cantero-Martínez et al., 2003, 2007). Plaza-Bonilla et al. (2014b) [SOURCE: Page_21]

469   compared three sites with different yield potentials along the semiarid Ebro valley (NE Spain). [SOURCE: Page_21]

470   They found that winter cereal production ranged from 0.4 to 8.2 Mg ha-1 in the 2009-10 [SOURCE: Page_21]

471   growing season. Simulated winter pea yields were very low in HWD-Senés, between 0.1 and [SOURCE: Page_21]

472   1.4 Mg ha-1, demonstrating the probability of crop failure and associated economic risk when [SOURCE: Page_21]

473   establishing grain legumes in the most extreme locations under Mediterranean conditions, as [SOURCE: Page_21]

474   reported by Álvaro-Fuentes et al. (2009). [SOURCE: Page_21]

475          4.2.    Management scenarios for N2O mitigation [SOURCE: Page_21]

476          We compared the potential to reduce N2O emissions in Mediterranean dryland field [SOURCE: Page_21]

477   crops production by different scenarios of management practices. All driving variables (i.e. [SOURCE: Page_21]

478   proportion of O$_2$ in the soil pores, temperature, mineral N and labile C) were directly affected [SOURCE: Page_21]

20 [SOURCE: Page_21]

479   by agricultural management practices (e.g. N fertilization, crop residues incorporation, soil [SOURCE: Page_22]

480   management, etc.), in interaction with the soil water status. The simulations carried out showed [SOURCE: Page_22]

481   the sensitivity of N2O emissions to the nature of the fertilizer used (including substitution of [SOURCE: Page_22]

482   legumes in the rotation instead of mineral N fertilizer) and residue management, effects well [SOURCE: Page_22]

483   documented in the existing literature (e.g. Bouwman et al., 2002; Ábalos et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_22]

484          The introduction of one winter grain legume (i.e. winter pea) in the traditional cereal- [SOURCE: Page_22]

485   based rotations reduced significantly the emissions of N2O only in the driest site (HWD-Senés), [SOURCE: Page_22]

486   given the low amount of winter pea residues produced susceptible of being decomposed during [SOURCE: Page_22]

487   the successive phase of the rotation (i.e. wheat phase). In LWD-Auzeville and MWD- [SOURCE: Page_22]

488   Selvanera, the reduction of N2O emissions simulated as a result of the N fertilizer savings in [SOURCE: Page_22]

489   durum wheat preceded by winter pea, was offset by an increase in emissions due to the [SOURCE: Page_22]

490   decomposition of the crop residues from the winter pea crop. The simulated N2O emissions [SOURCE: Page_22]

491   during the wheat phase preceded by winter pea in the CerLeg rotation were 35 and 48% larger [SOURCE: Page_22]

492   than the corresponding wheat phase of the Cer rotation in MWD-Selvanera and LWD-Senés, [SOURCE: Page_22]

493   respectively. In a recent meta-analysis, Basche et al. (2014) reported that the incorporation of [SOURCE: Page_22]

494   legume cover crop residues to the soil increased N2O emissions when compared to the [SOURCE: Page_22]

495   incorporation of cereal crop residues with a larger C:N ratio. The incorporation of low C:N [SOURCE: Page_22]

496   ratio residues to the soil usually increases net N mineralization contributing to higher nitrate [SOURCE: Page_22]

497   availability, and concomitantly to larger N2O emissions (Firestone and Davidson, 1989). [SOURCE: Page_22]

498          Contradictory results were obtained by De Antoni-Migliorati et al., (2015) when using [SOURCE: Page_22]

499   the DayCent model in a simulation study carried out in irrigated Oxisols under subtropical [SOURCE: Page_22]

500   conditions. The authors tested the interest of introducing legume pastures in summer cereal- [SOURCE: Page_22]

501   based (i.e. sorghum) cropping systems. They simulated a reduction in N2O emissions when [SOURCE: Page_22]

502   introducing legume pastures and lowering sorghum N-fertilization by 30%. However, under [SOURCE: Page_22]

503   those subtropical conditions sorghum is planted right after the incorporation of the legume, [SOURCE: Page_22]

21 [SOURCE: Page_22]

504   improving the synchrony between crop demand and N availability due to legume residues [SOURCE: Page_23]

505   decomposition, reducing the susceptibility to N2O emissions. Unfortunately, under dryland [SOURCE: Page_23]

506   Mediterranean conditions, the establishment of two crops per year (i.e. a cash and cover crop [SOURCE: Page_23]

507   succession or a double cropping) is impeded by water scarcity, being only possible in the [SOURCE: Page_23]

508   wettest sites and/or with irrigation. As a consequence, N2O emissions associated to residue [SOURCE: Page_23]

509   decomposition and mineral N release can occur during the fallow period between grain legume [SOURCE: Page_23]

510   harvest (i.e. June) and subsequent crop sowing (i.e. November) as already observed at the LWD [SOURCE: Page_23]

511   site (Peyrard et al. 2016). However, Barton et al. (2014) suggested that it is unlikely that grain [SOURCE: Page_23]

512   legume cropping would increase soil N2O emissions in semi-arid conditions. These [SOURCE: Page_23]

513   contradictory findings suggest the need to carry out more field-based measurements of N2O [SOURCE: Page_23]

514   emissions under semi-arid conditions when diversifying cereal-based cropping systems, which [SOURCE: Page_23]

515   could be designed with the help of model simulations. [SOURCE: Page_23]

516          According to our simulations, the removal of crop residues would reduce the emissions [SOURCE: Page_23]

517   of N2O by 0.45 kg N2O-N ha-1 yr-1 in LWD-Auzeville, the most productive site. After [SOURCE: Page_23]

518   compiling studies carried out in eastern Canada and northeastern US, Gregorich et al. (2005) [SOURCE: Page_23]

519   reported larger N2O emissions when incorporating stubble residues in soil instead of leaving [SOURCE: Page_23]

520   crop residues on the soil surface: 2.41 vs. 1.19 kg N2O-N ha-1 yr-1. Abalos et al. (2013) also [SOURCE: Page_23]

521   found a marked increase (+105%) in N2O emissions when incorporating maize stover in a non- [SOURCE: Page_23]

522   irrigated barley crop compared to crop residues removal. They attributed the increase to a larger [SOURCE: Page_23]

523   amount of dissolved organic carbon, nitrate availability and more anaerobic microsites [SOURCE: Page_23]

524   enhancing denitrification due to the maize stover. However, the removal of crop residues [SOURCE: Page_23]

525   represents a significant loss of soil organic carbon and soil quality which could counteract the [SOURCE: Page_23]

526   lower N2O emissions and increase the C footprint of the system. The input of C to the soil as [SOURCE: Page_23]

527   crop residues is of a paramount importance to maintain the stock of soil organic C under [SOURCE: Page_23]

528   dryland conditions (Plaza-Bonilla et al., 2016). Taking into account a GWP of 265 (IPCC, [SOURCE: Page_23]

22 [SOURCE: Page_23]

529   2013) the emission of 0.45 kg N2O-N ha-1 yr-1 would be equivalent to the sequestration of 50 [SOURCE: Page_24]

530   kg C ha-1 yr-1 in the soil, a value of C sequestration which can be reached under dryland [SOURCE: Page_24]

531   conditions when returning the crop residues to the soil (Lal, 2004). [SOURCE: Page_24]

532          Given its lower price, urea is one of the most commonly used nitrogenous fertilizers by [SOURCE: Page_24]

533   farmers in the Mediterranean basin (FAOSTAT; Ryan et al., 2009). However, the simulation [SOURCE: Page_24]

534   predicted an increase in N2O emissions when applying urea compared to ammonium nitrate, [SOURCE: Page_24]

535   due to greater losses of N2O by nitrification. Decock (2014) analyzed data collected in corn [SOURCE: Page_24]

536   cropping systems of the Midwestern US and South-East Canada and found a tendency of larger [SOURCE: Page_24]

537   N2O emission for ammonium nitrate than for urea. However, she pointed out that fertilizer N- [SOURCE: Page_24]

538   forms different from nitrate would enhance N2O emissions in dry conditions that favor [SOURCE: Page_24]

539   nitrification. Urea can also lead to larger NH$_3$ losses to the atmosphere than other N fertilizers, [SOURCE: Page_24]

540   particularly in the Mediterranean region where soils are commonly basic (Sanz-Cobena et al., [SOURCE: Page_24]

541   2008; Ryan et al., 2009). One technological option for the reduction of N2O losses from [SOURCE: Page_24]

542   synthetic fertilizers is the use of nitrification inhibitors. Although it has received much [SOURCE: Page_24]

543   attention, such chemicals are unlikely to be used extensively, due to its high cost and the low [SOURCE: Page_24]

544   productivity of field crops under dryland Mediterranean conditions (Ryan et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_24]

547      5. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_25]

548          Results from this simulation work indicate that N2O emissions from rainfed field crop [SOURCE: Page_25]

549   production in a water deficit transect, range from 0.3 to 2.5 kg N2O-N ha-1 yr-1, covering a large [SOURCE: Page_25]

550   panel of agricultural dryland production under Mediterranean conditions. The lower N2O [SOURCE: Page_25]

551   emissions in the driest sites were not only related to lower N fertilization rates but also to other [SOURCE: Page_25]

552   factors associated with the Mediterranean characteristics, particularly, the dryer water regime. [SOURCE: Page_25]

553   The interannual variability of rainfall, which is an intrinsic characteristic of the Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_25]

554   climate, strongly influenced the simulated emissions of N2O during the 9-yr period considered, [SOURCE: Page_25]

555   with an inter-annual variability ranging between 27 and 52% (depending on the site). Thus, [SOURCE: Page_25]

556   when designing experiments, emission measurements over at least a complete rotation and/or [SOURCE: Page_25]

557   several years should be favored. Simulated seasonal N2O emissions were driven by N [SOURCE: Page_25]

558   fertilization events and low C:N ratio of crop residues (i.e. legumes) decomposing after harvest. [SOURCE: Page_25]

559          All management options aiming at soil N2O emissions mitigation were affected by [SOURCE: Page_25]

560   climate. According to the model, the application of urea would increase N2O emissions when [SOURCE: Page_25]

561   compared to other fertilizers partially or totally based on nitrate. This effect was observed in [SOURCE: Page_25]

562   all cases but its intensity varies with the water deficit gradient. This model output, which [SOURCE: Page_25]

563   remains to be demonstrated experimentally, can be explained by winter crops growing in the [SOURCE: Page_25]

564   specific conditions of the Mediterranean climate, which favor nitrification N2O losses. The [SOURCE: Page_25]

565   introduction of winter pea in the traditional cereal-based rotation only reduced N2O emissions [SOURCE: Page_25]

566   in the driest site. In the sites with greater yield potential, the decomposition of pea residues [SOURCE: Page_25]

567   (with low C:N ratio) could have counteracted the N2O emissions associated with the reduction [SOURCE: Page_25]

568   in N fertilizer applied to the succeeding crop. Crop residue removal reduced N2O emissions in [SOURCE: Page_25]

569   the most productive site with the lowest water deficit, i.e. LWD-Auzeville. However, reducing [SOURCE: Page_25]

570   crop residues return to soil could lead to soil carbon losses which is negative both for the overall [SOURCE: Page_25]

571   C footprint of the system, soil quality and future yields. Our work demonstrates the usefulness [SOURCE: Page_25]

24 [SOURCE: Page_25]

572   of modelling approaches combined with experimental data, to take into account climatic [SOURCE: Page_26]

573   variability and explore how the efficiency of mitigation practices is affected by the agro-pedo- [SOURCE: Page_26]

574   climatic conditions. [SOURCE: Page_26]
