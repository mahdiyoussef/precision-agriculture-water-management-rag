30   Abstract 31   Analyzing how climate change has affected forest growth is crucial for predicting future [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   dynamics and adapting forest management to future climate change. In this paper, we [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   investigate how climate change has modified stand dominant height dynamics and site [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   index of 20 European tree species. We used an innovative method based on an annual [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   height increment equation to model stand dominant height as a function of climate back to [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   1872 and of other stand environmental conditions. We used these models to simulate stand [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   dominant height dynamics and site index under two different climates (prior to climate [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   change and actual recent climate) to analyze the impact of climate change over the past [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   century. To build our models, we combined the recently published FYRE long-term climate [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   database, which provides daily data since 1871, with data from more than 17,000 forest [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   stands of the French National Forest Inventory network. Higher temperature, precipitation [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   and climatic water balance generally favor stand dominant height dynamics when the [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   variables are considered separately. However, the positive effects often saturate at the [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   higher end of the variable distribution. Over the past century, the effect of climate change [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   on the site index has varied widely among species, ranging from a decrease of less than 3% [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   to an increase of more than 5%. The effect of climate change has also varied within species, [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   with more positive effects on initially temperature-limited stands for some species. For the [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   species and environmental conditions considered, our results highlight a positive response [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   of site index to past climate change for most species, albeit with between- and within- [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   species differences. Our results also suggest that this positive response could become [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   negative under continued climate change. These conclusions, as well as the quantitative [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   relationships we provide between climate and stand dominant height dynamics or site [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   index, will help design management strategies to adapt forests to climate change. [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   In the context of rapid climate change, indicators used in forest management should take [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   into account the impact of climate change on forest dynamics. This is a prerequisite for [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   implementing management strategies that promote adaptation to climate change. [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   Stand productivity, defined as “the potential of a particular forest stand to produce [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   aboveground wood volume” (Skovsgaard and Vanclay, 2008) is a key indicator for forest [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   management (Socha et al., 2020). Since it largely depends on climatic and soil factors, it is [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   important to develop models assessing the impact of climate change on site productivity. In [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   pure even-aged stands, the average height of the tallest trees, defined as “dominant height”, [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   is an interesting indicator to analyze stand productivity. In such stands, the increase in [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   stand dominant height indeed correlates with stand volume growth and is largely [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   independent of stand density, provided that thinning is not done from above (Skovsgaard [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   and Vanclay, 2008). A common indicator of stand productivity in pure even-aged forest is [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   therefore site index, defined as stand dominant height at a given reference age, e.g. at 100 [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   years (Skovsgaard and Vanclay, 2008). [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   Stand dominant height dynamics and site index depend on environmental conditions [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   (Vallet and Perot, 2016; Sharma et al., 2015; Scolforo et al., 2020). It is therefore important [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   to better understand how climate change affect both. Existing studies across a variety of [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   species and contexts have generally found a positive effect of higher temperature and water [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   availability on site index (e.g. Messaoud and Chen, 2011; Brandl et al. 2018; González- [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   Rodríguez and Diéguez-Aranda, 2021) and stand dominant height dynamics (e.g. Vallet and [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   Perot, 2016). However, the sign and magnitude of climate effect vary among species and [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   environmental conditions (Albert and Schmidt, 2010; Pau et al., 2022) and the positive [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   effects of temperature or precipitation may saturate when they reach a certain level (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   Brandl et al., 2018). Such climate–growth relationships have been well studied for some [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   iconic European tree species such as Picea abies (L.) H.Karst or Fagus sylvatica L.. However, [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   climate–growth relationships are scarcer for other species such as Quercus pubescens Willd., [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   Fraxinus excelsior L. or Larix decidua Mill., and it would be useful for forest managers to [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   have information for a larger number of species. [SOURCE: Page_4]

83    The site index for a given site depends on the year of establishment of the stand, in relation [SOURCE: Page_5]

84    to changes in climate, atmospheric CO$_2$ concentration, or N deposition (Sharma et al. 2012; [SOURCE: Page_5]

85    Socha et al., 2021; Messaoud et al., 2022). Bontemps et al. (2012) even found that [SOURCE: Page_5]

86    environmental changes during stand lifetime influence the stand dominant height [SOURCE: Page_5]

87    dynamics, suggesting that the site index evolves during stand lifetime. However, to our [SOURCE: Page_5]

88    knowledge, empirical quantitative relationships on the scale of stand lifetime between on [SOURCE: Page_5]

89    the one hand stand dominant height dynamicsor site index and on the other hand climate [SOURCE: Page_5]

90    change are still lacking for many species. Such relationships would be very useful for forest [SOURCE: Page_5]

91    management to anticipate short-term effects of climate change on stand productivity. [SOURCE: Page_5]

92    One challenge in establishing these relationships is relating stand dominant height [SOURCE: Page_5]

93    observations to climate data at the scale of the year (or a few years). One solution would be [SOURCE: Page_5]

94    to relate annual (or multi-year) climate data to height data at the same temporal resolution. [SOURCE: Page_5]

95    Such high-frequency height data come mainly from stem analysis (Bontemps and Bouriaud [SOURCE: Page_5]

96    2014). However, stem analysis often focuses on a low number of species and comes at the [SOURCE: Page_5]

97    cost of a small number of observations (Bontemps and Bouriaud 2014). This may limit the [SOURCE: Page_5]

98    ability to identify a relationship between climate and stand dominant height, and this may [SOURCE: Page_5]

99    limit the range of climate conditions to which the relationship applies. In contrast, national [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   forest inventories (NFIs) provide large-scale, spatially intensive, randomly sampled data [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   from forest stands and include a large number of species (Bontemps and Bouriaud, 2014; [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   Aguirre et al., 2022). They therefore allow the detection of climate impacts under different [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   resource limitations (Charru et al., 2017). National forest inventories have been used to [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   infer relationships between climate and site index (Brandl et al., 2018) and between climate [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   and stand dominant height dynamics (Vallet and Perot, 2016; Stimm et al., 2021). However, [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   these inventories generally do not include annual series of age and height measurements [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   from the year of establishment. This makes it difficult to study the effects of climate change [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   over the lifetime of stands, and generally leads to building stand dominant height models [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   based only on an aggregate equation linking mean climate to observed stand dominant [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   height (Vallet and Perot, 2016). [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   Spatial heterogeneity in climate influences the effects of climate change on stand dominant [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   height dynamics and site index for a given species or between species. For example, Albert [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   and Schmidt (2010) showed that Picea abies site index responded more negatively to [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   climate change in water-limited environments and Fagus sylvatica site index responded [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   more positively to climate change in temperature-limited environments. Generalizing such [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   results to a larger number of species could help to design forest adaptation strategies to [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   climate change. [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   This paper has three main objectives: first, to develop species-specific models linking [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   climate to stand dominant height dynamics for a large number of European tree species, [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   capable of capturing the impact of climate variation during stand life and accounting for [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   non-linear climate effects; second, to analyze the effect of each climatic variable on site [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   index; third, to assess the impact of climate change over the past century on stand dominant [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   height dynamics and site index. [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   To this end, we developed an original modeling strategy that takes into account the [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   influence of annual climate on stand dominant height dynamics even without repeated [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   measurements of stand dominant height. This strategy allowed us use the data from the [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   French National Forest Inventory (IGN, 2022) and thus to calibrate our models on more [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   than 17,000 stands covering a wide range of species and climate conditions. This modeling [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   strategy relies on the reconstruction of an unobserved stand dominant height trajectory [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   from stand origin based on initial stand dominant height, stand age, and a theory-based [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   stand dominant height increment equation (Tomé et al., 2006; Bontemps et al., 2009) [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   incorporating annual climate. This approach relies critically on the long-term FYRE climate [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   database, which dates back to 1871(Devers et al., 2021). We then used these models to [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   assess the impact of climate change during the past century by comparing simulated stand [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   dominant height dynamics (and site index) under two scenarios: with a climate prior to [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   climate change and with actual climate. [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   Based on these stand dominant height models taking into account annual climate and the [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   associated simulations, we examined the following question: [SOURCE: Page_7]

139     •   Question 1: How do individual climatic variables influence stand dominant height [SOURCE: Page_7]

140         and site index? We tested the following hypotheses: [SOURCE: Page_7]

141            –   Hypothesis 1: Higher temperature, precipitation and climatic water balance [SOURCE: Page_7]

142                during spring and summer favor site index, but these positive effects may [SOURCE: Page_7]

143                saturate beyond a certain value, and in the case of temperature may even be [SOURCE: Page_7]

144                reversed. [SOURCE: Page_7]

145     •   Question 2: What was the effect of climate change on stand dominant height and site [SOURCE: Page_7]

146         index during the last century? We tested the following hypotheses: [SOURCE: Page_7]

147            –   Hypothesis 2a: Climate change has had a different effect (sign and magnitude) [SOURCE: Page_7]

148                depending on the species during the period considered; [SOURCE: Page_7]

149             –   Hypothesis 2b: For a given species, the effect of climate change during the [SOURCE: Page_8]

150                 period considered varied depending on the climate context of the stand. [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   2. Materials and methods [SOURCE: Page_8]

152      2.1.       General approach [SOURCE: Page_8]

153   For each of 20 European tree species, we built a species-specific model to relate observed [SOURCE: Page_8]

154   stand dominant height (SDH) to (i) SDH at the stand establishment date, (ii) climate data [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   series from the stand establishment date, and (iii) other site variables. We then used these [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   models to simulate the SDH dynamics of each stand for a period of 70 years under a [SOURCE: Page_8]

157   reference climate representing the climate before climate change (1891-1920 mean [SOURCE: Page_8]

158   climate) and under the actual climate of the recent period (1950-2020 climate series). This [SOURCE: Page_8]

159   allowed us to evaluate the impact of climate change on SDH dynamics during the last [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   century. We then computed simulated site index as simulated SDH at the age of 70 years. [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   Therefore, simulated site index depends on stand climate history, which enabled us to study [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   the impact of climate change on site index. [SOURCE: Page_8]

163      2.2.       Data used for modeling and simulation [SOURCE: Page_8]

164          2.2.1. National Forest Inventory data and variables [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   We used data from the French National Forest Inventory (IGN, 2022) from 2006 to 2020. [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   Each year, 5000 to 6000 temporary forest plots are measured throughout the French [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   metropolitan territory, through a progressive survey of a grid with a resolution of 1 km. [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   This ensures uniform coverage of the territory for each measurement year. For each stand, [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   the NFI data provides canopy cover per tree species and forest vertical structure (even- [SOURCE: Page_9]

170   aged or uneven-aged forest), determined within a 25m-radius circle. Dominant trees are [SOURCE: Page_9]

171   defined as the six largest-diameter trees in a 15m-radius circle. Age and height are [SOURCE: Page_9]

172   generally measured for two of these dominant trees, referred to as “measured dominant [SOURCE: Page_9]

173   trees” in the rest of this section. In some specific configurations (a single living tree, or high- [SOURCE: Page_9]

174   value trees that could not be cored), a single dominant tree is measured. Supplementary [SOURCE: Page_9]

175   Material A give some details on these data, which are described in detail in IGN (2022) and [SOURCE: Page_9]

176   in Vallet and Perot (2016). [SOURCE: Page_9]

177          2.2.2. Stands and species selection [SOURCE: Page_9]

178   We considered even-aged pure stands. We defined a stand as even-aged if (i) the NFI [SOURCE: Page_9]

179   labeled it as such, and (ii) in case two dominant trees were measured, the difference in age [SOURCE: Page_9]

180   between the oldest measured dominant tree and the youngest measured dominant tree is [SOURCE: Page_9]

181   below 25% of the age of the youngest measured dominant tree. The latter condition [SOURCE: Page_9]

182   ensured that all dominant trees in the stand share roughly the same history. We considered [SOURCE: Page_9]

183   a stand to be pure if all of the following conditions were met: (i) a single species represents [SOURCE: Page_9]

184   more than 75% of the canopy cover, (ii) both measured dominant trees belong to the same [SOURCE: Page_9]

185   species, and (iii) the species with the highest canopy cover had the highest basal area. We [SOURCE: Page_9]

186   excluded stands with incomplete data (cf. section 2.2.4 and 2.2.5 for the list of explanatory [SOURCE: Page_9]

187   variables) and stands whose establishment date was older than the depth of the climate [SOURCE: Page_9]

188   data, namely 1871 (cf. section 2.2.3. for the calculation of stand establishment date). Finally, [SOURCE: Page_9]

189   we considered the 20 species with the highest number of observations in the NFI database. [SOURCE: Page_9]

190   We ended with 17,462 stands. For 15,802 of them, age and height were measured for two [SOURCE: Page_9]

191   trees, and for the others, age and height were measured for a single tree. Table 1 shows the [SOURCE: Page_10]

192   list of species studied together with the corresponding number of stands. We show the [SOURCE: Page_10]

193   geographic repartition of the stands per species in supplementary materials (Fig. A.1). [SOURCE: Page_10]

201   meters surface, so the mean height of these two trees provides an unbiased estimate of SDH [SOURCE: Page_10]

205   establishment date as the NFI observation year minus stand age. The NFI protocol defined [SOURCE: Page_10]

206   tree age as the number of years between the date when the tree height was 1.3 m and the [SOURCE: Page_10]

217             2.2.4. Climate data and climate variables [SOURCE: Page_12]

218   We used temperature and precipitation data from the FYRE database (Devers et al., 2020a; [SOURCE: Page_12]

219   2020b; 2021) for the period 1871-2012 and the Météo France SAFRAN database (Vidal et [SOURCE: Page_12]

220   al., 2010) for 2013-2020. This temporal depth was necessary to cover the full stand life [SOURCE: Page_13]

221   span observed in our data. The Safran data consist in an interpolation merging observations [SOURCE: Page_13]

222   and background data over 608 climatologically homogeneous zones covering metropolitan [SOURCE: Page_13]

223   France, then disaggregated onto a 8 km grid taking into account altitude (Devers et al., [SOURCE: Page_13]

224   2020). FYRE data consist in a reanalysis over the same 8 km grid, assimilating observation [SOURCE: Page_13]

225   into the SCOPE background (Devers et al., 2020). The FYRE data include 25 climate series, [SOURCE: Page_13]

226   which represent equally plausible meteorological series (Caillouet et al., 2019). We used the [SOURCE: Page_13]

227   average of these 25 climate series. For both the FYRE and SAFRAN data, we converted daily [SOURCE: Page_13]

228   data to monthly data by averaging daily temperature and summing daily precipitation. For [SOURCE: Page_13]

229   each grid cell, we computed the mean difference between FYRE data and SAFRAN data over [SOURCE: Page_13]

230   the period 1990-2012, and we added this difference to SAFRAN data over 2013-2020 to [SOURCE: Page_13]

231   correct for the bias between the two data sources. We used the 1990-2012 period to [SOURCE: Page_13]

232   compute the bias because it represents a compromise between having enough data and [SOURCE: Page_13]

233   covering a period close enough to the period over which we want to implement the [SOURCE: Page_13]

234   correction (namely 2013-2020). We then created a single gridded climate database by [SOURCE: Page_13]

235   concatenating FYRE data over 1871-2012 and SAFRAN unbiased data over 2013-2020. [SOURCE: Page_13]

236   To derive the temperature series at each NFI site, we corrected the grid-based climate data [SOURCE: Page_13]

237   for an elevation gradient following Devers et al. (2020). For each of the 608 homogeneous [SOURCE: Page_13]

238   climate areas, we defined an altitude gradient by fitting a linear model between the mean [SOURCE: Page_13]

239   temperature and the mean altitude of each grid cell. In case there was less than three grid [SOURCE: Page_13]

240   cells in a homogeneous climatic area, we grouped these cells with the cells of the closest [SOURCE: Page_13]

241   homogeneous climatic area. We used this gradient to correct the climate series for each NFI [SOURCE: Page_13]

242   site, based on the difference between the NFI site elevation and the corresponding grid cell [SOURCE: Page_13]

243   elevation. We applied a similar procedure to determine precipitation at each NFI site. [SOURCE: Page_14]

244   Because elevation correction for precipitation is not relevant in lowland areas, we applied [SOURCE: Page_14]

245   the correction only when the difference between the NFI site elevation and the [SOURCE: Page_14]

246   corresponding grid cell elevation was greater than 300m (Devers et al., 2020) [SOURCE: Page_14]

247   To combine the influence of temperature and precipitation, we calculated a monthly [SOURCE: Page_14]

248   climatic water balance, defined as the difference between precipitation and potential [SOURCE: Page_14]

249   evapotranspiration (Lebourgeois and Piedallu, 2005). Potential evapotranspiration was [SOURCE: Page_14]

250   derived from monthly temperature and radiation using the Turc formula (Lebourgeois and [SOURCE: Page_14]

251   Piedallu, 2005). Monthly radiation without nebulosity was obtained following Piedallu and [SOURCE: Page_14]

252   Gégout (2007). Since this radiation varies little between years, we assumed that the [SOURCE: Page_14]

253   monthly radiation was equal to the monthly values for the year 2000. To account for light [SOURCE: Page_14]

254   interception by clouds, we corrected the radiation value with the average monthly [SOURCE: Page_14]

255   nebulosity between 1960 and 2019, following Piedallu and Gégout (2007). The nebulosity [SOURCE: Page_14]

256   data were taken from Harris et al. (2020). [SOURCE: Page_14]

390          2.3.3. Assessment of model quality [SOURCE: Page_22]

391   To assess the risk of overfitting for each species-specific model, we calculated the difference [SOURCE: Page_22]

392   between model prediction error using data not used for calibration and model prediction [SOURCE: Page_22]

393   error using calibration data. We refer to this difference in prediction error as “model [SOURCE: Page_22]

394   optimism”. To do this, we implemented a fivefold cross-validation. First, we randomly [SOURCE: Page_23]

395   divided our sample into five equally sized subsamples. Then we repeated the following [SOURCE: Page_23]

396   procedure for each subsample: (i) we considered this subsample as a validation subsample [SOURCE: Page_23]

397   and the remaining four subsamples as a single calibration subsample, (ii) we calibrated the [SOURCE: Page_23]

398   model presented in equation 5 on this calibration subsample and (iii) we calculated the root [SOURCE: Page_23]

399   mean square error (RMSE) over this calibration subsample and the RMSE over the [SOURCE: Page_23]

400   validation subsample and (iv) we defined an intermediate optimism indicator as the [SOURCE: Page_23]

401   relative difference between the RMSE calculated on the validation subsample and the RMSE [SOURCE: Page_23]

402   calculated on the calibration subsample. If the difference was negative, we considered it to [SOURCE: Page_23]

403   zero. We defined the final optimism indicator as the average of the five intermediate [SOURCE: Page_23]

404   optimism indicators. The higher the optimism indicator, the higher the prediction error [SOURCE: Page_23]

405   when the model was applied to new data compared to the error when it was applied to [SOURCE: Page_23]

406   calibration data. Details can be found in Supplementary Material D. We also calculated the [SOURCE: Page_23]

407   RMSE, root mean square percentage error (RMSPE) and bias of each species-specific model [SOURCE: Page_23]

408   when calibration was done on the whole sample (cf. Supplementary Material D for [SOURCE: Page_23]

409   calculation details). [SOURCE: Page_23]

512   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_32]

513   In this paper, we analyzed the impact of past climate change on SDH dynamics and site [SOURCE: Page_32]

514   index for 20 common European tree species and we analyzed interspecific and intraspecific [SOURCE: Page_32]

515   differences in SDH and site index response to climate change. In the discussion, we first [SOURCE: Page_32]

516   point out the interest of our modeling approach; we will emphasize some limitations of this [SOURCE: Page_32]

517   approach; then we comment on the partial effect of climatic variable on site index; then we [SOURCE: Page_32]

518   discuss the impact of climate change during the last century on SDH dynamics and site [SOURCE: Page_32]

519   index, and finally we elaborate on implications for management. [SOURCE: Page_32]

520      4.1.       Interest of the approach [SOURCE: Page_32]

521   To our knowledge, this is the first time annual SDH trajectories have been empirically [SOURCE: Page_32]

522   modeled for 20 species, taking into account annual climate over the past century. We [SOURCE: Page_32]

523   achieved this by combining French NFI data and FYRE long-term climate data, and by using [SOURCE: Page_32]

524   a theory-based annual SDH increment equation. The FYRE data allowed us to model SDH [SOURCE: Page_32]

525   dynamics for stands as old as 150 years while accounting for annual climate. The French [SOURCE: Page_32]

526   NFI data include a large number of stands per species and cover a large climatic gradient, [SOURCE: Page_32]

527   which allowed us to identify climate effects. Different stand age classes at the time of stand [SOURCE: Page_32]

528   observation allowed us to partially decorrelate age with date. The French NFI data provided [SOURCE: Page_32]

529   us with field measurements of non-climatic variables to use as covariates, limiting potential [SOURCE: Page_32]

530   bias due to overrepresentation of older stands on infertile sites (Socha et al., 2021). The [SOURCE: Page_32]

531   theory-based SDH increment equation we used ensured that our approach was biologically [SOURCE: Page_33]

532   consistent (Tomé et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_33]

533   Our approach has operational advantages over stem analysis. Stem analysis requires [SOURCE: Page_33]

534   intensive field sampling for each stand, and is generally conducted in the context of a [SOURCE: Page_33]

535   specific study. The number of stands used in stem analysis studies can therefore be quite [SOURCE: Page_33]

536   limited (Bontemps and Bouriaud, 2014) although some studies are based on a large number [SOURCE: Page_33]

537   of stands (Socha et al., 2021; Pau et al., 2022). In contrast, the French NFI data are [SOURCE: Page_33]

538   representative of the species’ distribution in the study area. They cover a large climatic [SOURCE: Page_33]

539   gradient and include numerous stands of common species. Our approach allowed us to [SOURCE: Page_33]

540   derive SDH-dynamics models for rarely studied species such as Larix decidua, Quercus [SOURCE: Page_33]

541   pubescens and Fraxinus excelsior. The main advantage of stem analysis over our approach is [SOURCE: Page_33]

542   that it provides a temporal series of SDH measurements, whereas our approach relies on a [SOURCE: Page_33]

543   single SDH measurement. Stem analysis also provides more accurate height measurements [SOURCE: Page_33]

544   compared to NFI data. In the future, it would be interesting to compare our simulated SDH [SOURCE: Page_33]

545   dynamics with observed SDH dynamics and with SDH dynamics simulated by a model [SOURCE: Page_33]

546   based on stem analysis. [SOURCE: Page_33]

547   The RMSE we found are in the same order of magnitude as in other studies based on NFI [SOURCE: Page_33]

548   data (Seynave et al., 2008; Sharma et al., 2012) but higher than in studies based on stem [SOURCE: Page_33]

549   analysis (Socha et al., 2021). The optimism of our models is generally low, indicating good [SOURCE: Page_33]

550   robustness, especially for species with a large number of calibration stands. Parameter [SOURCE: Page_33]

551   values for species with high model optimism should be used with caution. [SOURCE: Page_33]

552      4.2.       Limits of the approach [SOURCE: Page_34]

553   The NFI data do not provide information on stand history. This forced us to make several [SOURCE: Page_34]

554   assumptions. First, we assumed that the dominant trees at observation time had been [SOURCE: Page_34]

555   dominant throughout stand development. This is a common assumption in SDH studies. [SOURCE: Page_34]

556   Second, we assumed that SDH dynamics were independent of competition history. This is [SOURCE: Page_34]

557   justified for a wide range of densities (Skovsgaard and Vanclay, 2008). The inclusion of a [SOURCE: Page_34]

558   competition variable could still be interesting (Vallet and Perot, 2016). Third, we did not [SOURCE: Page_34]

559   include interactions in our models because this could have led to overparameterized [SOURCE: Page_34]

560   models. Fourth, we did not include nitrogen deposition in our model because due to a lack [SOURCE: Page_34]

561   of historical data. This variable has been identified as an important driver of SDH in the late [SOURCE: Page_34]

562   20th century (Bontemps et al., 2011). As a proxy, we included the C:N ratio at the [SOURCE: Page_34]

563   observation date in our models. This allowed us to account for spatial heterogeneity, but [SOURCE: Page_34]

564   not temporal changes. Atmospheric CO$_2$ concentrations are sometimes considered to be an [SOURCE: Page_34]

565   important driver of the increase in tree growth during the last century, but this role is still [SOURCE: Page_34]

566   under debate, especially since the positive effect may be restricted to young stages (Asshoff, [SOURCE: Page_34]

567   Zotz, and Körner, 2006; Boisvenue and Running, 2006; McDowell et al., 2020). We did not [SOURCE: Page_34]

568   include this variable in our models because it would have created a temporal trend that [SOURCE: Page_34]

569   could have prevented us from identifying the effect of climatic variables. Because we did not [SOURCE: Page_34]

570   properly disentangle the effects of climate, atmospheric nitrogen deposition, and [SOURCE: Page_34]

571   atmospheric CO$_2$ concentration in our models, some of the effect we attributed to climate [SOURCE: Page_34]

572   could be related to the other two factors. Complementing this study with process-based [SOURCE: Page_34]

573   models may be useful to disentangle these effects. [SOURCE: Page_34]

574      4.3.       Partial effect of climatic variables on site index [SOURCE: Page_35]

575   Our results regarding the partial effect of climatic variables confirm our hypothesis 1 that [SOURCE: Page_35]

576   an increase in temperature, precipitation and climatic water balance during spring and [SOURCE: Page_35]

577   summer favors the site index, but that these positive effects may saturate when the climatic [SOURCE: Page_35]

578   variable reaches a certain level. However, contrary to our hypothesis, our results do not [SOURCE: Page_35]

579   clearly show a negative effect of temperature above a certain threshold. Indeed, the [SOURCE: Page_35]

580   decrease of site index at the upper range of the variable distribution for some species and [SOURCE: Page_35]

581   variables is not very pronounced and may simply reflect the fact that we integrated [SOURCE: Page_35]

582   saturation and non-monotonic effects in the model using a quadratic form. [SOURCE: Page_35]

583   For commonly studied species, our results are largely consistent with the literature, [SOURCE: Page_35]

584   especially (i) the generally positive effect of temperature and SGDD (Albert and Schmidt, [SOURCE: Page_35]

585   2010; Álvarez-Álvarez et al. ,2011; González-Rodríguez and Diéguez-Aranda, 2020), (ii) the [SOURCE: Page_35]

586   generally positive effect of precipitation and climatic water balance (Vallet and Perot, 2016; [SOURCE: Page_35]

587   Stimm et al., 2021), (iii) the saturation effect beyond a certain level of temperature [SOURCE: Page_35]

588   (Seynave et al. 2008; Caicoya and Pretzsch, 2021; Pau et al., 2022) or precipitation (Brandl [SOURCE: Page_35]

589   et al., 2018) and (iv) the existence of interspecific differences in the climatic variables and [SOURCE: Page_35]

590   seasons affecting SDH (Vallet and Perot, 2016). Table 2 compares the species-specific [SOURCE: Page_35]

591   effects we found with those reported in the literature for the five most common European [SOURCE: Page_35]

592   tree species, as listed by (Mahnken et al., 2022). The present work allows to extend such [SOURCE: Page_35]

593   results to species for which, to our knowledge, the climate – SDH relationship has hardly [SOURCE: Page_35]

594   been studied yet, such as Larix decidua, Fraxinus excelsior or Quercus pubescens. [SOURCE: Page_35]

595   These effects on site index are consistent with the expected response based on tree [SOURCE: Page_35]

596   physiology. The positive effects of higher spring and summer temperature probably relate [SOURCE: Page_35]

597   to an increase photosynthetic efficiency or a lengthening of the growing season (Brandl et [SOURCE: Page_36]

598   al., 2018). Saturation may occur because temperature is no longer the limiting factor, or [SOURCE: Page_36]

599   because high temperature increase evapotranspiration and hydric stress, and reduce [SOURCE: Page_36]

600   photosynthesis (Lindner et al., 2010; Anderson-Teixeira et al., 2022). The lack of a clear [SOURCE: Page_36]

601   signal of negative effects of temperature above a certain level may relate to the fact that [SOURCE: Page_36]

602   such extreme temperature have only occurred in recent years, and therefore their effects [SOURCE: Page_36]

603   are not well captured in our multi-decadal modeling strategy. The negative effect of high [SOURCE: Page_36]

604   winter temperature for some species may relate to disturbance of bud break or high winter [SOURCE: Page_36]

605   respiration (Seynave et al., 2008). The generally positive impact of spring and summer [SOURCE: Page_36]

606   precipitation and climatic water balance, and the saturation of this positive effect at the [SOURCE: Page_36]

607   wetter edge of the range, may be related to a positive effect of relaxing the water constraint [SOURCE: Page_36]

608   only in water-limited environments. With respect to fall and winter precipitation and [SOURCE: Page_36]

609   climatic water balance, the interspecific diversity of responses suggests that species have [SOURCE: Page_36]

610   different levels of tolerance to water excess or develop under specific stand conditions. [SOURCE: Page_36]

611   Negative responses can be explained by nutrient depletion in the case of too much water [SOURCE: Page_36]

612   (Álvarez-Álvarez et al., 2011) or by snow damage (Seynave et al., 2008). The outlier [SOURCE: Page_36]

613   behavior of Castanea sativa (negative effect of summer temperature) could be due to the [SOURCE: Page_36]

614   impact of the chestnut ink disease, which can hinder tree growth, especially under drought [SOURCE: Page_36]

615   conditions (Maurel et al., 2001). In our dataset, this may have translated into a correlation [SOURCE: Page_36]

616   between the warmer and drier recent climate and less dynamic growth in young stands. [SOURCE: Page_36]

618      4.4.       Analysis of climate change during the last century on stand [SOURCE: Page_38]

619          dominant height [SOURCE: Page_38]

620   Our models allow us to compare SDH dynamics under the actual recent climate (1950 to [SOURCE: Page_38]

621   2020) with the climate before climate change. For some species, the effects of climate [SOURCE: Page_38]

622   change were small in the first decades of the simulations and positive thereafter. This [SOURCE: Page_38]

623   relates to a period of relative cooling in France between 1950 and 1990, followed by a [SOURCE: Page_38]

624   period of strong temperature increase (cf. Figure 1). Such temporal variations in the effects [SOURCE: Page_38]

625   of climate change support the relevance of modeling SDH dynamics while taking into [SOURCE: Page_38]

626   account climate variations during stand life. At the final simulation age of 70 years, the [SOURCE: Page_38]

627   effect of climate change on SDH varied between species and between stands for a given [SOURCE: Page_38]

628   species. In the following sections, we analyze first the interspecific and then the [SOURCE: Page_38]

629   intraspecific variation of the site index in response to climate change, where site index Is [SOURCE: Page_38]

630   defined as SDH at the age of 70 years. [SOURCE: Page_38]

631          4.4.1. Interspecific analysis [SOURCE: Page_38]

632   Our results confirm our hypothesis 2a that climate change during the last century has had [SOURCE: Page_38]

633   different effects on different species, both in terms of sign and magnitude. The increase in [SOURCE: Page_38]

634   the site index over the period considered for the majority of species studied is consistent [SOURCE: Page_38]

635   with an increase in forest productivity over the last decades (Boisvenue and Running, 2006; [SOURCE: Page_38]

636   Bontemps et al. 2009; Messaoud et al., 2022). Together with nitrogen deposition and CO$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_38]

637   increase, climate change is a key driver of recent changes in forest growth in Europe [SOURCE: Page_38]

638   (Boisvenue and Running, 2006; Bontemps et al. 2009; Charru et al. 2017). [SOURCE: Page_38]

639   Interspecific differences in the effect of climate change on SDH may be related to differences [SOURCE: Page_39]

640   in species ecology, but also to interspecific differences in the pre-climate change climate [SOURCE: Page_39]

641   niche and the actual climate change experienced by the species (cf. Figure 1 and [SOURCE: Page_39]

642   Supplementary Material Fig. B.1 and Fig. B.2). We did not find a clear pattern linking the [SOURCE: Page_39]

643   species-specific mean effect of climate change on site index to species-specific mean initial [SOURCE: Page_39]

644   climate niche (cf. Supplementary Material Fig. F.1 and Fig. F.2). This suggests that species [SOURCE: Page_39]

645   ecology and experienced climate change are also key drivers of this effect. Studying another [SOURCE: Page_39]

646   growth variable and fewer species, Charru et al. (2017) found that species-specific mean [SOURCE: Page_39]

647   changes in basal area increment between 1982 and 2005 could be related to species- [SOURCE: Page_39]

648   specific mean initial climate niche, with a more positive effect for species that initially [SOURCE: Page_39]

649   experienced the coldest temperature and highest precipitation. When focusing on the same [SOURCE: Page_39]

650   species as Charru et al. (2017), our results suggest such a pattern. [SOURCE: Page_39]

651   Our results shed light on past limitations to species growth and provide insight into future [SOURCE: Page_39]

652   impacts of ongoing climate change. Over the period we considered, climate change affected [SOURCE: Page_39]

653   all species in two ways: increasing annual and summer mean temperature as well as [SOURCE: Page_39]

654   decreasing summer precipitation and / or climatic water balance (cf. Supplementary [SOURCE: Page_39]

655   Material Fig. B.1 and Fig. B.2). Increasing temperature favor simulated height growth, while [SOURCE: Page_39]

656   decreasing summer precipitation or climatic water balance negatively affects growth (cf. [SOURCE: Page_39]

657   section 4.3.). The generally positive effect of climate change on the site index we found for [SOURCE: Page_39]

658   the period 1950-2020 compared to the climate of 1891-1920 probably reflects that most of [SOURCE: Page_39]

659   the species studied were temperature-limited in France during the period 1891-1920. Our [SOURCE: Page_39]

660   finding of a strong positive effect of climate change on mountain species (Larix decidua, [SOURCE: Page_39]

661   Abies alba, and to a lesser extent Picea abies) is consistent with the fact that mountain [SOURCE: Page_39]

662   species are particularly temperature-limited (Charru et al., 2017). Nevertheless, the [SOURCE: Page_39]

663   positive effect of climate change on SDH could turn negative if temperature continue to [SOURCE: Page_40]

664   increase and precipitation continue to decrease, due to saturation of the positive partial [SOURCE: Page_40]

665   effect of temperature increase and the negative partial effect of precipitation (or climatic [SOURCE: Page_40]

666   water balance ) decrease (cf. section 4.3.). Studies have already found observations of [SOURCE: Page_40]

667   negative impacts of climate change on growth under water limitation (Lindner et al., 2014) [SOURCE: Page_40]

668   or projected negative impacts on site index of future climate change Albert and Schmidt [SOURCE: Page_40]

669   (2010). We also found such negative effects of climate change for Pseudotsuga menziesii and [SOURCE: Page_40]

670   Quercus robur. The negative effect on Castanea sativa should be interpreted with caution [SOURCE: Page_40]

671   (see 4.3). [SOURCE: Page_40]

672          4.4.2. Intraspecific analysis [SOURCE: Page_40]

673   Our results confirm our hypothesis H2b that, for a given species, the impact of climate [SOURCE: Page_40]

674   change over the last century varied among stands depending on their climate context. [SOURCE: Page_40]

675   Intraspecific variation in the impact of climate change on site index can be related to [SOURCE: Page_40]

676   differences in environmental conditions, management history, or plant genetics (Kremer et [SOURCE: Page_40]

677   al., 2012). Here, we focused on the first dimension, as the French NFI does not provide data [SOURCE: Page_40]

678   on management history or genetics. The intraspecific relationship we found between [SOURCE: Page_40]

679   climate change effects and initial stand temperature for a large number of species reflects [SOURCE: Page_40]

680   that, for a given species, different climatic contexts produce different growth limitations [SOURCE: Page_40]

681   (Lindner et al., 2014; Kunstler et al., 2021; Guyennon et al., 2023). For nine species, our [SOURCE: Page_40]

682   results suggest that climate change alleviated a temperature limitation at the cold edge of [SOURCE: Page_40]

683   the species’ distribution in France, while it had only a small positive or even negative effect [SOURCE: Page_40]

684   on stands at the warm edge of the distribution. Such a pattern is consistent with the results [SOURCE: Page_40]

685   of Albert and Schmidt (2010), Messaoud and Chen (2011) and Ols et al. (2020). The [SOURCE: Page_40]

686   mountain species we analyzed (Abies alba, Picea abies, Larix decidua) follow this pattern, [SOURCE: Page_41]

687   which is consistent with the temperature limitation they experience at high altitudes [SOURCE: Page_41]

688   (Charru et al., 2017). Finally, Pinus halepensis follows the same pattern, probably because it [SOURCE: Page_41]

689   is adapted to warmer climates than that of France. For the other species, the identification [SOURCE: Page_41]

690   of intraspecific patterns relating climate change effects to the reference climate is more [SOURCE: Page_41]

691   complex. [SOURCE: Page_41]

692      4.5.       Implication for management [SOURCE: Page_41]

693   Our results raise awareness of the risk of switching from positive climate change effect on [SOURCE: Page_41]

694   SDH to negative effects due to ongoing climate change. The magnitude and timing of this [SOURCE: Page_41]

695   switch for a given stand will depend on the species, the current climatic context and the [SOURCE: Page_41]

696   climate change that the stand will experience. Our results may help forest managers to [SOURCE: Page_41]

697   identify which species to favor when managing a pure even-aged stand. Furthermore, our [SOURCE: Page_41]

698   models could be used to project SDH dynamics under future climate scenarios, although [SOURCE: Page_41]

699   caution should be taken when running simulations outside the calibration range of [SOURCE: Page_41]

700   empirical models. Thus, it may be useful to integrate our work into models used to inform [SOURCE: Page_41]

701   management strategy. [SOURCE: Page_41]

702   Our results also suggest that climate change may alter the relative competitiveness of [SOURCE: Page_41]

703   species for light in mixed stands, due to interspecific differences in the effect of climate [SOURCE: Page_41]

704   change on SDH dynamics. This may lead to changes in forest composition if climate change [SOURCE: Page_41]

705   penalizes the height growth of some shade-intolerant species more than the height growth [SOURCE: Page_41]

706   of shade-tolerant species (Bontemps et al., 2012; Messaoud et al., 2022). Taking this effect [SOURCE: Page_41]

707   into account is important for the management of mixed stands. This is even more true in a [SOURCE: Page_41]

708   context where forest managers are encouraged to diversify species to adapt forests to [SOURCE: Page_42]

709   climate change. [SOURCE: Page_42]

710   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_42]

711   For 20 European species, we developed stand dominant height dynamics models taking [SOURCE: Page_42]

712   into account annual climate, based on data from more than 17,000 forest stands surveyed [SOURCE: Page_42]

713   by the French National Forest Inventory and a 150-years climate database. [SOURCE: Page_42]

714   We found that climate change over the past century had contrasting effects between and [SOURCE: Page_42]

715   within species. For the majority of species studied, most stands have benefited from climate [SOURCE: Page_42]

716   change, as shown by comparing the average climate of 1891-1920 with the actual recent [SOURCE: Page_42]

717   climate of 1950-2020. For some species, however, we found that a significant percentage of [SOURCE: Page_42]

718   stands were already experiencing negative impacts. The relationship between temperature [SOURCE: Page_42]

719   and within-species differences in climate change effects suggests that climatic context may [SOURCE: Page_42]

720   drive differences in response to climate change for a given species. [SOURCE: Page_42]

721   These results suggest that future forest response to continued climate change will vary by [SOURCE: Page_42]

722   species, initial stand climate context, and stand climate trends. They also suggest that [SOURCE: Page_42]

723   continued increases in temperature and decreases in summer precipitation may lead to [SOURCE: Page_42]

724   more negative trends than observed in the past. Consideration of these different aspects is [SOURCE: Page_42]

725   critical to inform management to adapt forests to future climate change. [SOURCE: Page_42]
