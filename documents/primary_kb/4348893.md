Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

Micro-drip irrigation was first introduced in Burkina Faso in 2002 by the International Crop [SOURCE: Page_3]

Research Institute for the Semi-Arid Tropics (ICRISAT) through the Desert Margins Project, [SOURCE: Page_3]

which aimed at promoting the production of date palms. Two years after, ICRISAT introduced [SOURCE: Page_3]

this new form of drip irrigation through the African Market Garden (AMG) project (2004- [SOURCE: Page_3]

2007). Funded by the United States Agency for International Development (USAID), this [SOURCE: Page_3]

project aimed at combining water management with improved crop production practices [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Pasternak et al. 2006; Woltering et al. 2011). The AMG promoters tested the so-called pre- [SOURCE: Page_3]

packaged Family Drip System (FDS) kits designed by NETAFIM, the main manufacturer of [SOURCE: Page_3]

drip irrigation equipment worldwide. The FDS is one among many different micro-drip kits, [SOURCE: Page_3]

which consist of a network of plastic pipes, water emitters (or drippers) and a set of valves and [SOURCE: Page_3]

filters, and that have been designed to cater to areas ranging from 25m² to 1000m². [SOURCE: Page_3]

By communicating that 2,000 micro-drip kits had been distributed in nine Sahelian countries, [SOURCE: Page_3]

ICRISAT framed the AMG project as a ‘Sahelian success’ (ICRISAT 2006). Its promoters [SOURCE: Page_3]

argued this success was linked to the suitability of the micro-drip technology for the specific [SOURCE: Page_3]

arid environment of the Sahel and its affordability for smallholder farmers (Pasternak et al. [SOURCE: Page_3]

2006; Woltering et al. 2011). In several other countries of sub-Sahara Africa (Kenya, [SOURCE: Page_3]

Zimbabwe and South Africa), micro-drip kits were likewise said to have been successfully [SOURCE: Page_3]

introduced for small-scale vegetable gardening (Kabutha et al. 2000; Karlberg et al. 2007). [SOURCE: Page_3]

This form of drip irrigation appealed to the Burkina Faso government and various development [SOURCE: Page_3]

agencies as a technology holding the promises of efficient water and labour management, [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

improved nutrition and food security, poverty alleviation and women's empowerment (see [SOURCE: Page_4]

Venot et al. 2014 for a description of the rationale to promote micro-drip kits in sub-Saharan [SOURCE: Page_4]

Africa). This enthusiasm underpinned a multiplication of projects centred on the promotion of [SOURCE: Page_4]

micro-drip kits, involving numerous development actors. [SOURCE: Page_4]

The traction this form of irrigation has among development practitioners is remarkable given [SOURCE: Page_4]

the little evidence of farmers actually using the technology beyond pilot projects. Wanvoeke et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al. (2015) for example highlight that only one out of the 245 micro-drip kits distributed by the [SOURCE: Page_4]

AMG project in Burkina Faso was still in use in 2012, echoing findings from other studies done [SOURCE: Page_4]

in Kenya (Belder et al. 2007) and Zimbabwe (Kulecho and Weatherhead 2005, 2006) where [SOURCE: Page_4]

farmers discontinued using micro-drip kits once the projects promoting them ended. Many [SOURCE: Page_4]

scholars have explained why this happens. They notably highlight: that costs of initial [SOURCE: Page_4]

investments are still high (Dittoh et al. 2010); the technical problems (with emitters and filters [SOURCE: Page_4]

clogging and deterioration of material) due to unreliable and low quality water supply and harsh [SOURCE: Page_4]

environmental conditions (Friedlander et al. 2013); the lack of spare parts, supply chains and [SOURCE: Page_4]

support mechanisms; the difficulties to access markets (Kulecho and Weatherhead 2005, [SOURCE: Page_4]

2006); a lack of capacity and knowledge on the part of smallholders; and maybe more [SOURCE: Page_4]

fundamentally a misfit between the technology and the cultural setting and agricultural [SOURCE: Page_4]

practices of smallholders (Garb et al. 2014) as many deterrents to widespread adoption. Even [SOURCE: Page_4]

though all these studies have criticised micro-drip kits, their starting assumption is that using [SOURCE: Page_4]

small scale irrigation technologies is potentially beneficial for smallholders' farmers. The [SOURCE: Page_4]

studies thus focus on how to make these technologies work (better) in farmers’ fields (for [SOURCE: Page_4]

instance by teaching farmers about how to use them), or on how to best disseminate them (for [SOURCE: Page_4]

instance by improving support services). [SOURCE: Page_4]

Our study builds on these studies, but has a different starting point. Rather than implicitly [SOURCE: Page_5]

identifying with designers and promoters in their appraisal of the technology as something [SOURCE: Page_5]

potentially positive, we empathize with farmers in an attempt to understand the technology [SOURCE: Page_5]

from their perspective. We do not aim to explain why farmers stop using micro drip kits after [SOURCE: Page_5]

projects that promote them have ended (a question already answered by many, see for instance [SOURCE: Page_5]

Belder et al. 2007; Kulecho and Weatherhead 2005). Rather, anchored in practice-based [SOURCE: Page_5]

theories of innovation and drawing on theoretical insights from the anthropology of [SOURCE: Page_5]

development, the paper consists of a systematic analysis of how and why farmers engage with [SOURCE: Page_5]

development projects that promote micro-drip kits. The origins of our desire to explore this [SOURCE: Page_5]

topic lie with the realisation referred to above that most farmers accept micro-drip kits while [SOURCE: Page_5]

projects are running, but appear little interested in the water and labour savings attributes that [SOURCE: Page_5]

are put forth by their promoters. [SOURCE: Page_5]

In the section that follows, we provide the analytical framework guiding this study. In section [SOURCE: Page_5]

three, we describe the methodology used. Through three case studies, we then further analyse [SOURCE: Page_5]

drip kits (section four). A short conclusion comes back to our main finding, which is that [SOURCE: Page_5]

farmers accept engaging with projects promoting this form of irrigation not for the technology [SOURCE: Page_5]

per se (or because of its promises in terms of yields and water savings) but for the anticipated [SOURCE: Page_5]

Research framework [SOURCE: Page_5]

A wide variety of disciplines is concerned with the way innovations are created, and with [SOURCE: Page_5]

understanding why and how they spread. Rogers' theory of the diffusion of innovation (Rogers [SOURCE: Page_5]

2003) is perhaps best known and most often used and referred to. Most attempts to explain the [SOURCE: Page_5]

success or failure of micro-drip kits indeed make use (sometimes implicitly) of Rogers' [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

classical approach, in identifying the factors facilitating or impeding adoption (see for instance [SOURCE: Page_6]

Friedlander et al. 2013; Kulecho and Weatherhead 2005, 2006; Malik et al. 2014; Namara et [SOURCE: Page_6]

al. 2014). [SOURCE: Page_6]

Although popular and widely used, Roger's approach has also been criticized for its over- [SOURCE: Page_6]

simplistic positing of linear causal linkages between design (or dissemination) intentions and [SOURCE: Page_6]

outcomes. This has the effect of attributing too much steering power to engineers and [SOURCE: Page_6]

innovation planners, to the neglect of end-users or other involved stakeholders. Moreover, [SOURCE: Page_6]

Rogers' theory makes it seem as if innovation happens in relative isolation from wider societal [SOURCE: Page_6]

processes and structures. Prominent among alternative ways to make theoretical sense of, and [SOURCE: Page_6]

help improve, innovation processes are knowledge systems thinking (Röling 1992), which [SOURCE: Page_6]

proposes a much less linear and predictable view of innovation and dissemination, and [SOURCE: Page_6]

participatory approaches to technology development inspired by the seminal work of Chambers [SOURCE: Page_6]

(Chambers 1994; Jiggins 1989). [SOURCE: Page_6]

The practice-based innovation theory of Akrich et al. (2002a, 2002b) shares with these latter [SOURCE: Page_6]

approaches the idea that innovation is open-ended and contingent. Perhaps different than most [SOURCE: Page_6]

other theories, which continue adhering to some kind of diffusion model, Akrich et al. do not [SOURCE: Page_6]

ascribe the success or failure of a technology to its 'intrinsic' properties. They instead look at [SOURCE: Page_6]

technologies-in-context to suggest that innovations are only taken up if an ever increasing [SOURCE: Page_6]

number of actors get interested in it. This is the model of interessement that postulates that for [SOURCE: Page_6]

actors to become interested in a technology, the latter needs to be translated to fit different [SOURCE: Page_6]

contexts, interests and discourses. In this light, while the discontinued use of micro-drip kits [SOURCE: Page_6]

diagnosed by many may mark a “failure” in conventional diffusion terms, in our framework, [SOURCE: Page_6]

the fact that farmers do accept the kits reveals that there is something to the technology that [SOURCE: Page_6]

does appeal to them. The model of interessement directs the attention to why this is so, [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

acknowledging that (the meaning of) an artefact may change depending on the actor-network [SOURCE: Page_7]

of which it comes to form a part. [SOURCE: Page_7]

In this paper, we are particularly interested in the influence of development projects in [SOURCE: Page_7]

influencing the meaning(s) that micro-drip kits have for farmers. To do this, we make use of [SOURCE: Page_7]

insights offered by scholars in the socio-anthropology of development who propose [SOURCE: Page_7]

conceptualizing development project contexts as arenas, that is, as bounded sites of interaction, [SOURCE: Page_7]

contestation and cooperation (Long 2001; Olivier de Sardan 2005). Within these arenas, actors [SOURCE: Page_7]

(re)interpret and (re)negotiate things and ideas that come ‘from outside’. Olivier de Sardan [SOURCE: Page_7]

(2005) proposed the term ‘logics’ to avoid explaining 'developees' behaviour only from the [SOURCE: Page_7]

normative interpretative frames of 'developers' and their projects. When ‘developees’ behave [SOURCE: Page_7]

differently than 'developers' expected, in other words, this is because their logics do not [SOURCE: Page_7]

coincide. 'Logics' is akin to strategy and refers to the reasons and motivations actors have for [SOURCE: Page_7]

their behaviors. Speaking of 'logics' also stems from recognition that while actors may display [SOURCE: Page_7]

an infinite variety of actions and responses, the number of behavioral patterns is limited. This [SOURCE: Page_7]

allows inductively explaining similarities in behaviour (Olivier de Sardan 2005: 138). Where [SOURCE: Page_7]

Long (2001) and Olivier de Sardan (2005) focused on the negotiated and contingent nature of [SOURCE: Page_7]

interpretations and meanings of development interventions, we suggest (inspired by practice- [SOURCE: Page_7]

based theories of innovation) that (the meanings of) technologies too are re-negotiated and re- [SOURCE: Page_7]

contextualized. [SOURCE: Page_7]

We show through three case studies that, beyond their technical and material properties, and [SOURCE: Page_7]

within development project arenas, micro-drip kits have different meanings to farmers than to [SOURCE: Page_7]

other development actors. This ‘other reality’ resulting from a process of interessement in [SOURCE: Page_7]

specific actor-networks, is what explains that the reasons for which farmers engage in projects [SOURCE: Page_7]

promoting micro-drip kits are often different than the ones assumed and intended by the project [SOURCE: Page_7]

and its promoters. [SOURCE: Page_7]
