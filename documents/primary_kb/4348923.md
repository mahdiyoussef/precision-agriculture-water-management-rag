11   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

12   This paper explains the processes behind the framing of drip irrigation as a promising technology 13   to address current poverty and environmental challenges in the developing world. I draw from 14   critical development and science and technology studies and highlight that this imagery has been 15   actively performed. Insiders elaborated a compelling narrative calling upon a will to improve 16   through technology and the moral legitimacy of social entrepreneurship in development; they 17   worked hard to establish a supportive coalition in an ever wider network. This story hinges on 18   several assumptions, which upon closer scrutiny appear to be problematic: the unicity of 19   smallholder farming, the attribution of inherent technical characteristics to a specific object - the 20   “drip kit” - regardless of the context in which it is used, and the framing of social 21   entrepreneurship and market-based approaches as alternative models even though these rather 22   constitute a re-working of existing arrangements within the international development 23   community. Nonetheless, the pro-poor and environmentally friendly smallholder drip irrigation 24   narrative still continues to be successful in harnessing the support of the international 25   development community, despite the little capacity drip irrigation has had to transform 26   smallholder farming, especially in sub-Saharan Africa. Unpacking the origins, actors and 27   building blocks of the discursive success of smallholder drip irrigation provides fresh 28   perspectives on the practices of development in the sector and is the first step towards more 29   meaningful engagement with smallholder farmers in the developing world. 500   When looking more specifically at drip irrigation, one can only observe that its development and [SOURCE: Page_30]

501   promotion has long been embedded in strongly ideological and political movements. The first [SOURCE: Page_30]

502   steps of modern day drip irrigation are said to have taken place in Israeli Kibbutzim in the Negev [SOURCE: Page_30]

503   desert (WFP, 2012; www.netafim.com). These coupled a strong collective ideology with a [SOURCE: Page_30]

506   development in the arid Western United States (see, among others, Reisner, 1993). [SOURCE: Page_30]

507   The promotion of drip irrigation in developing countries has, on the other hand, long been (and [SOURCE: Page_31]

508   still partly is) embedded in a ‘superior call to do good’ often tinged with Christian belief and [SOURCE: Page_31]

509   charity. Chapin Living waters, the non-for-profit foundation of drip-kit pioneer Richard Chapin [SOURCE: Page_31]

511   nations through small-scale drip irrigation technology” (www.chapinlivingwaters.org). Today, [SOURCE: Page_31]

512   many Christian and secular NGOs are involved in the promotion of drip irrigation systems for [SOURCE: Page_31]

513   smallholder farmers. Inspired by the rhetoric of aid and charity, many of these organizations give [SOURCE: Page_31]

514   away drip irrigation kits to farmers (sometimes against a symbolic contribution). [SOURCE: Page_31]

515   Social enterprises distance themselves from this approach on the ground that it would be [SOURCE: Page_31]

516   unsustainable and disrespectful to smallholders. They see ‘hand-outs’ as depriving farmers of [SOURCE: Page_31]

517   their own choices. However, they replace a belief in aid by a belief in entrepreneurship and the [SOURCE: Page_31]

518   underlying desire to do good is still driving action as can be seen in the following quote: [SOURCE: Page_31]

519      When farmers are given the opportunity, they invest in their own prosperity […] Together, we [SOURCE: Page_31]

520      can use engineering and entrepreneurship to achieve impact on a global scale. Together, we [SOURCE: Page_31]

521      can make prosperity an option for the next generation of small plot farmers (Frykman, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_31]

522   As such, social entrepreneurs share with other development actors an impulse, that of the “will to [SOURCE: Page_31]

523   improve” described by Li (2007) in her study of development interventions in Indonesia. This [SOURCE: Page_31]

524   will to improve is grounded in a government rationality focused on the welfare of the population [SOURCE: Page_31]

525   and prescribing the right manner of disposing things; the moral obligation attached to it means [SOURCE: Page_31]

526   that shortcomings and failures, instead of discrediting interventions, reinforce them. Indeed “we [SOURCE: Page_31]

527   can’t just give up”; what is important is to show the need for further action rather than results. [SOURCE: Page_31]

528   Echoing Li (2007), I have shown that this will to improve lies on two main pillars. First, a [SOURCE: Page_32]

529   ‘problematisation’ or the identification of a deficiency that needs to be rectified (in this case, the [SOURCE: Page_32]

530   high level of poverty and low water use efficiency among smallholders). Second, a process of [SOURCE: Page_32]

531   rendering technical, that is, of representing the problem to be solved as an intelligible field with [SOURCE: Page_32]

532   specifiable limits and particular characteristics (in this case, low income levels among farmers, [SOURCE: Page_32]

533   limited water availability, high value crops, and ‘proven’ drip irrigation equipment). Rendering [SOURCE: Page_32]

534   technical leads to simplification; broader political-economic processes that have a bearing on any [SOURCE: Page_32]

535   given situation are removed from the frame of analysis. Though this is clearly assumed by Paul [SOURCE: Page_32]

536   Polak and other social entrepreneurs in the name of action (see previous section), this also means [SOURCE: Page_32]

537   that their intervention can never achieve all it seeks. [SOURCE: Page_32]

538   This is because the problematisation and technicisation that characterize the smallholder drip [SOURCE: Page_32]

539   irrigation narrative articulated by social enterprises is problematic for several reasons. First, it [SOURCE: Page_32]

540   reduces the logic of smallholders to one of earning more income even though scholars [SOURCE: Page_32]

541   highlighted the very challenges in defining what makes a smallholder in relation to the [SOURCE: Page_32]

542   multiplicity of their logics (including of resistance to and autonomy from economic forces, and of [SOURCE: Page_32]

543   adaptability and enhanced resilience) and strategies to address poverty, environmental, health, [SOURCE: Page_32]

544   and energy challenges (Acloque et al., 2015; Sourisseau, 2014; van der Ploeg, 2009). Second, it [SOURCE: Page_32]

545   sees drip irrigation efficiency as a given, even though efficiency has been shown to be highly [SOURCE: Page_32]

546   dependent on farmers’ logics (for instance, Benouniche et al., 2014b) and has also been [SOURCE: Page_32]

547   questioned at a more conceptual level. Scholars have indeed long highlighted that the term [SOURCE: Page_32]

548   efficiency is often used differently by different people, can refer to different elements of the [SOURCE: Page_32]

549   water balance, and means different things at different scales (Perry, 2007; Lankford, 2012; van [SOURCE: Page_32]

550   der Kooij et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_32]

551   Moral legitimacy of social entrepreneurship [SOURCE: Page_33]

552   A second key feature I highlighted is the un-apologetic reference of smallholder drip irrigation [SOURCE: Page_33]

553   promoters to the notion of entrepreneurship both at the level of their organization (the adoption of [SOURCE: Page_33]

554   a market based approach) and at the level of the farmer. In doing so, they fit a broader socio- [SOURCE: Page_33]

555   ideological climate whereby entrepreneurship has become a reference point in development [SOURCE: Page_33]

556   discourse and has emerged as a new orthodoxy (Dey and Steyaert, 2010; see first section). [SOURCE: Page_33]

557   Smallholder drip irrigation has come to be closely associated to what is now seen as a preferred [SOURCE: Page_33]

558   model of organization, the social enterprise. Located at the juncture of two sides of development, [SOURCE: Page_33]

559   an ethical concern as ‘care for the other’ and an aesthetic ‘care of the self’ (for a discussion, see [SOURCE: Page_33]

560   Qarles van Ufford et al., 2003), social enterprises have acquired a “moral legitimacy” and are [SOURCE: Page_33]

561   widely presented as an alternative development model (see Dart, 2004 for an insightful analysis). [SOURCE: Page_33]

562   The moral legitimacy of the social enterprise reinforces the engineer-based framing of drip [SOURCE: Page_33]

563   irrigation as a modern and efficient technology. Together, they buttress the positive connotation [SOURCE: Page_33]

564   that smallholder drip irrigation has acquired. After all, if good organizations, acting for the right [SOURCE: Page_33]

565   reasons, promote a modern technology, it can only be positive. [SOURCE: Page_33]

566   That social enterprises constitute a truly alternative way of ‘doing development’ in the irrigation [SOURCE: Page_33]

567   sector does not hold under scrutiny. Admittedly, iDE and Driptech aim at strengthening small [SOURCE: Page_33]

568   scale private actors –maybe more than public development agencies, notably small entrepreneurs [SOURCE: Page_33]

569   who sell drip kits for a profit. They are, however, part and parcel of the broader international [SOURCE: Page_33]

570   development community, in which they found a supporting coalition, and of which they share [SOURCE: Page_33]

571   many of the codes and working culture. iDE and Driptech are largely funded (i.e. subsidized) by [SOURCE: Page_33]

572   development aid agencies, philanthropists and “angel investors”, and they sometimes work in [SOURCE: Page_33]

573   partnership with national governments and non-governmental organizations that tend to promote [SOURCE: Page_34]

574   new technologies through subsidy programs even if these are seen as harmful by social [SOURCE: Page_34]

575   entrepreneurs. Further, in sub-Saharan Africa, social enterprises partly adopt similar approaches [SOURCE: Page_34]

576   to those used by national and international research institutes and development aid agencies such [SOURCE: Page_34]

577   as pilot experiments, demonstration sites, farmers’ field schools, with similar results: everything [SOURCE: Page_34]

578   comes to an halt as soon as external support vanishes (see for instance, Garb and Friedlander, [SOURCE: Page_34]

579   2014, Kulecho and Weatherhead, 2005; Wanvoeke et al., 2015b). [SOURCE: Page_34]

580   Finally, like most development agencies, social enterprises evaluate their action (and [SOURCE: Page_34]

581   communicate about it) vis-à-vis what can be best termed ‘intermediary indicators’ such as their [SOURCE: Page_34]

582   volumes of sales instead of looking at whether and how the products they sell are actually used. [SOURCE: Page_34]

583   Such attention to intermediary indicators is made possible and justified by two major beliefs [SOURCE: Page_34]

584   sustaining the promotion of drip kits. First, the belief that poor farmers only buy what they really [SOURCE: Page_34]

585   need and will invest in specific products only if these yield rapid benefits (Polak 2008) even [SOURCE: Page_34]

586   though there is increased evidence that drip-kits are often seen by farmers as a gateway to other [SOURCE: Page_34]

587   benefits (Wanvoeke et al., 2015b). Second, the belief that the drip irrigation hardware has [SOURCE: Page_34]

588   intrinsic characteristics: whatever the context it is efficient and leads to increased yields – two [SOURCE: Page_34]

589   presuppositions that have been shown to be problematic (see above). [SOURCE: Page_34]

590   Technology: Immunity to context, configuring users and ascribing responsibilities [SOURCE: Page_34]

591   The story presented reveals the marginality of smallholders and dramatizes their incorporation in [SOURCE: Page_34]

592   the modern world of entrepreneurship. As highlighted by one respondent, however, [SOURCE: Page_34]

593      “a narrow focus on income generation might be detrimental to what should remain the [SOURCE: Page_34]

594      primary objective, that is, positive social change [… it might lead] to lose sight of equity and [SOURCE: Page_34]

595      justice issues as it is easier to show some success in generating additional income with [SOURCE: Page_35]

597   Beyond the issue of whom to target illustrated by the quote, I argue that by framing smallholders [SOURCE: Page_35]

598   as entrepreneurs primarily driven by economic rationality, social enterprises support a simplified [SOURCE: Page_35]

599   vision of poverty that overlooks its structural dimensions and call upon an individualistic [SOURCE: Page_35]

600   problem-solving script (see also, Dey and Steyaert, 2010). In this script, the choice and [SOURCE: Page_35]

601   responsibility to get out of poverty ultimately falls on individual smallholders who, if given an [SOURCE: Page_35]

602   opportunity, “will invest in their own prosperity” (Frykman, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_35]

603   What social enterprises propose to do then is to create the conditions for making this investment [SOURCE: Page_35]

604   possible. This is meant to be achieved through two main levers: the provision of an adapted [SOURCE: Page_35]

605   technology and its dissemination through a strengthened private sector, based on market [SOURCE: Page_35]

606   principles. Despite the proclaimed importance given to user-based design (Polak, 2007) and [SOURCE: Page_35]

607   system-wide technological capacity (Clark et al., 2003), social enterprises articulate a rather [SOURCE: Page_35]

608   traditional and problematic vision of technology. In that vision, material facts (in this case the [SOURCE: Page_35]

609   drip irrigation equipment) are largely independent of the context in which they are developed and [SOURCE: Page_35]

610   used, and even, of the people who use them. Garb and Friedlander (2014) see in the search for a [SOURCE: Page_35]

611   ‘perfect’ product that drives promoters of smallholder drip irrigation an attempt to sever the link [SOURCE: Page_35]

612   between artifact and socio-technical infrastructure. This search is thought to allow for a seamless [SOURCE: Page_35]

613   transfer of the artifact from one site to another and from one set of farmers to another. I share [SOURCE: Page_35]

614   their analysis that it is largely counterproductive and undermines the extent to which the drip kits [SOURCE: Page_35]

615   can be put to productive use given the socio-technical nature of technologies (Bijker et al., 1992). [SOURCE: Page_35]

616   In the case of smallholder drip irrigation, this decontextualized vision of technology translates in [SOURCE: Page_36]

617   pre-packaged ‘drip kits’, whose small size coupled with an infinite expandability (as for Legos, it [SOURCE: Page_36]

618   is said that kits can be combined one to another) gives ground to claims of universality: they can [SOURCE: Page_36]

619   be used anywhere, by anyone. In actual terms, however, the script embeds assumptions about [SOURCE: Page_36]

620   potential users (for instance, in sub-Saharan Africa smaller kits of 20 m^2 are said to be most [SOURCE: Page_36]

621   adapted to women, larger kits of 100 m^2 are often meant for individual male farmers while 500 [SOURCE: Page_36]

622   m^2 kits are geared at farmers groups), their respective resources (notably in terms of access to [SOURCE: Page_36]

623   land and water), and their primary objective, which would be earning more money. Further, the [SOURCE: Page_36]

624   consumer is not totally free to choose as clearly stated by one respondent: [SOURCE: Page_36]

625      While it is ideal to offer a range of products suited to farmers’ needs, price point, etc. the [SOURCE: Page_36]

626      challenges to do this well multiply quickly […] You need to start somewhere and this [SOURCE: Page_36]

627      somewhere is “what you have in stock” (interview data, August 16, 2013). [SOURCE: Page_36]

628   Here, the drip kit, externally promoted through social enterprises and other development agencies [SOURCE: Page_36]

629   and packaged in standard carton boxes, stands in stark contrast with small motorized pumps. The [SOURCE: Page_36]

630   latter are widely available “off the shelf” in multiple forms and for multiple purposes to [SOURCE: Page_36]

631   smallholders without external support, and have been shown to have a transformative capacity [SOURCE: Page_36]

632   (both in positive and negative terms) on irrigation in developing countries (see among others de [SOURCE: Page_36]

633   Fraiture and Giordano, 2014 and Shah, 2009). At a more conceptual level, the drip kit also stands [SOURCE: Page_36]

634   in stark contrast with the Zimbabwe Bush Pump described by Marianne de Laet and Annemarie [SOURCE: Page_36]

635   Mol (2000). The drip kit has indeed little fluidity, a term used by de Laet and Mol (2000) to [SOURCE: Page_36]

636   characterize a technology that has vague and moving boundaries, that is, a fluid identity, or said [SOURCE: Page_36]

637   more simply that is adaptable, flexible and responsive to its surroundings. Framed as a ‘perfect [SOURCE: Page_36]

638   product’, the neatly and definitively bounded drip kit is quite the contrary: it becomes [SOURCE: Page_37]

639   untouchable hence has little scope to play a part in any significant changes. [SOURCE: Page_37]

640   Finally, the idea that it is possible to design a technology that would meet the needs of [SOURCE: Page_37]

641   smallholder farmers writ large not only overlooks their diversity but reinforces the idea of [SOURCE: Page_37]

642   individual responsibility. Indeed, if an organization has designed “the simplest, most affordable, [SOURCE: Page_37]

643   and most field-ready drip irrigation system [that] provides a cost-effective means for even the [SOURCE: Page_37]

644   poorest farmers to increase their water use efficiency, growing more crops to feed their families [SOURCE: Page_37]

646   use it or not. Whose responsibility is it then if the technology does not spread like wild fire or [SOURCE: Page_37]

648   ways (which can be redressed through capacity building and extension) or of a non-conducive [SOURCE: Page_37]

649   environment. It is not the responsibility of the social entrepreneur animated by empathy and a [SOURCE: Page_37]

650   will to improve, and equipped with a ‘proven’ technological artifact. In a twist, it is not the [SOURCE: Page_37]

651   technology (i.e. drip irrigation) that needs to be fitted to meet farmers’ needs but the smallholders [SOURCE: Page_37]

652   themselves, who need to be configured to give a favorable environment for the technology to [SOURCE: Page_37]

653   express its potential. [SOURCE: Page_37]

685   provided they access appropriate technology to grow high value crops for the market. Drip kits [SOURCE: Page_39]

686   are said to fulfill a void. The story highlights that the latter share the characteristics of efficiency [SOURCE: Page_39]

687   and modernity of classic drip irrigation equipment but that their small size, low cost and ease of [SOURCE: Page_39]

688   use make them ‘perfect’ for smallholder farmers in the developing world and adaptable to any [SOURCE: Page_39]

689   condition. The story ends by highlighting the potential of drip kits for poverty alleviation from [SOURCE: Page_39]

690   household to global level. [SOURCE: Page_39]

691   This story is problematic for several reasons. Ideologically, it overlooks the multiplicity of [SOURCE: Page_39]

692   smallholder farming and reduces the latter to its economic rationality. It also promotes a way of [SOURCE: Page_39]

693   farming (the production of high value crops for the market) that is particularly risky even though [SOURCE: Page_40]

694   risk minimization and an enhanced capacity to face external shocks (such as price volatility) are [SOURCE: Page_40]

695   often key strategies and objectives of smallholders. Conceptually, the story ascribes inherent [SOURCE: Page_40]

696   features to an artifact, the drip kit, independently of the context and the people who use it thus [SOURCE: Page_40]

697   overlooking the strong interdependency between the social and the technical dimension of [SOURCE: Page_40]

698   technology (Bijker et al., 1992). At a technical level, claims that drip kits would be efficient per [SOURCE: Page_40]

699   se are particularly problematic, given recent work highlighting the need for a critical examination [SOURCE: Page_40]

700   of the notion of efficiency (for instance, Lankford, 2012; van der Koiij et al., 2013). At a more [SOURCE: Page_40]

701   fundamental level, framing drip-kits as ‘perfect’ products that could be seamlessly transferred, [SOURCE: Page_40]

702   leads to depriving them of the very fluidity (de Laet and Mol, 2000) they would need to have a [SOURCE: Page_40]

703   transformative effect on smallholder farming. It also leads to overlooking other options and ways [SOURCE: Page_40]

704   for promoting irrigation in the developing world. Ethically, finally, the story ascribes the [SOURCE: Page_40]

705   responsibility of (lifting oneself out of) poverty to smallholders themselves, even if that choice is [SOURCE: Page_40]

706   constrained by structural and political conditions and relations. [SOURCE: Page_40]

707   As highlighted by Li (2007), a complex situation is rendered technical (and a-political) and [SOURCE: Page_40]

708   translated into a stable interpretation: ‘drip irrigation is good because it will alleviate poverty’. [SOURCE: Page_40]

709   Mosse (2005) highlights that this interpretation only becomes ‘reality’ with a unity of points of [SOURCE: Page_40]

710   view. Insiders have actively sought to create interest and build alliances among an ever wider [SOURCE: Page_40]

711   network of development actors including bilateral and international development agencies, [SOURCE: Page_40]

712   private foundations, international research organizations, Non Governmental Organizations and [SOURCE: Page_40]

713   industrial manufacturers of irrigation equipment. What proved central to this exercise is the [SOURCE: Page_40]

714   unapologetic reference to the notion of entrepreneurship and the use of a market-based rhetoric [SOURCE: Page_40]

715   by charismatic individuals who became to be seen by others as examples of the mythical [SOURCE: Page_40]

716   Schumpeterian entrepreneur through whom change takes place. This positioned the promoters of [SOURCE: Page_41]

717   smallholder drip irrigation in a specific space, that of the social enterprise, which has acquired a [SOURCE: Page_41]

718   moral legitimacy (Dart, 2004) and is seen as a preferred model of organization by many actors [SOURCE: Page_41]

719   who are disenchanted by the poor records of development aid and doubtful regarding the role and [SOURCE: Page_41]

720   agenda of big business in development. Here too, the story is problematic. What can be observed, [SOURCE: Page_41]

721   indeed, is less an alternative development model than a delineation of a niche and the re-working [SOURCE: Page_41]

722   of existing arrangements within the international development community. Social enterprises [SOURCE: Page_41]

723   share many of the codes and the working culture of other development agencies, including a [SOURCE: Page_41]

724   concern with the prospect of having impact at scale and the use of intermediary indicators to [SOURCE: Page_41]

725   measure it. In this case, the volume of drip-kits sales is of primary importance to ensure [SOURCE: Page_41]

726   continued support from national and international development agencies. In the absence of other [SOURCE: Page_41]

727   coherent narratives, the story social enterprises articulate takes over: if sold, it means the drip kits [SOURCE: Page_41]

728   are bought by farmers hence that they are needed and used as intended by their promoters. [SOURCE: Page_41]

729   The pro-poor smallholder drip irrigation narrative appears to be successful to harness the support [SOURCE: Page_41]

730   of the international development community in which the notions of social entrepreneurship and [SOURCE: Page_41]

731   technical potential are particularly appealing. Until now, however, it has had little transformative [SOURCE: Page_41]

732   capacity in terms of triggering a smallholder-led movement of irrigation development, especially [SOURCE: Page_41]

733   in sub-Saharan Africa. By focusing on the actors who elaborated the storyline and the strategies [SOURCE: Page_41]

734   they used to do so, I show the narrative for what it is, that is, a social construct carefully crafted [SOURCE: Page_41]

735   by development agents and carrying with it an array of hidden and sometimes problematic [SOURCE: Page_41]

736   assumptions. The cause and effect relationships described in the story and the potential of drip [SOURCE: Page_41]

737   irrigation should not be taken for granted; they only exist if they are performed. [SOURCE: Page_41]

738   Unpacking development narratives, which work to blackbox issues and establish specific [SOURCE: Page_42]

739   interventions as illusory panaceas, can provide fresh perspectives on seemingly intractable [SOURCE: Page_42]

740   problems (in this case the little transformative capacity of smallholder drip irrigation). Rather [SOURCE: Page_42]

741   than providing counter narratives, it is about engaging with existing ones and debating their [SOURCE: Page_42]

742   apparent naturalness. In this case, the analysis highlights the need to better understand the logics [SOURCE: Page_42]

743   of smallholders to engage in drip irrigation development projects rather than assuming that their [SOURCE: Page_42]

744   motivations neatly echo a broader development narrative. Shedding light on these motivations [SOURCE: Page_42]

745   may lead to redefining what smallholder drip irrigation actually is for different actors (a nice [SOURCE: Page_42]

746   story, a technical object for improving agricultural productivity, a means to connect to certain [SOURCE: Page_42]

747   networks, etc.) hence to better assess the extent to, and the contexts in which it can actually lead [SOURCE: Page_42]

748   to positive transformation of smallholder farming (or not). For development practitioners, this [SOURCE: Page_42]

749   means leaving smallholders engage with drip irrigation in their own terms and potentially [SOURCE: Page_42]

750   redefine the technology, which, in turn, holds the promises of better targeting interventions rather [SOURCE: Page_42]

751   than pursuing illusory objectives. Such ‘grounded strategy’ implies distance with dominant [SOURCE: Page_42]

752   development narratives that all exhibit a measure of ‘success’ in the form of potential and [SOURCE: Page_42]

753   preferably large scale positive change. This can be done by shifting the analytical focus from the [SOURCE: Page_42]

754   development intervention itself to the actors who shape it and sustain particular value [SOURCE: Page_42]

755   interpretation about it. In this light, there is need to reflect on the way actors construct of perform [SOURCE: Page_42]

756   ‘successful outcomes’ and to make explicit the value systems that underpin such measures. [SOURCE: Page_42]
