Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

Throughout the past decades, agriculture has been in and out of favour among development [SOURCE: Page_4]

practitioners and international funding agencies. In 2007, the World Development Report [SOURCE: Page_4]

heralded the comeback of agriculture after a 25-year hiatus from the development agenda. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Today, agriculture is seen as ‘a vital development tool for achieving the Millennium [SOURCE: Page_4]

Development Goals’ (World Bank 2007a, p.v), leading to increased investments in the sector. [SOURCE: Page_4]

As an upshot, projects and reforms dedicated to agricultural water management are receiving [SOURCE: Page_4]

There is, thus, an apparent contradiction between prescriptions (what projects are meant to [SOURCE: Page_4]

understandings that contribute towards shaping them. Rather than asking how this apparent [SOURCE: Page_4]

paradox can be solved, the paper focuses on why and how it has emerged and is sustained in [SOURCE: Page_4]

the case of small reservoirs in sub-Saharan Africa. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Development and Anchoring Devices: An Analytical Framework [SOURCE: Page_6]

The field of anthropology has been particularly prolific in its studies of the relationship [SOURCE: Page_6]

between development policy and development practice. The ensuing debate, however, has [SOURCE: Page_6]

been bipolar (Crewe and Harrison 1999). On the one hand, an ‘instrumentalist school’ adheres [SOURCE: Page_6]

to the position that development practice follows development policy in a linear manner. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Development policy is thought to be the outcome of a logical problem-diagnosis exercise. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Where policy fails to meet its intended outcomes, the reasons are to be found in the socio- [SOURCE: Page_6]

political ‘externalities’ of the context of implementation. Critical anthropologists of [SOURCE: Page_6]

development, on the other hand, argue that development policy is a rationalizing, technical [SOURCE: Page_6]

discourse (Escobar 1995, Ferguson 1992). Such discourse is said to naturalize poverty, [SOURCE: Page_6]

objectify the poor and depoliticize development. It hides what these scholars view as the true [SOURCE: Page_6]

objectives of development: cognitive control, social regulation and bureaucratic dominance [SOURCE: Page_6]

(Ferguson 1992). [SOURCE: Page_6]

Mosse (2004), however, rejects this dichotomous understanding of the relationship between [SOURCE: Page_6]

development policy and practice. He argues that the instrumentalist school of thought is [SOURCE: Page_6]

dismissive of the institutional politics of development, while the critical anthropologists [SOURCE: Page_6]

replace ‘the instrumental rationality of policy with the anonymous automaticity of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

machine’ (Mosse 2004, p.644). He proposes an alternative perspective – the ‘new [SOURCE: Page_6]

ethnography of aid’ – that acknowledges the complex agency of all actors in development. In [SOURCE: Page_6]

other words, villagers, field and office workers, project managers and individuals responsible [SOURCE: Page_6]

for donor relations all ‘create everyday spheres of action that are autonomous from the [SOURCE: Page_6]

organizing (or legitimizing) project models [...], but that at the same time work actively to [SOURCE: Page_7]

sustain those same models – because it is in their interest to do so’ (Mosse 2004, p^665). [SOURCE: Page_7]

The paradigm of the new ethnography of aid is not whether development works, but how; not [SOURCE: Page_7]

whether development is successful, but how it is successful (see Olivier de Sardan 2001 for a [SOURCE: Page_7]

similar argument). As argued by critical discourse analysts, it is the interpretation of events [SOURCE: Page_7]

and outcomes that is critical to understanding policy impacts. Policy models or development [SOURCE: Page_7]

projects (such as small reservoirs) come into being and are sustained by discourse coalitions [SOURCE: Page_7]

(Hajer 1995) and interpretative communities; a range of actors who devote their energies to [SOURCE: Page_7]

maintaining coherent representations of the models. The more interests that are tied up with [SOURCE: Page_7]

These discursive and political analyses echo debates within the field of Science and [SOURCE: Page_7]

Technology around the concepts of boundary object (Star and Griesemer 1989) and [SOURCE: Page_7]

anchoring device (van der Sluijs et al. 1998). Boundary objects are relatively stable and [SOURCE: Page_7]

reproducible things, people, projects, texts, maps, and ideas. They are multivalent in character [SOURCE: Page_7]

(1998, p^312) introduce the concept of anchoring device as a ‘highly stable boundary object in [SOURCE: Page_7]

Agricultural Water Management Discourses in sub-Saharan Africa [SOURCE: Page_8]

By shaping beliefs and perceptions and by prescribing what is legitimate or illegitimate, [SOURCE: Page_8]

general discourse – and more detailed cause-and-effect narratives – contribute to framing [SOURCE: Page_8]

development knowledge and policy (Hajer 1995, Roe 1991). In this section, we engage with [SOURCE: Page_8]

discourses of agricultural water management in sub-Saharan Africa that set the boundaries [SOURCE: Page_8]

In mainstream development discourse, agricultural growth is central to rural poverty [SOURCE: Page_8]

reduction. The agricultural sector is said to account for 20% of sub-Saharan Africa’s GDP and [SOURCE: Page_8]

67% of the total labour force, and to be the main source of international exports (FAO 2008, [SOURCE: Page_8]

World Bank 2007a). But sub-Saharan Africa is also described as having the lowest [SOURCE: Page_8]

agricultural productivity in the world, and as having witnessed an increase in the numbers of [SOURCE: Page_8]

poor and malnourished people due to a mounting gap between agricultural output and [SOURCE: Page_8]

population growth (AfDB et al. 2008, FAO 2008). The underperformance of the agricultural [SOURCE: Page_8]

sector, further affected by adverse environmental and climatic conditions, is pointed out as a [SOURCE: Page_8]

major drag on the region’s development. [SOURCE: Page_8]

In the agricultural water sector, the picture is one of abundant yet very unequally distributed [SOURCE: Page_9]

renewable water resources with limited water control, little irrigation, and a persistent [SOURCE: Page_9]

tendency of expensive projects failing to achieve expected outcomes. Most studies point to [SOURCE: Page_9]

the low performance, productivity and profitability of existing irrigation systems, and to [SOURCE: Page_9]

various physical, institutional and economic constraints for their further development (AfDB [SOURCE: Page_9]

et al. 2008, World Bank 2007b). The sector is said to have experienced general neglect as [SOURCE: Page_9]

major donors perceive agricultural water projects as time consuming, expensive to design and [SOURCE: Page_9]

implement, and ‘more contentious than [projects] in other sectors, especially when they [SOURCE: Page_9]

involve [...] irrigation infrastructure’ (World Bank 2007b, p.34). Consequently, though most [SOURCE: Page_9]

poverty reduction strategies in the region are predicated upon agricultural growth, it is said [SOURCE: Page_9]

that ‘agricultural water management has generally not been seen as a vehicle for achieving [SOURCE: Page_9]

this [and] has had a low profile Poverty Reduction Strategy Papers’ (AfDB et al. 2008, p. x). [SOURCE: Page_9]

Collectively, these discursive elements signal that the potential of the agricultural water sector [SOURCE: Page_9]

in sub-Saharan Africa is high. But, although water is available locally to meet human needs, [SOURCE: Page_9]

the region is said to be facing ‘economic water scarcity’ as a result of low investments, scant [SOURCE: Page_9]

infrastructure, and weak human and institutional capital ultimately limiting access to water. [SOURCE: Page_9]

The situation is said to necessitate additional investments in water development for poverty [SOURCE: Page_9]

reduction (Molden 2007; AfDB et al. 2008, World Bank 2007b). In this vein, the New [SOURCE: Page_9]

Partnership for Africa’s Development (NEPAD) has formulated priorities for action and [SOURCE: Page_9]

investment in its Comprehensive Africa Agriculture Development Program (CAADP). Water [SOURCE: Page_9]

management and irrigation development feature as one of the ‘areas for primary action’. The [SOURCE: Page_9]

continent-wide rhetoric focuses on fulfilling targets for public investments in agriculture (a [SOURCE: Page_9]

minimum of 10% of national budgets as endorsed in the “Maputo Declaration on Agriculture [SOURCE: Page_9]

and Food Security in Africa” in 2003), agricultural productivity (to be increased by at least [SOURCE: Page_9]

6%), and irrigated area (to be increased from 8 to 15% of the arable land; NEPAD 2003). [SOURCE: Page_9]

Among the multiple options considered, the CAADP prioritizes ‘the identification and [SOURCE: Page_10]

preparation of investments to support small-scale irrigation’ (NEPAD 2003, p.28). Small [SOURCE: Page_10]

reservoirs are proposed as one of the solutions to agricultural development challenges in sub- [SOURCE: Page_10]

Saharan Africa. [SOURCE: Page_10]

How and Why Small Reservoir Projects “Fail” or “Succeed”: Performance and [SOURCE: Page_16]

Institutions as Mediatory Boundary Objects [SOURCE: Page_16]

Two notions are central in allowing small reservoirs to serve as anchoring devices; firstly, the [SOURCE: Page_16]

notion of performance, which conveys concern for efficiency, optimization and productivity, [SOURCE: Page_16]

and secondly, participation in relation to finding the “right institutions” for managing [SOURCE: Page_16]

common pool resources, such as small reservoirs. The following sections discuss these two [SOURCE: Page_16]

notions. We argue that performance and institutions act as mediatory boundary objects and [SOURCE: Page_16]

contribute to shape attached small reservoirs as anchoring devices. But they also contribute to [SOURCE: Page_16]

framing development in dichotomous terms of success/failure rather than as a continuous [SOURCE: Page_16]

process. [SOURCE: Page_16]

Multiple Vantage Points on the Notion of Performance [SOURCE: Page_16]

Irrigation performance assessment is presented as an important management tool to aid [SOURCE: Page_16]

irrigation projects to deliver on their promises (Molden et al. 2007). Long limited to [SOURCE: Page_16]

hydraulic, agronomic and economic indicators, performance assessments have been [SOURCE: Page_16]

broadened to account for multiple uses of water, environmental and gender dynamics (Bos et [SOURCE: Page_16]

al. 2005, Meinzen-Dick and van der Hoek 2001, van Koppen 2002). Institutional economists [SOURCE: Page_16]

have also successfully argued for recognizing the institutional dimension of performance [SOURCE: Page_16]

when identifying “guiding principles” for robust and enduring institutions for common [SOURCE: Page_16]

property resources management (Ostrom 1990). Performance (of irrigation) can assume [SOURCE: Page_16]

multiple meanings for different people and for diverse purposes (Venot and Cecchi 2011). A [SOURCE: Page_16]

focus on performance also frames development projects in dualistic terms in the sense that [SOURCE: Page_16]

they perform or do not. In this section, we engage with the meanings of performance for two [SOURCE: Page_16]

types of actors – extension agents of ministries/bureaus of agriculture and the local users. [SOURCE: Page_16]

These actors have a specific significance. The former act as brokers between policy making [SOURCE: Page_16]

and project implementation; their interpretation of events is passed on to higher levels of [SOURCE: Page_16]

decision making through the state apparatus. Local users, on the other hand, are the final [SOURCE: Page_17]

stewards of small reservoirs and projects are implemented in their names. [SOURCE: Page_17]

During the rapid appraisal, agricultural extension agents were asked to qualitatively assess the [SOURCE: Page_17]

performance of small reservoirs. They considered that between one third and two thirds of all [SOURCE: Page_17]

small reservoirs performed very poorly or poorly (figure 1 left panel). The situation was [SOURCE: Page_17]

deemed particularly critical in Ethiopia where siltation was commonly identified as the main [SOURCE: Page_17]

cause of poor performance (environmental problem; figure 1 right panel). In Ghana, design [SOURCE: Page_17]

and infrastructure problems were identified as the main causes for poor performance (figure 1 [SOURCE: Page_17]

right panel). In Burkina Faso, extension agents considered that two-thirds of the reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_17]

had an average, or higher, performance. Most reservoirs performing below average were not [SOURCE: Page_17]

equipped with irrigation infrastructures. Organisational problems, marked by the absence of a [SOURCE: Page_17]

Water User Association, were identified in all three countries as a major cause for low [SOURCE: Page_17]

performance (figure 1, right panel). [SOURCE: Page_17]

Extension agents evoke the efficiency and participation discourses in their performance [SOURCE: Page_17]

assessment of small reservoirs. However, they do so in binary terms: that either small [SOURCE: Page_17]

reservoirs perform or they do not; either the local population participates or it does not. The [SOURCE: Page_17]

specific attention given to infrastructural issues echoes the priorities of contractors and of an [SOURCE: Page_17]

irrigation bureaucracy dominated by civil engineers. By voicing a concern over poor [SOURCE: Page_18]

performance yet articulating possible improvements, extension agents provide a rationale for [SOURCE: Page_18]

national governments to call upon external donors or their own governmental agencies to fund [SOURCE: Page_18]

rehabilitation or construction of small reservoirs. [SOURCE: Page_18]

Another level of complexity emerges from investigating the perceptions of local users. In all [SOURCE: Page_18]

the communities studied, the local population expressed a level of satisfaction similar or [SOURCE: Page_18]

higher than the extension agents. We then explored the users’ satisfaction regarding the [SOURCE: Page_18]

physical infrastructures, modes of management, benefits derived, and equity aspects of small [SOURCE: Page_18]

reservoirs. Like extension agents, local users pointed to poor technical and managerial [SOURCE: Page_18]

performance. They, however, showed a higher level of satisfaction regarding the benefits they [SOURCE: Page_18]

derived and the equity aspects of small reservoirs. This highlights that small reservoirs are [SOURCE: Page_18]

invested with social meaning and valued for multiple reasons that may not be captured by [SOURCE: Page_18]

even the current generation of more comprehensive performance assessments. [SOURCE: Page_18]

These community views contribute to framing small reservoirs as promising interventions [SOURCE: Page_18]

compatible with local systems, hence reinforcing their status as sought-after development [SOURCE: Page_18]

interventions in sub-Saharan Africa. But rural communities are not homogenous. The surveys [SOURCE: Page_18]

revealed that small-scale water users (e.g. the poor, youth, women, fishermen) tend to be more [SOURCE: Page_18]

satisfied when irrigation activities are little developed. Conversely, they face difficulties in [SOURCE: Page_18]

reaping direct benefits when intensive cultivation becomes the main goal or is the main [SOURCE: Page_18]

activity. Indeed, capitals and capabilities (Bebbington, 1999) are key factors mediating the [SOURCE: Page_18]

access to and use of resources that are made available by small reservoirs. This brings us to [SOURCE: Page_18]

the other notion –institution- that is central to explaining the workings of small reservoirs as [SOURCE: Page_18]

anchoring devices. [SOURCE: Page_18]

Shaping Understandings of Institutions [SOURCE: Page_19]

Building on the idea of institutional crafting, as epitomized by the early work of Elinor [SOURCE: Page_19]

Ostrom who described design principles for robust and enduring institutions for common [SOURCE: Page_19]

property resource management (Ostrom, 1990, 1992), development actors have been looking [SOURCE: Page_19]

for the ‘right institution’ to manage their investments, though Ostrom herself has recently [SOURCE: Page_19]

warned against this elusive search for panaceas (Ostrom et al., 2007). Based on observations [SOURCE: Page_19]

of farmer-managed irrigation schemes in South Asia (Yoder, 1994), promoters and [SOURCE: Page_19]

implementers of small scale irrigation projects, such as small reservoirs, have asserted the [SOURCE: Page_19]

primacy of Water User Associations (WUA) as the rightful entities for maintaining and [SOURCE: Page_19]

managing them and enhancing their performance. But though they have acquired tremendous [SOURCE: Page_19]

traction among development partners, WUA face mounting critiques among researchers who [SOURCE: Page_19]

highlight the extraordinary sway the model continues to hold despite little evidence of its [SOURCE: Page_19]

success. Scholars point to the over-formalized approach to institutional formation as adopted [SOURCE: Page_19]

by development partners to explain past shortcomings and oppose to it the observation that [SOURCE: Page_19]

‘institutions elude design’ and that institutional formation is a socially embedded process [SOURCE: Page_19]

shaped by historic factors, power relations, norms and values (Cleaver and Franks 2005). [SOURCE: Page_19]

Figure 2 highlights that extension agents articulate this polar debate. On the one hand (left [SOURCE: Page_19]

panel), extension agents support the view that the presence of a WUA is positively correlated [SOURCE: Page_19]

to good performance of small reservoirs (the proportion of WUAs among well performing [SOURCE: Page_19]

reservoirs is higher than among poor performing ones).2 On the other hand (right panel), they [SOURCE: Page_19]

identify not less than seven categories of actors contributing to the governance of small [SOURCE: Page_19]

reservoirs. Those actors are said to assume different and complementary roles. Water [SOURCE: Page_19]

committees (e.g. WUAs) were identified as the main decision making body over small [SOURCE: Page_19]

reservoirs in about one quarter of the cases only, and their main tasks were considered to be [SOURCE: Page_19]

minor maintenance and daily management. Line ministries and government agencies are [SOURCE: Page_19]

rarely identified as the main decision makers but their role in procurement and construction [SOURCE: Page_20]

processes and in supporting farmers (extension, marketing) is considered crucial. Finally, [SOURCE: Page_20]

traditional authorities are seen as the most important decision makers regarding the uses and [SOURCE: Page_20]

management of reservoirs in about 25% of the cases. They appear to be crucial in settling [SOURCE: Page_20]

disputes, resolving conflicts, maintaining social cohesion (when ad-hoc resolution [SOURCE: Page_20]

mechanisms have not yielded any results), and oversee land allocation and redistribution. On [SOURCE: Page_20]

the one hand traditional authorities can lend their authority to the members and actions of the [SOURCE: Page_20]

WUAs, on the other there is evidence where traditional authorities simply corner [SOURCE: Page_20]

responsibilities and associated benefits. Finally, many decisions are also said to be reached [SOURCE: Page_20]

through consensus building at the community level (the community is said to be the main [SOURCE: Page_20]

decision maker in 22% of the cases) without a specific organization being singled out. [SOURCE: Page_20]

Again, the point here is not to engage in value judgments over which institutional [SOURCE: Page_20]

arrangements are the most adequate to govern small reservoirs. Rather, we argue that this [SOURCE: Page_20]

debate is an integral dimension of the workings of institutions as boundary objects. [SOURCE: Page_20]

Researchers, international donors, development practitioners, extension agents, and local [SOURCE: Page_20]

communities find in institutions a common platform to act and pursue their own objectives. [SOURCE: Page_20]

That small reservoirs are amenable to distinct institutional discourses –one on polycentric [SOURCE: Page_21]

governance and the contingency of institution formation (McGinnis, 1999; Cleaver and [SOURCE: Page_21]

Franks, 2005); the other promoting a specific institutional model, the WUA- reinforces their [SOURCE: Page_21]

nature as anchoring devices. What is important to note, however, is that the debate over [SOURCE: Page_21]

institutions unfolds essentially around their performance or their bearing on the performance [SOURCE: Page_21]

of specific interventions (here the small reservoirs). Such debates frame socio-political [SOURCE: Page_21]

dynamics as externalities rather than inherent dimensions of development, thus contributing to [SOURCE: Page_21]

lock small reservoirs and the agricultural water sector in a ‘business as usual’ approach. [SOURCE: Page_21]

Small Reservoirs and ‘Big Development’ [SOURCE: Page_21]

The lasting presence of small reservoirs in the rural development agenda of sub-Saharan [SOURCE: Page_21]

Africa hinges upon a stable, yet seemingly contradictory, narrative on their potential and [SOURCE: Page_21]

outcomes. Small reservoirs are said to offer the promises of improved livelihoods and poverty [SOURCE: Page_21]

alleviation, but they are also said to have fared well below expectations. The notions of [SOURCE: Page_21]

performance and institutions play an active role in setting up the terms of this debate. But [SOURCE: Page_21]

such interpretation of events has proved immutable only because it allows for a discourse [SOURCE: Page_21]

coalition (Hajer 1995) and an interpretative community (Mosse 2004) to form and support [SOURCE: Page_21]

small reservoirs against the backdrop of multiple higher level discourses. [SOURCE: Page_21]

First, small reservoirs fit development discourses that typify sub-Saharan Africa. As poverty [SOURCE: Page_21]

and population dynamics (growth, urbanization, and migration) are depicted as the two main [SOURCE: Page_21]

challenges facing the region, the future of agriculture is presented as one of intensification and [SOURCE: Page_21]

diversification (NEPAD 2003). Small reservoir projects are seen as an option to achieve these [SOURCE: Page_21]

objectives via the development of a market-oriented irrigated production and, in the process, [SOURCE: Page_21]

the “sensitization” and capacity building of the population. This makes them particularly [SOURCE: Page_21]

appealing to national decision makers and the international community alike. [SOURCE: Page_21]

Second, by their small size, small reservoirs are said to be adaptable to local conditions and [SOURCE: Page_22]

compatible with local farming systems (table 1); they can be held up as examples of bottom- [SOURCE: Page_22]

up development. This makes them amenable to prevailing governance discourses of [SOURCE: Page_22]

community-led management that are appealing to donors and national governments alike. [SOURCE: Page_22]

Further, by highlighting the role of local communities and institutions in the management of [SOURCE: Page_22]

natural resources, small reservoir projects fit in, and reinforce, the discursive trend towards [SOURCE: Page_22]

devolution that underpins decentralization reforms. In some other instances, such as in [SOURCE: Page_22]

Burkina Faso, small reservoirs also play a central role in the shaping and implementation of [SOURCE: Page_22]

Integrated Water Resources Management policies (Sally et al. 2011). [SOURCE: Page_22]

Third, small reservoirs are said to be an effective means to face environmental challenges that [SOURCE: Page_22]

threaten the region. By providing water storage locally, they are seen as a viable option to [SOURCE: Page_22]

adapt to desertification and climate change (McCartney and Smakhtin 2010) that further [SOURCE: Page_22]

hamper the productivity of rainfed agriculture. This is linked to the fact that small reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_22]

lend themselves to eco-technology discourses, casting them, on one hand, as modern and [SOURCE: Page_22]

efficient infrastructures (Aberra 2004) and, on the other, as low key and low cost options [SOURCE: Page_22]

providing a managerial solution to development problems (table 1). This makes them [SOURCE: Page_22]

attractive to development agencies that are increasingly worried about “bad investments”. [SOURCE: Page_22]

They also become a viable alternative for national governments that are said to ‘lack both the [SOURCE: Page_22]

resources to launch the investment projects and programmes necessary for expanded and [SOURCE: Page_22]

improved water control and crop irrigation on the required scale’ (NEPAD 2003, p.28). [SOURCE: Page_22]

Finally, by the sheer complexity of the processes they induce, small reservoirs lend [SOURCE: Page_22]

themselves to knowledge dynamics. Development researchers^3 engage with this complexity [SOURCE: Page_22]

and with what they consider being gaps, uncertainties or contradictions in the debate over [SOURCE: Page_22]

small reservoirs (such as their under-representation in official databases, the difficulties in [SOURCE: Page_22]

detecting them and accounting for them in macro-level analysis, their level of performance [SOURCE: Page_22]

and their modes of management to name a few). But this apparent knowledge vacuum [SOURCE: Page_23]

remains an integral part of a clearly articulated dual narrative which, in the absence of framed [SOURCE: Page_23]

alternative, appears to be resilient vis-à-vis social and scientific fluxes. [SOURCE: Page_23]

Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_23]

The objective of the paper is not to assess whether small reservoirs are ‘good’ or ‘bad’ options [SOURCE: Page_23]

for rural development in sub-Saharan Africa, nor is it to interrogate the validity of the [SOURCE: Page_23]

multiple perceptions and discourses at stake (the reader could have easily spotted [SOURCE: Page_23]

contradictions among some of the arguments). Rather, our main contention is that perceptions, [SOURCE: Page_23]

discourses and associated social networks are an integral part of small reservoirs projects, and [SOURCE: Page_23]

more broadly of any development or policy model. [SOURCE: Page_23]

It is the ability of a rural development model to act as an ‘anchoring device’ that sustains it in [SOURCE: Page_23]

the face of changing circumstances. Anchoring devices allow for holding separate [SOURCE: Page_23]

communities of practice together around a minimal common denominator (in our case, the [SOURCE: Page_23]

potential yet unfulfilled promises of small reservoirs), while at the same time allowing [SOURCE: Page_23]

multiple interpretations at the “boundary” (in our case, the framing of small reservoirs along [SOURCE: Page_23]

developmentalist, governance, environmental, eco-technological, and scientific lines). [SOURCE: Page_23]

Science and technology studies bring our attention on the dynamics of knowledge production [SOURCE: Page_23]

and associated social networks. Following Forsyth (2003), we argue that such framing can [SOURCE: Page_23]

help us to revisit our understanding of environment and development processes. Looking at [SOURCE: Page_23]

discourse and knowledge formation offers the opportunity to rethink policy recommendation [SOURCE: Page_23]

and formulation by moving away from cause-and-effect narratives that frame policy failures [SOURCE: Page_23]

as socio-political ‘externalities’ and instead recognizing these externalities as integral to the [SOURCE: Page_23]

policy process. [SOURCE: Page_23]
