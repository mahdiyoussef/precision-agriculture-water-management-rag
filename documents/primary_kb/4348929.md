351   Conclusion 352   The renewed attention to the agricultural sector as a driving force of development, notably in [SOURCE: Page_19]

353   sub-Saharan Africa, has led to a restored interest of the international community in agricultural [SOURCE: Page_19]

354   water management, and more specifically in irrigation, as an option for poverty alleviation and [SOURCE: Page_19]

355   livelihood improvement. Multipurpose water systems such as small reservoirs are now geared [SOURCE: Page_19]

356   towards the efficient use of land and water resources. This is exemplified by the priority given to [SOURCE: Page_19]

357   irrigation over other practices such as livestock watering, fishing, small handicraft, and domestic [SOURCE: Page_19]

358   uses that are generally considered as less productive, yet, are central to rural livelihoods. [SOURCE: Page_19]

359        Drawing lessons from past shortcomings partly due to too narrow a focus on infrastructure [SOURCE: Page_19]

360   construction, development projects have adopted a much broader approach deemed at being [SOURCE: Page_19]

361   inclusive of participation and institution building. Drawing from new institutional economics [SOURCE: Page_20]

362   (Ostrom 1992), development practitioners have associated participation processes with clearly [SOURCE: Page_20]

363   defined groups of resource users and structures of authority, rigorous application of graduated [SOURCE: Page_20]

364   sanctions, and transparent decision-making that is codified in written records (Cleaver 2000). In [SOURCE: Page_20]

365   the irrigation sector, these principles have been embedded in a specific form of organisation, the [SOURCE: Page_20]

366   Water User Association, which has been framed as a true panacea over the last 30 years despite [SOURCE: Page_20]

367   mounting critiques and mixed results (Mollinga 2004, Meinzen-Dick 2007, Shah, 2011). [SOURCE: Page_20]

368         We argue that Water User Associations mostly remain conduits serving the efficiency and [SOURCE: Page_20]

369   productivity paradigms, hence specific actors (generally downstream irrigators). Current [SOURCE: Page_20]

370   practices to the establishment of WUAs (for the sake of meeting pre-defined targets, as is still [SOURCE: Page_20]

371   too often the case) convey an over-formalized approach to water resources governance that [SOURCE: Page_20]

372   overlooks the pluralist nature of common property resources management and the multiple [SOURCE: Page_20]

373   arenas through which participation, authority, legitimacy and accountability are continuously [SOURCE: Page_20]

374   negotiated among multiple actors. Understanding and enhancing relationships between WUAs [SOURCE: Page_20]

375   and other entities has been identified as crucial early on (Uphoff 1986, Chambers 1988), but the [SOURCE: Page_20]

376   Burkinabe and Ghanaian case studies discussed in this paper show that this is still rarely the case. [SOURCE: Page_20]

377   There is, for example, a major disconnect along the project cycle. WUAs and more generally [SOURCE: Page_20]

378   users seem to be restricted to routine operations while external actors (government, donors and [SOURCE: Page_20]

379   NGOs staff) keep the upper hand on project’s design hence de-facto deciding ‘what is good for [SOURCE: Page_20]

380   the communities’. Local government institutions appear very little involved in small reservoir [SOURCE: Page_20]

381   matters and mostly for political reasons rather than to ensure downward accountability. [SOURCE: Page_20]

382   Development projects tend to overlook complexity and pluralism, yet, organisations and [SOURCE: Page_20]

383   institutions involved in the management of small reservoirs appear to be intertwined as [SOURCE: Page_20]

384   illustrated by the fact that agricultural extension agents and traditional authorities are part and [SOURCE: Page_21]

385   parcel of the workings of WUAs hence defining an institutional landscape that challenges [SOURCE: Page_21]

386   dichotomies such as ‘administration/consultation’ or ‘modern/traditional’. [SOURCE: Page_21]

387        Based on the existing literature, section 2 of the paper identified four core shortcomings of [SOURCE: Page_21]

388   current approaches to PIM/WUA, that is, that they remain externally triggered, fail to address [SOURCE: Page_21]

389   rent-seeking behaviours, adopt a too narrow focus on farmers and overlook the interplay between [SOURCE: Page_21]

390   water users and the administration, and lack context specificity. Small reservoir projects in [SOURCE: Page_21]

391   Ghana and Burkina Faso make no exception. We do not suggest here that attempts at institutional [SOURCE: Page_21]

392   building are doomed to failure. Rather, following Cleaver and Franks (2005) who diagnose that [SOURCE: Page_21]

393   institutions partly elude design, we argue that addressing the four above shortcomings starts by [SOURCE: Page_21]

394   better understanding social relationships and existing processes of decision-making. This implies [SOURCE: Page_21]

395   triggering co-learning among different actors as a basis of a polycentric accountability structure [SOURCE: Page_21]

396   that would create the conditions for genuine involvement of rural communities at project design [SOURCE: Page_21]

397   (when multiple agricultural water management options can be discussed), during implementation [SOURCE: Page_21]

398   monitoring (supervision of works), and finally for the elaboration of institutional arrangements [SOURCE: Page_21]

399   for the management of multi-purpose water systems such as small reservoirs. [SOURCE: Page_21]

400        Multi-purpose water systems appear to be embedded in competing institutional dynamics [SOURCE: Page_21]

401   such as decentralisation reforms, the formalisation of participation and the changing roles of [SOURCE: Page_21]

402   traditional authorities and public administration. Understanding this pluralism, and the bricolage [SOURCE: Page_21]

403   it gives rise to, acquires a singular significance in the African context where public authority is [SOURCE: Page_21]

404   not the exclusive possession of government or other formal institutions but is rather shaped [SOURCE: Page_21]

405   through the day-to-day encounters between multiple actors. [SOURCE: Page_21]
