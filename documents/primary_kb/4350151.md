7    Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

8    In Europe, increasing the area of legume crops has been identified as a key measure to [SOURCE: Page_1]

9    achieve the objectives set by the European Green Deal and transition toward more sustainable [SOURCE: Page_1]

10   food systems. Although the role of grain legumes in climate change mitigation has been closely [SOURCE: Page_1]

11   examined, little research has focused on how climate change will challenge the development [SOURCE: Page_1]

12   of these crops. This article systematically reviews recent simulation studies to assess the [SOURCE: Page_1]

13   impact of climate change on grain legume performances in Europe and the effect of adaptation [SOURCE: Page_1]

14   strategies. Forty papers using process-based, ecological niche, or statistical models were [SOURCE: Page_1]

15   selected to simulate the response of eight grain legume species to future climate (2020-2100) [SOURCE: Page_1]

16   in Europe. The lack of data on adaptation strategies in Europe was compensated for by [SOURCE: Page_1]

17   enlarging the study area to climatically similar regions. The review highlights a notable [SOURCE: Page_1]

18   imbalance between research about soybeans versus other grain legumes, with soybeans [SOURCE: Page_1]

19   representing approximately 80% of selected studies. Studies focused on soybeans found good [SOURCE: Page_1]

20   agreement, with yield or suitability gains simulated in Northern Europe and a higher probability [SOURCE: Page_1]

21   of yield losses in Southern and South-Eastern Europe. While a similar spatial pattern may be [SOURCE: Page_1]

22   expected for other grain legumes, the scarcity of data makes this result more uncertain. The [SOURCE: Page_1]

23   review also shows that several adaptation strategies have the potential to mitigate the negative [SOURCE: Page_1]

1 [SOURCE: Page_1]

24   impact of climate change on grain legume performances or enhance its positive impact. The [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   most promising strategies tested include irrigation, change in sowing date, and cultivar choice. [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   In addition, we identify several knowledge gaps that, if addressed, would support legume [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   development in Europe. In particular, key species such as field peas, faba beans, lentils, and [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   chickpeas remain blind spots, despite their prominent role in European environmental, [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   agricultural, and nutritional policies. Other knowledge gaps include a lack of accounting for [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   crop response to elevated CO$_2$, ozone, and future biotic pressure, and a limited range of [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   In Europe, increasing the area of legume crops has been identified as a key measure to [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   transition toward more sustainable food systems (Schneider and Huyghe, 2015). Thanks to [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   their ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen (N), legumes contribute to climate change mitigation by [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   reducing greenhouse gas emissions from synthetic fertilizer production and application [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   (Peoples et al., 2019). Legumes are known to provide numerous agronomic services including [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   increased soil N availability benefitting the subsequent crop, improved soil structure, enhanced [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   soil microbial activity, and “break-crop” effect (Jensen et al., 2010; Jensen and Hauggaard- [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   Nielsen, 2003; Köpke and Nemecek, 2010). Legumes grown in rotation can enhance main [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   crop yields, especially in low-input systems (Cernay et al., 2018; Preissel et al., 2015; Zhao et [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   al., 2022). Intercropped with cereals, they allow to increase the total yield in comparison to [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   mean sole crops (Bedoussac et al., 2015). Among all legumes, grain legumes such as [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   soybeans and peas are particularly interesting, as they represent a healthy source of proteins [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   for both animal feed and human food. Substituting grain legumes for animal-based proteins [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   has been proposed as a way to transition toward healthier and more sustainable diets (Davis [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   et al., 2010; Prudhomme et al., 2020; Röös et al., 2020). In this way, increasing the production [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   and consumption of grain legumes is considered a key measure to achieve the objectives set [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   by the European Green Deal and the Farm to Fork Strategy in terms of reducing the [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   environmental footprint of European food systems and ensuring protein self-sufficiency [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   (European Commission, 2020). [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   In spite of their agronomical, environmental, and nutritional benefits, the production area of [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   grain legumes remains low in Europe. In 2020, they represented less than 4% of European [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   cropland, in contrast to 15.2% worldwide (FAOSTAT, 2023) (Table S$_1$). Numerous interacting [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   factors (e.g. public policies, market dynamics, agronomic R&D activities, and breeding efforts), [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   resulting in a so-called technological lock-in, have hampered their development (Magrini et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   2016). Yield instability is one component of this lock-in (Watson et al., 2017). Indeed, grain [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   legume yields are often considered to be more variable than cereal yields (Cernay et al., 2015), [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   especially as they are frequently sown in spring, which makes them more exposed to heat [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   stress and water deficit during key stages of their growing cycle (Falconnier et al., 2020; [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   Reckling et al., 2018). Drought (Farooq et al., 2017), cold and heat stresses (Bhat et al., 2022; [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   Gogoi et al., 2018), or waterlogging (Pasley et al., 2020) can strongly affect biological nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   fixation (Salon et al., 2011), grain legume growth, reproduction, and yield. [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   Climate change is likely to increase the occurrence of such stresses, and thus impact grain [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   legume performances (Ahmed et al., 2022; Vadez et al., 2012). Although the role of grain [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   legumes in climate change mitigation has been closely examined (Jensen et al., 2012; [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   Prudhomme et al., 2020), little research has focused on how climate change will challenge [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   their development in Europe. In particular, although a large body of literature has evaluated [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   the effect of a single climatic factor - mainly elevated CO$_2$, temperature and water stress - on [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   grain legume physiology and performances (Ahmed et al., 2022; Bhattacharya, 2010; Dutta et [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   al., 2022), little is known on how these crops will respond to the simultaneous, climate change- [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   driven variations of all these factors (Hatfield et al., 2011; Rötter et al., 2018; Vadez et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   2012). [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   Crop models have been shown to be useful tools for assessing crop responses to climate [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   change, as they can use projections for future climate under different emission scenarios and [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   account for the simultaneous variations in several climate parameters. As several sources of [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   uncertainty are associated with crop models (Wallach and Thorburn, 2017), results may differ [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   among studies. Reviews are therefore needed to synthesize the outcomes of several [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   simulations and assess consensus on the direction of change. To our knowledge, reviews of [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   simulation studies have mainly focused on major crops such as cereals (e.g. Carr et al. 2022; [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   Challinor et al. 2014; Knox et al. 2016). Although some of them include major grain legumes [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   such as soybeans, results often focus on main production areas, for example Brazil, the United [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   States, and Asia in Hasegawa et al. (2022) and Zhao et al. (2017), excluding Europe. However, [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   the impact of climate change on crops is known to vary depending on regions and crop species [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

88    (Moore and Lobell, 2015). Therefore, the impact of climate change on grain legumes in Europe [SOURCE: Page_5]

89    needs to be assessed in a crop-specific spatially explicit manner. Recently, several studies [SOURCE: Page_5]

90    have simulated grain legume response to climate change in Europe using different types of [SOURCE: Page_5]

91    models, including process-based crop models (Nendel et al., 2023), machine learning [SOURCE: Page_5]

92    techniques (Guilpart et al., 2022), and ecological niche models (Manners et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_5]

93    However, their results may be diverging as in Guilpart et al. (2022) and Manners et al. (2020) [SOURCE: Page_5]

94    for soybeans, which motivates the need for a review. [SOURCE: Page_5]

95    Adjusting crop management can significantly modify the impact of climate change on crop [SOURCE: Page_5]

96    performances (Abramoff et al., 2023). Here again, crop response to adaptation has been [SOURCE: Page_5]

97    investigated for major crops but less frequently for grain legumes, especially in Europe. We [SOURCE: Page_5]

98    also lack data on the relative potential of different adaptation strategies, as the approach used [SOURCE: Page_5]

99    in existing reviews does not always allow for a comparison of different strategies (e.g. Abramoff [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   et al., 2023; Makowski et al., 2020). As adaptation can take many forms that differ in their [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   intent, spatial scale, timing, actors involved, and effort-to-benefit ratio (Iglesias et al., 2012; [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   Smit and Skinner, 2002), comparing several strategies may help plan an effective adaptation [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   to increase food systems resilience (Rosenzweig and Tubiello, 2007). [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   Altogether, these points highlight the need to synthesize recent simulation studies to answer [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   the following questions: (i) How will climate change impact grain legume performances in [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   Europe and how will this impact vary across regions and species? (ii) Which adaptation [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   strategies have been assessed and what is their potential to sustain grain legume [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   performances in the context of climate change? To that end, we systematically searched and [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   reviewed the literature on grain legume response to climate change in Europe, with and without [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   considering adaptation. [SOURCE: Page_5]

134            2.2. Study selection [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   Two collections of papers were brought together. The “impact” corpus consisted of simulation [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   studies that investigated the impact of climate change on grain legumes in Europe. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   “adaptation” corpus was composed of studies assessing how grain legumes respond to [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   adaptation strategies under future climate conditions. [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   For the “impact” corpus, the study area was limited to Europe, from ca. 35°N to 72°N and 15°W [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   to 40°E, excluding the Russian Federation. For the “adaptation” corpus, results in Europe were [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   too scarce to allow a comparison between several adaptation strategies. Therefore, the study [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   area was enlarged to all regions climatically similar to Europe at the global scale. Climatically [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   similar regions were identified using the Köppen-Geiger climate classification updated by [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   Kottek et al. (2006) and Rubel et al. (2017). The enlarged study area includes regions in [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   Northern America, Southern Brazil, Australia, Iran, Russia, and China (see Section 3.2.1). [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   To be selected in the “impact” corpus, a publication had to meet the following criteria: (i) study [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   one or several grain legume species; (ii) simulate the impact of future climate change on crop [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   performances using a climate scenario (Free-Air Concentration Enrichment experiments were [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   not considered in this review); (iii) provide data for Europe. Studies conducted at a global scale [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   were selected if it was possible to extract exploitable data for Europe. To facilitate comparison, [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   we considered only climate scenarios from IPCC assessment reports (AR$_4$ to AR$_6$) or the Half [SOURCE: Page_8]

152   a degree Additional warming, Prognosis and Projected Impacts initiative (HAPPI) project. [SOURCE: Page_8]

153   Studies assessing the sole impact of rising O$_3$ or CO$_2$ on crop performances were not included. [SOURCE: Page_8]

154   The indicators used to assess crop performances varied across studies. To facilitate [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   comparison and synthesis, we focused on the main indicators used in selected studies, namely [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   grain yield, crop production, and suitability index. Therefore, in this paper, the term “crop [SOURCE: Page_8]

157   performances” is used to refer to these indicators. Other features of interest such as harvested [SOURCE: Page_8]

158   biomass, water use efficiency, and biological nitrogen fixation (see Section 3.1.1 for the [SOURCE: Page_8]

159   complete list) are referred to as “other indicators”; their response to climate change or [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   adaptation is discussed but not included in figures. [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   To be selected in the “adaptation” corpus, a publication had to meet the following criteria: (i) [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   study one or several grain legume species; (ii) compare the impact of future climate change [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   on crop performances with and without adaptation; (iii) provide data for Europe or a climatically [SOURCE: Page_8]

164   similar area. Based on Lobell (2014), we defined adaptation as any action undertaken to [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   mitigate negative impacts or enhance positive impacts of climate change on crop [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   performances. We discarded papers in which the nature of the adaptation strategy simulated [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   could not be identified. [SOURCE: Page_8]

227            2.4. Data analysis [SOURCE: Page_11]

228   A spatial representation was chosen to illustrate the impact of climate change on crop [SOURCE: Page_11]

229   performances, highlight regional discrepancies, and identify areas where results diverge [SOURCE: Page_11]

230   between studies. To do so, we counted the number of papers that simulated a positive, neutral, [SOURCE: Page_11]

231   or negative impact of climate change per geographical area, without accounting for the [SOURCE: Page_11]

232   magnitude of change. Some papers provided more details than others (e.g. several [SOURCE: Page_11]

233   combinations of time periods, climate scenarios, and climate models), with results sometimes [SOURCE: Page_11]

234   diverging between the combinations tested. To account for these divergences without giving [SOURCE: Page_11]

235   too much weight to articles testing numerous combinations, an equal weight was given to each [SOURCE: Page_11]

236   article, as in White et al. (2011). For example, if three climate models were considered in a [SOURCE: Page_11]

237   paper, each climate model was given a weight of one third. A regional (NUTS$_1$) scale was [SOURCE: Page_11]

238   found a good compromise between local variability and data availability. When the spatial [SOURCE: Page_11]

239   resolution of simulations was too low, a national scale was used instead. A NUTS$_3$ scale was [SOURCE: Page_11]

240   used to represent site-based studies. [SOURCE: Page_11]

256                   3.1.1. Description of selected studies [SOURCE: Page_13]

257   Forty studies simulated the impact of climate change without adaptation on one or several [SOURCE: Page_13]

258   grain legumes in Europe. A large majority of them (80%) focused on soybeans, followed by [SOURCE: Page_13]

259   peas and beans (13% each) (Table 1). Only 45% of selected papers differentiated between [SOURCE: Page_13]

260   rainfed and irrigated conditions, while 35% considered a combination of both, and 20% failed [SOURCE: Page_13]

261   to specify the irrigation status. [SOURCE: Page_13]

262   Global scale studies were largely predominant (63% of papers) (Table 2a). The others were [SOURCE: Page_13]

263   almost equally distributed between European scale (10%), national or regional scale (15%), [SOURCE: Page_13]

264   and site-based studies (13%). Process-based models were more common (55%) than niche [SOURCE: Page_13]

265   models (25%) and statistical models (20%) (Table 2b). Future time horizons differed between [SOURCE: Page_13]

266   species (Figure 2a): for soybeans, a majority of studies focused on the far future, with two [SOURCE: Page_13]

267   peaks around 2050 and 2100, whereas for other pulses, the impact of climate change was [SOURCE: Page_13]

268   mainly assessed for the near future (before 2050). 55% of selected papers compared two or [SOURCE: Page_13]

269   more climate scenarios. RCP$_4$.5 (moderate warming) and RCP$_8$.5 (intense warming) were the [SOURCE: Page_13]

270   most studied scenarios, with 55% of selected papers using at least one of them (Table 2c). [SOURCE: Page_13]

271   Only a small number of papers considered crop response to elevated CO$_2$ (45% of the corpus) [SOURCE: Page_13]

272   or ozone (8%). Only five papers (13%) were found on the combined impact of climate change [SOURCE: Page_13]

273   and weeds or pests. Although the three soybean pests (the soybean stem fly M. sojae, the [SOURCE: Page_13]

274   southern armyworm S. eridania, and the red-banded stink bug P. guildinii) and two bean pests [SOURCE: Page_13]

275   studied (the Asian soybean rust P. pachyrhizi and the beet armyworm S. exigua) were not [SOURCE: Page_13]

13 [SOURCE: Page_13]

512   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_28]

513            4.1. The impact of climate change on future grain legume [SOURCE: Page_28]

514                 production remains uncertain in Europe [SOURCE: Page_28]

515   Conclusions on the impact of climate change mostly apply to soybeans, as very few data were [SOURCE: Page_28]

516   available for the other crops. For soybeans, we found a good agreement between studies, with [SOURCE: Page_28]

517   yield gains simulated in Northern Europe and a higher probability of yield losses in Southern [SOURCE: Page_28]

518   and South-Eastern Europe. This spatial pattern of climate change impact is consistent with [SOURCE: Page_28]

519   expectations from climate projections and crop physiology knowledge. Indeed, while climate [SOURCE: Page_28]

520   change is expected to lengthen crop growing seasons in the North, it may lead to faster crop [SOURCE: Page_28]

521   development in the South and increase the occurrence of stresses, eventually causing yield [SOURCE: Page_28]

522   losses (Osborne et al., 2013). A similar spatial pattern was found for wheat, another C$_3$ crop, [SOURCE: Page_28]

523   by Hristov et al. (2020). [SOURCE: Page_28]

524   These conclusions were relatively robust across climate scenarios, time periods, and types of [SOURCE: Page_28]

525   models. In some regions, the divergences between studies may be explained by differences [SOURCE: Page_28]

526   in model inputs (e.g. climate models and scenarios), structure, or parameters (Wallach and [SOURCE: Page_28]

527   Thorburn, 2017). Additional work is needed to quantify these sources of uncertainty, as it has [SOURCE: Page_28]

528   been made for major crops (Li et al., 2015; Rosenzweig et al., 2014). In particular, a major [SOURCE: Page_28]

529   source of uncertainty arises from models often neglecting the impact of CO$_2$ (considered in [SOURCE: Page_28]

530   only 36% of selected papers), ozone (4%), and abiotic stresses such as cold snap and [SOURCE: Page_28]

531   waterlogging (not assessed in this review). [SOURCE: Page_28]

532   Our analysis did not make it possible to assess whether yield gains in the North will [SOURCE: Page_28]

533   compensate for yield losses in current soybean production hotspots, mainly located in [SOURCE: Page_28]

534   Southern and Eastern Europe (Figure S$_2$). Indeed, the few results available were diverging. [SOURCE: Page_28]

535   While Guilpart et al. (2022) simulated a reduction of high-yielding production areas under [SOURCE: Page_28]

536   climate change, resulting in a decrease in the average soybean yield in Europe, Nendel et al. [SOURCE: Page_28]

28 [SOURCE: Page_28]

537   (2023) found an overall productivity gain. To assess how climate change will impact the [SOURCE: Page_29]

538   average grain legume yield and production at the European scale and identify future production [SOURCE: Page_29]

539   hotspots, we suggest that future simulations should provide absolute yield values instead of [SOURCE: Page_29]

540   relative values. Indeed, relative changes can easily reach very high values when the baseline [SOURCE: Page_29]

541   yield is low. Therefore, even with a strong positive impact of climate change in Northern [SOURCE: Page_29]

542   Europe, future soybean yields in these areas may not be attractive to farmers. [SOURCE: Page_29]

543            4.2. Adaptation strategies can mitigate the impact of climate [SOURCE: Page_29]

544                 change on grain legumes [SOURCE: Page_29]

545   This review showed that adaptation strategies have the potential to mitigate the negative [SOURCE: Page_29]

546   impacts of climate change or enhance its positive impacts. Yet, the strategies investigated [SOURCE: Page_29]

547   differed in their effects on yields. Overall, irrigation and crop management options resulted in [SOURCE: Page_29]

548   significant yield gains. A neutral or slightly negative effect on yields was frequently simulated [SOURCE: Page_29]

549   for alternative soil management and fertilization, which was not surprising given grain legume [SOURCE: Page_29]

550   reliance on biological nitrogen fixation (Liu et al., 2017). However, these options provided [SOURCE: Page_29]

551   secondary benefits, for example soil erosion prevention, which could motivate their adoption. [SOURCE: Page_29]

552   Our analysis revealed an imbalance between relatively well-studied strategies (e.g. irrigation [SOURCE: Page_29]

553   or change in sowing dates) and strategies whose potential remains to be examined (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_29]

554   intercropping). Combining different adaptation strategies also appears as a promising yet [SOURCE: Page_29]

555   underexplored strategy. In particular, we suggest water-saving soil management options [SOURCE: Page_29]

556   should be tested in combination with optimized irrigation systems, in order to sustain yields in [SOURCE: Page_29]

557   spite of a growing pressure on water resources. Engaging stakeholders in the co-design of [SOURCE: Page_29]

558   adaptation strategies would help identify relevant combinations and increase the scope of [SOURCE: Page_29]

559   strategies tested (Farrell et al., 2023; Tui et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_29]

560   Our assessment of the adaptation effect involves some limitations both from the method used [SOURCE: Page_29]

561   in the review and in selected papers. First, our work does not consider the dynamic evolution [SOURCE: Page_29]

29 [SOURCE: Page_29]

562   of adaptation efficiency. In particular, Lobell (2014) distinguishes between an “impact-neutral” [SOURCE: Page_30]

563   adaptation strategy (e.g. a strategy enhancing yields by 10% both in baseline and future), and [SOURCE: Page_30]

564   an “impact-reducing” strategy (e.g. a strategy enhancing yields by 10% in baseline climate and [SOURCE: Page_30]

565   20% in the future, and thus mitigating the impact of climate change on yields). Impact-reducing [SOURCE: Page_30]

566   strategies are likely to be the key to resilient and sustainable systems and should therefore [SOURCE: Page_30]

567   receive more attention than strategies whose efficiency decreases with time. However, in this [SOURCE: Page_30]

568   review, the effect of adaptation was quantified as the ratio between yield with and without [SOURCE: Page_30]

569   adaptation in the future, which did not allow us to identify impact-reducing strategies. Our [SOURCE: Page_30]

570   choice was mainly dictated by data availability, as only 36% of selected papers provided a [SOURCE: Page_30]

571   complete dataset including yields with and without adaptation for baseline climate. The few [SOURCE: Page_30]

572   results available sometimes diverged (Figure S12). In particular, irrigated soybeans benefitted [SOURCE: Page_30]

573   less from climate change than rainfed soybeans in several studies (Bao et al., 2015b; Ma et [SOURCE: Page_30]

574   al., 2021; Nendel et al., 2023), while contradictory results were found in others (Marković et [SOURCE: Page_30]

575   al., 2020; Timilsina et al., 2023). This issue needs further investigation in order to avoid [SOURCE: Page_30]

576   overestimating adaptation benefits from some strategies. [SOURCE: Page_30]

577   Second, the effect of adaptation was generally assessed independently of the technical and [SOURCE: Page_30]

578   economic feasibility of the strategy considered. In particular, 90% of studies assessing the [SOURCE: Page_30]

579   effect of irrigation did not estimate future water availability. It is also uncertain whether virtual [SOURCE: Page_30]

580   genotypes obtained by varying crop traits could realistically be developed in breeding [SOURCE: Page_30]

581   programs. Besides, studies generally overlooked the cost of adaptation. For strategies such [SOURCE: Page_30]

582   as irrigation and increased sowing density, this cost may outweigh the increase in crop [SOURCE: Page_30]

583   productivity (Elliott et al., 2014). Therefore, the evaluation of adaptation strategies would be [SOURCE: Page_30]

584   improved by the development of multi-criteria assessment methodologies including [SOURCE: Page_30]

585   stakeholders’ constraints and objectives (Naulleau et al., 2021, 2022). [SOURCE: Page_30]

30 [SOURCE: Page_30]

586            4.3. Knowledge gaps and future research avenues [SOURCE: Page_31]

587                      4.3.1. Grain legumes other than soybeans as blind spots [SOURCE: Page_31]

588   Our review highlighted a need for further European-scale modelling, especially for other grain [SOURCE: Page_31]

589   legumes than soybeans. In agreement with Magrini et al. (2019), we found a great imbalance [SOURCE: Page_31]

590   between soybeans and other grain legumes in the literature, with soybeans representing [SOURCE: Page_31]

591   approximately 80% of selected studies. The paucity of data for these crops contrasts with their [SOURCE: Page_31]

592   prominent positions in European environmental, agricultural, and nutritional policies. We [SOURCE: Page_31]

593   recommend prioritizing research on key species including peas, faba beans, lentils, and [SOURCE: Page_31]

594   chickpeas. To fully comprehend the impact of climate change on these crops, it seems [SOURCE: Page_31]

595   important to differentiate between spring and winter-sown cultivars, as well as rainfed and [SOURCE: Page_31]

596   irrigated crops. However, this differentiation was not consistently made in the papers selected [SOURCE: Page_31]

597   for this review. [SOURCE: Page_31]

598   Likewise, simulations at the European scale were scarce. Most data on climate change impact [SOURCE: Page_31]

599   originated from global-scale simulations whose coarse resolution masks a wide local [SOURCE: Page_31]

600   heterogeneity (Zhao et al., 2015). Enlarging the study area to climatically similar regions [SOURCE: Page_31]

601   allowed us to compensate for the lack of data in Europe. However, results transferability may [SOURCE: Page_31]

602   be limited by differences in non-climatic parameters such as soil and farm characteristics. [SOURCE: Page_31]

603   Additional work at the European scale seems necessary to support the development of grain [SOURCE: Page_31]

604   legumes, as targeted by European policies. [SOURCE: Page_31]

605                      4.3.2. Choice of the right time and spatial scales [SOURCE: Page_31]

606   The number of studies assessing the impact of climate change on soybeans in the far future [SOURCE: Page_31]

607   (second part of the XXIst century) contrasted with the paucity of results for the near future. [SOURCE: Page_31]

608   Identifying the timing of risks and key adaptations is necessary to ensure that simulated time [SOURCE: Page_31]

609   periods are not disconnected from stakeholders’ needs (Challinor et al., 2018). Therefore, we [SOURCE: Page_31]

610   suggest that farmers and other stakeholders should be included in the process of modelling in [SOURCE: Page_31]

31 [SOURCE: Page_31]

611   order to identify relevant inputs and outputs for future simulations (Naulleau et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_32]

612   Stakeholders may also guide an adequate choice of baseline period to avoid misestimating [SOURCE: Page_32]

613   the impact of climate change. [SOURCE: Page_32]

614   A reflection should also be undertaken on the choice of the spatial scale used in simulations. [SOURCE: Page_32]

615   The effect of some adaptation strategies (e.g. sowing date and cultivar choice) was found site- [SOURCE: Page_32]

616   specific, which points to the need for a local design of adaptation strategies. For other [SOURCE: Page_32]

617   strategies (e.g. shifting to species originating from other agricultural regions, switching grain [SOURCE: Page_32]

618   legume species), effect assessment may be more relevant at the European scale. [SOURCE: Page_32]

619   The right time and spatial scale will probably depend on the stakeholders. Therefore, multi- [SOURCE: Page_32]

620   scale modelling may be required, as advocated by Peng et al. (2020), who designed a [SOURCE: Page_32]

621   framework from gene to global scale. In the case of grain legumes, we suggest that such a [SOURCE: Page_32]

622   framework should also include crop sequence modelling, as benefits from legumes are [SOURCE: Page_32]

623   strongly dependent on their break-crop effects. Future analyses should also account for the [SOURCE: Page_32]

624   response of cropping mix to climate change. Indeed, the impact of climate change and the [SOURCE: Page_32]

625   effect of adaptation are crop-specific and may lead to changes in crop relative performances [SOURCE: Page_32]

626   and economic profitability. To our knowledge, no study has compared grain legume and cereal [SOURCE: Page_32]

627   response to climate change and adaptation. Further research should explore this issue to [SOURCE: Page_32]

628   investigate whether climate change could enhance or reduce grain legume attractiveness to [SOURCE: Page_32]

629   farmers compared to major crops or innovative minor candidate crops. [SOURCE: Page_32]

630                  4.3.3. A need to broaden the scope of estimated climate change impact [SOURCE: Page_32]

631   In our analysis, the impact of climate change and the effect of adaptation were mainly [SOURCE: Page_32]

632   quantified in terms of change in yield. However, yield is only one component of the lock-in [SOURCE: Page_32]

633   hindering the development of grain legumes in Europe (Magrini et al., 2016). Other indicators [SOURCE: Page_32]

634   of interest for the stakeholders were found poorly investigated, in particular the impact of [SOURCE: Page_32]

635   climate change on biotic stresses, yield stability, and services provided by grain legumes. [SOURCE: Page_32]

32 [SOURCE: Page_32]

636   Biotic stresses (weeds, pests, and pathogens) can lead to significant yield losses (Savary et [SOURCE: Page_33]

637   al., 2019) and are likely to be impacted by climate change. Indeed, with the global rise of [SOURCE: Page_33]

638   temperatures, some insects could establish in areas where their proliferation is currently limited [SOURCE: Page_33]

639   by cold temperatures (Bebber et al., 2013; Chaloner et al., 2021). Rising temperatures could [SOURCE: Page_33]

640   also lead to a shortening of reproductive cycles and an increased number of pest generations [SOURCE: Page_33]

641   during crop growing season, thus increasing the influence of pests on yield losses. Elevated [SOURCE: Page_33]

642   CO$_2$ could increase the relative competitiveness of C$_3$ weeds and make crops less nutritious [SOURCE: Page_33]

643   for insect pests, leading to increased damage (Olesen and Bindi, 2002). In our review, few [SOURCE: Page_33]

644   papers were found on the expected biotic pressure under climate change in Europe, although [SOURCE: Page_33]

645   it is likely that its importance should increase in the future. Most studies used suitability indexes [SOURCE: Page_33]

646   to assess the future overlap between areas suitable to crops and pests, but yield losses were [SOURCE: Page_33]

647   not estimated. Models simulating plant growth and insect life cycles have already been used [SOURCE: Page_33]

648   in other regions of the world to predict future insect damage (Taylor et al., 2018) and could [SOURCE: Page_33]

649   provide valuable information on potential biotic risks and adaptation strategies in Europe. [SOURCE: Page_33]

650   Farming systems are particularly sensitive to yield shocks (Hristov et al., 2020), and even [SOURCE: Page_33]

651   systems well-adapted to long-term trends will not necessarily be the most resilient against [SOURCE: Page_33]

652   extreme climate events (Rosenzweig and Tubiello, 2007). Therefore, we recommend that [SOURCE: Page_33]

653   projections of future yield variability or risks of crop failure should complement existing data on [SOURCE: Page_33]

654   future average yields. Assessing the impact of climate variability on yields will require [SOURCE: Page_33]

655   improving model calibration and representation of climate extremes and CO$_2$ effect, especially [SOURCE: Page_33]

656   for minor crops such as grain legumes (Kersebaum, 2022; Rötter et al., 2018). Designing [SOURCE: Page_33]

657   successful strategies to cope with climate variability may also require new approaches. For [SOURCE: Page_33]

658   example, instead of relying on future climate estimates, adaptation strategies could be [SOURCE: Page_33]

659   designed with the aim of being effective under a wide range of climate conditions (Corbeels et [SOURCE: Page_33]

660   al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_33]

661   Finally, as grain legumes are often grown for the N-related ecosystem services they provide, [SOURCE: Page_33]

662   we suggest that future simulations should investigate the impact of climate change on N [SOURCE: Page_33]

33 [SOURCE: Page_33]

663   fixation and provision. Non-yield benefits of adaptation strategies should also be assessed, as [SOURCE: Page_34]

664   well as potential interactions between adaptation and climate change mitigation. Given [SOURCE: Page_34]

665   Europe’s growing interest in grain legumes as high-quality protein crops, changes in grain [SOURCE: Page_34]

666   quality and protein content should be simulated to complement already existing experimental [SOURCE: Page_34]

667   data (Scheelbeek et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_34]

34 [SOURCE: Page_34]

668   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_35]

669   This systematic review provides an original synthesis of model-based studies simulating the [SOURCE: Page_35]

670   impact of climate change and the effect of adaptation on grain legume performances in Europe. [SOURCE: Page_35]

671   Overall, the positive impact of climate change on soybean yields in Northern Europe was [SOURCE: Page_35]

672   relatively consensual among studies, while yield losses may be expected in Southern and [SOURCE: Page_35]

673   Eastern Europe. Although the spatial pattern appeared similar to soybean, lack of data [SOURCE: Page_35]

674   prevented drawing a robust conclusion for other grain legumes at the European scale. [SOURCE: Page_35]

675   Irrigation, changes in sowing date, and cultivar choice were among the most promising [SOURCE: Page_35]

676   adaptation strategies, although authors seldom assessed their environmental desirability and [SOURCE: Page_35]

677   economic feasibility. Alternative soil management generally had a neutral or negative impact [SOURCE: Page_35]

678   on yields but provided secondary benefits, which could motivate its adoption. [SOURCE: Page_35]

679   The main knowledge gaps identified were a lack of data for other grain legumes than soybeans [SOURCE: Page_35]

680   and a need for more Europe-focused studies, especially for adaptation effect assessment. [SOURCE: Page_35]

681   Modelling the impact of climate change and adaptation remains an open research avenue for [SOURCE: Page_35]

682   key crops such as field peas and faba beans. Only a few studies considered crop response to [SOURCE: Page_35]

683   elevated CO$_2$, ozone, and biotic pressure. Therefore, incorporating these factors would [SOURCE: Page_35]

684   enhance climate change impact assessment. We also suggest that future simulations should [SOURCE: Page_35]

685   broaden the range of adaptation tested (e.g. intercropping, choice of the grain legume species, [SOURCE: Page_35]

686   combinations of several adaptation strategies) and indicators assessed (e.g. economic [SOURCE: Page_35]

687   indicators, yield variability), in the frame of a multi-criteria analysis. [SOURCE: Page_35]

688   Altogether, these points highlight a research focus on just a few aspects of climate change and [SOURCE: Page_35]

689   adaptation, leaving in the dark important issues and challenges for stakeholders. Involving [SOURCE: Page_35]

690   stakeholders would help orient future modelling, in order to provide relevant output to inform [SOURCE: Page_35]

691   adaptation, within the scope of a use-oriented approach. [SOURCE: Page_35]
