15   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   Mediterranean piedmonts are an important hydro-agricultural systems. They constitute the junction [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   between the mountains, where the streamflow is generated, and the surrounding plains, where the water [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   is used. In Morocco, these traditional systems extend largely along the High Atlas Mountains. Yet, [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   changing conditions in the Mediterranean basin as well as recurrent droughts in recent decades remain [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   poorly understood in terms of hydrological and agricultural impacts, particularly in traditional hydro- [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   agro-systems. The combined effects of climate variability and ineffective management of water resource [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   dynamics may lead to increased water scarcity in these regions. The present work aims to assess the [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   effects of climate variability and associated agricultural changes on water resources in a traditional [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   irrigated piedmont of the Moroccan High Atlas. To that end, a trend analysis, together with change [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   points detection, was carried out on annual and monthly precipitation, and streamflow from 1965 to [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   2018. Then, the standardized precipitation index (SPI) was employed to identify meteorological [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   droughts. Also, groundwater, and spring discharge data were analyzed and discussed from 1973 to 2021. [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   SPI outcomes revealed three major droughts, in 1981-1988, 1999-2008, and 2013-2018. Although the [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   precipitation data showed no significant trend, except for Tahannaout station, the average annual [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   precipitation over the piedmont area decreased by 28%. Similarly, streamflow decreased significantly [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   by almost 40% for all stations, as did the Abainou spring's discharge. Consistent with that, groundwater [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   level has declined dramatically over the past decades in the downstream piedmont. These decreases in [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   water cycle components were tightly aligned with droughts. Yet, irrigation diversions were maintained [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   in both dry and wet periods. Paradoxically, this decrease in water resources was associated with an [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   agricultural transition from seasonal crops (cereals) to perennial crops (olive trees). This conversion is [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   likely to amplify the water shortage, leading to groundwater resources overexploitation to satisfy the [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   growing agricultural demand. [SOURCE: Page_3]

38    Keywords: Mediterranean region, precipitation, streamflow, groundwater, agriculture, trends [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   analysis. [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   1 Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

41    The world's population has almost doubled from 1990 to the present, from 4.9 to 7.8 billion [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   people (U.S.Census Bureau, 2019). Representing roughly 6.8 % of the world's population, the [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   Mediterranean region was home to approximately 537 million inhabitants in 2015 with [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   projections to reach 670 million by 2050; of whom more than 250 million are distributed in [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   hydrological basins (World Bank, 2015; FAO 2015). Consequently, food and water demands [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   have increased in the Mediterranean region, putting higher pressure on water resources [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   (Godfray et al., 2010; Lopes, et al., 2020) and soils (Karamesouti et al., 2015), especially in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   east and southern parts, the most water-scarce areas (Daccache et al., 2014; Mekonnen and [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   Hoekstra 2016; United Nations 2017; Tabari, 2020). Moreover, the Mediterranean basin is [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   identified as a hotspot of climate change (Giorgi, 2006; Diffenbaugh and Giorgi, 2012) with a [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   foreseen increase in temperature, decline in precipitation, and an intensification of the number [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   and intensity of extreme events in terms of floods and droughts, as well as a change in the [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   seasonal distribution of rainfall (Schilling et al., 2012; EEA, 2012; Tramblay et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_4]

54    Mountains around the Mediterranean are the water towers for the adjacent lowland basins [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   (Jarlan et al., 2015, 2016). these mountains supply water for irrigation and other water needs in [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   piedmonts and downstream plains (Viviroli et al., 2007, 2011; Immerzeel et al, 2010). In a [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   recent study, Viviroli et al. (2020) showed that almost 1.5 billion people would depend on [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   runoff contributions from mountain areas by the mid-twenty-first century. However, mountains [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   are also sensitive systems to climatic variability (Parry et al., 2007; Solomon et al., 2007; [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   Viviroli et al., 2011; Cuni-Sanchez et al., 2016; Immerzeel et al., 2020). In semiarid basins, the [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   plains are characterized by low precipitation, high evapotranspiration, and deep unsaturated [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   zones. However, the piedmont areas at the interface between mountains and plain benefit from [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   milder climatic conditions and water availability from upstream runoff that have made them [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   suitable locations for the settlement of rural communities and their farming activities (Van [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   Steenbergen et al., 2011; Bresciani et al., 2018; Cody, 2018). Water availability in the piedmont [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   areas is closely related to the rainfall and snowmelt in the surrounding mountains that feed [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   streamflow and groundwater from winter to early summer. Those conditions have led to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   development of traditional irrigation in these piedmont areas. [SOURCE: Page_4]

69    Piedmont traditional irrigation systems use networks of gravity-fed surface irrigation earth [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   channels that divert floodwater from the river to supply irrigation from the piedmont to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   plain. The traditional irrigation system is mostly based on redistributing water from streams and [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   expanding the water flooded areas. In addition to satisfying the agricultural water requirements, [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   this practice promotes groundwater recharge (Bouimouass et al., 2020). Recent studies have [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   shown that it has a positive impact on groundwater quality through irrigation returns from high- [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   quality snowmelt-fed streamflow (Bouimouass et al., 2022, Rotiroti et al., 2019). Conversely, [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   modern irrigation uses water from dams, and a distribution network of concrete channels and [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   relies more on groundwater abstraction. This system uses also drippers that deliver water [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   directly to the plant's root zone, losing any additional groundwater recharge supplies, thus [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   generally inducing negative effects on groundwater quantity and quality (Scanlon et al., 2007; [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   Bouimouass et al., 2022). The so-called “spate irrigation” is one of the oldest traditional [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   irrigation systems widely used all over the world. It is found especially in the Middle East, [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   North Africa, West Asia, East Africa, Europe, and parts of Latin America (Van Steenbergen et [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   al. 2011; Fernald et al., 2015; Leibundgut & Kohn, 2014). However, this irrigation system [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   underwent a considerable decline in the second half of the 20th century (Leibundgut & Kohn, [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   2014b). This system has been in practical use in very different and contrasted environmental [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   regions around the globe (Fernald et al., 2015; Leibundgut & Kohn, 2014; Mehari et al., 2007; [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   Tesfai, & Stroosnijder, 2001). For instance, in the semi-arid upper Rio Grande of the United [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   States, Fernald et al. (2015) reported that community irrigation systems, known as Acequia, [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   which is derived from the word seguia in Arabic meaning an irrigation channel, have been [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   governed by users to divert water from streams to irrigate northern New Mexico's agriculture [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   for centuries. In the northern side of High Atlas in Morocco, traditional irrigation uses not only [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   floods (like spate irrigation), but also the baseflow as long as the wadis might flow during [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   several months, especially thanks to snowmelt (Bouimouass et al., 2020). It is practiced through [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   a secular network of irrigation channels locally named seguias. During the 20th century, the [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   main agricultural plains were converted to modern agriculture (Le Page et al., 2021), irrigated [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   by dams, concrete canals networks, and wells, thus making the traditional irrigation systems a [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   typical feature of the High Atlas piedmonts. [SOURCE: Page_5]

98    In a context characterized by droughts, water scarcity, and increasing water demand, [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   particularly in the semi-arid Haouz-Tensift basin, it is necessary to know how those hydro-agro- [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   systems are evolving in terms of agricultural activities and hydrological behavior. Towards the [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   end of the century, average winter temperatures in the Moroccan High-Atlas are expected to [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   increase by 2 to 4°C simultaneously with a 25% to 60% reduction in precipitations (Tuel et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   2020b; Hadri et al., 2020; Ayt Ougougdal et al., 2020; Driouech et al., 2020). Therefore, recent [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   studies based on hydrological models have pointed to a potential decrease in water resources [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   for the southern Mediterranean region (Prudhomme et al., 2014; Marchane et al., 2016; Tuel et [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   al., 2020a). Snow constitutes a significant part of the precipitation over the High Atlas [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   Mountains (Hajhouji et al. 2018; Tuel et al., 2020a). Snowmelt contribute substantially to [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   streamflow during spring, ranging from 7 to 50% depending on the year (Boudhar et al., 2009), [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   providing water for irrigation. Temperature rise may result in the degradation of the snowpack [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   and rapid snowmelt inducing changes in the wadis hydrological regimes (Lopez-Moreno et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   2004, 2017; Tramblay et al., 2018), threatening irrigation period. Additionally, an overarching [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   program, the Green Morocco Plan (GMP), was launched by the government to develop the [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   agricultural sector while preserving water resources by promoting a large expansion of drip [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   irrigation. However, the promised water savings allowed by this technology have been [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   challenged by evidence from the field. Indeed, the benefits obtained by increased productivity [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   and farmers’ incomes, and the shift to high-value crops have been accompanied by a greater [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   pressure on groundwater exacerbating its depletion (Venot et al., 2017). Therefore, assessing [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   the evolution of water resources and agriculture in a semi-arid context is a prerequisite for a [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   prospective analysis of their evolution which will help to guide public policies toward [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   sustainable management of water resources. [SOURCE: Page_6]

121      The present study aims firstly to assess the hydrological variability and water resource trends [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   in the northern piedmont of the High-Atlas range from 1965 to 2020. Then the evolution of [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   agricultural land use is analyzed from 1984 to 2020, with a focus on changes affecting the [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   expansion of trees. The challenges that traditional irrigation systems in the piedmont face, as [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   well as their sustainability issues are then discussed. Also, the study takes into account the [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   spatial heterogeneity of the central High-Atlas piedmont, which is divided into three subareas [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   with distinct characteristics (Fig. 1): the Central Haouz, which has high surface water [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   availability as traversed by the basin's main wadis, the Mejjate, where groundwater is almost [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   the only available water resource, and the Chichaoua valley, which is irrigated by the region's [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   largest spring. [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   2 Study area [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   2.1   Water resources [SOURCE: Page_7]

133    The northern piedmont of the High Atlas, covering approximately 4400 km^2, is located in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   Tensift watershed, in central Morocco. It is a transitional area between the High-Atlas [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   Mountains (culminating at 4167 m.a.s.l.) and the Haouz plain ranging between 300 and 600 [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   m.a.s.l. (Fig. 1). This piedmont is fed by intermittent and ephemeral wadis issued from the [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   mountains, but it is also crossed by larger wadis with mainly permanent streamflow, except [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   during some drought summers. From East to West these wadis are the Rdat, Zat, Ourika, [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   Rhiraya, N'Fis, Assif El Mal, and Seksaoua. During exceptional rainfall events, these wadis [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   may flow until the Tensift wadi at the north of the Haouz plain. The streams of the Eastern [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   piedmont have higher mean streamflow rates: Ourika 4.62 m^3s-1, Zat 3.33 m^3s-1, Rheraya 1.60 [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   m^3s-1, and Rdat 2.34 m^3s-1. The peaking point of runoff occurs during the springtime, around [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   April, as a result of snowmelt (Boudhar et al., 2009; Marchane et al., 2017; Hajhouji et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   2018). The average contribution of snowmelt to annual runoff represents around 19%, 36%, [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   41%, 22%, and 7% for N’Fis, Rheraya, Ourika, Zat, and Rdat basins respectively (Boudhar et [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   al., 2009). The wadis in the western piedmont have a pluvial runoff regime and lower average [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   flow: Seksaoua 0.65 m^3s-1. Groundwater is contained in a large Plio-Quaternary alluvial aquifer, [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   which extends continuously over the piedmont and the Haouz plain. Groundwater flows from [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   the piedmont to the plain. It is withdrawn by wells and boreholes for drinking water of rural [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   population and irrigation in dry seasons. Spring discharge is uncommon in the area, and only a [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   few springs have measurable flow rates. The largest one is Abainou which irrigates the [SOURCE: Page_8]

152   Chichaoua valley. [SOURCE: Page_8]

153   2.2   Agriculture and Irrigation [SOURCE: Page_8]

154    Traditional family agriculture is still the main activity in the piedmont area, consisting mainly [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   of olive trees and winter cereals (wheat and barley). Most of the olive orchards are associated [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   with an understory of annual crops, mainly cereals or forage, in order to optimize water use. [SOURCE: Page_8]

157   Local farmers use an extensive network of seguias, to divert water from the streams (Fig.2). [SOURCE: Page_8]

158   Each irrigation system is managed by local Water Users Associations (WUAs) jointly with the [SOURCE: Page_8]

159   Office Regional de Mise en Valeur Agricole du Haouz (ORMVAH) which ensures the [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   distribution of streamflow between seguias with respect of the water rights where the principle [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   of upstream priority of water use is generally adopted (Kharrou et al., 2021). The same system [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   is applied to Abainou spring, located in the valley of Chichaoua (Fig. 1). [SOURCE: Page_8]

200   3.2     Landsat imagery [SOURCE: Page_11]

201    To characterize the agricultural land use evolution, a time series of classifications based on [SOURCE: Page_11]

202   multispectral Landsat imagery was achieved. Crop classification was performed using a [SOURCE: Page_11]

203   decision tree (DT) approach based on expert rules. DT can be trained rapidly and executed [SOURCE: Page_11]

204   quickly (Gahegan & West, 1998). It performs well for land use mapping (Yang et al., 2003; [SOURCE: Page_11]

205   Waheed et al., 2006; Otukei, & Blaschke, 2010; Jiang et al., 2011; Sharma et al., 2013), [SOURCE: Page_11]

206   especially when phenological data or vegetation index threshold values are incorporated [SOURCE: Page_12]

207   (Wardlow et al., 2007; Simonneaux et al., 2008b; Masialeti et al., 2010; Ozdogan, 2010). To [SOURCE: Page_12]

208   that end, all available cloud-free Landsat images corrected in surface reflectance from 1984 to [SOURCE: Page_12]

209   2020 were downloaded from the USGS (the United States Geological Survey) website, [SOURCE: Page_12]

210   including Landsat 5 (TM), Landsat 7 (ETM+), and Landsat 8 (OLI). [SOURCE: Page_12]

211   3.3   Methods [SOURCE: Page_12]

212      3.3.1 Statistical analysis of hydro-meteorological time series [SOURCE: Page_12]

213    The non-parametric statistical test of Mann-Kendall (MK) (Mann 1945, Kendall 1975) and [SOURCE: Page_12]

214   Sen’s slope estimator (Sen, 1968) was performed on precipitation and streamflow time series [SOURCE: Page_12]

215   to assess the presence or absence of a clear trend in the long-term evolution of water resources [SOURCE: Page_12]

216   within the High Atlas piedmont. In addition, the Pettitt (1979) test was used to determine the [SOURCE: Page_12]

217   trend change points and check the homogeneity of the data. Also, the meteorological droughts [SOURCE: Page_12]

218   over the piedmont were assessed by the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI). SPI was [SOURCE: Page_12]

219   developed by McKee et al. (1993) and Edwards and McKee (1997) and recommended by the [SOURCE: Page_12]

220   World Meteorological Organization (Hayes et al., 2011; WMO, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_12]

221      3.3.2 Mapping agricultural land use [SOURCE: Page_12]

222   In traditional systems such as the High-Atlas piedmont, the use of Landsat imagery for [SOURCE: Page_12]

223   monitoring agricultural activities is quite challenging because of the predominance of small [SOURCE: Page_12]

224   agricultural fields of less than one hectare, as compared with the 30-meter resolution of Landsat. [SOURCE: Page_12]

225   However, the simplicity of the land cover, consisting of three classes, and the fact that they are [SOURCE: Page_12]

226   often aggregated in homogeneous patches, limits the problem of confusion. Another problem [SOURCE: Page_12]

227   is that historical Landsat data was not always acquired every 16 days and the occurrence of [SOURCE: Page_12]

228   cloud cover, especially during the crop development phases of winter crops, reduces the [SOURCE: Page_12]

229   availability of usable images. An additional key limitation was the lack of ground data to be [SOURCE: Page_13]

230   able to train the classifier for past years. Thus, in order to be able to build a land cover time [SOURCE: Page_13]

231   series for the period 1984-2020, we built a simple decision tree algorithm based on the temporal [SOURCE: Page_13]

232   signature of the crops using a limited number of key periods for which the land cover classes [SOURCE: Page_13]

233   have contrasted behaviors. Indeed, there are usually not enough clear Landsat images to be able [SOURCE: Page_13]

234   to build a temporal profile of vegetation development that would be required to apply a decision [SOURCE: Page_13]

235   tree approach as proposed by (Simonneaux and Francois, 2003; Simonneaux et al., 2008b). [SOURCE: Page_13]

236   Instead, for most of the years, we were able to find images distributed over the agricultural [SOURCE: Page_13]

237   season (February, April, June, and August). [SOURCE: Page_13]

238     The olive tree is an evergreen species and exhibits vegetation all along the year, which means [SOURCE: Page_13]

239   an NDVI value (Normalize Difference Vegetation Index) is always higher than the ones of bare [SOURCE: Page_13]

240   soils. Cereal crops are characterized by the first period of bare soil around December/ early [SOURCE: Page_13]

241   January, then a peak of vegetation around March, and another phase of bare soil after harvest. [SOURCE: Page_13]

242   Based on these simple rules, a decision tree classifier has been built based on images selected [SOURCE: Page_13]

243   at four periods (B1= February, B2 = April, B3 = July, and B4 = September) (Fig. 3). [SOURCE: Page_13]

398   5 Discussion [SOURCE: Page_27]

399   5.1   Temporal and spatial changes in water resources [SOURCE: Page_27]

400      Even though the piedmont areas benefited from water availability favorably than the plain, [SOURCE: Page_27]

401   all the analyzed hydrological variables have shown a decreasing trend over the past 50 years. [SOURCE: Page_27]

402   Therefore, the major hydrological changes were revealed since the 1980s. The precipitation has [SOURCE: Page_27]

403   declined by 28% (Fig.4a, 5b). Recently Ouatiki et al. (2019) pointed out the same general [SOURCE: Page_27]

404   decreasing tendency in rainfall over a neighboring basin, the Oum Er-Rbia basin. In addition, a [SOURCE: Page_27]

405   decreasing trend in precipitation for most Mediterranean countries was revealed by Philandras [SOURCE: Page_27]

406   et al. (2011) through a detailed analysis of data from 40 meteorological stations between 1900 [SOURCE: Page_27]

407   and 2009. Also, three main meteorological droughts were delineated: 1981-1988, 1999-2008, [SOURCE: Page_27]

408   and 2013-2018 (Fig.5a). The longer and more severe one is between 1999 and 2008, which has [SOURCE: Page_27]

409   also been reported in various countries of the Mediterranean basin (Cook et al., 2016; Hanel et [SOURCE: Page_27]

410   al., 2018). Additionally, streamflow has declined by almost 40% (Fig.6). These findings are [SOURCE: Page_27]

411   consistent with the recent study by Masseroni et al. (2020) who analyzed runoff data between [SOURCE: Page_27]

412   1965 and 2015 from more than 3,000 stations over the Mediterranean region. They found that [SOURCE: Page_27]

413   the declining trend of annual streamflow started in 1965, and by 1980 the volumes were [SOURCE: Page_27]

414   systematically below the average. The overall decrease in runoff resulted in a decrease in [SOURCE: Page_27]

415   diverted volume of streamflow to irrigation. Despite that, the diversion ratios are high in both [SOURCE: Page_27]

416   dry and wet periods, ranging from 70 % up to 80 %. They highlighted the great importance of [SOURCE: Page_27]

417   streamflow-based irrigation to local farmers in a semi-arid context and their strong adaptation [SOURCE: Page_27]

418   capacity to natural variations, as the area covered by cereals is strongly related to rainfall and [SOURCE: Page_27]

419   runoff. [SOURCE: Page_27]

420    Concerning the groundwater, except for the upstream areas benefiting from consistent [SOURCE: Page_27]

421   recharge by irrigation returns (Bouimouass et al., 2020), a severe decrease is observed in [SOURCE: Page_27]

422   piezometric levels elsewhere since the early 2000s. The observed depletion of the piezometric [SOURCE: Page_28]

423   level occurs over the downstream area at a rate of 2m.y-1 in Chouiter, while in Mejjate it was [SOURCE: Page_28]

424   around 1.5 m.y-1 between 2002 and 2016 and is close to 5m.y-1 since 2016. These are in line [SOURCE: Page_28]

425   with other studies highlighted rates, around 1-3 m/year on average (Fakir et al., 2015). The [SOURCE: Page_28]

426   decline of groundwater level is attributed to recurrent drought periods and intensive [SOURCE: Page_28]

427   groundwater pumping (Boukhari et al. 2015). Pump wells were introduced in the last century [SOURCE: Page_28]

428   firstly in the Haouz plain by the French colonization. Those wells have spread in the area and [SOURCE: Page_28]

429   currently, almost every farm has at least one well dedicated to irrigation during the dry season [SOURCE: Page_28]

430   and in some cases used for drinking and domestic needs. Unfortunately, there are no records of [SOURCE: Page_28]

431   the volumes being pumped or accurate statistics on the number of boreholes since most are dug [SOURCE: Page_28]

432   illegally in the region. Additionally, the Abainou spring, which provides water to irrigate [SOURCE: Page_28]

433   agriculture in Chichaoua valley, has also been declining since the 1980s, jeopardizing its socio- [SOURCE: Page_28]

434   economic importance and increasing the risks of its drying up (Fig. 7, c). [SOURCE: Page_28]

435   5.2   Agricultural land use dynamic [SOURCE: Page_28]

436   The evolution of annual crops (mainly cereals) extent was highly correlated to rainfall. The [SOURCE: Page_28]

437   decreasing trend in rainfall and droughts resulted in a lower extent and production of rainfed [SOURCE: Page_28]

438   cereals, which is considered critical for food security in Morocco (Schilling et al., 2012, Karrou [SOURCE: Page_28]

439   et al., 2012) and rural population incomes. This is a common issue in the semi-arid regions of [SOURCE: Page_28]

440   the southern Mediterranean, where about 80% of agricultural land is rainfed, therefore highly [SOURCE: Page_28]

441   vulnerable to droughts (Schilling et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_28]

442   The past distribution of trees in the studied High Atlas piedmont showed a clear spatial [SOURCE: Page_28]

443   variation. In the eastern piedmont of Central Haouz, the olive trees extended mainly along the [SOURCE: Page_28]

444   main wadis; the water availability has encouraged the settlement of rural communities that [SOURCE: Page_28]

445   logically led to the development of irrigated agriculture (Awulachew et al., 2007). The western [SOURCE: Page_28]

446   piedmont of Mejjate was known for rainfed seasonal agriculture. Over the last 36 years, the [SOURCE: Page_29]

447   interannual evolution of tree crops across the High-Atlas piedmont showed two highly disparate [SOURCE: Page_29]

448   trends. During a long period 1984-2013 (29 years), the expansion of trees remained near stable [SOURCE: Page_29]

449   over the area, with 2 slight variations: an increase in 1995-1999 and a slight decrease in 2000- [SOURCE: Page_29]

450   2007, which coincided respectively with a wet period and a dry period. Therefore, the [SOURCE: Page_29]

451   development of trees during this long period seems to be controlled by climatic drivers. [SOURCE: Page_29]

452   However, during the last 7 years, 2013-2020, the expansion of trees was spectacular (Fig.9). [SOURCE: Page_29]

453   Paradoxically, this rapid expansion is occurring in the context of severe droughts and could not [SOURCE: Page_29]

454   be explained by climate variability. In fact, in recent years several governmental support [SOURCE: Page_29]

455   programs were launched aiming at the development and modernization of the agricultural sector [SOURCE: Page_29]

456   (Faysse, 2015; Agricultural Development Agency. 2012, 2014). Particularly, the Green [SOURCE: Page_29]

457   Morocco Plan (GMP) launched in 2008 aimed to make agriculture a pillar of the socio- [SOURCE: Page_29]

458   economic development in Morocco through the development of modern agriculture with high [SOURCE: Page_29]

459   added value both in the irrigated and favorable rainfed areas; and the enhancement of [SOURCE: Page_29]

460   agricultural income in the most vulnerable areas (ADA, 2018). The GMP helped increase the [SOURCE: Page_29]

461   production of high-value perennial crops, such as citrus and olive trees, mostly by expanding [SOURCE: Page_29]

462   cultivated areas thanks to the subsidies awarded (Akesbi, 2013). In addition, the National [SOURCE: Page_29]

463   Irrigation Water-Saving Program (PNEEI), which is a major component of the GMP, was [SOURCE: Page_29]

464   implemented to ensure the rational and efficient use of irrigation water resources by promoting [SOURCE: Page_29]

465   the development of drip irrigation technology (Faysse, 2015; Faysse, and Simon, 2014 Akesbi, [SOURCE: Page_29]

466   2013). However, these measures have conversely favored the expansion of irrigated agriculture [SOURCE: Page_29]

467   to traditionally rainfed or uncropped areas (Jaramillo et al., 2020; Molle, 2017), relying on [SOURCE: Page_29]

468   groundwater withdrawals, which has subsequently led to increased water demand and [SOURCE: Page_29]

469   groundwater overexploitation (Fig. 3 S). [SOURCE: Page_29]

470   5.3   Sustainability of piedmonts’ traditional hydro-agro-systems [SOURCE: Page_30]

471    The piedmont of the High-Atlas Mountains has been sheltered for centuries and still adopt [SOURCE: Page_30]

472   over a large area a traditional irrigation system. The latter use large networks of gravity-fed [SOURCE: Page_30]

473   surface irrigation channels that divert the streamflow (baseflow and floods) at the outlet of the [SOURCE: Page_30]

474   Mountain ranges (Bouimouass et al., 2020). A key factor of survival for such an ancestral [SOURCE: Page_30]

475   system is the sense of mutualism between the seguias users, which is defined by Gunda et al [SOURCE: Page_30]

476   (2018) as the feeling of collective well-being including social identity, pride of place, and [SOURCE: Page_30]

477   maintenance of historical traditions. This mutualism has not excluded conflicts that have been [SOURCE: Page_30]

478   raised during periods of severe droughts. These hydro-agro-systems are also characterized by [SOURCE: Page_30]

479   great flexibility to water resources fluctuations. [SOURCE: Page_30]

480    During the last years, the planting of trees has been performed at a rapid pace, mainly driven [SOURCE: Page_30]

481   by socio-economic incentives under conditions of droughts and water scarcity. The trees [SOURCE: Page_30]

482   expansion is creating an increasing and rigid water demand that should be satisfied whatever [SOURCE: Page_30]

483   the conditions of climate and water resources. Thus, the observed decrease in precipitation and [SOURCE: Page_30]

484   streamflow would change the traditional irrigation in the piedmont from a surface water-based [SOURCE: Page_30]

485   irrigation to a groundwater-based one. This is already the case in the Mejjate area (western [SOURCE: Page_30]

486   piedmont) where the observed expansion of trees is associated with a decrease in groundwater [SOURCE: Page_30]

487   level. The same situation is spotted in the downstream limit of the central Haouz piedmont [SOURCE: Page_30]

488   receiving less irrigation water, only during flood events. Also, the irrigation in the Chichaoua [SOURCE: Page_30]

489   valley by the Abainou spring, whose discharge has been continuously reducing, is undergoing [SOURCE: Page_30]

490   the same evolution. The consequences of the shifting from surface water-driven irrigation to [SOURCE: Page_30]

491   groundwater-based irrigation are a severe depletion of groundwater, especially in the [SOURCE: Page_30]

492   downstream areas. Beyond the question of the durability of this type of agriculture, it raises the [SOURCE: Page_30]

493   question of the impact on the aquifer recharge downstream. In fact, in the Haouz plain, a [SOURCE: Page_30]

494   dramatic groundwater depletion of 1 to 3 m year-1 in average is observed since the 1990s [SOURCE: Page_30]

495   (Boukhari et al., 2015; Fakir et al., 2015). Although the depletion of the piezometric level [SOURCE: Page_31]

496   observed in these areas is due to extensive pumping by the plain agriculture itself, it may be [SOURCE: Page_31]

497   exacerbated by the less recharge from the piedmont that is subject to increasing groundwater [SOURCE: Page_31]

498   abstraction. [SOURCE: Page_31]

499    Indeed, this agricultural intensification is not only occurring in Morocco but also on a broader [SOURCE: Page_31]

500   scale. Worldwide, the equipped areas for irrigated agriculture increased from 289 to 342 million [SOURCE: Page_31]

501   hectares between 2000 and 2020, with nearly 38% being supplied by groundwater-based [SOURCE: Page_31]

502   irrigation (Crumpler et al., 2021; Siebert et al., 2010). Furthermore, by employing the PCR- [SOURCE: Page_31]

503   GLOBWB hydrological model for gross crop requirements for irrigation and the available water [SOURCE: Page_31]

504   to meet that demand, Wada et al. (2012) revealed that the use of non-renewable groundwater [SOURCE: Page_31]

505   withdrawals for irrigation is highest in India, followed by Pakistan, the United States, Iran, [SOURCE: Page_31]

506   China, Mexico, and Saudi Arabia. Their findings also suggested that globally this contribution [SOURCE: Page_31]

507   has been tripled over the period 1960-2000. Now, there is no doubt that India, a country with a [SOURCE: Page_31]

508   similar context as Morocco, has the largest extent of irrigated areas. Despite a low increase of [SOURCE: Page_31]

509   irrigated area from 58.8 % to 60.4 % between 1961 to 2016 (World Bank, 2017; Siebert et al., [SOURCE: Page_31]

510   2010), an intensification occurred, triggered by government irrigation facilities, cheap [SOURCE: Page_31]

511   electricity for pumping, and a diversified crop basket since the 1970s (Dangar et al., 2021; Paria [SOURCE: Page_31]

512   et al., 2021). The cropping intensity was also driven by the use of chemical fertilizers, and a [SOURCE: Page_31]

513   rapid rise in the number of electric and diesel pumps to reach higher yields (Paria et al., 2021; [SOURCE: Page_31]

514   Mishra et al., 2018; Shah, 2010). In such a context, the direct reliance on groundwater [SOURCE: Page_31]

515   abstraction to meet the growing irrigation water demand exacerbates groundwater resource [SOURCE: Page_31]

516   depletion, even in areas with abundant rainfall (Dangar et al., 2021; Zaveri et al., 2016; Rodell [SOURCE: Page_31]

517   et al., 2009; Gandhi and Namboodiri, 2009). [SOURCE: Page_31]

518    This issue has also been highlighted in the northern side of the Mediterranean basin (FAO [SOURCE: Page_31]

519   2016). In Spain, for instance, agricultural policies have supported irrigated farming to increase [SOURCE: Page_31]

520   food production and save water. Over the last three decades, the promotion of drip irrigation [SOURCE: Page_32]

521   over surface irrigation had several consequences. Wells construction has intensified, [SOURCE: Page_32]

522   redistributing access to groundwater and causing a significant lowering of the water table. In [SOURCE: Page_32]

523   addition, the expansion of irrigation in the piedmont and Mountain areas, previously rain-fed, [SOURCE: Page_32]

524   resulted in an increase in water consumption from 4% to 45% per hectare. There was also a [SOURCE: Page_32]

525   shift to higher-value fruit crops with higher water requirements (González-Cebollada, 2015). [SOURCE: Page_32]

526   and finally, the removal of traditional surface irrigation infrastructure dating back to the Middle [SOURCE: Page_32]

527   Ages (Sese-Minguez et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_32]

528    The socioeconomic and the hydrological functions of the traditional irrigation piedmont as a [SOURCE: Page_32]

529   secular hydro-agro-system that was sustained for tens of centuries through a sound connection [SOURCE: Page_32]

530   between natural and human elements to ensure food security, water balance, groundwater [SOURCE: Page_32]

531   recharge, and droughts survival, are being altered. Their sustainability is currently of great [SOURCE: Page_32]

532   concern and their degradation would be accelerated by climate change, particularly through [SOURCE: Page_32]

533   decreased precipitation (Polade et al., 2017; Barcikowska et al., 2018), increased [SOURCE: Page_32]

534   evapotranspiration (Le Page et al., 2021), and reduced groundwater recharge (Crosbie et al., [SOURCE: Page_32]

535   2013; Wu et al., 2020). Given that the traditional irrigation in the piedmont of the High-Atlas [SOURCE: Page_32]

536   Mountains in Haouz region has been identified as the main source of groundwater recharge [SOURCE: Page_32]

537   (Bouimouass et al., 2020), the preservation of this vulnerable hydro-agro-system should be [SOURCE: Page_32]

538   considered in the agricultural and socio-economic development policies. Particularly by [SOURCE: Page_32]

539   limiting the expansion of the irrigated agriculture over the Haouz plain which threatens the [SOURCE: Page_32]

540   groundwater resources sustainability. Additionally, the government agricultural support should [SOURCE: Page_32]

541   be revised in a such vulnerable hydro-agro-systems to mitigate the current changes and the [SOURCE: Page_32]

542   future climate change impacts. [SOURCE: Page_32]

543   6 Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_33]

544    The traditional irrigation system in the High-Atlas piedmont is similar to several other [SOURCE: Page_33]

545   systems around the world. They have played through centuries vital roles in socio-economic [SOURCE: Page_33]

546   development while maintaining a balance between water resources and water needs. The [SOURCE: Page_33]

547   present study of the High-Atlas piedmont has shown that the overall state of water resources is [SOURCE: Page_33]

548   declining and deeply affected by droughts during the last 5 decades. Average precipitation has [SOURCE: Page_33]

549   decreased by almost 28 % and the streamflow has decreased by more than 40% directly [SOURCE: Page_33]

550   influencing the amount of water diverted by seguias for irrigation. The groundwater is depleting [SOURCE: Page_33]

551   as attested by water table decline and by the Abainou spring’s dramatic drying. Conversely, in [SOURCE: Page_33]

552   recent years, there has been a spectacular expansion of tree crops despite water scarcity. Also, [SOURCE: Page_33]

553   it was shown that the agricultural land use is changing from seasonal crops (cereals) to perennial [SOURCE: Page_33]

554   crops (trees), which creates an increasing and rigid water demand to satisfy, whatever the [SOURCE: Page_33]

555   conditions of climate and water resources. [SOURCE: Page_33]

556    This challenge is also found in the northern parts of the Mediterranean basin as well as in [SOURCE: Page_33]

557   northwestern India. Government subsidies intended to support irrigated agriculture in order to [SOURCE: Page_33]

558   increase food production have led to a direct dependence on groundwater abstraction to meet [SOURCE: Page_33]

559   the growing demand for irrigation water, thereby exacerbating groundwater resource depletion. [SOURCE: Page_33]

560    The uncontrolled expansion of trees, or generally irrigated areas, which is mainly driven by [SOURCE: Page_33]

561   strict socioeconomic incentives induced by several governmental agricultural supporting [SOURCE: Page_33]

562   programs, the multiplication of pumping, and the climate variability, threaten seriously the [SOURCE: Page_33]

563   sustainability of such ancestral traditional hydro-agro-systems. The climate variability observed [SOURCE: Page_33]

564   over the last 50 years has already had major impacts on water resources in the Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_33]

565   region, particularly in snow-related basins. The land-use changes are evolving in a direction [SOURCE: Page_33]

566   that is incompatible with balanced hydro-agro-systems. Therefore, interdisciplinary research [SOURCE: Page_33]

567   and management strategies are needed to assess the occurring changes and to provide adaptation [SOURCE: Page_34]

568   and mitigation measures to preserve their long-term sustainability. [SOURCE: Page_34]
