Dynamics of groundwater recharge near a semiarid Mediterranean stream: wet vs. typical climate conditions Youssef Hajhouji^1, Younes Fakir^1,2*, Simon Gascoin^3, Vincent Simonneaux^3, Abdelghani Chehbouni^2 1 Faculty of Sciences Semlalia, Cadi Ayyad University, Marrakech, Morocco. 2 CRSA (Center for Remote Sensing Application), UM6P, Benguerir,                              Morocco. [EMAIL_REDACTED] 3 CESBIO, Université de Toulouse, IRD/UPS/CNRS/CNES, Toulouse, France [SOURCE: Page_2]

Abstract: In semiarid and arid stream-dominated hydrosystems, the temporal variability of groundwater recharge is not widely addressed. It still holds various questions about the sources of recharge, its patterns, and the appropriate measuring technics. Hence, the main objective of the present study was to assess the changes that might affect the pattern of recharge under wetter than normal surface water availability. To this aim, water table fluctuation (WTF) was monitored near an intermittent stream in a piedmont area, over two years (2014-2016) with different conditions: extreme wet and typical weather. The groundwater recharge was assessed using the Episodic Master Recession (EMR) algorithm. During the two years, the recharge pattern was dominated by episodic events and by a high seasonality from wet seasons to dry seasons. In the wet year (2014-2015) the highest recharge was recorded following an extreme flood, which has deeply replenished groundwater. Furthermore, an exceptional steady state of groundwater level took place induced by a steady recharge rate. For several events, the assessed recharges were multisource, mainly from streamflow at local scale, but possibly from precipitation, underflow, deep percolation or irrigation return, from the upstream part of the system. The local recharge by streamflow is likely to be short-lived and the lateral recharge is likely to last longer. Consequently, the EMR algorithm estimated the total recharge that could encompass various sources. More research works and multidisciplinary approaches should be carried out to partition those sources and determine their specific contribution. [SOURCE: Page_2]

In semiarid stream-dominated systems, different recharge patterns induced by extreme hydrological events (e.g. wet events) and various potential sources of recharge are to consider when assessing and predicting groundwater recharge. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Keywords: water table fluctuation; EMR algorithm; episodic recharge; wet year; replenishment; multisource recharge [SOURCE: Page_2]

1 Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

In arid and semiarid basins, numerous plain aquifers are experiencing chronical groundwater [SOURCE: Page_3]

depletion because of high groundwater abstraction and/or low groundwater recharge (Boukhari [SOURCE: Page_3]

et al., 2015; Watto and Mugera, 2016; Van Lanen and Peters, 2000; Ashraf et al., 2021). The low [SOURCE: Page_3]

groundwater recharge (GWR) is usually due to low precipitation and high evapotranspiration [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Walvoord et al., 2002; Flint et al., 2002). Therefore, in those basins, mountain front GWR [SOURCE: Page_3]

linked to streamflow was often considered the primary source of recharge (De Vries and [SOURCE: Page_3]

Simmers, 2002; Manning and Solomon, 2003; Simmers, 2003; Wilson and Guan, 2004; Dahan et [SOURCE: Page_3]

al., 2008). The infiltration processes of streamflow have been widely analyzed (Ronan et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

1998; Stonestrom and Constantz, 2003; Niswonger et al., 2005; Keery et al., 2007; Rau et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

2010; Lautz 2012; Clutter and Ferré, 2018; Kurylyk et al., 2019). However, there are few applied [SOURCE: Page_3]

studies on the processes of recharge and its temporal variability (Shanafield and Cook, 2014). [SOURCE: Page_3]

In general, the behaviour of GWR in arid and semi-arid stream-dominated hydrosystems [SOURCE: Page_3]

is still not well analyzed (Cuthbert et al., 2016). Indeed, when studying GWR in those systems, [SOURCE: Page_3]

particularly those with shallow groundwater, various questions might need to be addressed. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Existing studies have shown that the main source of GWR is from the water losses of [SOURCE: Page_3]

nonperennial streams, mainly from floods that bring high amounts of water during episodic [SOURCE: Page_3]

recharge events (Shentsis and Rosenthal, 2003; Dagès et al., 2008; Cuthbert et al., 2019; [SOURCE: Page_3]

Shanafield et al., 2021). However, in case of agricultural practices, substantial GWR could be [SOURCE: Page_3]

generated by irrigation returns from diverted streamflow (Bouimouass et al., 2020). Therefore, [SOURCE: Page_3]

the recharge could be of multiple sources rather than a single one. Moreover, the typical pattern [SOURCE: Page_3]

of GWR is characterized by episodic recharge, which is related to the intermittency of [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

streamflow and to the episodic character of floods. In this context, research works on the [SOURCE: Page_4]

variation of the pattern of recharge under specific climate conditions are still lacking; e.g. [SOURCE: Page_4]

extreme wet events could impact the hydrological regime of streams catchment, and hence the [SOURCE: Page_4]

subsequent recharge. Finally, the water table fluctuation (WTF) technique is one of the most [SOURCE: Page_4]

used techniques due to the advantage over the other methods of being simple to apply and of [SOURCE: Page_4]

providing the most direct possible observation and measure of recharge (Cuthbert et al., 2016; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Nimmo and Perkins, 2018). However, the WTF method might not be always applicable because [SOURCE: Page_4]

of some limitations largely described in Healy and Cook (2002). [SOURCE: Page_4]

In the present study, a well tapping into a shallow alluvial aquifer near a semiarid [SOURCE: Page_4]

Mediterranean intermittent stream was monitored over two hydrological years, one of extremely [SOURCE: Page_4]

wet climate (2014-2015) and the other of typical climate (2015-2016). GWR was assessed over [SOURCE: Page_4]

the two years by the water table fluctuation (WTF) method using the Episodic Master Recession [SOURCE: Page_4]

(EMR) algorithm. The main objectives of the study were to analyze 1) the changes that may [SOURCE: Page_4]

affect the recharge pattern under wetter than normal surface water availability, 2) the effects of [SOURCE: Page_4]

an extreme wet hydrological event on the GWR, and 3) the adequacy of the EMR algorithm to [SOURCE: Page_4]

assess GWR in stream-dominated systems. Thus, the study highlighted the complexity of [SOURCE: Page_4]

assessing and predicting groundwater recharge in a developed (semi)arid stream catchment. [SOURCE: Page_4]

2.2 Recharge assessment using WTF method [SOURCE: Page_8]

In the present study, the WTF approach (Healy and Cook, 2002) was used to estimate [SOURCE: Page_8]

recharge by applying the Episodic Master Recession method (EMR) developed by Nimmo and [SOURCE: Page_8]

Perkins (2018). The WTF method stipulates that rises in groundwater levels in unconfined [SOURCE: Page_8]

aquifers are due to episodic recharge reaching the water table. The recharge R (m) is calculated [SOURCE: Page_8]

as the product of two key terms (Eq. 1), the change in groundwater level (H, m) attributable to [SOURCE: Page_8]

recharge and the specific yield (Sy, unitless): [SOURCE: Page_8]

dH dt R  H  S y   (1) [SOURCE: Page_8]

H is the difference between the peak of the rise of groundwater and the low value of [SOURCE: Page_8]

groundwater level estimated for the extrapolated precedent recession phase, i.e. the level if no [SOURCE: Page_8]

recharge had occurred. The method is adequate to measure individual and short-term (hours or a [SOURCE: Page_8]

few days) recharge events of shallow aquifers with rapid water-level fluctuations and for which [SOURCE: Page_8]

groundwater drainage can be approximated by graphic extrapolation. It can also be applied to [SOURCE: Page_8]

assess long-term recharge rates (seasonal or annual); in that case, as the groundwater drainage [SOURCE: Page_8]

might be significant, what is measured is an estimate of change in subsurface storage (∆Sgw, [SOURCE: Page_8]

m^3), referred to as net recharge (Healy and Cook, 2002). The WTF approach considers the [SOURCE: Page_8]

recharge during a given period as the sum of the episodic recharges calculated from individual [SOURCE: Page_8]

water-level rises. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The main uncertainties of the method could be the frequency of groundwater level [SOURCE: Page_8]

measurements, and the difficulty in determining a accurate specific yield (Sy) (Crosbie et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

2005). Furthermore, the method excludes the situations of high drainage and steady rate of [SOURCE: Page_9]

recharge (Healy and Cook, 2002). In this study, the water table variation was measured at an [SOURCE: Page_9]

hourly time step, which is ideal for the WTF method. The specific yield (Sy) was estimated near [SOURCE: Page_9]

the experiment site (Fakir et al., 2021) as explained in the previous section. [SOURCE: Page_9]

The EMR algorithm automatically allows identifying the main discrete recharge episodes [SOURCE: Page_9]

and estimates the recharge generated during each of those episodes based on WTF rates. To [SOURCE: Page_9]

apply the EMR method, three site-specific parameters are required (Nimmo et al., 2015; Nimmo [SOURCE: Page_9]

and Perkins, 2018) and are inferred from the water table data: [SOURCE: Page_9]

   Lag-time (Tp): the time interval between the occurrence of a water-input event and its [SOURCE: Page_9]

resulting water table response. [SOURCE: Page_9]

   Fluctuation tolerance (σT): the maximum value of water level variation as a function [SOURCE: Page_9]

of time which can be considered as system noise rather than a response to incoming [SOURCE: Page_9]

recharge flux. [SOURCE: Page_9]

   Master recession curve: MRC is a mathematical representation of expected water [SOURCE: Page_9]

table decline (recession) in the absence of episodic recharge as a function of hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_9]

head (Heppner and Nimmo, 2005; Crosbie et al., 2005; Nimmo and Perkins, 2018). It [SOURCE: Page_9]

is considered as the relation between the water-table elevation (H) and the water-table [SOURCE: Page_9]

decline rate ( d H   d t ). [SOURCE: Page_9]

Based on a first analysis of the observed water table fluctuation, Tp is taken equal to 1 day [SOURCE: Page_9]

and the fluctuation tolerance (σT) is taken equal to 0.01 m. A Tp of 1 day is justified by the rapid [SOURCE: Page_9]

reaction of the water table to the hydrological events and by the multiday duration of recharge [SOURCE: Page_9]

episodes. A low value was assigned to σT (1 cm), that is small enough to detect all episodes of [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

significant recharge. Both parameters were determined once for a given site, consequently they [SOURCE: Page_10]

do not affect episode-to-episode comparisons (Nimmo et al., 2015; Delottier et al., 2018). The [SOURCE: Page_10]

MRC parameters, estimated based on the curve fit of d H   dt   as a function of H, were established [SOURCE: Page_10]

over the study period as a linear regression function with a coefficient R$_2$ of 0.64. Since the [SOURCE: Page_10]

monitored well is near the Wadi and far from pumping influence, and as the used water table [SOURCE: Page_10]

measurements are free of barometric-pressure oscillations, it is considered that the recorded [SOURCE: Page_10]

groundwater level rises are due to general recharge (not related to a single source) and the drops [SOURCE: Page_10]

to natural discharge. [SOURCE: Page_10]

4 Discussion [SOURCE: Page_19]

The experimental site is located near an intermittent stream that is generally characterized [SOURCE: Page_19]

by high snowmelt contribution from the High-Atlas mountains, responsible of high flow and [SOURCE: Page_19]

floods in spring and early summer. In our basin, the snow regime is a main characteristic as [SOURCE: Page_19]

shown by several previous studies. The annual contribution of snow to streamflow was assessed [SOURCE: Page_19]

from 15% to 50% depending on meteorological conditions of the year (Boudhar et al., 2009; [SOURCE: Page_19]

Marchane et al., 2013). According to SPI, the climate in the piedmont is characterised by 13% [SOURCE: Page_19]

of years considered as dry and 11% as wet years, the 76% remaining years being considered as [SOURCE: Page_19]

normal. [SOURCE: Page_19]

The present study focuses on the changes that may affect the recharge mechanisms [SOURCE: Page_19]

during wet years. In both the wet year (2014-15) and the typical year (2015-16), the majority of [SOURCE: Page_19]

the GWR occurred during the wet seasons. During the wet year, the very rainy winter, with its [SOURCE: Page_19]

associated streamflow and floods, was the highest GWR period (almost 70%), while in the [SOURCE: Page_19]

normal year, as winter had lower precipitation, the spring season with its floods issued from [SOURCE: Page_19]

snowmelt was the highest GWR period (46%). Therefore, the time of greatest recharge was [SOURCE: Page_19]

during high waters (precipitation, high-flows or floods) when the amount of water allows [SOURCE: Page_19]

saturating the underlying sediment and generates high amounts of recharge (McCallum et al., [SOURCE: Page_19]

2014). During summer, there was little (wet year) or no recharge (normal year). The seasonality [SOURCE: Page_19]

is a main characteristic of the temporal distribution of the recharge under semiarid climate. The [SOURCE: Page_19]

18 [SOURCE: Page_19]

seasonality was explained (Fakir et al., 2021) by high moisture of the sediment in the wet season, [SOURCE: Page_20]

which generates higher hydraulic conductivities and less unsaturated pores than during the dry [SOURCE: Page_20]

period characterized by dry sediment due to water scarcity. [SOURCE: Page_20]

During the wet year, two additional features were observed. First, the effects of the [SOURCE: Page_20]

largest flood of 22 November 2014 that was classified as an extreme event with a return period [SOURCE: Page_20]

estimated to approximately 20 years (Zkhiri et al., 2017). The amount of surface water generated [SOURCE: Page_20]

by this event led to above normal groundwater level rise and subsequently to a very high [SOURCE: Page_20]

recharge amount. The latter represents 60% of the recharge recorded during the wet year. The [SOURCE: Page_20]

alluvial aquifer was significantly replenished. Second, in spring 2015, an exceptional steady state [SOURCE: Page_20]

of groundwater took place during almost 1.5 months, due to a steady rate of GWR. In this case [SOURCE: Page_20]

the rate of recharge was constant and equal to the rate of groundwater drainage. This GWR [SOURCE: Page_20]

regime seems unusual in semi-arid stream catchments where the typical pattern is mainly [SOURCE: Page_20]

characterized by episodic recharge. For that regime, the recharge was not episodic and its [SOURCE: Page_20]

estimation falls beyond the scope of the WTF method (Healy and Cook, 2002). However, it was [SOURCE: Page_20]

possible to use the EMR algorithm to have an estimation of this recharge. Other techniques could [SOURCE: Page_20]

be used in such conditions, but they require further investigations and aquifer characteristics [SOURCE: Page_20]

(Eaton, 2019). In semi-arid catchments, such sustained recharge can only be linked to an [SOURCE: Page_20]

exceptionally high and constant streamflow. [SOURCE: Page_20]

The recorded duration of the recharge events, often of several weeks, was longer than the [SOURCE: Page_20]

one of the corresponding precipitation. It is also longer than the duration of floods that are [SOURCE: Page_20]

generally flashy (77% are lesser than 30 hours). Furthermore, low correlation exists between the [SOURCE: Page_20]

recorded recharge, precipitation and streamflow. Therefore, the groundwater recharge near the [SOURCE: Page_20]

streambed seems to be due not only to local recharge from streamflow, but could also [SOURCE: Page_20]

19 [SOURCE: Page_20]

encompasses upstream lateral recharge from several potential sources including underflow, deep [SOURCE: Page_21]

percolation from upper reach of the catchment, or irrigation return from upstream. The local [SOURCE: Page_21]

recharge by streamflow is likely to be short-lived and lateral recharge at slower rate is likely to [SOURCE: Page_21]

last longer. Therefore, the EMR algorithm estimated the amounts of total recharge that probably [SOURCE: Page_21]

integrated various components. Hence, the time lag used in the EMR algorithm should not be [SOURCE: Page_21]

considered as a representative characteristic of a unique source of recharge. [SOURCE: Page_21]

This fact emphasizes the importance of combining other methods (e.g. hydrochemistry, [SOURCE: Page_21]

isotopes) to assess each component of the total recharge. Consequently, in semiarid stream [SOURCE: Page_21]

dominated systems the variation of groundwater recharge is rather erratic, because controlled by [SOURCE: Page_21]

numerous parameters. Among them are the above mentioned multiple sources of recharge and [SOURCE: Page_21]

their time scale, the sediment moisture conditions (Batlle-Aguilar and Cook, 2012; Schwartz, [SOURCE: Page_21]

2016; Fakir et al., 2021), the soil depth above the water table (Cao et al., 2016), the sediment [SOURCE: Page_21]

permeability (Shanafield, 2012; Villeneuve et al., 2015), the climate variability (Moeck et al., [SOURCE: Page_21]

2020) and land use changes (Lasagna et al., 2020). Therefore, assessing and predicting recharge [SOURCE: Page_21]

variability in such developed semiarid catchments is complex and needs to consider the various [SOURCE: Page_21]

parameters controlling the processes of recharge. In addition, a long-term and large scale [SOURCE: Page_21]

monitoring would better characterize the temporal variability and the catchment-scale [SOURCE: Page_21]

heterogeneity of recharge. [SOURCE: Page_21]

5 Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_21]

Water table fluctuation was monitored near an intermittent stream, in a semiarid [SOURCE: Page_21]

piedmont, over two years with contrasted climate conditions: a very wet and a typical year. In [SOURCE: Page_21]

general, the recharge was episodic and presented a high seasonality from wet seasons to dry [SOURCE: Page_21]

20 [SOURCE: Page_21]

seasons. Extremely wet periods brought a large amount of water that introduced an unusual [SOURCE: Page_22]

groundwater dynamic and hence recharge rates. An extreme hydrological event induced a very [SOURCE: Page_22]

large rise in the groundwater level. Also, an unusual steady state regime of groundwater was [SOURCE: Page_22]

observed, induced by a steady recharge rate. Those conditions have strongly replenished the [SOURCE: Page_22]

alluvial aquifer. [SOURCE: Page_22]

The groundwater recharge near the stream is due to local recharge mainly from [SOURCE: Page_22]

streamflow and probably from heavy precipitation of the wet year. However, the duration of [SOURCE: Page_22]

some recharge events was longer than the recorded floods or precipitation events, showing the [SOURCE: Page_22]

importance of lateral recharge from various potential sources, probably combining underflows or [SOURCE: Page_22]

irrigation returns from a large part of the stream system. Therefore, the EMR algorithm allows to [SOURCE: Page_22]

estimate a total recharge that could encompass various sources. More research works should be [SOURCE: Page_22]

carried out at large temporal and spatial scales to segregate those sources and determine their [SOURCE: Page_22]

respective contribution. This is vital to assess and predict recharge in a such developed semiarid [SOURCE: Page_22]

stream catchment, especially considering the threat of climate change. [SOURCE: Page_22]

In arid and semiarid climates, the wet conditions are rare and their return period is [SOURCE: Page_22]

generally of several years or decades. Consequently, despite their importance for recharge and [SOURCE: Page_22]

aquifers replenishment, they could not counterbalance the effects of droughts and [SOURCE: Page_22]

mismanagement that have severely affected the groundwater resources. [SOURCE: Page_22]
