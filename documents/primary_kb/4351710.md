## 23       ABSTRACT

24       Climate change is projected to increase water resources limitation and to impact significantly [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   agricultural production. A big challenge for agriculture will be to reduce the amount of water used to fit [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   the environmental constraints, while maintaining a level of production that ensure food security. In this [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   context, we propose a methodology based on high spatial and temporal resolution remote sensing data [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   combined with a semi-empirical crop model coupling the Simple Algorithm For Yield estimates (SAFY, [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   Duchemin et al., 2008, 2015; Battude et al., 2016) and a water balance model adapted from the FAO-56 [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   method (Allen et al., 1998). A module was added to automatically simulate irrigation. The model was [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   used to assess the dynamics of actual Evapotranspiration (ETca) and water supplies of maize crop over [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   large areas and during contrasted climatic years in the south west of France. The model was first [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   calibrated and evaluated over an experimental field using four years of ETca measurements. The [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   validation was done over 18 maize fields and larger irrigated zones (135 ha to 450 ha) using total [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   irrigation depths. This work permitted to quantify the ability of different methods to estimate the [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   storage capacity (soil map vs in situ data) and the basal crop coefficient Kcb (standard vs remotely [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   sensed values) and their impact on total irrigation depths. Good estimations were obtained for ETca (R = [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   0.88; RRMSE = 20%). The model also reproduced correctly the total irrigation depth over the 18 maize [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   fields (R = 0.79; RRMSE = 18.8%) and three larger irrigated zones (R = 0.8; RRMSE = 42%). The [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   underestimation (Bias = -93 mm) may be due to different reasons such as errors in soil water storage [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   capacity estimates, but also to an overestimation of water needs by water managers or a potential over- [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   irrigation carried out by farmers. Finally, the work demonstrates the high potential of combining a [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   simple agro-meteorological model using only few parameters with satellite imagery for a large-scale [SOURCE: Page_2]

44   monitoring of total irrigation depth. [SOURCE: Page_2]

## 45            1. INTRODUCTION

46        Agriculture is by far the main consumer of fresh water with about 70% of all withdrawals devoted [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   to irrigation (UNESCO, 2015). With the multiplication of extreme weather events, irrigation has become [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   essential to ensure a reliable, stable and profitable production. In a world where population is [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   constantly increasing and with high climate extremes, a big challenge for agriculture will be to maintain [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   a sufficient level of crop production while reducing the amount of water used, and therefore to increase [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   its use efficiency (Kijne et al., 2003). Indeed, an important amount of water allotted to irrigation is not [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   efficiently used by crop (Smith et al., 1992). This naturally leads the scientific community to work on [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   management tools to both ensure food security and meet environmental issues. [SOURCE: Page_3]

54        The present study was conducted in the south west of France on irrigated maize fields. In this [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   region, maize crop (Zea mays) represents 60% of irrigated lands, consuming 70 to 80% of whole [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   irrigation water (around 250.106 m^3/year). [SOURCE: Page_3]

57        Several agronomical crop models are developed to assess specific agronomical needs like grain [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   yield or irrigation demand prediction (e.g. DSSAT (Jones et al., 2003), STICS (Brisson et al., 2003)). [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   However, if such crop models are quite suitable for monitoring plant development at the field scale, [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   their implementation over larger areas is often limited by the availability of input data. To overcome [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   these difficulties, a widely used solution is to integrate satellite observations into semi-empirical crop [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   models (see Dorigo et al., 2007 for review). Halfway between complex and empirical approaches, these [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   models combine the descriptions of the main biophysical processes and simple empirical [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   parameterizations (e.g., AquaCrop (Steduto et al., 2009), AqYield (Constantin et al., 2015), GRAMI [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   (Maas, 1992), Pilote (Mailhol et al., 1997), PolyCrop (Nana et al., 2014), SAFY (Duchemin et al., 2008; [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   Battude et al., 2016)). [SOURCE: Page_3]

67        This approach combining remote sensing data and crop models has been made possible by the [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   development of new sensors providing high resolution images, necessary to an accurate vegetation [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   monitoring, and has been particularly popular for the monitoring of water resources and irrigation water [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   supplies at the regional scale (Duchemin et al., 2006; Zwart et al., 2010; Droogers et al., 2010; Saadi et [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   al., 2015; Toureiro et al., 2016). Most of the models among the previously cited studies did not allow to [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   estimate both the water needs and supplies, the biomass production and yields. In a previous study, [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   Battude et al. (2016) proposed a remote sensing driven approach to estimate the maize biomass [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   production and yield for both irrigated and rainfed fields. An original methodology was developed [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   specifically for large areas with a limited use of in situ information. In that purpose, we used a quite [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   simple semi-empirical model, the SAFY (i.e. Simple Algorithm For Yield estimates) model (Duchemin et [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   al., 2008) driven by high spatial and temporal resolution images, which is able to take into account the [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   dynamic of vegetation without requiring information about soil characteristics. This last study led to a [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   new formalism of the Effective Light Use Efficiency (ELUE) and the Specific Leaf Area (SLA) that was [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   implemented in the original version of the SAFY model. Results revealed that the new version of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   model improves yield estimates both at field scale (RRMSE = 13.7%) and at regional scale (RRMSE = 5%). [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   However, this new version of the model did not include a water balance module and, thus, did not give [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   access to the crop water needs and supplies which knowledge is essential in a context of water [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   management. The original version of the SAFY model had already been coupled with FAO methods on [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   rainfed wheat crops (Duchemin et al., 2015) and had led to good estimates of biomass, ETca and soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   water content. We thus coupled the new version of the SAFY model with the FAO-56 method and a sub- [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   module simulating irrigation in which irrigation events are triggered according to the water stress level [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   of the crop, in order to simulate both biomass production and water needs and supplies. [SOURCE: Page_4]

89       Even though the FAO-56 methods are sometimes questioned, they remain the most commonly [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   approach used for the estimation of crop evapotranspiration from field to global scales (Pereira et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   2015) due to their relative simplicity. Indeed, the method allows estimating optimal crop [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   Evapotranspiration (ETc) with a crop coefficient (Kc) applied to the reference evapotranspiration (ET$_0$). [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   Such approach overcomes the difficulties encountered with direct measurements (e.g. Eddy-Covariance, [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   Bowen ratio energy balance, lysimeters) for which applications are restrained to field scale because of [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   the heavy and costly needed investment. The accurate estimation of actual crop Evapotranspiration [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   (ETca) over large areas is essential to improve water resource management. Given their strong impact [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   on ETca, the crop coefficient and the plant water availability must be correctly estimated which is one of [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   the major difficulties of the application of the FAO approach over large areas. Indeed, the standard crop [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   coefficient method based on tabulated values implies “standard conditions” that do not vary from field [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   to field and that are not actual most of the time. As crop characteristics correlate well with spectral [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   reflectances, numerous studies aimed at developing empirical relationships between Kc and remote [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   sensing data that allowed improving ETca estimates and irrigation scheduling (Bausch et al., 1987; Neale [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   et al., 1989; Hunsaker et al., 2003; Glenn et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_5]

104        Beside the importance of an accurate estimation of Kc, the knowledge of the Storage Capacity (SC) [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   of the soil is also useful as it is a widely used concept for a large panel of models. This integrative value [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   relies on the knowledge of soil properties that are rarely available over large areas. Moreover, some [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   sensitivity analyses have demonstrated the large impact of uncertainty of the SC values on yield [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   estimates (Pachepsky and Acock, 1998; Lawless et al., 2008) or on the soil hydraulic characteristics [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   (Baroni et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_5]

110        The objectives of this study were twofold. First, we aimed at reproducing the seasonal dynamics of [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   ETca and total irrigation depth over large areas and various contrasted climatic years. For that, we used [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   a water balance crop model combined with high spatial and temporal resolution remote sensing data. [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   Second, we aimed at evaluating the impact on simulated ETca and total irrigation depths of various [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   methods used to determine the soil water storage capacity (SC) and the crop coefficient (Kc). [SOURCE: Page_5]

## 117           2. MATERIAL & METHODS

118   2.1. Model description [SOURCE: Page_6]

119       In this study, we used the SAFY-FAO model. This model combines the SAFY crop model (Duchemin [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   et al., 2008; Battude et al., 2016) with a water balance model proposed by the FAO-56 method (Allen et [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   al., 1998) and a sub-module simulating irrigation events, hereafter referred to as “automatic irrigation [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   module”. The SAFY-FAO model simulates the dynamics of Green Area Index (GAI in m^2.m-2, ratio of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   photosynthetically active plant area, without organ distinction, per meter square ground; Baret et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   2010), Dry Aboveground Mass (DAM in g.m-2), actual crop Evapotranspiration (ETca in mm.day-1) and [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   Current Available Water (CAW in mm) in three soil layers (i.e. top, intermediate and deep) at a daily time [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   step from a date of plant emergence (D$_0$). It can be run in two different ways: using the real irrigation [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   supplies (forced mode) or activating the automatic irrigation module (automatic mode). Remotely [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   sensed GAI and green cover fraction (FCOVER), incoming global radiation (Rg in MJ.m-2), air temperature [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   (Ta in °C), reference evapotranspiration (ET$_0$ in mm.day-1), precipitation and irrigation (in the forced [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   mode case) are used as inputs. ET$_0$ is derived from climatic parameters according to the Penman- [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   Monteith equation recommended by FAO (Allen et al., 1998), which is adapted for a hypothetical grass [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   reference surface. [SOURCE: Page_6]

133       The GAI (in m^2.m-2) is simulated by the SAFY crop model (Duchemin et al., 2008). In the present [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   study, we used the new formulation of vegetation dynamics proposed in Battude et al. (2016). This new [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   version of the SAFY model includes sixteen parameters (Table 1) allowing the seasonal variation of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   Specific Leaf Area (SLA in m^2.g-1) and the Effective Light Use Efficiency (ELUE in g.MJ-1) (see Battude et [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   al., 2016 for details). The SLA is defined as the ratio of leaf area to dry mass and it allows converting [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   daily leaf mass production into daily leaf area growth. The SLA increase with time is due to the [SOURCE: Page_6]

139   increasing leaves thickness during the plant growth. The ELUE corresponds to the plant efficiency to [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   convert radiation into aboveground biomass. The rise of ELUE during the plant growth may be due to [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   several processes, among them the variation of the root-shoot ratio (Amos and Walters, 2006). The [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   biomass production (        , [Eq. 1]) is based on the Monteith’s light-use efficiency theory (Monteith, [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   1972). It depends on the effective light-use efficiency (ELUE), on a stress factor (FT) being function of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   daily air temperature (Ta) and on the daily photosynthetically active radiation absorbed by canopy [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   (APAR in MJ.m-2), and is limited by the water stress coefficient (Ks). The Ks ([Eq. 2]) daily value varies [SOURCE: Page_7]

208       The “Lamothe” experimental field (referred to as “LAM field”) was instrumented in 2005 to monitor [SOURCE: Page_12]

209   vegetation growth, soil water dynamics, turbulent energy, water and C02 fluxes. This 32.2 ha site is [SOURCE: Page_12]

210   located close to a river in a large valley (Figure 1). It belongs to an experimental farm managed by the [SOURCE: Page_12]

211   Purpan Engineering School and takes part of the European research infrastructure “Integrated Carbon [SOURCE: Page_12]

212   Observation System” (ICOS, [https://icos-eco.fr/]) and of the “Regional Spatial Observatory” (OSR, [SOURCE: Page_12]

213   [http://www.cesbio.ups-tlse.fr/fr/osr.html]). The LAM field is characterized by an irrigated silage [SOURCE: Page_12]

214   maize/rainfed winter wheat rotation. We used four years of data over maize in 2006, 2008, 2010 and [SOURCE: Page_12]

215   2012, which correspond to years when maize was sown. The LAM field has a homogenous clay (around [SOURCE: Page_12]

216   50% clay, 36% loam and 14% sand) deep soil (around 1.5 m), presenting a large water storage capacity. [SOURCE: Page_12]

316        The nine water balance model parameters (Table 1) include five soil parameters (SLT$_1$, SD, Hfc, Hwp [SOURCE: Page_17]

317   and β) and four vegetation parameters (Kcbmax, Etrp, Dft and Vpr). Except for the surface layer thickness [SOURCE: Page_17]

330   capacity is regarded as full at this date. Soil diffusive fluxes have not been taken into consideration. [SOURCE: Page_18]

## 505           4. CONCLUSION

506        One main objective of this study was to evaluate the potential of the SAFY-FAO model combined [SOURCE: Page_30]

507   with remote sensing optical imagery to provide reliable estimations of water needs and total irrigation [SOURCE: Page_30]

508   depths of irrigated maize crop over large areas. For that purpose, we used a new version of the SAFY [SOURCE: Page_30]

509   model proposed by Battude et al. (2016) coupled with a water balance module adapted from FAO-56 [SOURCE: Page_30]

510   method (Allen et al., 1998). The SAFY-FAO model was chosen for its suitablility for spatial approaches [SOURCE: Page_30]

511   through the use of remotely sensed GAI time series. Another objective was to evaluate the impact of [SOURCE: Page_30]

512   different methods used to determine the soil water storage capacity (SC) and the basal crop coefficient [SOURCE: Page_30]

513   (Kcb) on simulated ETca and total irrigation depths. [SOURCE: Page_30]

514       After calibration over the experimental field using a four-year in situ ETca dataset, the model [SOURCE: Page_30]

515   outputs were evaluated over 18 maize fields and three irrigated zones (ASA) for which the total [SOURCE: Page_30]

516   irrigation depth applied during the growing season were known. Overall, good results were obtained for [SOURCE: Page_30]

517   biomass production (R = 0.97; RRMSE = 23%) and ETca estimates (R = 0.88; RRMSE = 20%) after [SOURCE: Page_30]

518   calibration over the experimental field. The model also reproduced well the total irrigation depths over [SOURCE: Page_30]

519   the 18 maize fields (R = 0.79; RRMSE = 18.8%). We observed an underestimation of the total irrigation [SOURCE: Page_30]

520   depth (-93 mm) for the three ASA. This might be explained by either the use of the standard Kcb by [SOURCE: Page_30]

521   water managers (leading to an overestimation of water needs), over-irrigation practices or even the SC [SOURCE: Page_30]

522   values that might be too high when using the soil map. Despite this, the model reproduces correctly the [SOURCE: Page_30]

523   trends observed between years and ASA (R = 0.8). [SOURCE: Page_30]

524       This work permitted to highlight the impact of different methods to estimate the SC and the Kcb on [SOURCE: Page_30]

525   ETca and on the total irrigation depth over large areas. The use of a standard Kcb led to a larger [SOURCE: Page_30]

30 [SOURCE: Page_30]

526   dispersion on ETca compared to results found using a remotely sensed (RS) Kcb (R = 0.77 with standard [SOURCE: Page_31]

527   Kcb and R = 0.88 with RS Kcb). Same conclusions were drawn for the total irrigation depth over the 18 [SOURCE: Page_31]

528   maize fields (R = 0.68 with standard Kcb and R = 0.79-0.87 with RS Kcb). Furthermore, the standard Kcb [SOURCE: Page_31]

529   requires the knowledge of the phenological stages of the crop that are not available over large areas and [SOURCE: Page_31]

530   even difficult to accurately obtain at field scale. Such results highlight the potential of the RS Kcb to [SOURCE: Page_31]

531   improve irrigation water management. [SOURCE: Page_31]

532       The impact of various SC estimations (soil map vs in situ measurements or farmers’ values) on ETca [SOURCE: Page_31]

533   and total irrigation depths was also investigated. Results highlighted that the soil depth (SD) provided by [SOURCE: Page_31]

534   the soil map induced negative bias on simulated ETca and total irrigation depth because of a high [SOURCE: Page_31]

535   imprecision on soil properties (SD, Hfc and Hwp) partly due to its coarse spatial resolution. Future work [SOURCE: Page_31]

536   will investigate ways to retrieve SD, for example using the future Global Soil Map given at 90 m spatial [SOURCE: Page_31]

537   resolution ([http://www.globalsoilmap.net/]). [SOURCE: Page_31]

538       This work also points out some limitations that should be investigated in future work. We chose to [SOURCE: Page_31]

539   calibrate the water module using four-year averaged parameters based on ETca measurements. The [SOURCE: Page_31]

540   validation using ETca measurements over other fields or years may allow evaluating the robustness of [SOURCE: Page_31]

541   the calibrated parameters and the associated error. [SOURCE: Page_31]

542       We also chose to disable the diffusive fluxes in soils as results showed minor improvement on [SOURCE: Page_31]

543   simulated ETca during the whole growing season. However, this process should be taken into account [SOURCE: Page_31]

544   when simulating annual soil water budget. [SOURCE: Page_31]

545       The model was used on irrigated crops with a diagnostic approach. In the future, we planned to [SOURCE: Page_31]

546   investigate its prognostic potential and enlarge its application on rainfed crops. This study demonstrates [SOURCE: Page_31]

547   the high potential of an agro-meteorological crop model combined with high spatial and temporal [SOURCE: Page_31]

548   resolution remote sensing data for a large-scale monitoring of total irrigation depths over maize fields. It [SOURCE: Page_31]

549   offers encouraging perspectives when using Sentinel-2 images in the near future. The Sentinel-2 dataset, [SOURCE: Page_31]

31 [SOURCE: Page_31]

550   available since May 2015, covering the whole globe and freely available, will be really interesting for [SOURCE: Page_32]

551   future applications over large areas. [SOURCE: Page_32]
