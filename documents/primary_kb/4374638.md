15   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

16   Cover crops are crucial to diversify cropping systems into more agroecological systems by providing [SOURCE: Page_3]

17   ecosystem services, such as reduction of nitrate leaching, provision of a green manure effect, and soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

18   carbon storage. However, they can influence water drainage and nitrogen (N) or water availability for [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   the succeeding crop, depending on their management and the climate. In this simulation study, we used [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   the STICS model to predict the influence of cover crop species, date of sowing and termination, and [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   cover crop residue management on N and water balances, and the yield and stress of the succeeding cash [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   crop. We performed 25-year simulations for five contrasting sites in south-western France, which is a [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   temperate region of Europe with dry summers. As expected, cover crops decreased nitrate leaching [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   effectively but also decreased drainage by a mean of 5-40 mm. This decrease depended mainly on the [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   sowing and termination dates, while the decrease in nitrate leaching varied greatly among sites and [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   depended most on sowing date, followed by cover crop species and then termination date. Cover crops [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   had little influence on soil water content in the upper 0.1 m of soil at sowing of the succeeding cash [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   crop, but decreased soil water content of the total soil profile by 0-30 mm. Soil water content depended [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   most on termination date, followed by species and then site. Total soil mineral N content (SNC) also [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   decreased, by 5-40 kg N.ha-1, at the three driest sites and up to 10 kg N.ha-1 at the rainiest site. [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   Termination date, the second-most influential factor on SNC, indicated that later termination resulted in [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   lower SNC than that after bare soil. N uptake by the succeeding cash crop depended on species and [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   termination date, and a legume cover crop and earlier termination date resulted in higher N uptake. The [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   decrease in maize and sunflower yield was due mainly to changes in the N stress index during the [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   vegetative phase for maize, and both vegetative and reproductive phases for sunflower. No water stress [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   or increase in irrigation volumes was predicted or was correlated with yield changes, except at dry sites [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   for the few years that experienced a severe drought in spring. While cover crops decreased nitrate [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   leaching effectively, they decreased drainage and could induce N stress for the succeeding crop, [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   particularly in dry regions. Including legumes in mixtures and adapting the termination date to local [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   climate conditions could decrease or avoid these negative effects. [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

42   Cover crops, also called catch crops or green manure, are one of the pillars of agroecological practices [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   and are essential for diversifying cropping systems (Duru et al., 2015). Sown during the fallow period [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   between two annual cash crops and usually terminated long or immediately before sowing the [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   succeeding cash crop, they are returned to the soil. Cover crops can provide a wide range of ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   services, such as provision of a green manure effect; storage of carbon in the soil; reduction of nitrate [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   leaching and erosion, a better greenhouse gas balance and weed control (Constantin et al., 2010; Ryder [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   and Fares, 2008; Schipanski et al., 2014; Tonitto et al., 2006; Tosti et al., 2014; Tribouillois et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   As cover crops increase evapotranspiration during the fallow period compared to bare soil (Unger and [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   Vigil, 1998) and take up mineral nitrogen (N) from the soil, they change water and N balances during [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   and following this period. Cover crops generally decrease drainage (Meyer et al., 2019) and can decrease [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   soil water content (SWC) before sowing of the succeeding crop, particularly when they are terminated [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   immediately before sowing (i.e. “late termination”) (Meyer et al., 2020). The increase in water and [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   mineral N uptake can decrease water and N availability for the succeeding crop, and this pre-emptive [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   competition occurs particularly in dry regions (Thorup-Kristensen et al., 2003). In the context of climate [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   change, with temperatures and lower rainfall in arid and temperate regions (IPCC, 2013), sowing cover [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   crops during the fallow period could increase issues for groundwater recharge and decrease yield of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   succeeding cash crop, by reducing drainage (Tribouillois et al., 2018) and soil water and N availability [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   for the following crop (Alonso-Ayuso et al., 2018; Meyer et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   The magnitude of these processes depends on the soil, climate and cover crop management, such as [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   choice of species, dates of sowing and termination, and residue management (Constantin et al., 2015b; [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   Meyer et al., 2019; Tribouillois et al., 2015a). Some field-experiment studies highlighted the importance [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   of the termination date to avoid hindering the succeeding cash crop (Alonso-Ayuso et al., 2014; Clark [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   et al., 1997). Some showed that cover crops without late termination could decrease nitrate leaching [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   effectively, as well as the risk of decreasing drainage and yield for the succeeding crop (Constantin et [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   al., 2015b). A meta-analysis by Tonitto et al. (2006) highlighted that, under certain conditions, the green [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   manure effect of a legume cover crop can replace mineral N fertilisation for maize (Zea mays L). [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   Understanding these interactions and their potential positive or negative influence on the succeeding [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   cash crop is crucial for farmers and agricultural advisers to manage cover crops better. This can provide [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   the best compromise for supplying ecosystem services without producing dis-services for the succeeding [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   cash crop. [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   Thus, cover crop management is crucial to avoid or limit the cover crop’s potential negative influence [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   on the succeeding crop. Management practices should be adapted locally to a site, its soil and climate [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   characteristics, and the succession of cash crops, which determine the degree of potential pre-emptive [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   competition. Crop models can be a valuable tool to assess the influence of cover crops on water and N [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   balances under multiple cropping system management practices, particularly over large areas (Bergez [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   et al., 2010). The objective of the present study was to quantify the influence of cover crop management [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   scenarios on water and N balances and on the succeeding cash crop in multiple soils and climates at the [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   regional scale. The study was conducted for the Adour-Garonne catchment (120 000 km²), a large [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   catchment in south-western France which has frequent water deficit in aquifers during summer and [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   irrigation restriction (Mazzega et al., 2014), using the STICS soil-crop model (Brisson et al., 2009, [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   2003). [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   2. Materials and methods [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   2.1. Study area [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   The study area was located in the Adour-Garonne catchment, in south-western France (Figure 1). This [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   area of France has a temperate climate, with several climate types according to the definitions of Joly et [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   al. (2010): mainly oceanic, altered oceanic, semi-continental and south inland oceanic from west to east. [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   Conditions during the summer, when cover crops are sown, vary, with wet and warm conditions in the [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   west with an oceanic influence, and dry and hot conditions in the east. Soils in this catchment range [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   from sandy soils in the west to more clayey soils in the east. The catchment aquifers are also influenced [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   by nitrate pollution in winter and a water deficit in summer. To represent the range of soils and climates [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   in the catchment, we chose five agricultural fields, from west to east: RDL, LAL, LEC, AUZ and PEY. [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   2.2. Virtual experimental design [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   The simulation approach consisted of using the STICS soil-crop model (Brisson et al., 1998; 2002; 2003; [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   2009) to simulate the fallow period and the succeeding crop for five soil-climate combinations over 18 [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   years. We tested several fallow-period management practices, including bare soil or cover crops of [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   multiple species, sowing and termination dates and cover crop residue management practices, which [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   were followed by maize or sunflower (Helianthus annuus) in a continuous simulation to take into [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   account the effect of cover crop residues on the cash crop. The simulation were re-initialized at each [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   beginning of the fallow period to avoid the cumulative effect of cover crops (Constantin et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   For all five sites, we simulated bare soil as a control and four types of cover crops commonly sown [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   during the fallow period between a winter and spring crop: white mustard (Sinapis alba), common vetch [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   (Vicia sativa), Italian ryegrass (Lolium multiflorum) and a bispecific mixture of red clover (Trifolium [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   incarnatum) and Ethiopian mustard (Brassica carinata). These four types were selected for their [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   contrasting growth, production and potential biomass C:N ratio; variable sensitivity to water stress, high [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   temperatures and frost; and contrasting sensitivity to soil mineral N availability (i.e. legume vs. non- [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   legume). [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   We tested four cover crop sowing dates (August 5, August 20, September 5 and September 20), [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   combined with four cover crop termination dates from late autumn to early spring (November 15, [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   December 15, March 15 and April 15) (Table 1). At termination, we simulated two cover crop residue- [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   management practices: ploughed or left as mulch on the soil surface. Finally, we simulated two cash [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   crops sown on April 16: irrigated maize, with automatic irrigation triggered by the STICS model if a [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   water satisfaction index fell below 85%, and sunflower grown under rainfed conditions (without [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   irrigation). Mineral N inputs for the cash crops were determined from surveys of cropping practices in [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   the Adour-Garonne catchment. N fertilisation was 185 kg N ha-1 divided into two applications for maize [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   and 50 kg N ha-1 two weeks after sowing for sunflower. The simulations were conducted over 1990- [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   2017 to represent the actual climate variability of each site. For each cash crop, year and site, we ran [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   129 “species × sowing date × termination date × residue management” scenarios. [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   2.3. Simulation methods [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   2.3.1. STICS model overview [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   The STICS soil-crop model is a dynamic process-based model that runs on a daily time step. It simulates [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   crop growth and water, carbon and N balances under the influence of weather, soil and crop management [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   practices. STICS simulates cropping systems over time, from a crop cycle to a long-term succession, [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   including the management of fallow periods between cash crops. Soil water is modelled using a "tipping- [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   bucket" approach. Water demand uses a crop-coefficient approach, while N demand is based on the N [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   dilution curve, which is generic and robust (Gastal and Lemaire, 2002). Depending on the SWC and soil [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   mineral N content (SNC) in the rooting zone, water and N stresses decrease crop growth when demands [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   are not met by soil availability. When residues are left on the soil surface, the mulch decreases soil [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   evaporation as a function of the amount of biomass in the mulch. Mulch can also intercept some rainfall [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   and irrigation, and this water, retained in the mulch, evaporates according to the evaporative demand [SOURCE: Page_8]

152   (Brisson et al., 2009). STICS has been evaluated for French soil and climate contexts and for several [SOURCE: Page_8]

153   crops, including cash and cover crops (Coucheney et al., 2015). These authors classified STICS’ [SOURCE: Page_8]

154   predictions as "satisfactory" to "very good" for most of the variables analysed, especially the SWC and [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   crop biomass under differing levels of N and water availability. STICS has been used to simulate cover [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   crops, in particular to predict their influence in the short to long terms on the SWC, SNC and the green [SOURCE: Page_8]

157   manure effect (Constantin et al., 2012; Tribouillois et al., 2018). Cover crop species simulated in the [SOURCE: Page_8]

158   present study were previously calibrated and validated for STICS based on several field experiments [SOURCE: Page_8]

159   (Constantin et al., 2015b; Meyer et al., 2020). The version 10 of STICS was used in this study as in [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   Meyer et al. (2020). Even if the version is different from the one used in the main paper on STICS [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   evaluation by Coucheney et al. (2015), the evolution of STICS is conducted by the STICS team with [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   automatic evaluation of the model accuracy on the same database as used in this paper. The fitting [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   obtained by the following versions of the model are checked to give similar results. The level of accuracy [SOURCE: Page_8]

164   for the different variables analysed in this paper are then still very close. [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   2.3.2 STICS model initialisation [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   Soil water and mineral N were initialised in STICS in the same way for all five sites. To initialise the [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   SWC of each soil layer, we simulated a wheat crop preceding the sowing of the cover crop. Thus, water [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   initialisation differed for each climate year, which depended on the weather of the previous year. We [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   initialised the SNC of the soil profile with a low SNC of 18 kg ha-1, homogeneously distributed over the [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   soil profile. This value corresponds to that after a well-managed wheat crop, for which the uptake of N [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   by a cover crop is more likely to influence the succeeding crop. [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   2.4. Data analysis and statistics [SOURCE: Page_9]

173   Soil-crop models produce many output variables. The selected STICS outputs we focused on were : (1) [SOURCE: Page_9]

174   cover crop biomass at termination; (2) cumulative water drainage, evapotranspiration and nitrate [SOURCE: Page_9]

175   leaching during the fallow period and the crop succession; (3) SWC and SNC at cash crop sowing and [SOURCE: Page_9]

176   (4) irrigation volume simulated by the STICS model according to the maize water requirement, water [SOURCE: Page_9]

177   (WSI) and N (NSI) stress, and yields of the succeeding cash crop. For SWC, we analysed it over the [SOURCE: Page_9]

178   whole profile and in the seedbed (upper 0.1 m of soil). We calculated differences and ratios of the outputs [SOURCE: Page_9]

179   of interest between the cover crop management (i.e. each species, sowing and termination dates, and [SOURCE: Page_9]

180   residue management) and bare soil per site and year. [SOURCE: Page_9]

181   We analysed the influence of the factors site, cover crop species, sowing date, termination date, and [SOURCE: Page_9]

182   residue management on the outputs of interest using analysis of variance (ANOVA) within the R [SOURCE: Page_9]

183   software (https://cran.r-project.org/), considering climate years as replicates. After testing the normality [SOURCE: Page_9]

184   and homogeneity of the variances in the simulated outputs, we used the ANOVA to determine the [SOURCE: Page_9]

185   percentage of variance explained by each factor and their interactions, as all factors were significant due [SOURCE: Page_9]

186   to the large number of simulations. We ranked the factors in descending order of explanation of variance [SOURCE: Page_9]

187   for each output of interest. We also calculated correlations between changes in water and N stress due [SOURCE: Page_9]

188   to differences in cover crops and yields for maize and sunflower compared to bare soil. [SOURCE: Page_9]

211   3.2. Cover crop biomass at termination [SOURCE: Page_10]

212   Cover crop biomass varied greatly among species, and sowing and termination dates (Figure 2). The [SOURCE: Page_10]

213   two factors that explained the most variance in biomass were cover crop species and termination date [SOURCE: Page_10]

214   (16% each) (Table 4). Species, termination date and their interaction explained 39% of the variance. [SOURCE: Page_10]

218   and 0-4.3 Mg.ha-1, respectively. These two types grew strongly in winter and spring, with their biomass [SOURCE: Page_10]

219   doubling from December 15 to March 15, and often doubling again from March 15 to April 15. [SOURCE: Page_10]

224   of August 5 was 67-75% lower. [SOURCE: Page_11]

226   Site was the last factor that explained a large percentage of the variance: species, termination and sowing [SOURCE: Page_11]

227   dates, site and their interactions explained 60%. Cover crops grew more and with less variability at wet [SOURCE: Page_11]

232   3.3. Changes in water and N balance during the crop succession [SOURCE: Page_11]

233   3.3.1. Cover crop evapotranspiration, drainage and nitrate leaching [SOURCE: Page_11]

234   Cover crops increased evapotranspiration by a mean of 15-60 mm during the fallow period due to plant [SOURCE: Page_11]

235   transpiration, depending on the cover crop management practice. Termination date, residue [SOURCE: Page_11]

236   management, species, sowing date, site and their interactions explained only 31% of the variance in [SOURCE: Page_11]

237   evapotranspiration difference. The most influential explanatory factor was termination date (7%): the [SOURCE: Page_11]

238   later a cover crop was terminated, the greater the difference in evapotranspiration with bare soil (Table [SOURCE: Page_11]

239   3), highlighting the greater water consumption due to cover crop growth. Residue management also [SOURCE: Page_11]

240   explained approximately the same level of variance in evapotranspiration well, with a lower increase in [SOURCE: Page_11]

241   evapotranspiration because mulch decreased evaporation. When cover crops were terminated on [SOURCE: Page_11]

242   November 15, evapotranspiration of the two residue-management practices differed by ca. 30 mm, but [SOURCE: Page_11]

243   this difference decreased with later termination dates (Figure S$_1$). Cover crop species was the third most [SOURCE: Page_11]

244   influential factor that explained the variance, 4% alone and 26% combined with the previous ones. For [SOURCE: Page_11]

245   example, white mustard, with relatively low biomass, increased evapotranspiration less than vetch or [SOURCE: Page_11]

246   ryegrass, which grew for a longer period. The spring regrowth of ryegrass and the bispecific mixture [SOURCE: Page_11]

247   increased evapotranspiration strongly. Site and sowing date explained the variance only slightly. [SOURCE: Page_11]

248   Cover crops decreased water drainage by a mean of 5-40 mm, mainly during the fallow period, [SOURCE: Page_12]

249   depending on the site. This decrease depended mainly on the sowing and termination dates, which [SOURCE: Page_12]

250   explained 13% and 7% of the total variance, respectively. For all treatments at each site, earlier sowing [SOURCE: Page_12]

251   and later termination decreased water drainage due to the increase in the duration of the cover crop [SOURCE: Page_12]

252   growing season (Figure S$_2$). The site explained 5% of the variance, while species explained only 2%: a [SOURCE: Page_12]

253   slightly larger decrease in drainage was predicted for the bispecific mixture and Italian ryegrass. Sowing [SOURCE: Page_12]

254   and termination dates, site, species and their interactions explained 38% of the variance in drainage [SOURCE: Page_12]

255   compared to bare soil in the fallow period. [SOURCE: Page_12]

256   Cover crops decreased nitrate leaching mainly during the fallow period, but the decrease varied greatly [SOURCE: Page_12]

257   depending on the soil and climate of the site. Site alone explained 65% of the variance in the decrease. [SOURCE: Page_12]

258   Sites with the highest nitrate leaching under bare soil conditions (LAL and RDL) had the largest decrease [SOURCE: Page_12]

259   in nitrate leaching: 15-45 kg N.ha-1 per year. The other sites, with lower nitrate leaching with bare soil, [SOURCE: Page_12]

260   had a smaller decrease: ca. 10 kg N.ha-1 at AUZ, and 5 kg N.ha-1 or less at LEC and PEY (Figure S$_3$). [SOURCE: Page_12]

331   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_15]

332   4.1. Cover crops decreased nitrate leaching and drainage as a function of their [SOURCE: Page_15]

333   sowing date [SOURCE: Page_15]

334   The simulations indicate that cover crops decreased nitrate leaching effectively during the fallow period [SOURCE: Page_15]

335   compared to bare soil and were less effective with legume than with non-legume species, as experiments [SOURCE: Page_15]

336   and other simulation studies have shown (Constantin et al., 2010; Tonitto et al., 2006; Tribouillois et al., [SOURCE: Page_15]

337   2015a). As cover crop biomass and N uptake depend greatly on sowing and termination dates along with [SOURCE: Page_15]

338   climate conditions, the sowing date is crucial for optimal growth in autumn and to decrease nitrate [SOURCE: Page_15]

339   leaching effectively (Constantin et al., 2015a). For instance, late sowing does not allow for optimal [SOURCE: Page_15]

340   growth of cover crops due to low temperatures and solar radiation, which are reinforced by a dry autumn [SOURCE: Page_15]

341   (Bodner et al., 2010). Sowing date also has a strong influence on decreasing water drainage. The earlier [SOURCE: Page_15]

342   the cover crop is sown, the larger the decrease in water drainage, as shown in a previous study in this [SOURCE: Page_15]

343   region (e.g. Meyer et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_15]

344   Termination date also influenced the production of cover crop biomass, which also depended on the [SOURCE: Page_15]

345   species. In a dry autumn, late sowing of vetch can limit its growth strongly. Species with rapid growth [SOURCE: Page_15]

346   in summer and a short crop cycle, such as white mustard, stop growing in late autumn/early winter, [SOURCE: Page_15]

347   when they reach senescence (Bodner et al., 2007). Conversely, species such as ryegrass have a strong [SOURCE: Page_15]

348   ability to resume growth in spring, and their biomass can double or triple in a few weeks in spring. White [SOURCE: Page_15]

349   mustard, with relatively low biomass, increased evapotranspiration less than vetch or ryegrass, which [SOURCE: Page_15]

350   grew for a longer period. The spring regrowth of ryegrass and the bispecific mixture increased [SOURCE: Page_15]

351   evapotranspiration strongly. This kind of growth had the greatest influence on the water balance in the [SOURCE: Page_15]

352   present study, with the largest decrease in drainage when the cover crop was terminated in April. [SOURCE: Page_16]

353   Although drainage varied greatly among sites (by a mean of 100 to more than 600 mm), the decrease [SOURCE: Page_16]

354   due to cover crops varied little among them. Unlike the decrease in nitrate leaching, which was similar [SOURCE: Page_16]

355   to that under bare soil, the decrease in drainage could represent a small or large percentage of total [SOURCE: Page_16]

356   drainage. Consequently, the influence of cover crops on drainage could be limited through management [SOURCE: Page_16]

357   choices at sites with low annual drainage. Management practices should be adapted in certain cases to [SOURCE: Page_16]

358   decrease biomass production in order to decrease cover crop evapotranspiration and the decrease in [SOURCE: Page_16]

359   drainage (Nielsen et al., 2015; Tribouillois et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_16]

360   4.2. Cover crops decreased total SWC but did not influence seedbed SWC [SOURCE: Page_16]

361   Termination dates are crucial for determining the influence of cover crops on the amount of water [SOURCE: Page_16]

362   available in the soil profile for the succeeding crop. Several studies highlighted the risk of lower SWC [SOURCE: Page_16]

363   at sowing of the succeeding cash crop (Mitchell et al., 2015; Unger and Vigil, 1998). When SWC [SOURCE: Page_16]

364   decreased, particularly at dry sites with late cover crop termination and strong growth in spring, no [SOURCE: Page_16]

365   influence on the seedbed SWC was predicted, as rainfall on days before sowing was sufficient to refill [SOURCE: Page_16]

366   this soil layer. However, when cover crops were terminated the same day the cash crop was sown, as in [SOURCE: Page_16]

367   conservation agriculture, seedbed SWC could be lower, especially under dry spring conditions. Lower [SOURCE: Page_16]

368   SWC could thus induce late emergence and delay development of the succeeding cash crop. We [SOURCE: Page_16]

369   observed similar results in a previous experimental study conducted under similar soil-climate [SOURCE: Page_16]

370   conditions, during which the SWC in the upper 0.2 m of soil in April did not differ significantly between [SOURCE: Page_16]

371   bare soil and cover crops (Meyer et al., 2020). This could be due to the frequent rewetting of the upper [SOURCE: Page_16]

372   soil layers with spring rainfall that does not saturate the soil, but wets the surface sufficiently to provide [SOURCE: Page_16]

373   homogeneous conditions for sowing the cash crop and thus avoids water stress at emergence. [SOURCE: Page_16]

374   4.3. Cover crops induced mainly N stress, sometimes decreasing cash crop yield [SOURCE: Page_16]

375   Cover crops have a well-documented influence on succeeding crop yields. Increased maize yields have [SOURCE: Page_16]

376   been reported, particularly with legume cover crops such as vetch (Clark et al., 1997). We also predicted [SOURCE: Page_16]

377   increased maize and sunflower yields after vetch in the present study. This increase is due to the [SOURCE: Page_16]

378   additional supply of mineral N after decomposition of legume residues, which are richer in N due to [SOURCE: Page_17]

379   symbiotic N$_2$ fixation and the green manure effect of these legumes (Tosti et al., 2014; Tribouillois et [SOURCE: Page_17]

380   al., 2015b). Conversely, sowing a non-legume cover crop during the fallow period can result in mineral [SOURCE: Page_17]

381   N pre-emption, which is detrimental to the growth of the succeeding cash crop when doses of N fertiliser [SOURCE: Page_17]

382   are not increased (Alvarez et al., 2017). In the present study, decreases in SNC were predicted as a [SOURCE: Page_17]

383   function of the termination date and cover crop species. A late termination date could induce a larger [SOURCE: Page_17]

384   decrease in SNC. We predicted a decrease in maize and sunflower yields under certain conditions, which [SOURCE: Page_17]

385   was explained mainly by N stress but also by water stress in specific cases. Maize experienced water [SOURCE: Page_17]

386   stress in spring 2011 during the vegetative phase, despite automatic irrigation, which did not increase [SOURCE: Page_17]

387   due to cover crops. Severe drought and high temperatures in April and May 2011 explained this high [SOURCE: Page_17]

388   stress, which strongly decreased yield. Except for a few cases, the succeeding cash crop suffered mainly [SOURCE: Page_17]

389   from N stress and pre-emptive competition from cover crops, resulting in a decrease in yield (by 0.5-3.0 [SOURCE: Page_17]

390   Mg.ha-1), especially for late spring termination. Marcillo and Miguez (2017) reported the importance of [SOURCE: Page_17]

391   adapting the cover crop termination date to avoid a decrease in yield due to lower SNC. We also [SOURCE: Page_17]

392   predicted the largest decrease in yield with the latest termination date (the day before sowing the cash [SOURCE: Page_17]

393   crop), due to a phase of net N organisation after incorporating cover crop residues. This influence was [SOURCE: Page_17]

394   greater for cover crops with strong growth in spring, resulting in lower SNC. For sunflower, grown [SOURCE: Page_17]

395   under dry conditions, we also predicted a decrease in yield due to water and N stress. However, as [SOURCE: Page_17]

396   mentioned, sowing vetch, regardless of its termination date, or mustard, which stops growing in winter, [SOURCE: Page_17]

397   or autumn termination of ryegrass, can avoid the decrease in yield of the succeeding cash crop. [SOURCE: Page_17]

398   Sowing a legume cover crop increases the yield of the succeeding cash crop as it avoids N stress due to [SOURCE: Page_17]

399   pre-emptive competition (Tonitto et al., 2006). However, legume cover crops increased the risk of nitrate [SOURCE: Page_17]

400   leaching compared to that with non-legumes, as they take up less N from the soil due to symbiotic N$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_17]

401   fixation (Thorup-Kristensen and Nielsen, 1998). Nevertheless, sowing legumes remains more effective [SOURCE: Page_17]

402   than bare soil and could be beneficial, especially for sites with low nitrate leaching, to promote the green [SOURCE: Page_17]

403   manure effect and thus use less N fertiliser. Combining legumes and non-legumes could be an interesting [SOURCE: Page_17]

404   compromise as this type of mixture has performed well for the management of mineral N, to [SOURCE: Page_17]

405   significantly decrease leaching and increase the green manure effect (Tosti et al., 2014, 2012; [SOURCE: Page_18]

406   Tribouillois et al., 2015a). Cover crop mixtures that include legumes and non-legumes, along with [SOURCE: Page_18]

407   adapted management (i.e. species, sowing and termination dates), could be an effective solution to [SOURCE: Page_18]

408   provide several ecosystem services depending on the specific objectives and a site’s soil and climate, [SOURCE: Page_18]

409   especially to avoid water problems (White et al., 2017). In zones prone to nitrate leaching, farmers [SOURCE: Page_18]

410   should sow more legume/non-legume mixtures and limit the use of pure legumes during fallow periods. [SOURCE: Page_18]

411   In dry regions, late termination of grass cover crops, such as ryegrass, is strongly discouraged to avoid [SOURCE: Page_18]

412   the risk of mineral N and water pre-emptions which decrease the yield of the succeeding cash crop [SOURCE: Page_18]

413   (Alonso-Ayuso et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_18]

414   4.4. Study boundaries and extrapolation in space and time [SOURCE: Page_18]

415   Using STICS, we assumed that physical soil properties, such as bulk density and soil infiltration [SOURCE: Page_18]

416   capacity, remained constant throughout the crop succession, with or without cover crops. However, on [SOURCE: Page_18]

417   bare soil, a crust can form on the soil surface that decreases water infiltration. Sowing cover crops during [SOURCE: Page_18]

418   the fallow period changes the physical properties of the soil and increases the proportion of water that [SOURCE: Page_18]

419   infiltrates (Basche and DeLonge, 2019; Yu et al., 2016). This could result in greater drainage volume [SOURCE: Page_18]

420   with cover crops than that predicted by STICS, especially when fields slope, which could induce runoff. [SOURCE: Page_18]

421   Studies also suggest that cover crops change soil bulk density to increase the soil water reserve slightly [SOURCE: Page_18]

422   (de Lima et al., 2012; Strudley et al., 2008). In addition, soil decompaction increases the depth of soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

423   explored by roots of the succeeding cash crop (Chen et al., 2014; Chen and Weil, 2011). [SOURCE: Page_18]

424   Nevertheless, changes in soil structure due to changes in pore distribution and size can increase the [SOURCE: Page_18]

425   reservoir size over the long term. Gabriel et al (2019) showed that changes in soil physical properties, [SOURCE: Page_18]

426   an increase in reservoir size and increased hydraulic continuity would partially compensate for water [SOURCE: Page_18]

427   losses due to transpiration of cover crops compared to bare soil. As our study focused on the succession [SOURCE: Page_18]

428   of a cover crop and a short-term cash crop, we assumed that the increase in water infiltration in the soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

429   and the size of the reservoir due to cover crops was negligible. We simulated all sites using a soil depth [SOURCE: Page_18]

430   of 0.9 m, and simulating deeper or shallower soils could broaden results for crop water or N stress. In a [SOURCE: Page_18]

431   shallower soil, with a lower water-retention capacity and more rapid nitrate leaching, stress of the [SOURCE: Page_18]

432   succeeding cash crop could increase. In a deeper soil, the stress could decrease, as roots can grow more [SOURCE: Page_19]

433   deeply and access more water and abiotic resources. [SOURCE: Page_19]

434   The present study highlighted the influence of termination date on evapotranspiration, water drainage [SOURCE: Page_19]

435   and soil water and mineral N availability for the succeeding cash crop, confirming the results obtained [SOURCE: Page_19]

436   by Alonso-Ayuso et al. (2018). Residue management after termination also modified the influence of [SOURCE: Page_19]

437   cover crops on the soil water balance. Stopping growth and leaving cover crop residues on the soil [SOURCE: Page_19]

438   surface limits the increase in evapotranspiration caused by the decrease in soil evaporation compared to [SOURCE: Page_19]

439   mouldboard ploughing (Gabriel et al., 2019). This difference in evapotranspiration can increase surface [SOURCE: Page_19]

440   SWC, as several studies show for the upper 0.2 m of soil (Moschler et al., 1967; Stipešević and Kladivko, [SOURCE: Page_19]

441   2005). [SOURCE: Page_19]

442   Considering other locations, these results are valid in similar climate conditions with temperate climate [SOURCE: Page_19]

443   and dry summers. We simulated a range of sites with contrasted annual precipitation and potential [SOURCE: Page_19]

444   evapotranspiration and a large range of cover crops species and management. It gave some trends on the [SOURCE: Page_19]

445   potential impact of cover crops on several water and N components depending on termination date, [SOURCE: Page_19]

446   presence of legumes or strong regrowth at spring for temperate climate such as in Europe or USA. Since [SOURCE: Page_19]

447   we also gave the interaction between the different factors among which is the site, one can extrapolate [SOURCE: Page_19]

448   the results more easily, particularly if the site does not influence the results. They should however be [SOURCE: Page_19]

449   careful in to different conditions and run the scenario again within the new pedoclimatic context. [SOURCE: Page_19]

450   In climate change context, cover crops are an interesting tool to overcome some crucial issues by [SOURCE: Page_19]

451   reducing efficiently nitrate leaching avoiding indirect N2O emissions in aquifers. Some studies have [SOURCE: Page_19]

452   shown that this efficiency remains in climate change context as well (Alonso-Ayuso et al., 2018; [SOURCE: Page_19]

453   Tribouillois et al., 2018). Cover crops also increase C storage in soil and enhance the green gashouse [SOURCE: Page_19]

454   emissions balance over the long term, contributing to mitigate climate change effect (Launay et al., [SOURCE: Page_19]

455   2021; Tribouillois et al., 2018). However two main possible drawbacks should be point out, particularly [SOURCE: Page_19]

456   in dry region with climate change: the reduction of drainage could increase with higher biomass [SOURCE: Page_19]

457   production of cover crops and pre-emptive competition for water and N for the following crop could [SOURCE: Page_19]

458   worsen, as suggested by Alonso-Ayuso et al. (2018). According to these authors and our results, [SOURCE: Page_19]

459   adapting the termination date of the cover crops with earlier termination should allow to avoid or limit [SOURCE: Page_20]

460   these effects and still reduce N and GHG pollution. [SOURCE: Page_20]

461   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_20]

462   Our study highlights relevant interactions between soil, climate, choice of cover crop species and its [SOURCE: Page_20]

463   management, including sowing and termination dates and residue management after termination. Cover [SOURCE: Page_20]

464   crops decrease nitrate leaching effectively but also decrease drainage by increasing evapotranspiration [SOURCE: Page_20]

465   during the fallow period and crop succession in the climate conditions of the Adour-Garonne catchment. [SOURCE: Page_20]

466   The extent of this influence depends on the amount of biomass cover crop produces, indicating that [SOURCE: Page_20]

467   adapted management could control the negative effects. In areas with low drainage in winter and little [SOURCE: Page_20]

468   rainfall in spring, late spring termination of a cover crop could result in high water and mineral N pre- [SOURCE: Page_20]

469   emption before sowing of the succeeding cash crop, thus decreasing its yield, mainly via N stress. Well- [SOURCE: Page_20]

470   managed cover crops could limit or preclude water and N stress for the succeeding cash crop and its [SOURCE: Page_20]

471   yield (i.e. late termination should be avoided in dry climates). Sowing legume cover crops is clearly [SOURCE: Page_20]

472   beneficial for yield due to the green manure effect. However, their use must be adapted to the risk of [SOURCE: Page_20]

473   nitrate leaching to be mitigated in areas with high water drainage. Bispecific mixtures could be a useful [SOURCE: Page_20]

474   compromise to limit nitrate leaching and promote green manure effects. Nevertheless, they should be [SOURCE: Page_20]

475   adapted to decrease their influence on the water balance by adapting the date of termination, particularly [SOURCE: Page_20]

476   in dry climates, where quantitative water management is an issue and can worsen with climate change. [SOURCE: Page_20]

477   Finally, cover crop management should be optimised locally depending on the ecosystem services and [SOURCE: Page_20]

478   issues targeted, and this could be done via decision-making tools using a soil-crop model. [SOURCE: Page_20]
