## 36   ABSTRACT

37   Water scarcity is a global concern, with irrigation of food crops contributing significantly to freshwater [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   depletion. Drip irrigation technology reduces water consumption but faces issues like clogging in [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   narrow discharge sections, diminishing efficiency, and increasing costs. Accurate prediction of flow [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   characteristics and understanding parameters affecting biofilm growth and particle deposition is [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   crucial for effective anti-clogging strategies. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) using turbulence [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   models can be a valuable tool. This study evaluated the accuracy and efficiency of different turbulence [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   models (standard k-ε, Reynolds Stress Model, and Large Eddy Simulation) in predicting the flow [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   characteristics of a commercial emitter in a drip irrigation system. Results showed the standard k-ε [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   model as a preferred choice for simulating mean flow characteristics and emitter discharge due to its [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   balance between accuracy and computational efficiency. However, the Large Eddy Simulation model [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   provided the most accurate results, considering the emitter discharge, unsteady flow behavior, wall [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   shear stress distribution, and oscillatory index, despite requiring more computational resources. This [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   model is valuable for understanding hydrodynamic effects on emitter clogging. The study also [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   investigated the impact of velocity fluctuations, wall shear stress, and oscillatory shear index on biofilm [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   growth and deposition in the emitter. Low shear stress in inlet and return zones reduced self-cleaning [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   ability, leading to particle and microorganism attachment. Maintaining appropriate wall shear stress [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   values in other regions proved crucial for improving anti-clogging ability. High oscillatory shear index [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   values enhanced mass transfer, nutrient mixing, diffusion within the biofilm, and self-cleaning capacity. [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   In summary, this study greatly enhances our understanding of how flow dynamics and biofilm [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   management impact drip irrigation systems. It provides practical insights for engineers and [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   practitioners, aiding in the creation of more efficient and clog-resistant systems. By optimizing these [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   dynamics and strategies, this research promotes sustainable water use in agriculture, while also [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   minimizing maintenance costs and maximizing crop yields [SOURCE: Page_3]

98   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   To date, different irrigation techniques such as surface, sprinkler, and drip irrigation are employed [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   around the world. However, compared to other technologies, drip irrigation has proven to be an [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   efficient technology for improving irrigation efficiency by up to 95%, using 30 - 60% less water than [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   surface irrigation, and improving crop yield (20 - 50%) by ensuring a uniform wetting of the soil, and [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   also convenient in distributing mineral fertilisers to cultivated fields (Abduraimova et al., 2021; van der [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   Kooij et al., 2013). In addition, drip irrigation is considered the safest irrigation technology for [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   wastewater reuse (Tripathi et al., 2019). Nevertheless, drippers that generate low flow rates (between [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   0.5 and 8 l h-1) have only narrow discharge sections that can be easily clogged thus perturbing the [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   irrigation uniformity, reducing irrigation efficiency, and inducing additional cost (Amaral et al., 2022; [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   Shi et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   In general, the structure of the emitters in drip irrigation systems is complex and characterised by a [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   small size flow path ranging from 0.2 to 1.2 mm (Al-agele et al., 2021). Thus, clogging can occur when [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   the irrigation water used contains particles or microorganisms. It has been found in the literature that [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   clogging occurs due to biomass growth, chemical precipitations, and/or physical deposits (Petit et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   2022; Shi et al., 2022). Several factors control this process including biological processes (biofilm [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   attachment, growth, and detachment) (Rizk et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022), water quality (pH, [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   conductivity, temperature, and size and particle concentration) (Liu et al., 2022; Oliveira et al., 2017) [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   and hydrodynamic conditions (Lequette et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   Several studies have investigated the impact of hydrodynamic conditions on limiting the growth of [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   biofilms and the deposition of particles within complex geometries, with particular attention being [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   paid to emitters. These studies have revealed that both velocity and wall shear stress play a crucial role [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   in determining the growth rate, detachment rate, and biofilm structure. Specifically, higher velocity [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   values have been found to limit biofilm growth and particle deposition, while lower values can promote [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   their accumulation. While high levels of shear stress can be effective in removing biofilms from [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   surfaces (self-cleaning), there exists a threshold level below which the shear stress does not have a [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   significant effect. Moreover, excessive shear stress can result in the compression of the biofilm and [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   have negative consequences as the transport and renewal of microorganisms and nutrients can be [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   facilitated (Gamri et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2019; Paul et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2020). It is important to [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   note that the threshold of shear stress required for biofilm detachment from the walls can depend on [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   various factors, such as the strain of the biofilm, the age of the biofilm, the composition of the biofilm, [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   and the properties of the irrigation emitter material (Tsvetanova, 2006; Yan et al., 2009). In general, [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   the threshold required for loosening biofilm, without achieving complete detachment, is believed to [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   fall within the range of 0.1 - 10 Pa, as demonstrated in previous work (Nejadnik et al., 2008; Wang et [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   In order to improve anti-clogging ability and to ensure high irrigation uniformity at a low discharge [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   rate, current commercial emitters usually adopt a milli-labyrinth channel approach with discharge [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   areas in the order of 1 mm^2. This induces the development of a turbulent flow regime inside the [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   channel but also creates some stagnant zones and zones with vorticity (Al-Muhammad et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   However, analysing the flow characteristics in such complex and small channels using traditional [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   experimental methods remains a challenge. Thus, in the field of drip irrigation, Computational Fluid [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   Dynamics (CFD) approaches have been used as a crucial tool for studying hydrodynamics and [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   anticlogging performance. Several experimental studies coupled with numerical simulations were [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   performed to analyse the effect of the labyrinth path on the hydraulic and anticlogging performance [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   of different emitters. For example, a CFD simulations of flow in labyrinth emitters, using laminar or [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   steady k-ε models, concluded that the movements of swirls as well as the stagnant flowing zones [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   existing in the emitter channels were the main reason for emitters clogging (Ait-Mouheb et al., 2019; [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   Li et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2018). Recently, Al-Muhammad et al. (2019) analysed the flow in a three [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   baffles-fitted labyrinth channel using the steady Reynolds stress model (RSM) modelling and the micro- [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   PIV technique. Their results showed that the flow in the labyrinth channel is turbulent. Furthermore, [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   the RSM model results have shown good concordance with the experimental data, with a difference [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   of about 5% in the prediction of the mean velocity in the mainstream flow. However, a difference that [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   possibly reaches 75% was observed in the estimation of the mean velocity. Such unsteady occurrences [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   are particularly difficult to predict with the usual steady-state modeling approaches used in previous [SOURCE: Page_8]

152   studies. The transition to an unsteady simulation technique is crucial to improve the accuracy of [SOURCE: Page_8]

153   numerical simulations and to be able to better understand the flow characteristics in a labyrinth [SOURCE: Page_8]

154   channel. In this context, the Large Eddy Simulation (LES) could provide a promising alternative for the [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   common Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) turbulence models. Previous studies have utilised [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   LES modeling to investigate the hydraulic performance of drip irrigation emitters, including those [SOURCE: Page_8]

157   equipped with milli-labyrinth channels (Feng et al., 2018; Li et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2013). The results [SOURCE: Page_8]

158   of these studies suggest that the LES model is superior to the standard k-ε (SKE) model in predicting [SOURCE: Page_8]

159   flow velocities within the labyrinth region. However, these analyses have been limited to characterising [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   the discharge curve of the emitter and identifying the vortex regions and main flow pathways within [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   the labyrinth. Furthermore, the studies have not provided a comprehensive investigation of hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   parameters, including the unsteady behavior and fluctuation of the shear stress at the wall. [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   Additionally, there has been no exploration of the potential correlation between these hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_8]

164   parameters and the clogging effect of the emitter. Therefore, further research is needed to fully [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   understand the hydraulic behavior of drip irrigation emitters and optimize their design. [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   This study aims to simulate the flow in a commercial drip irrigation emitter using three different [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   turbulence models: SKE, RSM, and LES. The simulation was performed using a 3D CFD approach. The [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   objective is to determine the unsteady turbulence model that could provide the most accurate [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   prediction of the flow in the industrial emitter by comparing the results of these simulations with [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   experimental data obtained from the discharge tests. In addition to the comparison of turbulence [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   models, the study also aims to understand the effects of the flow characteristics on the clogging [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   process that occurred in the drip irrigation emitter presented in a previous study (Lequette et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

173   2020). The simulation results were analysed in order to identify the regions of the emitter that are [SOURCE: Page_8]

174   most susceptible to clogging and the main hydrodynamic parameters that cause the clogging effect. [SOURCE: Page_8]
