8    Abstract (96 words) 9    Over the last 15 years, smallholder drip irrigation has gained an almost unanimous popularity 10   as an effective tool to achieve the combined goals of sustainable water use, food security and 11   poverty alleviation in the developing world. 77   Our explanation for the continued ‘success’ of smallholder drip irrigation is theoretically 78   anchored in the practice-based theory of innovation proposed by (Akrich, Callon, Latour, & 79   Monaghan, 2002a, 2002b). Different from 'classical' innovation analyses, the innovation theory 80   of Akrich et al. (2002a, 2002b) does not ascribe the success or failure of a technology to its 81   'intrinsic' properties (the diffusion model), but instead looks at technologies-in-context to 82   suggest that innovations are only taken up if they manage to interest more and more actors 83   (ibid., 203): this is what they call the model of interessement (Akrich et al., 2002a, 2002b) 84          This model postulates that for different actors to become interested in a technology, the 85   technology needs to be translated to fit different contexts, interests and discourses. Callon and 86   Latour (1981) have defined translation as “the negotiations, intrigues, calculations, acts of 87   persuasion and violence, thanks to which an actor or force takes, or causes to be conferred on 88   itself, authority to speak or act on behalf of another actor or force” (p.279). Translation theory 89   was originally developed as a framework to reveal that “scientific facts” are built through 90   networks; hence shedding light on the underpinnings of controversies that surround these 91   “facts”. Latour (1987) broadened the scope of the translation theory to the analysis and 92   socialisation of tools and machines. Problematisation refers to the 97   articulation of a problem on the basis of observations or experiences drawn from the “real 98   world”. 134   professionals generally consider drip irrigation a more sophisticated form of irrigation as 135   compared to, for instance, surface irrigation. Its use is commonly associated with 136   modernization and progress, and with larger and wealthier farmers. 137         Drip systems for smallholder farmers in the developing world were first developed in the 138   1970s by a US based company called Chapin Watermatics Inc. (Postel et al., 2001). Over the 139   last 20 years, several other organizations, such as the Non-Governmental Organization 140   International Development Enterprises (iDE), as well as the two major manufacturers of drip 141   irrigation equipment, NETAFIM and Jain irrigation Systems Ltd. have also engaged in efforts 142   to design and disseminate smallholder drip irrigation systems meant to irrigate relatively small 143   gardens in the developing world. Such initiatives are said to have had beneficial impacts at 144   scale on smallholders’ livelihoods in south Asia in the early to mid 2000 (for instance, Polak 145   et al., 1997; Postel et al., 2001), which in turn has raised much enthusiasm regarding the 146   prospect of drip irrigation in sub-Saharan Africa. In the literature, different names are given to 147   these smallholder drip irrigation systems: ‘low cost drip system’, ‘low pressure drip system’, 148   ‘low-tech gravity system’. For the sake of clarity, we will use the term smallholder 3 drip 149   irrigation in the remaining part of this paper. Depending on the size of the plot they can irrigate 150   and the type of water storage, Postel et al. (2001) distinguished between three different types 151   of smallholder drip systems, all referred to by the generic term “kit”: bucket kits, drum kits and 152   family drip kits. Depending on the designers and manufacturers, smallholder drip systems have 153   drip laterals of 8-16 mm in diameter and are made of thick-tube, rigid polyethylene with in- 154   line drips emitters, PVC, or flexible tape. Through 403   this campaign, smallholder drip irrigation has come to be seen as a technology that is 404   potentially instrumental in simultaneously meeting a large number of objectives. It is associated 405   with poverty alleviation, improvements in nutrition, food security, and agricultural 406   productivity; with economic growth and women's empowerment; and with water conservation, 407   environmental protection and adaptation to climate change. Through these associations, 408   smallholder drip irrigation thus acquired properties that allowed it to attach itself to a wide 409   coalition of actors, including development agents, researchers, NGO staff and some exemplary 410   farmers. These actors find in smallholder drip irrigation a way to meet their own goals: social 411   entrepreneurs looking for good causes to support; drip irrigation manufacturers looking for new 412   markets; development organisations looking for 'best practices' they can use to convince 413   funders; etc (table 4). 414         In comparison to the innovations described by Akrich et al. (2002a, 2000b), what is 415   remarkable in the case of smallholder drip irrigation is that the success of the technology 416   appears largely unrelated to whether and how end-users appreciate it. The act of translation in 417   this particular case is one between the technology and the 'development sector', rather than 418   between the technology and end-users. As most actors agree on the potential and promises of 419   the technology, studies that report dissemination, adoption and use challenges are but calls to 420   reiterate (and adjust) past efforts. Actual practices (or the lack thereof) by farmers have thus 421   become secondary to the imagery of success that surrounds the technology. 422         This study holds important lessons for policy makers. First, the need for in-depth 423   independent evaluation of drip irrigation projects that would reflect the perspectives of 424   smallholders and not only of the promoters of the technology. Second, the need to acquire a 425   deeper understanding of how different actors understand and measure what makes the success 426   of a technology before investing in or supporting any technological package. Public campaigns, 427   websites, news stories, and experimental results indeed do not necessarily reflect smallholders' 428   perspectives, which should remain the guiding principles of any development intervention. 429 430 431 432 433   Acknowledgements [SOURCE: Page_14]
