The role of development brokers [SOURCE: Page_2]

Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

Smallholder drip irrigation is widely held as a promising technology for water saving, poverty [SOURCE: Page_2]

reduction and food security despite a dearth of evidence of benefits to farmers, especially in [SOURCE: Page_2]

sub-Saharan Africa. In this paper, we document three development programs promoting drip [SOURCE: Page_2]

irrigation in Burkina Faso. Using Actor Network Theory and insights from critical [SOURCE: Page_2]

development studies, we show that development brokers play a key role in aligning interests, [SOURCE: Page_2]

shaping activities and interpreting project outcomes. They are accountable towards each [SOURCE: Page_2]

other rather than to farmers. This means that success is interpreted through development [SOURCE: Page_2]

agencies lenses and with the intention of continuing involvement in future projects. Small [SOURCE: Page_2]

farmers’ interests and uptake of the technology are of secondary importance. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

Drip irrigation is a method to supply water in small and frequent quantities to the root zone of crops [SOURCE: Page_3]

through a system of perforated plastic pipes. Initially associated with capital-intensive forms of [SOURCE: Page_3]

agriculture, efforts to specifically design drip irrigation systems for smallholder farmers began in [SOURCE: Page_3]

the late 1990s, first for small scale farmers in South Asian countries. Smallholder drip systems take [SOURCE: Page_3]

the form of “drip kits”1 (See Polak, Nanes, & Adhikari, 1997; Postel, Polak, Gonzales, & Keller, [SOURCE: Page_3]

2001) and exist in different types (see figure 1). Regardless of the type and the manufacturer, the [SOURCE: Page_3]

overall principle is the same: the ‘drip kit’ operates under low pressure to provide localized irrigation [SOURCE: Page_3]

to a small plot, from a few square meters to a few hundred square meters. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Smallholder drip irrigation is widely presented as a promising option for poverty alleviation and [SOURCE: Page_3]

sustainable intensification because it allows to efficiently use water for the production of high-value [SOURCE: Page_3]

vegetable crops (for instance Pasternak, Nikiema, Senbeto, Dougbedji, & Woltering, 2006; Postel [SOURCE: Page_3]

et al., 2001; Woltering, Ibrahim, Pasternak, & Ndjeunga, 2011). As such, and from the mid 2000s [SOURCE: Page_3]

onwards, international research institutes, non-governmental organisations, national governments [SOURCE: Page_3]

and aid agencies have started promoting it in sub-Saharan Africa. Although often dubbed as an [SOURCE: Page_4]

‘agricultural water management strategy’, smallholder drip irrigation is significantly different from [SOURCE: Page_4]

the soil and water conservation strategies that have long been promoted in sub-Saharan Africa. The [SOURCE: Page_4]

latter aimed at rehabilitating degraded soils through better control of rainfall and runoff and [SOURCE: Page_4]

improving soil fertility management; they often built on ‘indigenous’ practices to increase the [SOURCE: Page_4]

productivity of rainfed cereals, the mainstay of rural livelihoods (see for instance Douxchamps, [SOURCE: Page_4]

Ayantunde, & Barron, 2014; Reij, Tappan, & Belemvire, 2005). Smallholder drip irrigation, in [SOURCE: Page_4]

contrast, is an alternative method to the calabash and watering cans that are commonly used for [SOURCE: Page_4]

vegetable gardening, which is an off-season activity conducted on small plots and depending on [SOURCE: Page_4]

labour and water availability. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Burkina Faso is one of the countries where development initiatives centred on the promotion of [SOURCE: Page_4]

smallholder drip irrigation have multiplied over the last 10 years (see Wanvoeke, Venot, [SOURCE: Page_4]

Zwarteveen, & de Fraiture, 2015). This multiplication of development projects is testimony of a [SOURCE: Page_4]

widely shared enthusiasm for smallholder drip irrigation among a variety of actors: national [SOURCE: Page_4]

government, local and international NGOs, private companies, research organisations, aid agencies. [SOURCE: Page_4]

So far, this enthusiasm is not backed up with evidence of success in farmers’ fields. Our own [SOURCE: Page_4]

observations are in line with studies that suggest that the use of drip irrigation remains limited to [SOURCE: Page_4]

experimental or pilot sites. Farmers seldom use drip irrigation without external support and almost [SOURCE: Page_4]

invariably stop using it once development projects come to an end (Belder, Rohrbach, Twomlow, & [SOURCE: Page_4]

Senzanje, 2007; Kulecho & Weatherhead, 2005). Similar to what has been observed in many other [SOURCE: Page_4]

cases in which specific crops or technologies are being promoted by development agencies, this lack [SOURCE: Page_4]

of uptake and enthusiasm of farmers can be attributed to a number of factors. These include the still [SOURCE: Page_4]

high costs of initial investments (Dittoh, Akuriba, Issaka, & Bhattarai, 2010); technical problems [SOURCE: Page_4]

(with emitters and filters clogging and deterioration of material) due to unreliable and low quality [SOURCE: Page_5]

water supply and harsh environmental conditions (for instance Friedlander, Tal, & Lazarovitch, [SOURCE: Page_5]

2013); lack of spare parts, supply chains and support mechanisms (Belder et al., 2007); difficulties [SOURCE: Page_5]

to access markets (Kulecho & Weatherhead, 2006); a lack of capacity and knowledge on the part of [SOURCE: Page_5]

smallholders and a misfit between the technology and the cultural setting (Garb & Friedlander, [SOURCE: Page_5]

2014). New projects often explicitly set out to address the above constraints, for instance through [SOURCE: Page_5]

technological improvements, more integrated approaches (including micro-credit schemes and links [SOURCE: Page_5]

to market), enhanced participation, etc. [SOURCE: Page_5]

We turn towards the field of Anthropology of Development, and more specifically towards recent [SOURCE: Page_5]

ethnographic studies of aid, to propose a different explanation of the discrepancy between the lack [SOURCE: Page_5]

of results on farmers’ fields and the continued enthusiasm of development actors for smallholder [SOURCE: Page_5]

drip irrigation. We focus on the ‘making’ of on-going development efforts around this technology [SOURCE: Page_5]

and draw from Mosse (2005) suggestion that it is the interpretation of events, rather than the events [SOURCE: Page_5]

themselves, that is pivotal in creating policy enthusiasm for particular development approaches or [SOURCE: Page_5]

technologies. It follows that the success of a project (or of a technological package) not so much [SOURCE: Page_5]

stems from what it does for its end-users, but instead is the result of the existence and activities of a [SOURCE: Page_5]

supportive coalition: network of actors devoting their energies to maintain a coherent representation [SOURCE: Page_5]

of that specific project or technological package. In our case, this representation is that of drip [SOURCE: Page_5]

irrigation as a suitable technology (because of its size and affordability, and because of the efficiency [SOURCE: Page_5]

with which it uses water) to meet the needs of smallholder farmers in sub-Saharan Africa, and thus [SOURCE: Page_5]

to realize the goals of water saving (or environmental conservation) and poverty alleviation, while [SOURCE: Page_5]

also helping improve food security. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Our analysis thus aims to unravel the network of development actors who shape and underpin the [SOURCE: Page_6]

promotion of drip irrigation in Burkina Faso, a Sahelian country of West Africa. We describe the [SOURCE: Page_6]

strategies they use and the reasons they have to do so, as well as the relations of interdependency [SOURCE: Page_6]

that tie these multiple actors together. The next section further develops the analytical framework of [SOURCE: Page_6]

the study around the notion of brokerage as discussed by anthropology of development and science [SOURCE: Page_6]

and technology scholars. We then present our methodology, before a detailed description of three [SOURCE: Page_6]

development programs promoting drip irrigation in Burkina Faso. The discussion draws generic [SOURCE: Page_6]

lessons about the networks and practices of drip irrigation development in Burkina Faso. Finally, a [SOURCE: Page_6]

short conclusion reflects on what brokerage means in the context of development, beyond the [SOURCE: Page_6]

facilitation of an innovation process. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Brokerage and translation [SOURCE: Page_6]

In Burkina Faso, and more generally in West Africa, drip irrigation reaches smallholder farmers [SOURCE: Page_6]

through development projects, which still are the preferred mode of intervention of development [SOURCE: Page_6]

agencies and non-governmental organisations despite repeated calls for programmatic and sectoral [SOURCE: Page_6]

support since the Paris declaration in 2005. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Anthropology of development scholars have long highlighted the key role played by development [SOURCE: Page_6]

brokers (courtiers en développement in French) in shaping the reality of these projects (Bierschenk, [SOURCE: Page_6]

Chauveau, & Olivier de Sardan, 2000, 2002). They have proposed understanding of brokerage as a [SOURCE: Page_6]

process, going beyond the normative representation generally associated to this activity of [SOURCE: Page_6]

intermediation, as for instance found in rural innovation studies (Klerkx, Hall, & Leeuwis, 2009). [SOURCE: Page_6]

Discussing brokerage in relation to place-specific interventions, scholars of the anthropology of [SOURCE: Page_6]

development have defined development brokers as these “project’s local social carriers, at the [SOURCE: Page_6]

interface between the people (the ‘target group’) aimed at by the project and the development [SOURCE: Page_7]

institutions” (Bierschenk et al., 2000, p. 8). Development brokers are the “social actors implanted in [SOURCE: Page_7]

a local arena […] who serve as intermediaries who drain off […] external resources in the form of [SOURCE: Page_7]

development aid” (Bierschenk et al., 2000, p. 8). They can be extension agents, project staff, [SOURCE: Page_7]

community leaders, locally elected officials, etc. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Within critical development studies, ideas of brokerage also exist, but do not just apply to the ‘local’. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Drawing from Actor Network Theory (ANT), Lewis and Mosse (2006) for instance talk of [SOURCE: Page_7]

‘translation’ to refer to an activity that encompasses the production and stabilization of knowledge, [SOURCE: Page_7]

meanings and interpretations. In a similar vein, Rottenburg (2009) sheds light on the important role [SOURCE: Page_7]

that development experts and consultants have in ‘translating’ projects activities and outcomes as [SOURCE: Page_7]

they often mediate between funding agencies and national governments. Lewis and Mosse (2006) [SOURCE: Page_7]

go one step further in arguing that the mutual enrolment and interlocking of interests within networks [SOURCE: Page_7]

of actors (funding and governmental agencies, NGOs, research centres, private companies, pilot [SOURCE: Page_7]

farmers, etc.) is what produces project ‘realities’: multiple actors engage in joint work to produce [SOURCE: Page_7]

and sustain a coherent vision regarding a specific sphere of intervention. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Through their use of the notion of translation, these scholars in critical development studies deal [SOURCE: Page_7]

with themes that have long been central to science and technology studies and innovation studies. [SOURCE: Page_7]

These two fields are particularly relevant to us given our study object, drip irrigation, which has a [SOURCE: Page_7]

strong material dimension. Scholars in innovation system studies tend to adopt a more structural [SOURCE: Page_7]

view on brokerage than development anthropologists. They indeed see brokers as ‘key facilitators’ [SOURCE: Page_7]

for the diffusion of technological innovations, or in other words, for their success (see among others [SOURCE: Page_7]

Klerkx et al., 2009). Science and technology scholars and notably ANT theorists, on the other hand, [SOURCE: Page_7]

highlight that the very definition of the success of a technology is the result of creating interest and [SOURCE: Page_8]

coalition building (Akrich, Callon, Latour, & Monaghan, 2002). [SOURCE: Page_8]

Though using slightly different definitions of the terms brokerage and translation and though used [SOURCE: Page_8]

in relation to different study objects, scholars in critical development studies, innovation studies, [SOURCE: Page_8]

and Science and Technology Studies all shed light on a similar phenomenon, that of ‘(inter)- [SOURCE: Page_8]

mediation’ within a network of actors (people, organization) and objects. Because of the key [SOURCE: Page_8]

importance of intermediation in shaping policy interventions, development projects, or [SOURCE: Page_8]

technological artefacts, they argue that intermediation deserves thorough analysis. [SOURCE: Page_8]

In this paper we analyze the process of intermediation for the case of smallholder drip irrigation in [SOURCE: Page_8]

Burkina Faso. We use three case studies to illustrate the role of brokers, interpreting it beyond that [SOURCE: Page_8]

of facilitating the diffusion of this new technology. We map the actors involved in drip irrigation, [SOURCE: Page_8]

describe their activities and identify their relations of dependency and accountability. By doing so, [SOURCE: Page_8]

we set out to generate a different interpretation of the fate and travels of smallholder drip irrigation. [SOURCE: Page_8]

To date most, if not all, drip irrigation studies have focused on understanding the constraints and [SOURCE: Page_8]

opportunities smallholders in the developing world face in using drip irrigation. In our analysis, we [SOURCE: Page_8]

turn the research gaze to those promoting the technology in an attempt to shed light on, how ‘it [SOURCE: Page_8]

makes its way’ to them. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_25]

The number of development actors involved in the promotion of smallholder drip irrigation in [SOURCE: Page_25]

Burkina Faso has been increasing since it was first introduced in 2004. Over the last decade, we [SOURCE: Page_25]

identified eight major development projects involved in the dissemination of drip kits. Despite a [SOURCE: Page_25]

lack of systematic data on uptake and livelihood impacts, aid agencies, government officials and [SOURCE: Page_25]

NGOs staff widely present drip irrigation as a promising innovation for smallholders, one that allows [SOURCE: Page_25]

turning subsistence farmers into small agricultural entrepreneurs. We show that such positive [SOURCE: Page_25]

imagery comes about and is sustained through the strategic efforts of multiple development brokers [SOURCE: Page_25]

and is reinforced via its circulation within this coalition. [SOURCE: Page_25]

In conventional innovation studies, brokers are seen as key facilitators who form an essential link [SOURCE: Page_25]

between the providers/funders of an innovation and its end-users (see for example Klerkx et al. [SOURCE: Page_25]

2009). They are a ‘positive force’. In the case of drip irrigation, the role of brokers and intermediaries [SOURCE: Page_25]

has for instance been shown to be pivotal to the rapid expansion of this technology in the North [SOURCE: Page_25]

African context (see for instance, Benouniche, Errahj, & Kuper, 2014; Poncet, Kuper, & Chiche, [SOURCE: Page_25]

2010). In the context of development, conventional innovation theory position brokers or [SOURCE: Page_25]

intermediaries as dependent on and accountable to both international development agencies [SOURCE: Page_25]

(upwards) as well as to smallholder farmers (downwards). In this setting, negative feedback on the [SOURCE: Page_25]

usefulness of an innovation is immediately felt by brokers and project implementers: if smallholder [SOURCE: Page_25]

farmers would not be using drip kits because they do not see the advantages, they would stop buying [SOURCE: Page_25]

them, putting local NGO’s and retailers out of business. Project implementers would not be able to [SOURCE: Page_25]

reach project goals and ultimately, funding agencies would stop financing these projects. However, [SOURCE: Page_25]

what we observed in Burkina Faso deviates substantially from this theory. We combined practice [SOURCE: Page_25]

based theory of innovation (Akrich et al., 2002) with insights from Anthropology of Development [SOURCE: Page_26]

(Bierschenk et al., 2000; Lewis & Mosse, 2006) to explain why. [SOURCE: Page_26]

From our case studies we conclude that development brokers do much more than bridging the gap [SOURCE: Page_26]

between those who design, fund and implement projects and smallholder farmers, i.e. the intended [SOURCE: Page_26]

beneficiaries of innovations: they actively contribute to shaping the representations and successful [SOURCE: Page_26]

outcomes of development projects. A large diversity of actors, such as local and international [SOURCE: Page_26]

NGO’s, government agencies, private companies and local retailers act as development brokers. In [SOURCE: Page_26]

fact, there is not one single (type of) broker but, rather, a brokering network. This network of actors, [SOURCE: Page_26]

often supported and sustained by personal relationships, facilitates the flows of money, equipment, [SOURCE: Page_26]

ideas and staff (i.e. experience and knowledge) between different projects, and thus substantially [SOURCE: Page_26]

influences project design and implementation modalities. Actors within the network create [SOURCE: Page_26]

coalitions and interdependencies (for example, by signing contracts for the provisioning of [SOURCE: Page_26]

equipment or training). Although their objectives of engaging in drip irrigation projects may be [SOURCE: Page_26]

different, these objectives are nevertheless compatible or made compatible (i.e. selling equipment [SOURCE: Page_26]

for profit, receiving funding, creating a market, reputation). [SOURCE: Page_26]

These actors are united in their desire to build and maintain an image of a ‘successful innovation’, [SOURCE: Page_26]

something that is achieved by reporting high numbers of drip kits disseminated, showcasing [SOURCE: Page_26]

demonstration sites, and telling success stories of pilot farmers on radio and television. Rather than [SOURCE: Page_26]

to smallholders, actors in the network are accountable to each other and to those who provide funds [SOURCE: Page_26]

for project activities. The consequence is that the actor network, which only includes a few select [SOURCE: Page_26]

pilot farmers held as successful examples of innovators and entrepreneurial farmers, shapes new [SOURCE: Page_26]

project activities and determines the ways success is measured and reported. [SOURCE: Page_26]

Thus shifting the analytical focus away from smallholder farmers (project beneficiaries) to the actors [SOURCE: Page_27]

who promote smallholder drip irrigation yields a different explanation of why the technology [SOURCE: Page_27]

continues to be hailed as a success in spite of little evidence of impacts in farmers’ fields. We show [SOURCE: Page_27]

that drip irrigation development projects work as resource arenas in which multiple actors tap to [SOURCE: Page_27]

fulfil their objectives (Bierschenk et al., 2000; Meyer, 1995; Platteau, 2004). The motives of these [SOURCE: Page_27]

actors are both altruistic - a desire to help farmers - and pragmatic - the wish to ensure personal [SOURCE: Page_27]

advancement, status, or remuneration. The positive imagery and discursive success of drip irrigation [SOURCE: Page_27]

in the developing world is not only linked to the technical attributes of the technology, but also [SOURCE: Page_27]

importantly stems from the fact that drip irrigation serves important other functions: it helps generate [SOURCE: Page_27]

hopes and funds; it creates new markets; it helps build and mobilise social networks, etc. [SOURCE: Page_27]

Our analysis of the making and practices of development projects and policies has relevance beyond [SOURCE: Page_27]

the case of drip irrigation in pointing at how feed-back loops and accountability relations become [SOURCE: Page_27]

distorted when development projects and actors overtly depend on outside sources of funding. As [SOURCE: Page_27]

for Burkina Faso, our case studies highlight the still central role that public agencies have in shaping [SOURCE: Page_27]

the way the epistemic community of development agents works, in spite of the private sector rhetoric [SOURCE: Page_27]

of many development aid agencies. More broadly, our findings point to a worrisome lack of [SOURCE: Page_27]

downward accountability in development cooperation. Concerns about reputations and survival [SOURCE: Page_27]

create pressures to report success, providing strong incentives to all involved to use very selective [SOURCE: Page_27]

measures and accounts of impact. Rather than reporting to end-users or final clients, or being [SOURCE: Page_27]

influenced by the opinions and feed-back of ultimate beneficiaries, development actors depend for [SOURCE: Page_27]

the continuation of their operations on the approval of each other and of funders. This vicious web [SOURCE: Page_27]

of mutual dependencies can only be broken by allowing and indeed actively promoting beneficiaries [SOURCE: Page_27]

to co-determine how development funds are (to be) used. [SOURCE: Page_27]
