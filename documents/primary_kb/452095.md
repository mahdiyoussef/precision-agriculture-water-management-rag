Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

The European Water Framework Directive (WFD) aims at improving the ecological status of [SOURCE: Page_2]

continental waters, including man-made water bodies. Thereby it raises the question of the [SOURCE: Page_2]

reference conditions for reservoirs. A number of limnologists consider reservoirs as [SOURCE: Page_2]

intermediate systems between lakes and rivers. Hence, the aim of this study is to contribute to [SOURCE: Page_2]

the implementation of the WFD by comparing the fish communities across these three types of [SOURCE: Page_2]

ecosystems. This was achieved using fish sampling data from 21 natural lakes, 50 reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_2]

and 549 river stations. The lists of occurring species are very similar between lakes and [SOURCE: Page_2]

reservoirs, and appear as a subset of the species occurring in rivers. Lakes and reservoirs are [SOURCE: Page_2]

also very similar in terms of common and rare species. Conversely, the comparison of [SOURCE: Page_2]

community structures (summarised by correspondence analysis axes) supports the hypothesis [SOURCE: Page_2]

of an intermediate position of reservoirs between lake and river systems. This latter result could [SOURCE: Page_2]

reflect the effect of large-scale processes undergone by freshwater ecosystems whatever their [SOURCE: Page_2]

type and the non-independence of water bodies within their catchments, particularly when [SOURCE: Page_2]

considering the communities of highly mobile organisms like fishes. Although the major [SOURCE: Page_2]

conservation concerns are about natural systems, artificial ones should also be considered in [SOURCE: Page_2]

monitoring and assessment programs in order to allow efficient catchment-scale management [SOURCE: Page_2]

policies. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

The objective of the European Water Framework Directive (WFD) is to obtain the good [SOURCE: Page_3]

ecological status of natural continental water bodies and the good « ecological potential » (EP) [SOURCE: Page_3]

for artificial and heavily modified water bodies (European Community, 2000). Potentially, [SOURCE: Page_3]

various methods can be implemented to define the maximum EP, however, the ECOSTAT [SOURCE: Page_3]

working group proposed that « the maximum EP biological conditions should reflect, as far as [SOURCE: Page_3]

possible, the biological conditions associated with the closest comparable natural water body [SOURCE: Page_3]

type at reference conditions » (ECOSTAT, 2003). Accepting this position raises the question of [SOURCE: Page_3]

how to choose the relevant natural hydrosystem type that will serve as a reference for reservoirs, [SOURCE: Page_3]

which is a cross-ecosystem question. However, cross-ecosystem studies are not very common [SOURCE: Page_3]

despite their interest in addressing the issue of generalisation in ecological patterns, mechanisms [SOURCE: Page_3]

and theories (Pace, 1991). For example, cross-ecosystem studies provided a significant [SOURCE: Page_3]

contribution to the debate on the relative strength of bottom-up vs. top-down controls of food [SOURCE: Page_3]

chains (Chase, 2000; Pace et al., 1999; Shurin et al., 2002; Strong, 1992), on the response of [SOURCE: Page_3]

ecosystems to disturbances (Fisher and Grimm, 1991) or on fisheries science and management [SOURCE: Page_3]

(F.A.O., 1978). These kinds of studies also proved to be informative both on basic and applied [SOURCE: Page_3]

issues when comparing freshwater ecosystem types (F.A.O., 1978; Ryder, 1978; Ryder and [SOURCE: Page_3]

Pesendorfer, 1989). [SOURCE: Page_3]

Reservoirs are frequently termed artificial lakes and satisfy some of the definition criteria of [SOURCE: Page_3]

lakes (Politou et al., 1993). Most of the major processes, i.e. internal mixing, nutrient uptake, [SOURCE: Page_3]

primary production or predator-prey interactions, occur in both lakes and reservoirs (Thornton, [SOURCE: Page_3]

1990). However, in a review that contrasted the properties of natural lakes and reservoirs, [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Wetzel, 1990) opposed a long list of ecological, hydrological, physico-chemical and [SOURCE: Page_3]

morphological differences between these types of water bodies. Some limnologists also [SOURCE: Page_3]

considered reservoirs as intermediate ecosystems between riverine and lacustrine environments [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Gelwick and Matthews, 1990; Kimmel et al., 1990; Ryder, 1978) with regard to morphology [SOURCE: Page_3]

and hydrology. Reservoirs can also be considered as having an intermediate status with regard [SOURCE: Page_4]

to nutrient and organic matter supply (Kimmel et al., 1990). [SOURCE: Page_4]

Comparative ecological studies between rivers and reservoirs are not common in the scientific [SOURCE: Page_4]

literature, maybe because they give rise to sampling issues. However, these systems are not so [SOURCE: Page_4]

contrasting. River systems typically encompass both lentic and lotic waters and the upper zone [SOURCE: Page_4]

of reservoirs is generally riverine (Thornton, 1990). In fact, the transition between typical [SOURCE: Page_4]

riverine conditions and truly still waters takes place along a spatio-temporal continuum of [SOURCE: Page_4]

hydraulic conditions. Therefore, conventional thresholds are used to define the geographical [SOURCE: Page_4]

boundary between a reservoir and its tributaries or to classify the reaches of rivers influenced by [SOURCE: Page_4]

a weir as lentic or lotic. However, ecological processes ignore these conventions and the issue [SOURCE: Page_4]

of the «closest comparable natural water body» has to be addressed to assess reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_4]

reference conditions for each of the biological elements taken into account in the WFD. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Although the WFD considers reservoirs as parts of the «lake-type water bodies», there is a risk [SOURCE: Page_4]

that referring to lakes to assess the EP of reservoirs without considering alternatives could be a [SOURCE: Page_4]

methodological mistake. Therefore, we investigated whether the analyses of fish community [SOURCE: Page_4]

patterns in both lakes and rivers could be useful to assess the reference conditions for fish [SOURCE: Page_4]

communities of reservoir systems. Thus, we developed a comparative study of the attributes of [SOURCE: Page_4]

fish communities in these three types of systems. The hypothesis that reservoirs are intermediate [SOURCE: Page_4]

systems between rivers and lakes, considered in a fish community perspective, leads to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

hypothesis that they display intermediate patterns of 1. species occurrences 2. species [SOURCE: Page_4]

commonness and rarity 3. fish community structure. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Materials and methods [SOURCE: Page_4]

The data set [SOURCE: Page_4]

The lakes and reservoirs data set was compiled from various sources (mostly unpublished study [SOURCE: Page_4]

reports). In the absence of a national monitoring network, these studies addressed local [SOURCE: Page_4]

concerns. Most of the surveys were carried out with gillnets but we also used fish censuses [SOURCE: Page_4]

produced when the reservoirs were drained. Eventually, 50 reservoirs and 21 natural lakes were [SOURCE: Page_5]

included. They range from sea level to 1100masl. Mountain lakes and reservoirs have been [SOURCE: Page_5]

excluded from the analysis because they have been proven to have very different fish [SOURCE: Page_5]

populations compared to lowland sites, mainly as a result of human-mediated introductions [SOURCE: Page_5]

(Argillier et al., 2002). A more thorough description of the data set was made in previous papers [SOURCE: Page_5]

(Argillier et al., 2002; Irz et al., 2004). [SOURCE: Page_5]

The 549 river stations data were extracted from the database held by the Conseil Supérieur de la [SOURCE: Page_5]

Pêche, covering a period of 13 years of survey (1985 to 1998). All sites were sampled using [SOURCE: Page_5]

electric fishing techniques during low flow periods. The size of each sampled site was sufficient [SOURCE: Page_5]

to encompass complete sets of the local characteristic river habitat (generally > 100m for [SOURCE: Page_5]

wading sites and >500m for boat sites ((Yoder and Smith, 1999))). Two main sampling [SOURCE: Page_5]

strategies were used, depending on river size. When possible (river depth < 0.7m), river reaches [SOURCE: Page_5]

were sampled by wading (one passage). In large rivers, sampling was done by boat mainly in [SOURCE: Page_5]

near shore areas. We only retained one fishing occasion per site. Sites belonging to the trout [SOURCE: Page_5]

zone and sites characterized by the presence of only two species were excluded. [SOURCE: Page_5]

To limit the biases induced by the differences in sampling methods, fish communities were [SOURCE: Page_5]

characterised by the presence/absence of the species. The river stations are well distributed [SOURCE: Page_5]

throughout France, but the distribution of lakes and reservoirs is patchier (Figure 1). The main [SOURCE: Page_5]

characteristics of the study sites display a strong heterogeneity (Table 1). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Sampling adequacy [SOURCE: Page_5]

One of the major concerns associated with comparisons of very different types of environments [SOURCE: Page_5]

is the differences in sampling scheme. No single method allows an accurate fish sampling of [SOURCE: Page_5]

both lentic and lotic systems, and it seems that the absence of the eel in both lakes and [SOURCE: Page_5]

reservoirs is a consequence of the use of gillnets. The bitterling is also frequently too small to be [SOURCE: Page_5]

effectively caught by gillnets unless they comprise very fine mesh (which was not the case in [SOURCE: Page_5]

our data set, the lower limit generally being 10mm knot-to-knot). [SOURCE: Page_5]

However, it is quite commonly recognised that gillnetting is the most appropriate technique to [SOURCE: Page_6]

despite the admitted sampling biases. Using a parallel with (Pielou, 1977) consideration on the [SOURCE: Page_6]

sampling biases in biogeographic studies, we could state that cross-ecosystem type comparative [SOURCE: Page_6]

studies require the assumption that the signal-to-noise ratio of the data is high enough to ensure [SOURCE: Page_6]

that, by appropriate statistical analysis, the signal may be recovered and correctly interpreted. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Analyses [SOURCE: Page_6]

The choice was made to use three different descriptors of lacustrine communities (list of [SOURCE: Page_6]

occurring species, species occurrence rates and community structure) in order to obtain [SOURCE: Page_6]

complementary views on fish community patterns (Samuels and Drake, 1997). The lists of [SOURCE: Page_6]

occurring species were simply compared by distinguishing those that were specific of a [SOURCE: Page_6]

particular type of environment from those that were more widespread, and by calculating [SOURCE: Page_6]

Jaccard’s distances between the three types of systems based on the species occurrences. [SOURCE: Page_6]

In order to compare the patterns of rarity or commonness of species among the three types of [SOURCE: Page_6]

systems, the relationship between the occurrence rates of the species in lakes, reservoirs and [SOURCE: Page_6]

rivers were assessed using Spearman rank correlation. Cross-ecosystem similarities in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

identity of the dominant and rare species are expected to produce positive correlations. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Then the fish occurrence matrices were analysed by means of Correspondence Analysis (CA) [SOURCE: Page_6]

for each type of system. This ordination method allows a reduction of the dimensionality of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

data set (Ter Braak, 1995). Hence, the first two CA axis of each analysis were considered as [SOURCE: Page_6]

summaries of a primary and secondary between-site community structure. The six axes were [SOURCE: Page_6]

Discussion [SOURCE: Page_9]

Cross-ecosystems comparisons [SOURCE: Page_9]

Our initial hypothesis was that the fish communities of reservoirs would display intermediate [SOURCE: Page_9]

patterns between those of lakes and river stations with respect to 1. species occurrences 2. [SOURCE: Page_9]

species commonness and rarity 3. fish community structure. Considering the first two points, [SOURCE: Page_9]

reservoir fish communities are clearly more similar to the communities of natural lakes than to [SOURCE: Page_9]

those of river stations. The lists of species dwelling in the two types of lentic systems are [SOURCE: Page_9]

almost identical and clearly divergent from that of rivers. Apart from the two lake specialists [SOURCE: Page_9]

(Salvelinus alpinus and Coregonus sp.), the list of lentic species is a subset of the lotic species [SOURCE: Page_9]

list, which is likely to result from historical influences. The western European fish fauna has [SOURCE: Page_9]

been quite depauperated since the last ice age drove many species to local extinction. At the [SOURCE: Page_9]

scale of a large catchment (i.e. with sufficient latitudinal and/or altitudinal extension), a [SOURCE: Page_9]

population of a river species can respond to climatic variations through an adaptation of its [SOURCE: Page_9]

geographic range (Gaston, 2003), for example by reaching refugial zones. Conversely, lakes [SOURCE: Page_9]

are frequently regarded as biogeographic islands due to their relative isolation from each other [SOURCE: Page_9]

(Barbour and Brown, 1974; Magnuson, 1976; Magnuson et al., 1998). Thus, typical lacustrine [SOURCE: Page_9]

species have restricted means to escape an environment becoming less and less favourable. [SOURCE: Page_9]

Therefore, it appears reasonable to assume that lacustrine species, if they existed in Western [SOURCE: Page_9]

Europe before the last ice age, have undergone higher extinction rates than riverine ones. [SOURCE: Page_9]

Furthermore, the post-glacial westward re-colonisation of fishes from the Danubian refugial [SOURCE: Page_9]

zones occurred through the hydrographic network, which means that even for those lentic [SOURCE: Page_9]

species that maintained populations in refugial zones, re-colonisation through this unfavourable [SOURCE: Page_9]

network of flowing waters was unlikely. [SOURCE: Page_9]

The primary structure of reservoir fish communities displays similarities with all four axes of [SOURCE: Page_9]

the analysis carried out on rivers and lakes, which supports our initial hypothesis. Drawing [SOURCE: Page_9]

conclusions would have been easier with clear correspondences with either and not both the [SOURCE: Page_9]

Conversely, the secondary structure of fish communities in the two types of natural systems [SOURCE: Page_10]

Consequently, the observation of natural systems is of no help in analysing the secondary [SOURCE: Page_10]

structure of reservoirs’ fish communities. [SOURCE: Page_10]

The fact that the patterns in fish community structure are not so contrasted between the three [SOURCE: Page_10]

types of ecosystems could further reveal that lakes, rivers and reservoirs are not independent [SOURCE: Page_10]

from each other. They are all components of catchments and interconnected in a network. The [SOURCE: Page_10]

catchment corresponds to the natural borders within which freshwater fish populations express [SOURCE: Page_10]

their dynamics. Several of the species that were found in lakes and reservoirs are considered as [SOURCE: Page_10]

typically riverine and do not reproduce in these systems (Penczak and Kruk, 2000). Hence, [SOURCE: Page_10]

considering stream reaches, lakes or reservoirs as isolated from each other does not take into [SOURCE: Page_10]

consideration the high mobility of fishes compared to most other freshwater organisms, [SOURCE: Page_10]

Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_11]

The initial hypothesis that reservoirs were intermediate environments between natural lakes [SOURCE: Page_11]

and rivers was only partially supported by the results obtained in our study. We highlighted [SOURCE: Page_11]

both differences and similarities in the patterns of fish communities among ecosystem types. [SOURCE: Page_11]

The conclusions drawn on the basis of species occurrences, commonness and rarity, and [SOURCE: Page_11]

community structure were quite different, thereby confirming the complementarity of these [SOURCE: Page_11]

descriptors of the communities (that are also likely to respond differently to anthropogenic [SOURCE: Page_11]

stresses). To some extent, this is also the spirit of the WFD that states that several attributes of [SOURCE: Page_11]

fish communities must be taken into consideration for the assessment of the ecological status of [SOURCE: Page_11]

water bodies. Using continuous descriptors of the hydraulic conditions (e.g. water velocity, [SOURCE: Page_11]

Froude number) is certainly a perspective that should allow the simultaneous consideration of a [SOURCE: Page_11]

wide array of hydrosystems regardless their type. [SOURCE: Page_11]

The present study carried out on fish does not mean that the other biological compartments [SOURCE: Page_11]

follow equivalent rules, but suggests that the a priori choice of natural lakes as references for [SOURCE: Page_11]

reservoirs may be questionable. [SOURCE: Page_11]

Despite the close deadlines scheduled in the implementation of the WFD, the studies aiming at [SOURCE: Page_11]

proposing reference conditions for reservoirs are rare as most efforts have been concentrated on [SOURCE: Page_11]

natural environments that represent major conservation concerns. However, reservoirs also [SOURCE: Page_11]

represent important environments particularly in southern Europe (in France, 500 of the 600 [SOURCE: Page_11]

lake-type water bodies over 50ha are artificial) that should not be neglected if one wishes an [SOURCE: Page_11]

efficient catchment-scale management policy. [SOURCE: Page_11]
