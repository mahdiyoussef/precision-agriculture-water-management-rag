30   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   Background & aims: Insufficient screening and management of malnutrition leads to increase [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   morbidity and mortality. Aims: to evaluate the characteristics and clinical outcomes of [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   malnourished patients referred to a specialized outpatient clinic for the management of [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   malnutrition (primary); to compare the latter according to malnutrition severity; to determine [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   the factors associated with severe malnutrition and mortality; to compare the consistency of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   decided medical nutrition therapy with the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   Metabolism (ESPEN) guidelines (secondary). [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   Methods: This retrospective, observational study included malnourished adults referred for the [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   first time to the specialized nutrition consultation of a teaching University Hospital during 50 [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   months. Malnutrition was diagnosed according to the Global Leadership Initiative on [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   Malnutrition criteria, including body composition assessment by bioimpedance analysis. [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   Statistics: Chi^2 or Fisher, Student or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests; multivariable logistic regression [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   to assess the factors associated with severe malnutrition and mortality. [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   Results: 108 malnourished adults were included: 74% had severe malnutrition. The main [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   secondary diagnosis was digestive cancer (48.2%). During the follow-up (median=70 days) [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   after the first nutritional consultation, 11% of patients were admitted at hospital, 19% had [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   infections and 23.1% died, without any difference according to malnutrition severity. Severely [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   malnourished patients had lower body mass index, a smaller fat mass index (FMI) (4.6±1.8 vs [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   6.0±2.5kg/m²; p=0.01), and a higher level of total body water (64.7±7.1 vs 60.6±5.4%; p=0.02), [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   compared to moderately malnourished individuals. A low FMI (odds ratio=0.72 [0.54-0.96]) [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   was the only factor significantly associated with severe malnutrition. We did not find any factor [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   associated with mortality. There was a moderate consistency (47.1%) between the decided [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   medical nutrition therapy and the ESPEN guidelines of nutritional care. Conclusions: Adults [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   referred for the first time to a specialized nutritional consultation present mostly with severe [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   malnutrition and are at risk for significant complications, leading to a high mortality rate. In [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   this population, a low FMI is associated with severe malnutrition. An earlier diagnosis and care [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   of malnutrition and an earlier referral to clinical nutrition units would improve outcomes. [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   Malnutrition, related to insufficient intake or assimilation of nutrients regarding needs, leads to [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   altered body composition and function. Its screening and adequate care in essential as [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   malnutrition is associated with adverse clinical outcomes such as increase morbidity, mortality [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   and length of hospital stay [1]. In patients at hospital or with specific diseases, insufficient food [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   intake, plasma albumin <35 g/l and phase angle <5°, measured by bio-impedance analysis [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   (BIA), were associated with severe malnutrition or mortality [2–6]. [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   The risk of malnutrition has been poorly evaluated in outpatients: in Australia, one study [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   showed that 17% of ambulatory cancer patients were malnourished [7], while another in the [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   USA found that 11% of the older people followed in medical outpatient clinics were [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   malnourished and an appropriate intervention was only performed in 14% of cases [8]. The [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   recent consensus of the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM), revised the [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   diagnostic criteria for malnutrition in order to unify terminology and improve clinical [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   management [9]. Combining body mass index (BMI) with weight loss calculations is essential, [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   especially nowadays due to increasing prevalence of obesity [10], resulting in situations of [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   malnutrition with normal/high BMI. To accurately detect decreased muscle mass, validated [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   methods of body composition measurement, such as dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   or BIA, easier in clinical practice, and their related thresholds, should be used [9]. [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   Since 2000, the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) has [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   published a great number of disease specific guidelines for medical nutrition therapy [11]. All [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   disease specific guidelines recommend oral nutrition as the first route of feeding in [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   malnourished patients with an intact gastrointestinal tract. Enteral tube feeding is recommended [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   when the nutritional requirements cannot be met via oral route or in patients not reaching 75% [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   of their energy and protein targets within 5 to 7 days. Parenteral nutrition is proposed only if [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   enteral nutrition is insufficient or badly tolerated. Older people with reasonable prognosis shall [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   be offered enteral nutrition, if oral intake is expected to be below 50% of energy requirements [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   for more than one week or impossible for more than three days [12]. [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   While many studies have focused on the management of hospital malnutrition, to our [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   knowledge, the phenotypes and outcomes of malnourished patients followed on an outpatient [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   basis has been poorly studied, despite the incitation of health policies to increase outclinic [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   follow-ups. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the characteristics and [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   clinical outcomes of malnourished patients referred to a specialized outpatient clinic for the [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   management of malnutrition. The secondary aims were: to compare these characteristics and [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   clinical outcomes according to malnutrition severity, to determine the factors associated with [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   severe malnutrition and mortality; and to compare the consistency of the decided nutritional [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   care with the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) guidelines. [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   Methods [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   Study design and patients [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   This was a retrospective observational study including all consecutive adult patients referred [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   for the first time to the specialized nutrition consultation of one single physician (RT) at Rennes [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   University Hospital (CHU Rennes), Rennes, France, between September 29, 2016 and [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   December 31, 2020, diagnosed with malnutrition. Individuals were referred in case of positive [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   screening of malnutrition by using the French guidelines [1], in case of insufficient daily food [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   intake on the 10-point analogue scale, e.g. the Simple Evaluation of Food Intake (SEFI®) [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   (www.sefi-nutrition.com, Knoë - Groupe Get, Le Kremlin-Bicêtre, France)[13], or in case of [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   clinical suspicion of malnutrition after examination by a physician in a hospital unit. Patients [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   with anorexia nervosa, obesity (with or without obesity surgery) and patients with intestinal [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   failure needing home parenteral nutrition were excluded. [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   Data collection [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   The data were collected prospectively during the medical consultation, except for hospital [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   admission within the month following the consultation, infections and death that were collected [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   retrospectively. The follow-up time was calculated starting from the patient’s first consultation [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   to the patient’s last consultation or death. [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   The Clinical Nutrition consultations were conducted by one single physician (RT) and their [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   course was standardized. The mean duration was 30 minutes. Were collected: demographic data [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   as age, gender, addictions (alcohol use, smoking, others), living conditions (at home, nursing [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   home, alone or not), comorbidities, current and previous treatments, digestive symptoms [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   (diarrhea, nausea, vomiting), as well as weight loss within the last one and six months or [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   compared to the usual weight. Main diagnosis apart from malnutrition was collected. [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   Malabsorption/maldigestion refers to diseases or clinical situations with impaired nutrient [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   absorption or digestion (of one or more nutrients) at any point across the gastrointestinal tract. [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   Weight and height were measured, and BMI calculated. Clinical examination also included the [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   seek for ascites, edema, and amyotrophy. Body composition was assessed by BIA (Bodystat [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   1500 MDD, Bodystat ltd, Man Island, UK). BIA was performed by a specialized nurse before [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   the physician consultation after a 10 min bed rest. The following parameters were reported: fat [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   mass (kg, %), free-fat mass (kg, %), dry free-fat mass (kg, %) and total body water (l, %). The [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   fat mass index (FMI) was calculated as: fat mass (kg)/ height (m²)), and the fat-free mass (FFM) [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   index (FFMI) as: FFM (kg)/ height (m²)). Reduced FFM was defined by a FFMI <17 for male [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   or <15 for female. Food intake was assessed using the 10-point visual analogue scale of SEFI®. [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   The SEFI® allowed a visual assessment of the food intake, with an immediate result, not [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   requiring food collection, and is very well correlated with real daily energy intakes [2]. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   reliability of SEFI® for diagnosing malnutrition or the risk of malnutrition has been [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   demonstrated in patients in general practice, inpatients or outpatients and in nursing homes [14– [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   16]. Insufficient food intake correlated with malnutrition or the risk of malnutrition was defined [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   by SEFI® <7 [2,14,15]. [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   Malnutrition and its severity were retrospectively diagnosed according the GLIM criteria [9]. [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   Malnutrition was defined by the combination of at least one phenotypic criterion among BMI [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   <20kg/m² (or <22 if age ≥70 years), weight loss >5% within the past 6 months or >10% beyond [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   6 months, or reduced muscle mass (FFMI on the first BIA), with at least one etiological criterion [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   among reduced food intake defined by SEFI® score <7 (considered as equivalent to the GLIM [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   ≤50% of energy requirements) or reduced food assimilation (malabsorption or previous history [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   of gastro-intestinal surgery), or chronic disease-related inflammation (cancer or chronic organ [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   failure), or acute disease/injury. [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   Malnutrition was considered as severe if: weight loss >10% within the past six months or >20% [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   beyond six months, and/or BMI <18.5kg/m² if age <70 years or BMI <20kg/m² if age ≥70 years. [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   Otherwise, malnutrition was considered as moderate. [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   Main diagnosis, besides malnutrition, was categorized as: chronic organ failure, digestive [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   cancer with or without surgery, non-digestive cancer, non-oncological digestive surgery, [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   psychogenic anorexia (reduction of food intake with no intention to lose weight), intestinal [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   malabsorption, digestive disease without malabsorption, pancreatic disease, neuromuscular [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   disease, or auto-immune disease. [SOURCE: Page_8]

152   The medical nutrition therapy accepted by the patient, namely the prescription made at the end [SOURCE: Page_8]

153   of the consultation, was recorded. This was not always the first line proposal of the clinician. [SOURCE: Page_8]

154   The different options were: food fortification, oral nutritional supplements (ONS), enteral or [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   parenteral nutrition, depending on functionality of the digestive tube and patients’ acceptance. [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   These therapeutics could have been prescribed alone or in association. [SOURCE: Page_8]

158   Study endpoints [SOURCE: Page_9]

159   The primary endpoint was the nutritional characteristics of patients referred for the first time to [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   an outpatient clinic for the management of malnutrition. This was a composite end point [SOURCE: Page_9]

161   regrouping: malnutrition severity, main diagnosis, comorbidities, physical signs of [SOURCE: Page_9]

162   malnutrition, body composition parameters, complications (infections, hospital admission [SOURCE: Page_9]

163   within 1 months) and mortality. [SOURCE: Page_9]

164   The secondary endpoints were the nutritional prescription after this first consultation, and the [SOURCE: Page_9]

165   consistency of this medical nutrition therapy with the ESPEN guidelines for nutritional care. [SOURCE: Page_9]

166   ESPEN has published since 2000 a great number of disease specific guidelines for nutritional [SOURCE: Page_9]

167   care [17]. To sum up, all disease specific guidelines recommend oral nutrition as the first route [SOURCE: Page_9]

168   of feeding for nutritional therapy of malnourished patients who are able to ingest food [18]. [SOURCE: Page_9]

169   Enteral tube feeding should be implemented in medical inpatients who could not reach 75% of [SOURCE: Page_9]

170   their energy and protein targets or when the nutritional requirements cannot be met via oral [SOURCE: Page_9]

171   route during the recommended 5 to 7 days (according to the majority of the guidelines, although [SOURCE: Page_9]

172   this period can be extended to 8 weeks in chronic infectious diseases). In patients with an intact [SOURCE: Page_9]

173   gastrointestinal tract, parenteral nutrition should be seen as a last resource when patients cannot [SOURCE: Page_9]

174   be fed sufficiently orally or enterally. The older people with reasonable prognosis shall be [SOURCE: Page_9]

175   offered enteral nutrition, if oral intake is expected to be impossible for more than three days, or [SOURCE: Page_9]

176   expected to be below half of energy requirements for more than one week, despite interventions [SOURCE: Page_9]

177   [12]. [SOURCE: Page_9]

178   Taking into account the recommendations cited above, the medical nutrition therapy provided [SOURCE: Page_9]

179   was considered consistent with the ESPEN guideline if: food fortification or ONSs were [SOURCE: Page_9]

180   prescribed in moderately malnourished patients with spontaneous oral intakes ≥2/3 of [SOURCE: Page_9]

181   requirements or SEFI® ≥7; enteral nutrition was prescribed in moderately malnourished [SOURCE: Page_9]

182   patients with insufficient food intake (<2/3 of energy needs or SEFI® <7) and in severely [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

183   malnourished patients, except patients with non-functioning digestive for whom parenteral [SOURCE: Page_10]

184   nutrition was indicated. As the follow up of the medical nutrition therapy was not collected, the [SOURCE: Page_10]

185   data about a potential second-line nutritional therapy were not available. [SOURCE: Page_10]

186 [SOURCE: Page_10]

187   Statistical analysis [SOURCE: Page_10]

188   Categorical variables were reported as n- (%) and compared using Chi^2 or Fisher tests. [SOURCE: Page_10]

189   Quantitative variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile [SOURCE: Page_10]

190   intervals; and compared using Student tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Logistic regressions [SOURCE: Page_10]

191   were used to assess variables associated with i) malnutrition and ii) mortality. Odds ratios (OR) [SOURCE: Page_10]

192   with a 95% confidence interval [IC95%] were reported. All tests will be carried out with a 5% [SOURCE: Page_10]

193   significance level (p <0.05). Statistical analysis was performed with SAS statistical software [SOURCE: Page_10]

194   V$_9$.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). [SOURCE: Page_10]

259   Discussion [SOURCE: Page_13]

260   Seventy-four % of the adult patients referred for the first time to the specialized nutrition [SOURCE: Page_13]

261   consultation of a university teaching hospital for the management of malnutrition were severely [SOURCE: Page_13]

262   malnourished. After a median follow up of 70 days, these patients were frequently complicated [SOURCE: Page_13]

263   with infections (19%) and mortality (23%). A low FMI was the only factor significantly [SOURCE: Page_13]

264   associated with severe malnutrition. We did not find any factor associated with mortality. A [SOURCE: Page_13]

265   medical nutrition therapy was prescribed in 96.5% and 95% of patients with moderate and [SOURCE: Page_13]

266   severe malnutrition, respectively. There was a moderate consistency (47.1%) between the [SOURCE: Page_13]

267   medical nutrition therapy decided at the end of the consultation and the ESPEN guidelines of [SOURCE: Page_13]

268   nutritional care. [SOURCE: Page_13]

269   At present, body composition parameters are considered as a more appropriate as simple [SOURCE: Page_13]

270   anthropometric measurement, such as BMI in order to assess nutritional status [19]. The non‐ [SOURCE: Page_13]

271   invasive, safe and inexpensive characteristics of BIA make this method applicable in [SOURCE: Page_13]

272   outpatients’ populations. It is a valid alternative for body composition evaluation and has been [SOURCE: Page_13]

273   validated against existing reference methods as DXA. BIA is a more practical, nonradioactive [SOURCE: Page_13]

274   and well‐tolerated method that can be completed in a short operating time. When used in [SOURCE: Page_13]

275   outpatients’ nutritional clinics it allows early individualized nutritional intervention, improving [SOURCE: Page_13]

276   clinical outcomes and long‐term health [19]. [SOURCE: Page_13]

277   Patients with severe malnutrition had smaller FMI and higher total body water than patients [SOURCE: Page_13]

278   with moderate malnutrition. There were no significant differences in FFMI and proportion of [SOURCE: Page_13]

279   patients with amyotrophy, suggesting that, in a population of malnourished patients, the [SOURCE: Page_13]

280   identification of reduced muscle mass is not discriminant enough to evaluate the severity of [SOURCE: Page_13]

13 [SOURCE: Page_13]

281   malnutrition. Future studies should evaluate whether FMI and total body water could be useful [SOURCE: Page_14]

282   body composition parameters to determine the severity of malnutrition and should try to [SOURCE: Page_14]

283   identify thresholds. The fact that a high total body water could make uninterpretable the levels [SOURCE: Page_14]

284   of fat-free mass [20] with regard to muscle mass could explain the fact that FFMI was poorly [SOURCE: Page_14]

285   related to malnutrition severity in our study. Albumin plasma levels were not significantly [SOURCE: Page_14]

286   different between patients with severe and moderate malnutrition, that is concordant with the [SOURCE: Page_14]

287   fact that albumin plasma levels fail to distinguish the degree of malnutrition on their own [21]. [SOURCE: Page_14]

288   This reinforces the relevance of the recent suppression of this measurement as a phenotypic [SOURCE: Page_14]

289   diagnostic criterion for malnutrition [9]. The SEFI® was also not different between patients [SOURCE: Page_14]

290   with severe and moderate malnutrition. Whereas a low level of food intake seems a good [SOURCE: Page_14]

291   indicator of malnutrition [2,14–16], the SEFI® <7 threshold is not an indicator of malnutrition [SOURCE: Page_14]

292   severity in our study. Future studies could try to identify a SEFI® cut-off associated to [SOURCE: Page_14]

293   malnutrition severity. Another semi-quantitative method for assessing food intake is the [SOURCE: Page_14]

294   assessment of the consumed food portions as proposed in the NutritionDay® survey [22]. Using [SOURCE: Page_14]

295   this method, a correlation between reduced food intake and low BMI has already been reported [SOURCE: Page_14]

296   [22]. Although this could be also done using the SEFI®, consumed food portions were not [SOURCE: Page_14]

297   assessed in this study because this more likely suits to the hospital setting, where the health- [SOURCE: Page_14]

298   care staff can directly observe the food consumed when clearing the meal tray [23]. [SOURCE: Page_14]

299   Nutrition support, i.e. fortification, ONS, enteral or parenteral nutrition, was prescribed in [SOURCE: Page_14]

300   92.8% of the malnourished patients of this series. This is much higher that common practice in [SOURCE: Page_14]

301   French hospital wards, where only 57.6% of malnourished patients received nutrition support [SOURCE: Page_14]

302   [24]. This higher number could be easily understood by the fact that the patients were managed [SOURCE: Page_14]

303   by a physician specialized in clinical nutrition. In our study, malnourished patients received [SOURCE: Page_14]

304   food fortification in 25% of cases, ONS in 59.3%, enteral nutrition in 42.6% and parenteral [SOURCE: Page_14]

305   nutrition, in 10.8% of cases. The consistency between the medical nutrition strategy decided at [SOURCE: Page_14]

14 [SOURCE: Page_14]

306   the end of the consultation and the ESPEN guidelines of nutritional care is moderate (47.1%). [SOURCE: Page_15]

307   This figure may have been underestimated, as we did not collect the information of the medical [SOURCE: Page_15]

308   nutrition therapy received within the weeks following the consultation. The patients' rejection [SOURCE: Page_15]

309   to receive the therapy was also an important reason why the ESPEN guidelines were not [SOURCE: Page_15]

310   followed. This was especially the case when proposing to set up enteral or parenteral nutrition [SOURCE: Page_15]

311   that the patient could consider as invasive and/or uncomfortable. Thus, the decision of initiating [SOURCE: Page_15]

312   enteral or parenteral nutrition would have been postponed to the next consultation due to [SOURCE: Page_15]

313   patient’s initial reluctance to accept the indicated nutrition support. Physician prescribed in this [SOURCE: Page_15]

314   case fortification and ONS. Another obstacle to the implementation of enteral and parenteral [SOURCE: Page_15]

315   nutrition is the difficulty of quickly implementing and properly managing these modalities at [SOURCE: Page_15]

316   home, particularly among older or dependent people. Nevertheless, this finding indicates the [SOURCE: Page_15]

317   difficulty to initiate the appropriate medical nutrition therapy in the setting on a 30-minute [SOURCE: Page_15]

318   outpatient consultation in the real life. [SOURCE: Page_15]

319   It is well known that adequate nutritional care allows reduction of hospital stay, risk of [SOURCE: Page_15]

320   infections as well as post-surgical complications, and improves quality of life [25,26]. [SOURCE: Page_15]

321   Moreover, adequate nutritional care in community has a great medico-economic benefit. It [SOURCE: Page_15]

322   allows a reduction of hospital admissions and length of stay, as well as care costs. Only [SOURCE: Page_15]

323   prescription of ONS by a general practitioner saves around 200 euros per patient per year [27]. [SOURCE: Page_15]

324   Therefore, efforts have to be made in order to improve malnutrition diagnosis and nutritional [SOURCE: Page_15]

325   care in outpatients, and its timing that should be as early as possible, by improving physicians’ [SOURCE: Page_15]

326   knowledge regarding the guidelines and the acceptance of the proposed therapies by the patient. [SOURCE: Page_15]

327   In our population of malnourished patients, infections were reported in 19.4% of cases, with [SOURCE: Page_15]

328   pneumonia as the most frequent type (6.5%). These figures are consistent with a cohort study [SOURCE: Page_15]

329   performed in hospitalized malnourished patients, in which 5.9% of patients reported pneumonia [SOURCE: Page_15]

330   [28]. A very high mortality rate of malnourished patients (23.1%) was found in our study, two- [SOURCE: Page_15]

15 [SOURCE: Page_15]

331   fold higher than in hospitalized patients, according to a previous study (12.4%) [28]. This can [SOURCE: Page_16]

332   be explained by the high proportion of patients with severe malnutrition and digestive cancers, [SOURCE: Page_16]

333   that could act as intricate confounding factors. [SOURCE: Page_16]

334   Phase angle measurement, derived from BIA, is a prognostic marker in various diseases and [SOURCE: Page_16]

335   one of the most sensitive markers of malnutrition [4,5]. In this study, there was no association [SOURCE: Page_16]

336   between a phase angle <5° and mortality, but this lack of difference could be due to the small [SOURCE: Page_16]

337   number of patients in whom phase angle was reported, and the high proportion of patients with [SOURCE: Page_16]

338   severe malnutrition itself related to poor prognosis. Hypoalbuminemia is also a well-known [SOURCE: Page_16]

339   poor prognostic factor, and has been associated with higher mortality rates in outpatients with [SOURCE: Page_16]

340   heart failure or colorectal cancer [6,29]. We did not find this relation in our study, potentially [SOURCE: Page_16]

341   due to a lack of power because of missing data. [SOURCE: Page_16]

342   Strengths and weaknesses [SOURCE: Page_16]

343   To our knowledge, this is the first study that has evaluated the characteristics and outcomes of [SOURCE: Page_16]

344   malnourished patients’ referend for the first time to an outpatient clinic for the management of [SOURCE: Page_16]

345   malnutrition in France. It brings new data concerning socio-demographic characteristics, [SOURCE: Page_16]

346   comorbidities, main diagnosis and malnutrition diagnosis parameters. Another strength of this [SOURCE: Page_16]

347   study is the use of the GLIM criteria to diagnose malnutrition, including food intake evaluation [SOURCE: Page_16]

348   and, when available, body composition assessment by BIA. As this is a retrospective study, [SOURCE: Page_16]

349   there are some missing data, such as for the assessment of body composition by BIA, which [SOURCE: Page_16]

350   could have led to lack of statistical differences for some parameters. The evaluation of [SOURCE: Page_16]

351   comorbidities was not made by using the Charlson Comorbidity Index that could have reduced [SOURCE: Page_16]

352   arbitrariness in extracting background diseases. The information about the long-term outcomes [SOURCE: Page_16]

353   was not collected. It would have been interesting to include a longitudinal follow-up of the [SOURCE: Page_16]

354   patients and achieve medico-economic analyses, in order to determine if the medical nutrition [SOURCE: Page_16]

16 [SOURCE: Page_16]

355   therapy decided in the consultation is effective and associated with reduction of the costs [SOURCE: Page_17]

356   generated by the malnutrition in the long term. [SOURCE: Page_17]

357 [SOURCE: Page_17]

358   Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_17]

359   Malnourished adults referred for the first time to a specialized nutritional consultation present [SOURCE: Page_17]

360   mostly with severe malnutrition and are at risk for significant complications, leading to a high [SOURCE: Page_17]

361   mortality rate. A higher body fat mass is associated with a lower risk for severe malnutrition. [SOURCE: Page_17]

362   Further studies should evaluate whether a specific threshold of body fat mass index should be [SOURCE: Page_17]

363   used to improve the diagnosis of severe malnutrition. More effort should be made to develop [SOURCE: Page_17]

364   multidisciplinary teams dedicated to outpatient nutritional care to better apply the ESPEN [SOURCE: Page_17]

365   recommendations of nutritional care. Future studies should evaluate whether a better screening [SOURCE: Page_17]

366   of malnutrition, an earlier referral to clinical nutrition units and a more adequate medical [SOURCE: Page_17]

367   nutrition therapy should improve outcomes. [SOURCE: Page_17]
