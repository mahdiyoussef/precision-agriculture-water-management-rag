17   Introductory paragraph [SOURCE: Page_3]

18   Plant water storage is fundamental to the functioning of terrestrial ecosystems by participating in plant metabolism, [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   nutrient and carbohydrates transport, and maintenance of the plant hydraulic system’s integrity. However, global [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   observations of the size and dynamics of the water pools stored in plant tissues do not exist. Here we reveal the [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   global patterns of seasonal variations in ecosystem-scale plant water storage and their relationship with foliar [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   phenology, based on the novel space-borne measurements of L-band vegetation optical depth (L-VOD). We find [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   that seasonal variation in plant water storage is highly synchronous with foliar phenology for the boreal and [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   temperate forests, but asynchronous with a time lag of up to 180 days for the tropical dry woodlands. Contrasting [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   patterns of the time lag between plant water storage and terrestrial groundwater storage anomaly are also presented [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   amongst these ecosystems. Comparison of the water-cycle components in seasonally-dry tropical woodlands [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   suggests a buffering effect of plant water storage on the seasonal dynamics of water supply and demand. Our [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   results offer new insights into ecosystem-scale plant water relations globally and provide a basis for an improved [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   parameterization of process-based eco-hydrological and earth system modelling. [SOURCE: Page_3]

31           Water stored in plant tissues participates in the physiological and biochemical processes (e.g. metabolism [SOURCE: Page_4]

32   and nutrient/sugar transport) and sustains the integrity of the plant hydraulic system through buffering the [SOURCE: Page_4]

33   imbalance between transpirational water loss and root-soil water uptake^1,2. Water movement in plants is a passive [SOURCE: Page_4]

34   process, following a gradient of water potential (from higher to lower) generated by transpirational demand [SOURCE: Page_4]

35   (regulated by stomata)3 and different solute concentrations between cells (osmosis)4. In response to changes in [SOURCE: Page_4]

36   environmental conditions and metabolic activities, plant water storage varies from diurnal to seasonal timescales [SOURCE: Page_4]

37   as a function of plant structure and hydraulic strategies^5-7. Studies on the dynamics of plant water storage typically [SOURCE: Page_4]

38   use measurements of sap flow hysteresis and leaf water potential on individual trees over relatively limited [SOURCE: Page_4]

39   periods^7-10. Scaling up tree-level data to the ecosystem scale is challenging due to the high spatial heterogeneity of [SOURCE: Page_4]

40   plant composition and soil properties, thus precluding large-scale (continental or global) estimates of the dynamics [SOURCE: Page_4]

41   of plant water storage. [SOURCE: Page_4]

42   Satellite passive microwave radiometers sense the natural earth surface thermal emission at wavelengths of 0.1-30 [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   cm from both the soil and vegetation layers, quantified as brightness temperature. The vegetation layer is semi- [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   transparent, attenuating the microwave radiations passing through and creating a vegetation optical depth (VOD) [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   proportionally linked to the vegetation water content (kg/m-2)11,12. The higher the microwave frequency, the [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   stronger the vegetation attenuation effect. The low frequency L-band (1.4 GHz) VOD (L-VOD) can detect changes [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   in plant water storage even for dense forest canopies^13-15, where the woody tissues of stems and branches hold the [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   majority of the plant water^16. This detection was not possible from VOD products at higher frequencies (>6 GHz), [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   because their signals were dominated by the water status of canopy leaves^13,14,17-19. Satellite-based VOD retrievals [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   from X-band (10.7 GHz) observations over day-time and night-time have recently been used to map ecosystem- [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   scale anisohydricity, an indicator of stomatal and xylem regulation^20,21. Since 2010, the first satellite-based L-band [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   radiometer carried by the Soil Moisture and Ocean Salinity (SMOS) mission^22 further enables global scale [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   monitoring of the dynamics in forest plant capacitive water storage, which is another key functional trait in [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   ecohydrological processes. [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   Here, we used the SMOS L-VOD product^23,24 as a proxy for time and space variations in ecosystem-scale plant [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   water storage and examined its seasonal dynamics globally over the period 2011-2016. Since leaf area scales the [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   transpirational flux and photosynthetic productivity, we also examined the coupling between seasonal variations [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   in L-VOD and foliar phenology (defined as the seasonality of satellite observed Leaf Area Index; LAI). [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   Results and discussion [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   Seasonal amplitude of L-VOD [SOURCE: Page_5]

61           We analysed the seasonal dynamics of ecosystem-scale plant water storage by concatenating six-year [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   (2011-2016) SMOS L-VOD data into a mean yearly daily time series at a spatial resolution of 0.25° (Methods; [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   Extended Data Fig. 1). Large land regions had substantial data gaps due to the influence of radio frequency [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   interference (RFI) on the SMOS signal^25 (mainly in Asia; Extended Data Fig. 2), open water bodies, and lack of [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   data retrievals from frozen soil in the northern latitudes during the cold season. We therefore restricted our analysis [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   to pixels with continuous L-VOD data over 120 days and focused on four regions representing various biomes of [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   contrasting ecosystem functioning: boreal, temperate, tropical dry, and tropical moist, based on a map of terrestrial [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   ecoregions^26 (Extended Data Fig. 3). To ensure a high accuracy of the L-VOD seasonal patterns, we excluded [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   areas with sparse vegetation cover where the tree cover fraction is below 5%27 (Extended Data Fig. 4) and areas [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   that were seasonally inundated^28, i.e. >5% area covered by flooding within an L-VOD pixel (Extended Data Fig. [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   5). [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   Globally, the annual maximum L-VOD was highly correlated with the distribution of tree cover fraction and tree [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   height (Extended Data Fig. 6)29,30. The absolute peak-to-peak seasonal amplitude of the mean L-VOD time series [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   was similar (within 0.3 L-VOD units) amongst the four regions (Fig. 1a), even though plant productivity in these [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   regions is limited by different climatic variables (e.g. temperature, water and radiation) and by different nutrient [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   availability. The L-VOD seasonal amplitude relative to the annual maxima, however, differed significantly [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   amongst regions (Fig. 1b), and were largest in the tropical dry region (>30% on average) and smallest in the [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   tropical moist region (<10% on average), with temperate and boreal regions having intermediate values. [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   Seasonal coupling of L-VOD and LAI [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   Water stored in plant tissues and its seasonal dynamics differ substantially across vegetation lifeforms (e.g. woody [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   vs. herbaceous plants). Herbaceous plants are usually annuals, so the seasonal amplitude of herbaceous water [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   storage is determined primarily by the amount of foliage in the growing season. In contrast, trees store the majority [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   of their water in woody tissues, with water-holding capacities varying amongst functioning tissues16,31. Also, [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   determining the dynamics of water storage is more complex for woody than herbaceous plants, because trees may [SOURCE: Page_7]

91   access deep soil water and have more sophisticated hydraulic strategies^32-34. We examined the spatial relationships [SOURCE: Page_7]

92   between the absolute seasonal amplitudes of L-VOD and LAI in each of the four regions. As expected, linear [SOURCE: Page_7]

93   correlations between the two signals were higher in areas with limited tree cover (5-10%) (R ≈ 0.56, P < 0.05; Fig. [SOURCE: Page_7]

94   2), except in the boreal region (R = 0.24, P < 0.05) where data gaps in winter may cause a partial loss of information [SOURCE: Page_7]

95   in the transitional period between winter and spring/fall. The seasonal amplitudes of L-VOD, however, were [SOURCE: Page_7]

96   positively correlated with LAI across space for areas with high tree cover (>60%) in the boreal (R = 0.35, P < 0.05) [SOURCE: Page_7]

97   and temperate (R = 0.32, P < 0.05) regions, but the relationship was much lower in the tropical moist (R = 0.09, P [SOURCE: Page_7]

98   < 0.05) region and not significant (R = -0.02, P > 0.05) in the tropical dry region. These findings suggest a lower [SOURCE: Page_7]

99   influence of foliar fluctuations on the seasonal amplitude of plant water storage in tropical than boreal or temperate [SOURCE: Page_7]

100   forests. [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   Seasonal water balance [SOURCE: Page_10]

134           Plants regulate water exchange between sub-soil layers and the atmosphere through soil water uptake and [SOURCE: Page_10]

135   transpiration. We evaluated the role of plant water storage on the seasonal water balance at ecosystem scale by [SOURCE: Page_10]

136   analysing the development of various components of the water cycle, including plant water storage (L-VOD), [SOURCE: Page_10]

137   terrestrial groundwater storage anomaly (TWS), surface soil moisture (SM), rainfall, and estimates of transpiration. [SOURCE: Page_10]

138   The TWS integrates the ground water from the soil surface to deep ground^36. Seasonal variations in TWS indicate [SOURCE: Page_10]

139   the net balance between soil water replenishment (e.g., by rainfall) and consumption (e.g., by [SOURCE: Page_10]

140   evapotranspiration)37. We calculated the seasonal time lag between L-VOD and TWS as it was done for L-VOD [SOURCE: Page_10]

141   and LAI. Large time lags of about 120 and 90 days were obtained in the entire regions of southeastern US and [SOURCE: Page_10]

142   central Siberia, respectively (Fig. 4). By contrast, a time lag varying from 0 to around 90 days was found in the [SOURCE: Page_10]

143   central south African savannah/woodland region (the Miombo region), following the gradient of vegetation density [SOURCE: Page_10]

144   (L-VOD and tree cover; Fig. 4) thus suggesting the unique role of certain tree species in the eco-hydrological [SOURCE: Page_10]

145   processes as compare to the herbaceous vegetation. [SOURCE: Page_10]

168   weeks before the onset of the rainy season, confirming the pre-rain green-up reported by previous studies^39. [SOURCE: Page_12]

170   measurements (for the tree species Brachystegia spiciformis) in this region^40. SM remained at the lowest level [SOURCE: Page_13]

171   during the entire dry season with almost no rainfall, but TWS decreased continuously, suggesting that deep [SOURCE: Page_13]

172   groundwater was used by trees during the dry season^41. In contrast, L-VOD increased at the beginning of the dry [SOURCE: Page_13]

173   season, peaked around the time when LAI was lowest, and then decreased when LAI increased again. During the [SOURCE: Page_13]

174   rainy season, both transpiration and SM remained high, TWS was continuously recharged, and L-VOD increased [SOURCE: Page_13]

175   during the latter part of the rainy season. These results together indicate a critical role of the stored plant water on [SOURCE: Page_13]

176   buffering the seasonal dynamics of water supply and demand in these tropical dry woodlands. [SOURCE: Page_13]

177   An endogenous whole-plant control of foliar phenology has been documented in some seasonally dry tropical tree [SOURCE: Page_13]

178   species^42,43, where water stored in the stems directly contributes to the foliar emergence during the pre-rain green- [SOURCE: Page_13]

179   up period^44,45, which may explain the decrease in L-VOD at the end of the dry season. The cambium of Miombo [SOURCE: Page_13]

180   trees mainly grows during a short period late in the rainy season^46,47, when L-VOD increased. The continued [SOURCE: Page_13]

181   increase in L-VOD at the end of the rainy season and at the beginning of the dry season is consistent with the [SOURCE: Page_13]

182   reduced loss of transpirational water associated with leaf shedding that enables the rehydration of stem tissues and [SOURCE: Page_13]

183   the subsequent foliar emergence^44,46. The Miombo woodland region was the largest hot-spot area characterized by [SOURCE: Page_13]

184   long time lags between L-VOD and LAI, but the northern African woodlands and the Brazilian Cerrado have [SOURCE: Page_13]

185   similar patterns as well (Extended Data Fig. 11). [SOURCE: Page_13]

186   The specific seasonal behaviour of various water cycle components within each region indicate the adaptation of [SOURCE: Page_13]

187   plants to local climatic conditions (e.g., seasonal variations in rainfall, temperature, and photosynthetic active [SOURCE: Page_13]

188   radiation) and abiotic conditions (e.g. soil types and soil water supply) at the ecosystem scale. The spatial [SOURCE: Page_13]

189   heterogeneity in species composition needs to be considered for further in-depth hydrological/physiological [SOURCE: Page_13]

190   interpretations on the large-footprint satellite signal because closely located tree species sharing the same [SOURCE: Page_13]

191   environmental conditions can be characterized by different hydraulic strategies, e.g., between soft wood species [SOURCE: Page_13]

192   and hard wood species^44. At the same time, different species across ecosystems may have similar hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_13]

193   mechanisms as root hydraulic lift and redistribution of deep ground water were identified in both temperate forests [SOURCE: Page_13]

194   and tropical woodlands ecosystems48,49. [SOURCE: Page_13]

196   storage and foliar phenology. The satellite-based L-VOD product allows for an efficient and repeated monitoring [SOURCE: Page_14]

199   LAI/TWS as well as the resulting spatial patterns in relation to vegetation density and types provide a convenient [SOURCE: Page_14]

200   way to condense patters of plant water storage across the globe, which can be used to parameterize and test relevant [SOURCE: Page_14]

207   Methods [SOURCE: Page_14]

208   Data sets [SOURCE: Page_14]

209   The SMOS satellite covers the earth surface nearly daily with both ascending (6 am) and descending (6 pm) [SOURCE: Page_14]

210   overpasses since the beginning of 2010. The whole system underwent the so-called commissioning phase until [SOURCE: Page_14]

211   June 2010 when various operation modes were tested, leading to less observations in comparison to the following [SOURCE: Page_14]

212   years. Therefore, we used data during the period of 2011-2016. We selected the ascending observations as plant [SOURCE: Page_14]

213   water storage usually peaks at dawn. The SMOS-IC algorithm^23 was applied to retrieve L-VOD and SM [SOURCE: Page_14]

214   simultaneously from SMOS observations, without any external vegetation products as inputs, so the current L- [SOURCE: Page_14]

215   VOD retrievals (version 103) were independent from any other vegetation indices. Conversely to other SMOS [SOURCE: Page_14]

216   products (e.g., Level 2 and Level 3)51, no remotely sensed LAI data were used in the SMOS-IC retrieval process [SOURCE: Page_14]

217   to estimate L-VOD over heterogeneous pixels, neither SM data from ECMWF (European Centre for Medium- [SOURCE: Page_14]

218   Range Weather Forecasts) were used to estimate SM over the forest fraction in mixed pixels. The native SMOS [SOURCE: Page_14]

219   spatial resolution (3 dB footprint) varies between ~27 and ~55 km depending on the incidence angles (observations [SOURCE: Page_14]

220   at high incidence angles were filtered out in SMOS-IC). The data were originally projected in the standard EASE- [SOURCE: Page_15]

221   2 grid at a 25 km spatial resolution and we further converted it into a geographical projection at a 0.25° spatial [SOURCE: Page_15]

222   resolution. [SOURCE: Page_15]

223   The LAI data were generated from the standard MODIS Collection 6 LAI products (MOD15 and MYD15; 8-day [SOURCE: Page_15]

224   composites at a 0.05° spatial resolution) from Terra and Aqua for 2011-201652. The MODIS LAI algorithm [SOURCE: Page_15]

225   accounts for sun-sensor geometry. We refined the raw LAI data into 16-day composites by averaging the cloud- [SOURCE: Page_15]

226   free observations indicated by the quality flag information to minimize the influences from environmental [SOURCE: Page_15]

227   contamination, e.g. clouds, aerosols, shadows, and poor sun-sensor geometries. All high-quality LAI data were [SOURCE: Page_15]

228   then resampled to 0.25° by averaging and linearly interpolating to a daily time step to match the spatial and [SOURCE: Page_15]

229   temporal resolutions of the L-VOD data. [SOURCE: Page_15]

230   The TWS data were from the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) satellites, which measure the [SOURCE: Page_15]

231   changes in the Earth’s gravitational field that are highly correlated with changes in both surface and sub-surface [SOURCE: Page_15]

232   water storage^53,54. The monthly GRACE TWS products are provided in three solutions by the [SOURCE: Page_15]

233   GeoForschungsZentrum Potsdam (GFZ), the Center for Space Research at the University of Texas (CSR), and the [SOURCE: Page_15]

234   Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) at a 1° spatial resolution. We used the average of the three products and the [SOURCE: Page_15]

235   associated standard deviations, as suggested by recent studies^55. Only 10 observations were available for 2011 and [SOURCE: Page_15]

236   2012 due to battery management. [SOURCE: Page_15]

237   The X-VOD data were from the land parameter data record (LPDR v^2) developed by the Numerical Terradynamic [SOURCE: Page_15]

238   Simulation Group at The University of Montana^35. The X-VOD was retrieved from the AMSR sensors at a [SOURCE: Page_15]

239   microwave frequency of 10.7 GHz for both ascending (1.30 pm) and descending (1.30 am) overpasses at a spatial [SOURCE: Page_15]

240   resolution of 0.25°. We selected the descending X-VOD retrievals for 2011-2016. The same principle (ω-τ model) [SOURCE: Page_15]

241   was used for retrieving X-VOD from AMSR microwave observations as for the SMOS-IC L-VOD retrieval [SOURCE: Page_15]

242   algorithm, which was also independent from optical vegetation indices. [SOURCE: Page_15]

243   The data for tree cover fractions were produced by Chiba University for 200827, since this dataset outperforms the [SOURCE: Page_15]

244   MODerate-resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) Vegetation Continuous Fields product (MOD44B) for [SOURCE: Page_15]

245   African drylands where MOD44B largely underestimates the woody cover^56,57. Tree height data was from [SOURCE: Page_16]

246   spaceborne light detection and ranging (lidar) data obtained by the Geoscience Laser Altimeter System (GLAS) 58. [SOURCE: Page_16]

247   The rainfall data were from the Tropical Rainfall Measurement Mission (TRMM) 3B42 v^7 daily product^59, which [SOURCE: Page_16]

248   were estimated from satellite measurements at a 0.25° spatial resolution. [SOURCE: Page_16]

249   The transpiration data were from estimates by the Global Land Evaporation Amsterdam Model (GLEAM) v^3.1a, [SOURCE: Page_16]

250   which used a set of algorithms for estimating a series of water-cycle components (without plant water storage) at [SOURCE: Page_16]

251   a daily time step and 0.25° spatial resolution^60,61. [SOURCE: Page_16]

252   Analysis of seasonal lag [SOURCE: Page_16]

253   We concatenated the six-year (2011-2016) data into a mean yearly daily time series for each pixel to obtain a clear [SOURCE: Page_16]

254   and reliable seasonality in L-VOD (see an example pixel in Extended Data Fig. 1a and b). The median of raw L- [SOURCE: Page_16]

255   VOD retrievals from the same day of the year for 2011-2016 was first used to form an average seasonality. Second, [SOURCE: Page_16]

256   a 30-day moving median was determined, followed by calculating the anomalies between the raw data and the [SOURCE: Page_16]

257   median curve. The data points larger/smaller than the median curve +/- the standard deviation of the anomalies [SOURCE: Page_16]

258   were defined as outliers and thus removed from the subsequent analysis (de-noise). Finally, a 30-day moving- [SOURCE: Page_16]

259   average curve was calculated for the valid data points. The seasonality of LAI and X-VOD were determined using [SOURCE: Page_16]

260   the same method as for L-VOD. [SOURCE: Page_16]

261   For the L-VOD seasonality, we accounted for both the data noise and the actual seasonal variations in plant water [SOURCE: Page_16]

262   storage, using the ratio between the seasonal amplitude and the standard deviation of the anomalies as an indicator [SOURCE: Page_16]

263   of the intra-annual variability (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Pixels with a ratio >1.2 (a buffer of 20% standard deviation) [SOURCE: Page_16]

264   were deemed to be characterized by a clear seasonality, despite the data noise, and were thus selected for further [SOURCE: Page_16]

265   lag analysis with the LAI seasonality, and pixels with a ratio ≤1.2 were deemed to have no clear seasonal patterns [SOURCE: Page_16]

266   in L-VOD. Double growing seasons will introduce large uncertainties in quantifying the time lag, so we limited [SOURCE: Page_16]

267   the analysis to pixels characterized by a single growing season in both L-VOD and LAI seasonality. We normalized [SOURCE: Page_16]

268   the mean seasonality of each pixel to 0-1 by subtracting the minimum value and then dividing by the amplitude, [SOURCE: Page_16]

269   and identified pixels with only two 0.5 crossing points as being characterized by a single growing season62. [SOURCE: Page_16]

270   Sensitivity analysis on the L-VOD seasonality [SOURCE: Page_17]

271   The SMOS-IC algorithm applies a constant soil surface roughness and effective scattering albedo over time and [SOURCE: Page_17]

272   use an effective temperature input from ECMWF estimates^23,24, which will introduce uncertainties in the L-VOD [SOURCE: Page_17]

273   retrievals. We performed a series of sensitivity analyses focusing on the possible influences from these uncertainty [SOURCE: Page_17]

274   settings/sources on the L-VOD seasonal pattern retrieved from the SMOS-IC algorithm over the Miombo region, [SOURCE: Page_17]

275   where L-VOD peaks during the dry season. Specifically, we applied various value settings of these input [SOURCE: Page_17]

276   parameters covering a dynamic range larger than the expected ones to examine the possible resulting changes in [SOURCE: Page_17]

277   the L-VOD seasonality. [SOURCE: Page_17]

278   Soil surface roughness may change in relation to agricultural practices, but very unlikely over natural [SOURCE: Page_17]

279   environmental conditions at the satellite footprint scale as for the Miombo region. The roughness parameter (HR) [SOURCE: Page_17]

280   used in the SMOS-IC algorithm is 0.3 for the forested pixels in the Miombo region^63,64. We compared the L-VOD [SOURCE: Page_17]

281   retrievals over this region by setting HR to 0.2 and 0.4, respectively, but found little difference in the seasonal [SOURCE: Page_17]

282   cycle from a setting of 0.3 used in the original SMOS-IC algorithm (Extended Data Figure 9). Because the seasonal [SOURCE: Page_17]

283   changes in soil surface roughness are expected to be much smaller than an HR range of 0.2, they will have very [SOURCE: Page_17]

284   limited influence on the observed L-VOD seasonal trends. [SOURCE: Page_17]

285   Canopy leaves have dominant effects on the emissivity and effective scattering albedo of deciduous forests at [SOURCE: Page_17]

286   higher frequencies (>6 GHz), but the leaf effects are limited at L-band as suggested by several studies which show [SOURCE: Page_17]

287   that stems and branches are the main elements contributing to forest emissivity at L-band^13-15,63. Therefore, in the [SOURCE: Page_17]

288   SMOS-IC algorithm, a constant effective scattering albedo (Omega) value of 0.06 was used for the Miombo [SOURCE: Page_17]

289   woodlands although dominated by a deciduous forest type with a clear annual cycle in the canopy leaf [SOURCE: Page_17]

290   conditions^23,65,66. To test the influence from an expected small change in the effective albedo (lower than 0.01) on [SOURCE: Page_17]

291   the retrieved L-VOD values, we ran the SMOS-IC algorithm by setting the Omega value to 0.05 and 0.07, [SOURCE: Page_17]

292   respectively over the Miombo region^67. Again, the results show little difference in the retrieved L-VOD seasonal [SOURCE: Page_17]

293   patterns between these different settings (Extended Data Figure 10). Therefore, using a constant effective [SOURCE: Page_17]

294   scattering albedo of 0.06 in the SMOS-IC algorithm will have a very limited influence on the observed L-VOD [SOURCE: Page_17]

295   seasonal trends over the Miombo woodlands. [SOURCE: Page_17]

296   The ECWMF soil and vegetation temperatures used to estimate the effective surface temperature (Ts) in the [SOURCE: Page_18]

297   SMOS-IC algorithm^68 may have a root mean square deviation as high as a few Kelvin^69,70. We applied a drastic [SOURCE: Page_18]

298   approach to investigate its possible impacts on the L-VOD seasonal patterns, that is, setting a constant effective [SOURCE: Page_18]

299   temperature (Ts) value over the course of the year. Two constant values (291 K and 295K) with a range of 4 K [SOURCE: Page_18]

300   were considered based on the observed average value (~293 K) and standard deviation (~2 K) of seasonal changes [SOURCE: Page_18]

301   in Ts over the Miombo region. Results were compared to L-VOD retrievals considering dynamic ECMWF [SOURCE: Page_18]

302   estimations of Ts, as applied in the SMOS-IC algorithm. Again, little difference in the retrieved L-VOD values [SOURCE: Page_18]

303   from the different Ts settings were observed (Extended Data Figure 11). This suggests a relatively small impact [SOURCE: Page_18]

304   on L-VOD seasonality from possible uncertainties in the estimates of Ts computed from ECMWF data, which [SOURCE: Page_18]

305   may be attributable to the narrow seasonal changes in temperature over the Miombo region. Therefore, the [SOURCE: Page_18]

306   uncertainties and possible erroneous trends associated with the ECMWF temperature products cannot explain the [SOURCE: Page_18]

307   specific L-VOD signatures observed in this region. [SOURCE: Page_18]

308   Analysis of seasonal water balance [SOURCE: Page_18]

309   A variety of water-cycle components were plotted together to investigate the water balance at a US temperate [SOURCE: Page_18]

310   forest site and a tropical Miombo woodland site. Specifically, the SMOS L-VOD and SM data were smoothed [SOURCE: Page_18]

311   using a moving-average method (window size of 30 days). Daily estimates of transpiration were aggregated to a [SOURCE: Page_18]

312   16-day mean to match the MODIS LAI temporal resolution for better visualization. The onset of the rainy season [SOURCE: Page_18]

313   (or end of the dry season) in the tropical Miombo site was determined following the criteria^39: (1) a total rainfall [SOURCE: Page_18]

314   of 25 mm within 10 successive days, followed by (2) a total rainfall >20 mm within 20 days. The start of the dry [SOURCE: Page_18]

315   season (or end of the rainy season) was defined as the last day with rainfall >10 mm. [SOURCE: Page_18]
