10   The destruction of components within the manure is specifically the nitrogen load (by aerobic/anoxic treatment) or organic load (by aerobic or anaerobic treatment). Nitrogen removal 12   is via a process of nitrification (ammonia converted to nitrites and/or nitrates) followed by de- nitrification (nitrites and nitrates broken down to di-nitrogen gas). The technique is used in the 14   Brittany region of France to deal with nitrogen surpluses (Beline et al, 2004). Any biological process can be expected to breakdown organic matter; for aeration, this will be to carbon dioxide 16   and water; for anaerobic processes, this will be to acetic acid then methane. Components of manure that can not be eliminated (such as phosphorus or heavy metals) can only be removed by 18   separation, concentration and exportation (Daumer et al, 2004). Indeed, this may be desired for all excess nutrients including nitrogen and organic matter when there is a recognised value. 20   Separation can be by screening machines but these have a limited effect on finer suspended particles. Alternatively, there is sedimentation which is often by gravity using large shallow 22   vessels; this typically results in sludges with concentrations between 5 and 10% dry matter (Martinez and Burton, 1995). The process can be accelerated and extended by the use of a 24   decanter centrifuge which can take the concentration of the sludge to over 30%. Solid products whether sludges, screened materials or solid litter, can be blended with wheat straw and 26   composted to produce a potentially useful organic product sometimes saleable. 8 4.     Environmental impacts from intensive livestock production 10 4.1    Water pollution 12 Water pollution from animal production is caused by the release of effluent through or running 14   over soil. Nitrogen and phosphorus can be taken up by the growing crop but excesses will be released to the aquatic system; limited amounts can be immobilised in soil organic matter but 16   only up to the system capacity. The impact of free ammonia (rather than the ammonium ion) presents the greater impact on surface water as it is toxic to many fish even at very low 18   concentrations. In pig and cattle slurries, the proportion of N as ammonia can exceed 60 % of the total. This can be quickly nitrified after land spreading, leading to an excess of nitrates in the 20   soil. Organic-N is not immediately available to plants but it is estimated that around half will be converted to ammonium during the first 12 months after spreading. (Choudhary et al., 1996). 22   The phosphorus content of manure is of concern because there is a direct relationship between the amount of extractable-P in the soil and the concentration of dissolved P in the surface run-off 24   that can produce eutrophication of surface waters that are contaminated (Withers and Sharpley, 1995). The P in manure is present in both organic and inorganic forms, but the organic fraction 26   can be rapidly solubilised, so the mobility of P in animal manures can approach 100 %. In manures and slurries potassium is present as soluble salts, and it comes almost totally from the 28   urine of the animals. About 90 % of K in feed is excreted by the animals. Usually, the concentration is greater in cattle slurry than for pig slurry; K availability to plants often close to 30   100 % (Bernal et al., 1993). [SOURCE: Page_5]

32   4.2    Air pollution [SOURCE: Page_5]

34   Animal production is a major contributor to certain sources of atmospheric pollution (Pain, 1999). The ventilation air from livestock housing will contain odorous substances, methane, 36   nitrous oxide (N2O) and especially ammonia all of which can detrimentally effect the environment. In Europe, CH$_4$ emissions originate from manure management but mostly (two 38   thirds) from enteric fermentation of ruminants. Ammonia present in slurries largely arise from the degradation of urea. Emissions also follow field spreading and include, in addition, 40   quantities of nitrous oxide produced from the breakdown of nitrates themselves resulting from the nitrification of ammonia in the soil. 42 The threat to the environment from global warming is mostly attributed to the huge amounts of 44   carbon dioxide released into the atmosphere from anthropogenic activity. However the relatively small amounts of methane and nitrous oxide, have a disproportionate effect owing to their higher 46   warming potentials of x^25 and x^300 respectively (IPCC, 1995): they now contribute around 25 and 5% of the total effect. Concentration of all three greenhouse gases have increased by 30, 48   145 and 15 % for carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide respectively over the last 250 years (Moisier, 1998) although it should also be noted that the growth rate is increasing. For its part, 50   agriculture contributes 25, 65 and 90 % respectively of the net emission of these gases (Duxbury, 1994). The figure for carbon dioxide is greatly increased as a result of forest clearing but 52   emissions of methane and nitrous oxide relate closely to agricultural production. 6   4.3    Disease risks and health issues [SOURCE: Page_6]

8   In Europe, public concern related to food hygiene have been raised in recent years due to cases of contamination of certain agricultural food products (including salmonella, e-coli, 10   campylobacter and listeria). In addition there have been outbreaks of animal disease including foot and mouth, classical swine fever and more recently, avian influenza. Incidences of 12   contamination of water by zoonoses are relatively few, but they often represent a very serious event with human fatalities in some cases (Guan and Holley, 2003). In response to such 14   incidents, there have been some additional restrictions on where and how manures may be used but so far, not mandatory treatment. Suitable treatment options do exist including aeration, 16   anaerobic treatment, use of disinfectant chemicals and prolonged isolated storage. Thermal treatments present a more rigorous and reliable approach (Turner and Burton, 1997) but the use 18   of such technology is limited to specific areas where high risks occur. In response to the concerns on the quality of the farm produce, increasing limitations are applied on the use of 20   manure on crops; in some of the most vulnerable crops (eg: leaf crops eaten raw), applications of manure, even treated, is discouraged. Such a trend if extended may ultimately make land 22   application of manures as part of a farm cycle increasingly difficult. [SOURCE: Page_6]
