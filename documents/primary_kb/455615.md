The transport of sediment through river channels has major consequences for public safety, [SOURCE: Page_2]

management of water resources, and environmental sustainability. The overwhelming [SOURCE: Page_2]

majority of investigations of sediment transport in rivers has focused on mass flux and its [SOURCE: Page_2]

relation to water flow. Yet after more than a century of work we have no satisfactory theory [SOURCE: Page_2]

for bedload, the important component of the sediment load transported in contact with the [SOURCE: Page_2]

stream bed. Transport formulae often overpredict the actual rate by orders of magnitude (1). [SOURCE: Page_2]

Hence it is difficult to assess, for example, the impact on the channel of disturbances such as [SOURCE: Page_2]

extreme sediment-laden floods causing casualties, loss of property and public infrastructure, [SOURCE: Page_2]

and compromising water quality and aquatic habitat. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Bedload can also be viewed as a granular phenomenon. Much progress has been made [SOURCE: Page_2]

recently in the study of dry granular flows, but the knowledge gained has not, in general, [SOURCE: Page_2]

diffused into the river science community. Bedload transport can be divided into two stages [SOURCE: Page_2]

(2): partial mobility of local bed surface material when, at any one time, part of the bed [SOURCE: Page_2]

remains static but any exposed grain might eventually move; and full mobility, when all [SOURCE: Page_2]

grains move to a depth of several grain diameters. Grain-grain interactions over short time and [SOURCE: Page_2]

length scales bear importantly on the predictability of both stages. [SOURCE: Page_2]

No single constitutive law reproduces the diversity of behaviors observed in cohesionless [SOURCE: Page_2]

granular materials (3). Granular flows are often classified into three different states: a gaseous [SOURCE: Page_2]
