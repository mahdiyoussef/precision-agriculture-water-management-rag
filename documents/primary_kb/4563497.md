s^0010    5.1 Introduction p^0075 The Mediterranean region is particularly vulnerable to the adverse consequences of multiple and mutually reinforcing structural changes that include global warming, bio- diversity loss, soil erosion, and population growth. Many of these changes contribute to water scarcity, one the most pressing challenges the region is facing in the very near term. The southern and western shores of the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and North Africa (MENA) are already the most water-stressed regions in the world. But the Euro- pean shore is not spared, as Greece, Cyprus, Macedonia, and Spain also top the list of the world’s most water-stressed countries.a In many places, absolute water scarcity is com- pounded by a marked degradation in water quality due to pollution and salinization. The heightened competition over an increasingly scarce resource raises fears of social unrest and uncontrolled population flows. p^0080      If all economic sectors consume ever larger quantities of water, irrigated agriculture is still by far the largest user. Between 80% and 90% of all available water is consumed by irrigation in the southern shore, although that ratio is halved at the other end of the spectrum, in the case of France. Therefore, the widening gap between water supply and demand is primarily a challenge for food production, especially as all governments appear anxious to prioritize other uses (i.e., domestic and industrial) should a water shortage erupt. p^0085      To what extent, then, can water reuse be part of a broader policy mix aiming at pro- viding food production with a larger and more reliable supply of water when the resource is limited and irregular? And, in addition to its strictly quantitative potential, under what hydrological, agronomic, institutional conditions can water be reused safely and at rea- sonable cost? “water reuse” can be applied to a wide variety of situations. To begin with, the extent to which reused water is treated can vary considerably. Today, it has been calculated that a total of 36 million hectares worldwide (some 12% of all irrigated land) receive some urban wastewater; however, only around 15% of this water is deemed adequately treated [1]. When it is not, those who use wastewater typically disregard specific practices that might protect them or their consumers from health risks. Second, irrespective of whether it is adequately treated or not, wastewater can either be tapped at the point of rejection (direct reuse) or withdrawn a little farther away, mixed to varying degrees with surface or groundwater (indirect reuse). The latter is by far the most common, although it is not adequately captured by national statistics that define “reuse” only as direct reuse (Box 5.1). Finally, some projects are formally planned and regulated by state authorities while other types of uses occur informally, although in the latter case state officials may still exercise some measure of informal control. Again, and by definition, unplanned uses are not well captured by national statistics that focus on planned reuse. Thus, although examples of unplanned water reuse are common on the Southern shore of the Mediter- ranean, total wastewater reuse is only few volume of all officially quantified reclaimed wastewater. p^0090     Against this complex background, it should be noted that, over the past few decades, the development of more efficient treatment systems for domestic and industrial waste- water has made water reuse increasingly safe for human health, and an increasingly viable option in economic terms. Many projects now have an attractive cost-benefit ratio compared to the construction of dams that would provide a comparable volume of water. [SOURCE: Page_3]

b^0010      BOX 5.1 Main stages and types of wastewater treatment processes [2]. •    Before treatment, sewage usually goes through pretreatment to remove grit, grease, and gross solids that could hinder subsequent treatment stages. •    Next, primary treatment aims to settle and remove both organic and inorganic suspended solids. The most common primary treatments are primary settlers and septic tanks. •    During secondary treatment, soluble biodegradable organics are degraded and removed by bacteria and protozoa through (aerobic or anaerobic) biological processes. This process may include the removal of nutrients (mainly nitrogen and phosphorous). Typical secondary treatments include aerated lagoons, activated sludge, trickling filters, oxidation ditches, and other extensive processes such as constructed wetlands. •    Tertiary treatment aims at refining the effluent before it is discharged or reused and can include the removal of some nutrients and residual suspended matter by filtration, nutrients and residual suspended matter, or microorganisms (disinfection with chlorine, ozone, ultraviolet radiation, or others). Tertiary treatment can include membrane filtration (micro-, nano-, ultra- and reverse osmosis), infiltration/percolation, and activated carbon. This third stage/level seldom exists in low-income countries. [SOURCE: Page_3]

The potential for water reuse, and the fact that it could provide some good quality water in a reliable way throughout the year, is now widely recognized. However, water reuse can also be justified on grounds other than purely quantitative ones. At least three additional justifications are routinely given. First, wastewater supplies valuable additional nutriments (mainly nitrogen and phosphorus) to crops. Second, when officially orga- nized, it can provide an additional source of revenue for water utilities. Third, it can be considered a supplementary treatment stage that improves the quality of water ulti- mately discharged into ecosystems. Given these rationales, it is important to take a careful look at how this resource is currently managed, to assess whether it can live up to its promises, and to think about possible ways to better harness it by combining legal reforms, policy incentives, technological improvements, and better management practices. p^0095     Despite the ancient and well-recognized use of wastewater around the Mediterra- nean, scientific knowledge is still in need of improvement, especially with respect to real practices on the ground. The aim of this chapter is to provide an overview of the current situation across the region, including the types of treatment processes, legal frameworks, policy orientations, irrigation practices, and agronomical performances. Some local cases are analyzed in greater detail. Finally, an integrated approach is recommended for future studies that includes environmental, economic, and equity considerations. [SOURCE: Page_4]

s^0015    5.2 Water’s second life for agriculture s^0020    5.2.1 Definitions and qualities of water reuse p^0100 Water reuse is probably as old as cities themselves. According to Angelakis et al. [3], domestic wastewater (sewage) has been used for irrigation by a number of civilizations since the beginning of the Bronze Age (around 3200–1100 BC), including cities in Egypt, Mesopotamia, and Crete. Later (around 1000 BC–AD 330), wastewater was used for irrigation and fertilization by both Greek and Roman civilizations, especially around major cities such as Athens and Rome. Throughout human history, the term “reuse” has referred to the use of raw or only partially treated wastewater for beneficial purposes, mostly in agriculture. p^0105      In recent decades, a fundamental change has taken place, as the diffusion of treatment plants has allowed treated water reuse to become an object of public policies. Many coun- tries now consider water reuse as an important component of their overall water mix, mainly for the agricultural sector as irrigated area continues to expand [4, 5]. Countries like Jordan, where 87% of the safely treated wastewater is now reused directly or with little dilution, Israel, where treated wastewater accounted for 40% of all water used for irrigation in 2011, and to a lesser degree Tunisia, where about 25% of treated water is now reused, have become recognized leaders in this rapidly expanding field [6]. Israel, in particular, uses more than 300 Mm$_3$/year of tertiary-treated effluents or secondary [SOURCE: Page_4]

b^0015       BOX 5.2 Diversity of wastewater reuse on the Mediterranean rim. •    Direct use of untreated wastewater occurs in areas where collective treatment plants are not in place and where alternatives are limited, usually in arid or semiarid areas. For example, these practices may involve direct pumping of raw wastewater into the pipe before it arrives at the treatment plant. The reasons for such uses may also be linked with the poor quality of conventional water resources (e.g., groundwater salinity) or the exorbitant cost of access to fresh water (e.g., pumping costs). Although officially banned in most countries, direct use of untreated wastewater occurs in many urban and peri-urban areas [8]. •    Indirect or “de facto” use of untreated or treated wastewater is by far the most common type of use [2, 9]. In this case, untreated wastewater or wastewater from treatment plants is discharged into surface water streams and/or percolates to groundwater, where it dilutes and is subsequently used—most often unintentionally—by downstream farmers. For example, in the South of France, the estimated impact of wastewater effluents in the Montpellier river basin was estimated to vary between 1.5% and 51% on average, depending on flow conditions and seasons [10]. •    The planned use of reclaimed water (treated wastewater) is typically driven by water scarcity, but where the environment is highly degraded, the reclaimed wastewater is also used to save fresh water and ecosystems. Reclaimed water can be used directly for many purposes such as irrigation, city landscaping, and golf courses. Along the Mediterranean rim, wastewater is rarely used for groundwater recharge or indirectly used for drinking purposes, as is the case of Windhoek in Namibia [11]. The planned use of reclaimed water is now one of the priorities in the Middle East and North African countries, as well as in the Eastern and Mediterranean countries. [SOURCE: Page_6]

As mentioned above, part of the difficulty involved stems from the lack of official, aggre- gate data on illegal uses. However, case studies in Settat, Marrakesh, and Tiznit have all shown that at least part of the raw wastewater is already used downstream. The new pro- jects (for golfing resorts in Marrakesh, for agriculture in Settat and Tiznit) are undoubt- edly safer, as they are formally organized around treatment plants. But they all entail some reallocation from previous users to new beneficiaries, and in one case at least (i.e., Mar- rakech) this reallocation clearly shifted upward, targeting wealthy international con- sumers. However, to go beyond such anecdotal evidence, we would need better aggregate data on all current uses, including information on the types and numbers of beneficiaries, and the incomes they derive from wastewater, whether directly (through productive uses) or indirectly (through the rental value of their land). s^0110    5.5 Main conclusions p^0400 As the effects of climate change intensify, the levels of rainfall steadily decline and aquifers become systematically overexploited across the Mediterranean region, planned water reuse for agriculture is increasingly put forward by national governments as a valuable solution within a wider menu of options. In this sense, it is fundamentally different from desalinization. The reallocation it entails is often innocuous, when waste- water was highly diluted in a river before being reused downstream. But it can be more contentious when wastewater was either already reused directly (typically informally), or when it played a major support role during periods of low river flow. In such cases, reuse projects can still be justified on an economic basis, but former users need to be compen- sated for their loss, either economically or by being allowed to access other sources of water. p^0415     Finally, much like the water cycle in general, the water reuse chain for agriculture has chemical, hydrological, agronomical, economic, social, and political dimensions that cannot be viewed in isolation from each other when it comes to tackling real-world problems and situations. Interdisciplinary approaches and interdisciplinary teams of researchers should be encouraged. For instance, many fields of expertise should be called for to conduct sensible cost-benefit analysis where all types of relevant costs and benefits are appropriately considered and weighted. Different backgrounds are also appropriate to assess the many social, economic, and agronomical implications of technological inno- vations such as small-scale, decentralized treatment facilities. Abstract Climate change, irrigation development, and a growing population across the Mediterranean region are increasing the pressure on water resources in terms of both quantity and quality. High-quality water should be primarily reserved to drinking water while water reuse is, and should be, a growing part of the water mix for other uses. This chapter provides an overview of the current water reuse situation in agriculture across the region, including planned projects and unplanned practices. It highlights the diversity of policy contexts (legal frameworks, national strategies, and governance arrangements) by reviewing the specific experiences of Jordan, France, Morocco, and Algeria. It also highlights the diversity of irrigation practices on the ground, and discusses health-related issues and agronomical performances. It concludes by stressing the potential for planned water reuse, both direct and indirect, while reminding that water reuse should not be primarily perceived as a new resource by policy-makers, but rather as a means to raise water productivity. Finally, an integrated approach is recommended, capable of including environmental, economic, and equity considerations. [SOURCE: Page_32]
