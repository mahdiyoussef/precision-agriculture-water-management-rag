Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

Pressures on resources and climate change are likely to strongly impact the availability of water, which [SOURCE: Page_3]

directly affects agricultural systems. To estimate these impacts, we develop a prospective approach combining [SOURCE: Page_3]

an agricultural supply side economic model and a crop model. We extend previous work by incorporating water [SOURCE: Page_3]

resource constraints and apply our model to a large part of the French agricultural sector under three climate [SOURCE: Page_3]

scenarios over 2010-2010. Results indicate that, at a given water price, potential change in irrigation water [SOURCE: Page_3]

demand would differ strongly according to the region concerned and the scenario applied. In France as a whole, [SOURCE: Page_3]

irrigation increases in all scenarios, by 60% under the intermediate scenario, by 40% under the least extreme [SOURCE: Page_3]

scenario and by 20% under the toughest scenario. Differentiating the northern and southern regions, the relative [SOURCE: Page_3]

increase is more pronounced in the north, while demand in the south significantly increases under the [SOURCE: Page_3]

intermediate scenario and decreases under the toughest scenario. When considering autonomous adaptation of [SOURCE: Page_3]

farming systems to climate change, agricultural income in northern regions is likely to be negatively affected to a [SOURCE: Page_3]

greater extent than in southern regions. [SOURCE: Page_3]

### 1.   Introduction

Agriculture is a water-intensive economic sector in France. In 2009, 3 billion cubic meters of water were [SOURCE: Page_4]

extracted, among which 1 billion came from groundwater (Eau France 2012, Ministère de l’environnement [SOURCE: Page_4]

2017). Since water for agriculture is extracted without immediate return, this sector accounts for the greatest net [SOURCE: Page_4]

withdrawal. Given the projected 50% increase in food demand between 2012 and 2050 (Alexandratos and [SOURCE: Page_4]

Bruinsma 2012), the growing bioenergy market requiring more feedstock, the intensification of production [SOURCE: Page_4]

factors and the expansion of irrigated land, water demand is expected to increase in the up-coming years. As [SOURCE: Page_4]

domestic and industrial water uses sharply increase, the competition over water for agriculture can only intensify [SOURCE: Page_4]

(International Water Management Institute 2007) and further aggravate water scarcity. [SOURCE: Page_4]

The vulnerability of farms to less reliable water availability could be reduced by access to multiple water [SOURCE: Page_4]

resources (Mukherjee and Shwabe 2014). However, water availability for agriculture is expected to drop by 18% [SOURCE: Page_4]

as a result of competition over water resources by 2050 (Strzepek and Boehlert 2010). This risk is enhanced by [SOURCE: Page_4]

the potentially harmful consequences of climate change (CC). Changing precipitation patterns and inter-annual [SOURCE: Page_4]

variability, rising temperatures (which increase crop water requirements and alter growing season length), and [SOURCE: Page_4]

increasing frequency of extreme weather events would have strong impact on agriculture, especially on irrigation [SOURCE: Page_4]

(IPCC 2013; Foster et al. 2018). Pressures on water quantity have a direct impact on water quality and on [SOURCE: Page_4]

freshwater ecosystems. For instance, in aquifers, the pollutant concentration increases as water quantities [SOURCE: Page_4]

dwindle. Freshwater ecosystems are sensitive to both water quantity and quality (Oberdorff et al. 2002). In [SOURCE: Page_4]

France, the existence of disparate irrigation practices from one region to another, intensive in the south and less [SOURCE: Page_4]

widespread in the north, complicates territorial decision-making in terms of integrated resource management. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Tensions over water resource will be intensified by the threat of increasing restrictions on water withdrawals and [SOURCE: Page_4]

uses. [SOURCE: Page_4]

In the global context of economic and environmental changes, especially the constant evolution of water [SOURCE: Page_4]

resources both in time and geographical space, the aim of the present study is to provide economic prospective [SOURCE: Page_4]

views on future irrigation demand jointly with fertilizer demand and major crop supply in France. Our work [SOURCE: Page_4]

specifically focuses on the change in the economic demand for agricultural water under changing economic and [SOURCE: Page_4]

physical environments. Our study differs from previous works in accounting for irrigation costs and potential [SOURCE: Page_4]

land use change in terms of crops. In order to integrate the increasing pressure over water resources, we consider [SOURCE: Page_4]

that irrigation cost (or water price) reflects water scarcity. From an economic perspective, when water demand [SOURCE: Page_4]

exceeds the available resources, the cost is not high enough to regulate the use of water. A possible decline in [SOURCE: Page_5]

water availability is consequently taken into account by conducting simulations using increasing water prices. [SOURCE: Page_5]

The analysis focuses on four aspects: (i) the direct impact of evolving CC over the “climatic years” of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

period 2010-2100 in three contrasting scenarios, (ii) the impact on mean and variance of demand for irrigation [SOURCE: Page_5]

water when considering climatic years as probabilistic occurrences of CC, (iii) the impact of increasing water [SOURCE: Page_5]

price pressure, and (iv) geographical contrasts at an infra-national level. Our approach incorporates two levels of [SOURCE: Page_5]

autonomous adaptation practices by farming systems, namely at the plot and the farm-group levels. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Various methods have been developed in order to assess the environmental and economic effects of CC, such [SOURCE: Page_5]

as climatic approaches (Van der Velde et al. 2010; Supit et al. 2010), econometric models (Mendelson et al. [SOURCE: Page_5]

agronomic information (Janssen and van Ittersum 2007). In other words, we analyze producers’ choices in terms [SOURCE: Page_5]

of crop area and the use of inputs when anticipating changes in climate affecting crop-growth processes. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Moreover, in the papers that discuss the impacts of climate change on the agricultural water resource, most [SOURCE: Page_5]
