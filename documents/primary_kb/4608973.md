## 31   ABSTRACT

32   Understanding the distribution of moisture throughout the soil profile is crucial for effective [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   water management in the field of agriculture. This knowledge enables farmers and water [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   managers to make informed decisions regarding the timing and amount of irrigation needed [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   to optimize crop growth. To estimate the soil moisture profile, this study utilized L-band and [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   P-band radiometry with three multilayer incoherent models, based on a zero-order (IZ), first [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   order (IF), and incoherent solution (IS) approximation, as well as the conventional tau-omega [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   (TO) model. The result of these models was also compared with the coherent model results [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   published by Brakhasi et al. (2023). Two mathematical shape functions - linear (Li) and [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   second-order polynomial (Pn$_2$) - were used to represent the soil moisture profile. [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   Observations from a tower-based experiment were used under different land cover [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   conditions, namely bare, bare-weed, grass, wheat, and corn. The Root Mean Square Error [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   (RMSE) was calculated between the observed and estimated soil moisture profiles, with the [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   results indicating that RMSE values were similar for all four radiative transfer models, with [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   the Pn$_2$ function outperforming the Li function in deeper layers. The multilayer incoherent [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   models generated slightly better results than the conventional TO model, particularly for the [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   shallow layers, but their complexity was not justified for the small gain in performance. [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   Additionally, the comparison between conventional and multi-layer incoherent models with [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   stratified coherent Njoku model was reveal that the latter slightly outperformed the formers [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   under dry bare soil condition. The conventional TO model provided an average profile [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   estimation depth ranging from 1cm (under corn) to 39cm (under bare), depending on the soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   moisture profile gradient and value in the shallow layers. These findings pave the way for [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   estimating soil moisture profile on a global scale using combined L-band and P-band [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   radiometry from future satellite missions operating at these two bands. [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   Keywords: Soil moisture profile, P-band, L-band, Incoherent, tau-omega, Estimation depth [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   Soil moisture information is a must for all important ecological aspects and plays a critical [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   role in all physiological processes (Swaroop Meena and Datta, 2021). From a hydrological [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   perspective it is a small fraction (0.05%) of the freshwater globally (2.5%). However, its [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   importance to water, energy, and carbon cycles and the distribution of precipitation far [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   surpasses its physical amount (Oki and Kanae, 2006; Robinson et al., 2008). It is highly [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   variable both in space, especially in the top 20cm of the soil (Shi et al., 2014), and time, due [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   to variability in precipitation (Schoener and Stone, 2020; Manoj et al., 2022), vegetation, and [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   soil texture (Baroni et al., 2013). The roots of major agricultural crops with the highest [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   density are found at depths of 20cm or less, according to Haberle and Svoboda (2015). Lack [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   of adequate soil moisture in this region can therefore restrict plant growth and crop yield [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   (Svoboda, P. et al., 2020), thereby impacting food security (Sadri et al., 2020). In a study [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   conducted by Ma et al (2023), the effects of surface drip irrigation and direct root-zone [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   irrigation on the productivity of grapevine and berry were compared. The results revealed that [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   the implementation of direct root-zone irrigation substantially improved the crop productivity [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   and quality of Chardonnay grapes by 23 – 34%, while also reducing the necessary irrigation [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   amount by 16 – 23%. [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   Passive microwave remote sensing is a well-accepted technique for estimation of soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   moisture, with the two current missions of SMOS (Soil Moisture and Ocean Salinity) and [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   SMAP (Soil Moisture Active Passive) operating at L-band (~ 21cm wavelength). However, [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   they are limited to estimating soil moisture in the top ~ 5cm of the soil column. Currently [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   there are eight global root zone soil moisture products, including GLDAS NOAH, ERA-5, [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   MERRA-2, NCEP R$_1$, NCEP R$_2$, JRA-55, SMAP level 4 and SMOS level 4, which are [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   derived by model-based simulations sometimes combined with the shallow soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   observations from satellite. But the accuracy of these products is highly dependent on several [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   factors, such as the forcing data, model structure and parameterization, data assimilation [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   method and assimilated variables (Xu et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   The advent of P-band radiometry presents a novel opportunity for the remote sensing [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   community, enabling the investigation of soil moisture at deeper layers. Having a wavelength [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   of approximately 40cm, means that emissions can be observed from deeper in the soil than [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   other frequencies (Shen et al., 2020), such as L-band, and are also comparatively less [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   susceptible to interference from vegetation and surface roughness (Shen et al., 2022a, 2022b). [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   By integrating L-band and P-band signals, emanating from different depths of the soil, it is [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   expected that soil moisture can be estimated beyond the extent of each frequency [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   individually. Accordingly, Brakhasi et al. (2023a) successfully estimated the soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   profile using a multilayer coherent approach through the utilization of combined L-band and [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   P-band radiometry on bare soil. Upon implementation of a second-order polynomial (linear) [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   profile shape function, the outcome yielded 28cm(20cm) and 5cm(5cm) as the respective soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   moisture profile estimation depths during dry and wet periods respectively. [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   The estimation of soil moisture variation within the profile from microwave observations [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   becomes increasingly complex when there is a layer of vegetation present on the surface, as [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   opposed to barren soil conditions. Furthermore, in comparison to incoherent models, coherent [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   models are more computationally intensive, yet their accuracy in estimated brightness [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   temperature is comparable to that of incoherent models (Brakhasi et al. 2023b). Additionally, [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   the linearity of soil moisture at depths of less than 30cm potentially allows for the utilization [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   of the conventional Tau Omega model as a replacement for multilayer incoherent models. [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   Therefore, this study assessed the multi-frequency soil moisture profile estimation capability [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   using combined L- and P-band with incoherent models under conditions of bare, bare-weed, [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   grass, wheat and corn. [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   2. Experimental Data [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   This study is based on radiometric measurements acquired from a tower-based experiment [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   established from December 2017 to June 2022 at Coral Lynn, Victoria, Australia (Brakhasi et [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   al, 2023b). The tower carrying two radiometers, namely PLMR (Polarimetric L-band Multi- [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   beam Radiometer) and PPMR (Polarimetric P-band Multi-beam Radiometer), was at the [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   center of a paddock (150m×150m). The paddock was divided into four quadrants and [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   managed with different conditions in terms of surface roughness and vegetation. For this [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   study, a variety of land surface conditions are explored, including dry and wet profiles under [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   two different bare soil periods (2nd to 30th April 2019, 3rd to^16th March 2020), one bare soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   with weeds (18th November 2020 to 8th December 2020), one grass (1st to 27th March 2018), [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   one wheat (1st to 23rd December 2018), and one corn (20th December 2020 to 4th March 2021) [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   with very low to high vegetation water contents (VWC from 0.5 to 22 kg/m^3). [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   The dataset includes brightness temperature at L- and P-band frequencies at an incidence [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   angle of ~ 40°, soil moisture and soil temperature profile measurements at ground stations [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   installed at the border of the quadrants from the surface to a depth of 60cm in 5cm [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   increments, and weekly measurements of VWC and surface roughness. The radiometer was [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   Hydra-probe Data Acquisition System (HDAS; Merlin et al., 2007). The composition of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   soil texture, at an average depth of 0 to 60cm, is made up of 18.3% clay, 13.7% sand, and [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   68% silt. This composition indicates a silty loam type, with a surface layer having a bulk [SOURCE: Page_6]

142   3. Methodology [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   The goal of this study is to investigate the performance of the incoherent models for [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   estimating soil moisture profile variation using combined L-band and P-band radiometer [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   observations. Furthermore, these models will be evaluated in comparison to the performance [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   of the stratified coherent Njoku model utilized by Brakhasi et al. (2023). Estimating soil [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   moisture throughout the profile allows for a more detailed understanding of the distribution [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   and availability of water in the soil, which can inform irrigation and water management [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   decisions, as well as predictions of crop yield and water use efficiency. Additionally, [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   monitoring soil moisture at different depths can provide insight into the movement of water [SOURCE: Page_8]

7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   through the soil and the dynamics of the water cycle in a given area. In this study, the [SOURCE: Page_9]

152   performance of four different incoherent models is compared, including the conventional tau- [SOURCE: Page_9]

153   omega model and three multilayer incoherent models based on zero-order, first order and [SOURCE: Page_9]

154   incoherent solution approximation of volume scattering inside the soil, to determine which is [SOURCE: Page_9]

155   most effective at estimating the soil moisture profile. [SOURCE: Page_9]

156   3.1    Radiative transfer models [SOURCE: Page_9]

157   The soil moisture content of a near-surface layer of soil can be estimated by utilizing a [SOURCE: Page_9]

158   microwave emission model that converts brightness temperature observations from a [SOURCE: Page_9]

159   microwave radiometer to soil moisture. Almost all of the efforts from the early 1970s to [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   1990s were concentrated on developing, verifying and improving the basic microwave [SOURCE: Page_9]

161   emission models for smooth and rough soils (i.e. Mo et al., 1982; Njoku, 1976; Stogryn, [SOURCE: Page_9]

162   1970; Wilheit, 1978) using the data acquired from some controlled field campaigns including [SOURCE: Page_9]

163   ground-based (by a flatbed truck or a mobile tower; Lee, 1974; Wang et al., 1982) and [SOURCE: Page_9]

164   airborne ( by an airplane or a helicopter; Ferrazzoli et al., 1992; Paloscia et al., 1993). [SOURCE: Page_9]

165   From these investigations, baseline approaches to account for three major variables; physical [SOURCE: Page_9]

166   temperature, surface roughness and vegetation water content, were developed. These [SOURCE: Page_9]

167   advances resulted in the current form of the radiative transfer equation that has been applied [SOURCE: Page_9]

168   to vegetative conditions (the tau-omega model; Mo et al., 1982), which serves as the basis for [SOURCE: Page_9]

169   almost all operational retrieval algorithms, including those used by the SMOS and SMAP [SOURCE: Page_9]

170   missions (Entekhabi et al., 2014; Kerr et al., 2012; Mladenova et al., 2014; Wigneron et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

382   5.   Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_29]

383   Four incoherent passive microwave models including the conventional tau-omega model [SOURCE: Page_29]

384   (TO), and three multilayers models based on zero-order (IZ), first-order (IF) and incoherent [SOURCE: Page_29]

385   solution (IS) scattering approximations were compared to estimate soil moisture profile. The [SOURCE: Page_29]

386   result of these models was also compared with the stratified coherent model results of Njoku [SOURCE: Page_29]

387   model (NM; Njoku and Kong, 1977) employed in Brakhasi et al. (2023). Observations from a [SOURCE: Page_29]

388   tower-based experiment at Cora Lynn, Victoria, Australia under different land cover [SOURCE: Page_29]

389   conditions (bare, bare-weed, grass, wheat, and corn) including L-band and P-band brightness [SOURCE: Page_29]

390   temperature, soil moisture and temperature at different depths in the profile to 60cm, along [SOURCE: Page_29]

391   with weekly measurements of vegetation water content and surface roughness were used. A [SOURCE: Page_29]

392   time series approach was employed by combining L-band and P-band brightness temperature [SOURCE: Page_29]

393   observations with either a linear (Li) or second-order polynomial (Pn$_2$) profile shape function [SOURCE: Page_29]

394   to represent the soil moisture profile variation with depth. The RMSE between observed and [SOURCE: Page_29]

395   estimated soil moisture profiles was calculated at various depths, with the multilayer [SOURCE: Page_29]

396   incoherent models IZ, IF, and IS having slightly better results than the conventional TO [SOURCE: Page_29]

397   model, especially at shallow layers. However, the complexity of these multi-layer models and [SOURCE: Page_29]

398   the additional information on soil temperature throughout the profile, was found to be [SOURCE: Page_29]

399   unwarranted. Moreover, it was found that the Pn$_2$ profile shape function outperformed the Li [SOURCE: Page_29]

400   shape function in terms of accuracy and robustness, particularly at deeper layers. However, [SOURCE: Page_29]

401   the estimation depth under the target RMSE of 0.04m^3/m^3 varied widely depending on the [SOURCE: Page_29]

402   soil moisture conditions, ranging from 1cm to 39cm. Overall, the results of this study [SOURCE: Page_29]

403   showcase the potential of integrating L-band and P-band radiometry to estimate soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_29]

404   profile information at the global scale, utilizing incoherent models that perform just as [SOURCE: Page_29]

405   effectively as the coherent model. This has important implications for a wide range of [SOURCE: Page_29]

406   applications, including agriculture, climate science, and disaster management, and [SOURCE: Page_29]

28 [SOURCE: Page_29]

407   demonstrates the value proposition for a combined L- and P-band passive microwave satellite [SOURCE: Page_30]

408   mission for global soil moisture profile mapping. [SOURCE: Page_30]
