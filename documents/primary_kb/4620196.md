## 31   ABSTRACT

32   Precision irrigation management and crop water stress assessment rely on accurate estimation [SOURCE: Page_4]

33   of root zone soil moisture. However, only the top 5 cm soil moisture can be estimated using [SOURCE: Page_4]

34   the two current passive microwave satellite missions, Soil Moisture and Ocean Salinity [SOURCE: Page_4]

35   (SMOS) and Soil Moisture Active Passive (SMAP), which operate at L-band (wavelength of [SOURCE: Page_4]

36   ~21 cm). Since the contributing depth of the soil to brightness temperature increases with [SOURCE: Page_4]

37   observation wavelength, it is expected that a P-band (wavelength of ~40 cm) radiometer could [SOURCE: Page_4]

38   potentially provide soil moisture information from deeper layers of the soil profile. Moreover, [SOURCE: Page_4]

f oo 39   by combining both L- and P- bands, it is hypothesized that the soil moisture profile can be [SOURCE: Page_4]

r 40   estimated even beyond their individual observation depths. The aim of this study was to [SOURCE: Page_4]

41 -p demonstrate the potential of combined L-band and P-band radiometer observations to estimate re 42   the soil moisture profile under flat bare soil using a stratified coherent forward model. lP [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   Brightness temperature observations at L-band and P-band from a tower based experimental na [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   site across a dry (April 2019) and a wet (March 2020) period, covering different soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   profile shapes, were used in this study. Results from an initial synthetic study showed that the ur [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   performance of a combined L-band and P-band approach was better than the performance of Jo [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   using either band individually, with an average depth over which reliable soil moisture profile [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   information could be estimated (i.e. with a target root mean square error (RMSE) of less than [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   0.04 m^3/m^3) being 20 cm for linear and 15 cm for second-order polynomial functions. Other [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   functions were also tested but found to have a poorer performance. Applying the method to [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   the tower-based brightness temperature achieved an average estimation depth of 28 cm (20 [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   cm) and 5 cm (5 cm) during the dry and wet periods respectively when using a second-order [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   polynomial (linear) function. These findings highlight the opportunity of a satellite mission [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   with L-band and P-band observations to accurately estimate the soil moisture profile to as deep [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   as 30cm globally. [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   Soil moisture is a key state variable in the water, energy, and carbon cycles (Falloon et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   2011; Zhang et al., 2019). While soil moisture accounts for only a small fraction of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   freshwater globally (0.15%), it has an important impact on rainfall-runoff processes (Brocca et [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   al., 2012), regulates net ecosystem exchange (Chu et al., 2019), constrains food security (Sadri [SOURCE: Page_5]

f 63   et al., 2020), and influences land-atmosphere interactions (Yuan et al., 2020). However, many [SOURCE: Page_5]

oo 64   studies have shown large variability in the spatial and temporal distribution of soil moisture, [SOURCE: Page_5]

r 65                                                -p especially in the top 20 cm of the soil (Shi et al., 2014), emphasizing the necessity of monitoring re 66   these variations. Moisture in this region of the soil profile limits the plant's photosynthetic lP [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   activity and transpiration (Seneviratne et al., 2010; Reich et al., 2018). In addition, information [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   on the root zone soil moisture is used for irrigation scheduling (Liang et al., 2016), na [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   understanding of plant stress and pesticide management (Malone et al., 2004; Jiang et al., ur [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   2021). As compared to estimation of moisture in the shallow layer, root zone soil moisture Jo [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   estimation is more challenging (Etminan et al., 2020). Accurate spatial and periodic mapping [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   of this vital variable through direct measurement is almost impossible due to its cost-intensive [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   and time-consuming measurement, higher spatio-temporal variability, and non-linear [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   relationship with surface soil moisture (Das and Mohanty, 2006; Sabater et al., 2007; Hu and [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   Si, 2014; Gao et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   Microwave remote sensing techniques have been identified as the most promising approach for [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   global observation of near-surface soil moisture content (Karthikeyan et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   Specifically, passive microwave remote sensing at L-band has been widely adopted with [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   current remote sensing satellites dedicated to the monitoring of soil moisture, including the [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   European Space Agency (ESA) Soil Moisture and Ocean Salinity (SMOS; Kerr et al., 2010) [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   and the National Aeronautics and Space Agency (NASA) Soil Moisture Active Passive [SOURCE: Page_6]

82   (SMAP; Entekhabi et al., 2014) missions. While L-band can observe a deeper layer of soil than [SOURCE: Page_6]

83   shorter wavelengths, its soil moisture measurement is limited to just a few centimeters of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

84   soil (Zheng et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2020, 2022a). Therefore, researchers have investigated [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   different techniques for estimating the root zone soil moisture from surface soil moisture [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   including multiple regression (Qiu et al., 2010; Mahmood et al., 2012), data assimilation [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   (Walker et al., 2001; Baldwin et al., 2017), and statistical/empirical methods such as machine [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   learning (Carranza et al., 2021; Karthikeyan and Mishra, 2021; Xia et al., 2022), principle of [SOURCE: Page_6]

f oo 89   maximum entropy (Mishra et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2016), and exponential filters (Mishra et [SOURCE: Page_6]

r 90   al., 2020). The approach chosen is usually based on the application, level of complexities [SOURCE: Page_6]

91 -p involved and the amount of a priori information available. When it comes to applications on a re 92   large scale, the number of inputs needed is of utmost importance, especially in areas where data lP [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   is scarce. As a result, approaches that require minimal inputs have gained attention in these na [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   types of applications. Generally, all the models require establishing a relationship between [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   surface and root-zone soil moisture. However, the relationship between the two is often ur [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   nonlinear and becomes weaker with depth, making it challenging to capture using conventional Jo [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   statistical techniques (Ford et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   The multiple regression models are simple and relatively straightforward to interpret, but they [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   have limitations in handling complex non-linear relationships between input and output [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   variables. These models are also sensitive to outliers, which means that even a small number [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   of extreme values can significantly affect the results. Conversely, machine learning algorithms [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   are well-suited to handle non-linear relationships between inputs and outputs, making them [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   suitable for modeling complex soil moisture patterns. However, they require large amounts of [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   training data to produce accurate estimates, which can be difficult to obtain in some cases. [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   Additionally, some machine learning algorithms can be difficult to interpret, making it [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   challenging to understand the physical basis for their predictions and to identify areas for [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   improvement. There is also a risk of overfitting, where the algorithm becomes too specialized [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   to the training data and does not generalize well to new data. The principle of maximum entropy [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   method does not rely on prior information about the profile, but it requires the values of surface [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   soil moisture, the average moisture content, and the moisture content of the bottom-most layer, [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   which are difficult to obtain. The exponential filter only requires the time series of surface soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   moisture, which is easily available from microwave sensors, however sensitivity is reduced [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   during prolonged dry periods and in deeper layers where plant uptake is the main factor [SOURCE: Page_7]

f oo 114   affecting root-zone moisture movement due to the assumption of no transpiration and constant [SOURCE: Page_7]

r 115   hydraulic conductivity. [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   be more sensitive to soil moisture over deeper layers (∼10 cm; Shen et al., 2020). As the L- [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   band and P-band emissions are derived from different depths in the soil (Shen et al., 2020), [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   2015, 2016, 2017; Sadeghi et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2018; Etminan et al., 2020; Yueh et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   concept to be promising, they have typically applied constraints, been limited to snapshot [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   retrieval, and been applied to radar rather than radiometer observations. Moreover, a [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   comprehensive investigation of the different mathematical functions that might be used to [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   represent the soil moisture profile is lacking. Importantly, compared with a radiometer, radar [SOURCE: Page_8]

f oo 139   is more sensitive to vegetation and surface roughness (Engman and Chauhan, 1995), and thus [SOURCE: Page_8]

r 140   it is possible that multi-frequency L-and P-band radiometer observations could provide more [SOURCE: Page_8]

141 -p accurate soil moisture profile estimation than that obtained from radar. re 142   The aim of this research was to study the use of multi-frequency (L- and P-band) radiometry lP [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   to estimate the root zone profile for flat bare soil. The forward stratified coherent model of na [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   Njoku and Kong (1977) was applied to calculate brightness temperature (TB) from soil ur [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   moisture and temperature profiles. While the coherent and incoherent models have the same Jo [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   general trend, the former includes phase-interference oscillations (Ulaby and Long, 2014). The [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   main differences between them relate to the effects of interference, which is a function of [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   frequency and the steepness of soil moisture profile near the surface (Schmugge and [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   Choudhury, 1981). When data from regions of rapid sub-surface moisture variations (rapid [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   drying out or a region having a subsurface water table) are interpreted with depth, the [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   incoherent models become inaccurate, since coherent reflections are not accounted for. Also, [SOURCE: Page_8]

152   when there is considerable diurnal surface temperature variation, incoherent models become [SOURCE: Page_8]

153   inaccurate for longer wavelengths. Thus, the coherent models of Njoku and Kong (1977) and [SOURCE: Page_8]

154   Wilheit (1978) were introduced and formulated in terms of continuous and discrete varying [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   dielectric constant within the soil, respectively. Only a small difference was observed between [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   the Njoku and Wilheit models (Schmugge and Choudhury, 1981) and so in this research the [SOURCE: Page_9]

157   Njoku model was used. [SOURCE: Page_9]
