12                 Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

13    The environmental impact of subsurface drainage and agricultural activities has been widely studied [SOURCE: Page_1]

14    in the literature. Agricultural subsurface drainage modifies the hydrological behavior and accelerates [SOURCE: Page_1]

16    is often limited to winter. The main objective of this study is to present a modeling approach allowing [SOURCE: Page_1]

21    values > 0.75) and predict, with good accuracy, the drain flow start time (with a median value of 5 days [SOURCE: Page_1]

31   1    Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   Land improvements contribute to agricultural intensification, which in turn leads to greater crop [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   production from the same parcel. Among such improvements, subsurface drainage (SD) is practiced to [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   remove the temporary excess water from the root zone for purposes of: better aeration, higher [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   temperatures and enhanced workability of the soil in humid regions. In arid and semi-arid regions, the [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   primary function of SD is to prevent irrigation-induced waterlogging and soil salinization (Ritzema, [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   1994). In Europe, SD is mainly devoted to preventing waterlogging in specific hydromorphic soils; this [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   practice is principally employed in Northern Europe, on over 50% of arable land (from the Netherlands [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   to Finland), vs. approximately 10-20% for other European countries. In France, out of the 27 million [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   hectares of arable land, more than 10% are subsurface drained (AGRESTE, 2010; Vincent, 2020). [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   Subsurface drainage was established in France later than in other European countries, i.e. during the [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   1980's; it was promoted by European and national-level subsidies until 1992, when current water laws [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   began limiting the extent of additional SD practices. [SOURCE: Page_2]

44   Agricultural SD introduces significant modifications to the hydrological behavior and results in nutrient [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   and fertilizer losses from farmland to surface water (Blann et al., 2009; Gramlich et al., 2018). During [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   the drainage period, the application of mobile pesticides or fertilizers before a heavy rainfall increases [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   their losses toward the SD outlet and the water surface (Boithias et al., 2014; Nolan et al., 2008). The [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   temporary functioning of SD systems, mainly during the wet season, allows adapting the timing of [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   when to apply farm inputs to reduce losses, e.g. increasing the interval between an application and [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   the first drain flow of the drainage season (Brown and van Beinum, 2009; Trajanov et al., 2015; Wilson [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   et al., 2020). An accurate prediction of this first drain flow is key to optimizing the timing of farm input [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   application, thereby decreasing the risk of their transfer toward the water surface (L Jones et al., 2000; [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   Lewan et al., 2009; Nolan et al., 2008; Trajanov et al., 2015; Willkommen et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   The behavior of SD systems can be simulated by means of a physical or hydraulic approach. The [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   physical approach is based on the numerical resolution of a physical law, such as the 3D Richards [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   equation. Several physically-based models propose specific conditions for considering the presence of [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   SD, e.g. in HYDRUS, SOIL, CATHY, DDM and 2D-DPERM (Al Jabri and Youngs, 2015; Boivin et al., 2006; [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   Dusek and Vogel, 2014; Gärdenäs et al., 2006; Gatel et al., 2019; Gerke et al., 2013; Mehdinejadiani [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   and Fathi, 2020; Shokri and Bardsley, 2016). The hydraulic-based approach merely considers the flow [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   in a saturated zone and proposes a simplified equation, derived from both the Boussinesq equation [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   (1904) and Forchheimer's hypothesis (Guyon, 1963; Henine et al., 2014; Van Schilfgaarde, 1963), to [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   calculate drainage discharge as a function of water table height at mid-drain. The Hooghoudt drainage [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   equation (Hooghoudt, 1940), which relates steady-state drainage discharge to the water table height [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   at mid-drain, is the most widely used approach in SD modeling. When the water table rises to the soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   surface for relatively long periods, drainage discharge is computed using the Kirkham equation [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   (Kirkham, 1949) for ponded water. The approach proposed by Moriasi et al. (2013) couples Kirkham's [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   simplified equations with the Hooghoudt equation (Hooghoudt, 1940; Kirkham, 1949) to examine long [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   time series of subsurface drained experimental facilities; this approach offers the advantage of [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   simulating both small and peak flow discharges. The Kirkham and Hooghoudt equations were [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   integrated into the DRAINMOD model (Skaggs, 1980). The models MACRO (Jarvis and Larsbo, 2012), [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   SWAP (Kroes et al., 2008) and many others adopt different equations (such as Hooghoudt, Kirkham’s [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   and Ernst equations) or use seepage potential theory by considering the drain as a sink term to take [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   into account subsurface drainage (Qi and Qi, 2016; Youngs, 1980). Guyon (1963) and Lesaffre and [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   Zimmer (1987) developed the SIDRA (SImulation of DRAinage) model to simulate drainage discharge [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   during the drainage season, based on the numerical integration of Boussinesq's equation between the [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   drain and mid-drain. In relying on this same Boussinesq equation, Stillman et al. (2006) proposed [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   another semi-analytical equation to simulate drainage discharge. These various simplified approaches [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   have been incorporated into several distributed hydrological and GIS-based models for the purpose of [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   integrating the SD function, e.g. SWAT (Moriasi et al., 2012), HYPE (Lindstrom et al., 2010) and ADAPT [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   (Gowda et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   The models derived from the Boussinesq equation are well adapted to simulate the hydrological [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   behavior of SD, including peak flow prediction (Lesaffre and Zimmer, 1988). However, they become [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   less suitable once the goal turns to predicting the environmental impact of agricultural SD. It is [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   necessary to take into account the hydrodynamic behavior of the unsaturated zone as well as the plant [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   growth impact on water table recharge and, hence, on drainage discharge. In this context, a coupling [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   of the behavior in both saturated and unsaturated zones within a simple hydraulic approach for SD [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   behavior modeling would offer a better understanding of the underlying environmental impacts. A [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   coupled agronomic-hydrological model, namely STICS-SIDRA (Tournebize et al., 2004), was developed [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   to manage the saturated and unsaturated zones; however, this crop model requires numerous [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   parameters only available in certain fields of experimental research. [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   This paper integrates a new conceptual soil reservoir module into the hydraulic-based SIDRA model in [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   order to predict both drainage discharge and drain flow starts. The conceptual module simulates water [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   transfer in the unsaturated zone and calculates the recharge of the perched water table, which then [SOURCE: Page_4]

94   serves as SIDRA input data. To simplify the model as much as possible, this conceptualization needs to [SOURCE: Page_4]

95   be easily implemented and should improve both the prediction of drainage discharge and drain flow [SOURCE: Page_4]

96   start every hydrological year. [SOURCE: Page_4]

186   3   Model description [SOURCE: Page_10]

187   Drain flow occurs when the perched water table rises above the drains. The SIDRA model simulates [SOURCE: Page_10]

188   drainage discharge during the soil profile saturation period, defined as the Intensive Drainage Season [SOURCE: Page_10]

189   (IDS) by Lesaffre and Zimmer (1988). The new SIDRA-RU model presented herein allows SIDRA to [SOURCE: Page_10]

190   simulate SD discharges and hydrological behavior at the plot scale during a full hydrological year and [SOURCE: Page_10]

191   extending for several subsequent years. The proposed model has been based on three coupled [SOURCE: Page_10]

192   modules (Figure 2) managing the flow paths: in the soil surface, in the unsaturated zone, and in the [SOURCE: Page_10]

193   water table temporally perched above the drains. These three modules, integrated into the current [SOURCE: Page_10]

194   version of SIDRA-RU, are as follows: [SOURCE: Page_10]

195            1. Module 1: Estimation of net infiltration from climatic data, by integrating a more realistic [SOURCE: Page_10]

196                evapotranspiration algorithm that takes into account the potential evapotranspiration [SOURCE: Page_10]

197                rate (PET) and available soil water content in the soil reservoir S(t). The new value [SOURCE: Page_10]

198                calculated during this step is referred to as “corrected evapotranspiration” (or CET). Net [SOURCE: Page_10]

199                infiltration is equal to rainfall (P) minus corrected evapotranspiration (CET); [SOURCE: Page_10]

10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

200            2. Module 2: Management of water transfer in the unsaturated zone, in order to calculate [SOURCE: Page_11]

201                the net recharge into the drained water table, based on the conceptual reservoir [SOURCE: Page_11]

202                approach. This module calculates net recharge as a function of the available soil water [SOURCE: Page_11]

203                content in the soil reservoir S(t), relative to three distinct soil reservoir levels, namely [SOURCE: Page_11]

204                minimum (Smin), intermediate (Sinter) and maximum (Smax); [SOURCE: Page_11]

205            3. Module 3: Simulation of the SD discharge (Q) as a function of the mid-drain water table [SOURCE: Page_11]

206                height above drain (H) by applying the SIDRA model (Lesaffre and Zimmer, 1988). [SOURCE: Page_11]

237   3.2 Management of water flow in the soil reservoir [SOURCE: Page_13]

238   It has been assumed herein that throughout the hydrological season, the soil water content only varies [SOURCE: Page_13]

239   under the effects of rainfall, evapotranspiration and drainage discharge. [SOURCE: Page_13]

240   Modeling soil water movement in unsaturated zone to assess water table recharge should cover [SOURCE: Page_13]

241   several complex processes (Milly, 1988; Nielsen et al., 1986; S\imů nek and Bradford, 2008; van [SOURCE: Page_13]

242   Genuchten and Jury, 1987). In the literature, numerous models describing water flow in this zone are [SOURCE: Page_13]

243   available and they use either physical-based or conceptual approach. In this study, we present a [SOURCE: Page_13]

244   conceptual calculation of the water table recharge. Downward water movement through homogenous [SOURCE: Page_13]

245   soil profile is governed mainly by both gravity and matric pressure gradients ( ψ) effects. When the soil [SOURCE: Page_13]

246   is dry, the ψ gradients effect is dominating, and when the soil is very wet, gravity effect becomes [SOURCE: Page_13]

247   dominant (Nimmo, 2005). Along the soil profile (extending from the soil surface to the impervious layer [SOURCE: Page_13]

248   under the drains), the soil water content is not uniform and variable with respect to time (Zimmer and [SOURCE: Page_13]

249   Lesaffre, 1989). Indeed, the soil profile can present at the same time dry condition near the soil surface [SOURCE: Page_13]

250   and wet condition at the drain level. Thus, instead of considering this spatial variation, the soil reservoir [SOURCE: Page_13]

251   model depicts the filling of the soil profile and calculates water table recharge as a function of three [SOURCE: Page_13]

252   global thresholds (see Figure 2), i.e.: [SOURCE: Page_13]

253   1) The minimum soil reservoir level (Smin) represents the lower limit of the soil reservoir, below which [SOURCE: Page_13]

254   no water moves in the soil under the effects of either evaporation or root extraction, herein set equal [SOURCE: Page_13]

255   to zero; this reflects the driest soil profile condition. [SOURCE: Page_13]

256   2) The maximum soil reservoir level (Smax) represents the upper limit, at which the total net infiltration [SOURCE: Page_13]

257   recharges the water table gravitationally. In this case, the soil water content lies close to the saturation [SOURCE: Page_13]

258   condition throughout the soil profile. The SD network reaction to a rainfall event is rapid, mainly [SOURCE: Page_13]

259   occurring during the high rainfall events that generate drainage discharge peaks. This subsurface [SOURCE: Page_13]

260   drainage period is referred to as Intense Drainage Season (IDS). [SOURCE: Page_13]

13 [SOURCE: Page_13]

261   3) Between Smin and Smax, the reservoir model includes an intermediate threshold (Sinter). When the soil [SOURCE: Page_14]

262   reservoir lies between Sinter and Smax, the soil water content can exhibit a saturation condition close to [SOURCE: Page_14]

263   the drain, while remaining unsaturated at the upper part of the soil profile. In this case, one portion of [SOURCE: Page_14]

264   the infiltrated water contributes to the water table recharge and flows toward drains. The other [SOURCE: Page_14]

265   portion of the infiltrated water continues to fill the soil reservoir. The distribution of net infiltration [SOURCE: Page_14]

266   between water table recharge and soil reservoir filling was conducted by means of partition coefficient [SOURCE: Page_14]

267   α. Thus, the Sinter defining the start of the drainage season is used as first soil reservoir parameter. The [SOURCE: Page_14]

268   soil water level difference between Sinter and Smax, is referred to as the intense drainage season reservoir [SOURCE: Page_14]

269   level (SIDS). As Smax variations are dependent on that of Sinter (Smax must be greater than Sinter), SIDS is used [SOURCE: Page_14]

270   as second soil reservoir parameter. The two parameters Sinter and SIDS of the conceptual soil reservoir [SOURCE: Page_14]

271   are difficult to estimate from a field measurement; they are mainly determined through model [SOURCE: Page_14]

272   calibration. [SOURCE: Page_14]

434   4.3 Sensitivity analysis of the performance criteria [SOURCE: Page_22]

435   The objective of the sensitivity analysis is to identify the model input parameters exerting the greatest [SOURCE: Page_22]

436   influence on two performance criteria, namely drainage discharge (using KGE criterion) and the drain [SOURCE: Page_22]

437   flow start criterion (Xdiff). [SOURCE: Page_22]

438   Several sensitivity analysis methods are available to analyze model effectiveness (Iooss and Lemaître, [SOURCE: Page_22]

439   2015; Saltelli, 2008). In light of the low number of input parameters, it is possible to perform a global [SOURCE: Page_22]

440   sensitivity analysis (GSA) of SIDRA-RU using the Sobol method (Sobol, 1993), based on the variance [SOURCE: Page_22]

441   decomposition principle. The purpose of this method is to allocate a fraction of the total variance in [SOURCE: Page_22]

442   the evaluation criterion to each model parameter. The main advantage of this method is its strong [SOURCE: Page_22]

443   performance with nonlinearities that can be often problematic in hydrological modeling (e.g Gatel et [SOURCE: Page_22]

444   al., 2019; Muñoz-Carpena et al., 2007). When considering a model with several parameters, the Sobol [SOURCE: Page_22]

445   method allows quantifying their impact on the model response as well as their interactions regarding [SOURCE: Page_22]

446   the evaluation criterion (Pianosi et al., 2016; Saltelli et al., 2000). [SOURCE: Page_22]

447   The total variance of the model output, in this case the quality criteria of drainage discharge and Xdiff, [SOURCE: Page_22]

448   is allocated to each parameter using two sensitivity indices: the first-order sensitivity index (Si), and [SOURCE: Page_22]

449   the total sensitivity index (TSi). The Si index represents the direct impact of each parameter taken alone [SOURCE: Page_22]

450   on total variability, while TSi is defined as the sum of the impact of each parameter taken alone and in [SOURCE: Page_22]

451   interaction with others on total variability. Several methods exist to define the Si and TSi index values, [SOURCE: Page_22]

452   and no single one prevails over the others. Consequently, it is recommended to use several methods [SOURCE: Page_22]

453   to compute the sensitivity indices in order to assess the robustness of GSA. In this study, we tested [SOURCE: Page_22]

454   three variance estimators from (i) Saltelli (2002), named Saltelli^2002, (ii) Jansen (1999), named Jansen, [SOURCE: Page_22]

455   and (iii) Martinez (2011), named Martinez. Si has been computed based on the same estimators as TSi, [SOURCE: Page_22]

456   plus the Roalhs estimator (Tissot and Prieur, 2015), named ROALHS. [SOURCE: Page_22]

457   The SIDRA-RU sensitivity analysis is focused on the following parameters: soil hydraulic conductivity [SOURCE: Page_22]

458   (Ksat), drainable porosity (μ), intermediate reservoir level (Sinter), the intense drainage season reservoir [SOURCE: Page_22]

22 [SOURCE: Page_22]

459   level (SIDS), and partition coefficient α. The Sobol sampling procedure is based on the probability [SOURCE: Page_23]

460   distribution function (pdf) and the limits defined for all calibration parameters presented in Table 3 [SOURCE: Page_23]

461   and the partition coefficient α, which follows a uniform distribution and ranging from 0 to 1. [SOURCE: Page_23]

462   In practice, input parameter was sampled in its respective pdf, as defined in Table 3. The sampling size [SOURCE: Page_23]

463   was n = 1,000, and the total number of simulations for Np = 5 parameters was thus: Ntot = (Np+2)*n = [SOURCE: Page_23]

464   (5+2)*1,000 = 7,000. Bootstrap methods were then implemented to evaluate the uncertainty [SOURCE: Page_23]

559   6    Discussion [SOURCE: Page_29]

560   The main objective of integrating a soil reservoir module (RU) into the SIDRA model consists of [SOURCE: Page_29]

561   simulating a continuous drainage discharge over several hydrological years, as opposed to the original [SOURCE: Page_29]

562   SIDRA model, which is only applied during the intense drainage season and a priori qualified by users. [SOURCE: Page_29]

563   The SIDRA-RU performance analysis has been based on optimizing the four model parameters (Ksat, µ, [SOURCE: Page_29]

564   Sinter, SIDS) to maximize calibration criterion KGE, based on drainage discharge. The comparison between [SOURCE: Page_29]

565   daily observation and simulation of drainage discharge proves to be satisfactory in most seasons [SOURCE: Page_29]

566   examined. Simulations reveal good flow dynamics, with KGE values greater than 0.75, which are [SOURCE: Page_29]

567   efficient in hydrological modeling (Knoben et al., 2019). The peak flows have been correctly simulated, [SOURCE: Page_29]

568   yet some events have been slightly under-or overestimated. These small deviations might be related [SOURCE: Page_29]

569   to the input data or the model's simplifying hypothesis, e.g. neglecting deep infiltration and [SOURCE: Page_29]

570   preferential flow (Pluer et al., 2020). In terms of yearly cumulative drainage discharge and surface [SOURCE: Page_29]

571   runoff, these results show that the simulations lie very close to the observations. As for the [SOURCE: Page_29]

572   observations themselves (Table 2), the simulated surface runoff is less than 12% of total outflow. [SOURCE: Page_29]

573   The model evaluation demonstrates that for most drainage seasons, the observed drain flow start [SOURCE: Page_29]

574   times are predicted with good accuracy. However, large gaps can be noticed when predicting the drain [SOURCE: Page_29]

575   flow start times for certain seasons; the main cause may be related to the rainfall regime. Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_29]

576   continuous rainfall does in fact cause a progressive rewetting of the soil profile until formation of the [SOURCE: Page_29]

577   perched water table. Any heavy rainfall on dry soil can generate preferential flow toward the drains [SOURCE: Page_29]

578   before total saturation of the soil profile (Zimmer, 1992). Since this process is neglected in SIDRA-RU, [SOURCE: Page_29]

579   the prediction of drain flow start time gets delayed. Despite such simulation differences, the model is [SOURCE: Page_29]

580   still capable of predicting drain flow start with a relatively acceptable standard deviation of 10 days. [SOURCE: Page_29]

29 [SOURCE: Page_29]

581   Thus, the model can offer farmers and agricultural operators accurate critical period to avoid the [SOURCE: Page_30]

582   application of pesticides and fertilizers, other than changing or limiting their application to the autumn [SOURCE: Page_30]

583   or winter period (Brown and van Beinum, 2009; Lewan et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_30]

584   The sensitivity analysis of SIDRA-RU parameters, according to the Sobol method, indicates that model [SOURCE: Page_30]

585   performance with respect to the drainage discharge (using KGE Criterion) solely depends on [SOURCE: Page_30]

586   hydrodynamic parameters Ksat and µ. The soil reservoir parameter Sinter, which describes the threshold [SOURCE: Page_30]

587   when the soil reservoir is capable of recharging the drained water table, has negligible impact on model [SOURCE: Page_30]

588   sensitivity, as regards drainage discharge. Meanwhile, model performance relative to the drain flow [SOURCE: Page_30]

589   start (as described by the Xdiff criterion) mainly depends on the parameter Sinter. [SOURCE: Page_30]

590   In our modeling approach, the soil hydrodynamic parameters Ksat and µ, governing the water table [SOURCE: Page_30]

591   flow, have zero impact on the determination of the drain flow start dates. Indeed, the water table only [SOURCE: Page_30]

592   starts to form when water storage in the soil reservoir lies above the Sinter threshold in the RU module [SOURCE: Page_30]

593   (Section 3.2). This module considers the unsaturated zone as a single reservoir system defined by its [SOURCE: Page_30]

594   storage level parameters (Sinter and SIDS), with instantaneous input (net infiltration) and output (water [SOURCE: Page_30]

595   table recharge). Thus, the RU module does not take into account dynamic flow in the unsaturated zone [SOURCE: Page_30]

596   (e.g. (Niswonger et al., 2006)) nor any influence of the soil hydrodynamic parameters. [SOURCE: Page_30]

597   The sensitivity analysis has also shown that model parameter α has a negligible impact on the model [SOURCE: Page_30]

598   performance regarding the drainage discharge, thus confirming the assumption to set this parameter [SOURCE: Page_30]

599   to 1/3, within the limit of the SIDRA-RU model proposed in this study (Section 3.2). However, this [SOURCE: Page_30]

600   parameter has a little influence when defining the drain flow start. [SOURCE: Page_30]

601   In situ, the soil hydrodynamic parameters (Ksat, µ) can be estimated using Guyon’s pumping test [SOURCE: Page_30]

602   (Chossat, 1995; Guyon and Lesaffre, 1986; Lesaffre, 1990). However, the field estimation of the soil [SOURCE: Page_30]

603   reservoir parameters (Sinter and SIDS) is difficult. Theoretically, their values lie close to the measured [SOURCE: Page_30]

604   WHC of the soil profile, which is available from farming professionals. We would suggest determining [SOURCE: Page_30]

605   these values by means of model calibration based on the drain discharge observations. [SOURCE: Page_30]

606   7    Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_31]

607   This study has presented a modeling strategy based on the integration of a new module (RU) in order [SOURCE: Page_31]

608   to manage the behavior of the SIDRA model's soil reservoir. This novel concept allows calculating the [SOURCE: Page_31]

609   recharge of the perched water table. Integration of the RU module makes it possible to simulate the [SOURCE: Page_31]

610   flow of soil water through the drainage system. This model is run with four parameters, serving to [SOURCE: Page_31]

611   control the behavior of both soil water storage and hydrodynamic flow of the perched water table, [SOURCE: Page_31]

612   both of which are determined by means of model calibration. [SOURCE: Page_31]

613   Application of SIDRA-RU at the “La Jaillière” experimental site has revealed its efficiency in simulating [SOURCE: Page_31]

614   drainage discharge during both wet and dry seasons. Simulation output is in good agreement with the [SOURCE: Page_31]

615   observed daily drainage discharge and yearly cumulative volume in most of the seasons studied; in [SOURCE: Page_31]

616   addition, the model correctly identifies the drain flow start, i.e. accurate to within a median value of 5 [SOURCE: Page_31]

617   days and a standard deviation of 10 days. [SOURCE: Page_31]

618   The sensitivity analysis conducted for five model parameters (Ksat, µ, α, Sinter, SIDS) indicates various [SOURCE: Page_31]

619   dependencies on the performance criteria tested. The calibration of drainage discharge is mainly [SOURCE: Page_31]

620   sensitive to hydraulic conductivity and drainable porosity, whereas the determination of drain flow [SOURCE: Page_31]

621   start time is sensitive to the soil reservoir parameter Sinter. To reduce simulation uncertainties, it is [SOURCE: Page_31]

622   recommended to calibrate Sinter and SIDS independently of the Ksat, µ calibration using the observed [SOURCE: Page_31]

623   drainage discharges. [SOURCE: Page_31]

624   The model's ability to predict drain flow start times with good accuracy allows preventing the transfer [SOURCE: Page_31]

625   of agricultural pollutants into the water surface. Predicting the first drainage flows, using 6-days [SOURCE: Page_31]

626   weather forecasts, allows farmers and agricultural operators to plan for the critical application timing [SOURCE: Page_31]

627   and, hence, reduce the level of pesticide transfer. Moreover, this new model simulates the drained [SOURCE: Page_31]

628   yearly volume with a good level of approximation, thus offering the potential for realistic estimations [SOURCE: Page_31]

629   of the annual transfer of various pollutants of agricultural origin by means of SD systems. [SOURCE: Page_31]
