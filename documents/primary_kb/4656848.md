12    Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

13    Maize monoculture systems often cause environmental impacts such as soil erosion, nitrate [SOURCE: Page_1]

14    leaching and pesticide transfer. Conservation tillage and the use of cover crops appear to hold [SOURCE: Page_1]

15    promise for limiting these impacts, but questions about their performance remain, particularly [SOURCE: Page_1]

16    during the transition period after conventional soil tillage ceases. In this study, effects of no- [SOURCE: Page_1]

17    tillage maize and fallow-period management with different cover crop species, either pure or [SOURCE: Page_1]

18    in mixtures, were studied on the agronomic performance of maize and on soil N and water [SOURCE: Page_1]

19    dynamics. The study combined field experiments over three years (2012-2014) and simulation [SOURCE: Page_1]

20    modelling with the STICS model. The biomass of cover crops produced during the fallow [SOURCE: Page_1]

21    period varied from 0.8-10.5 t dry matter ha-1 depending on the cover crop species and date of [SOURCE: Page_1]

22    termination. Differences in soil water content were quantified among the treatments studied, [SOURCE: Page_1]

23    with the lowest water content at the beginning of the maize growing season with late [SOURCE: Page_1]

24    termination of a cover crop composed of pure faba bean or a vetch-oat mixture in 2014 (25- [SOURCE: Page_1]

25    50% of plant-water available capacity). Maize grain yields ranged from 8.5-13.6 t dry matter [SOURCE: Page_1]

34   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   Maize monoculture systems cause environmental problems, particularly related to nitrate [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   leaching and herbicide transfer to surface and ground water. These systems are usually based [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   on tillage practices for residue management and seedbed preparation that lead to soil erosion [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   and a decrease in soil organic matter content. In southwerstern France, conventional maize [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   management indeed consists mainly of short (< 2 years) or monoculture conducted with [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   regular mouldboard ploughing and a high mineral nitrogen fertilization (> 200 kg N ha-1) and [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   irrigation doses (> 200 mm ha-1) (Mas and Dinaucourt, 2019). Among the approaches [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   considered to increase the sustainability of these systems, conservation tillage, based on [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   minimal or no soil disturbance, and quasi-permanent soil cover with crops and/or crop [SOURCE: Page_2]

44   residues, is one way to improve soil properties and to decrease several environmental impacts [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   of agriculture (especially water runoff and soil erosion) (Blevins and Frye, 1993; Gebhardt et [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   al., 1985). The combination of these two levers with a diversified crop rotation, thus [SOURCE: Page_2]

47   corresponding to the definition of conservation agriculture, makes it possible to further [SOURCE: Page_2]

48   promote ecosystem services (Palm et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_2]

49   Soil tillage is a major source of variability in soil properties in both space and time (Messing [SOURCE: Page_2]

50   and Jarvis, 1993; Strudley et al., 2008), and reducing tillage intensity progressively leads to [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

51   soil properties that vary less over time (Alletto and Coquet, 2009). Soil bulk density is [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   generally higher in no-tillage soils (Hussain et al., 1998; Alletto and Coquet, 2009), which, in [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   interaction with higher soil moisture in the seedbed due to lower evaporation (Tan et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   2002), can decrease germination, seedling development and root development. For example, [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   in fine-textured soils with hydromorphic features (e.g. many soils in alluvial corridors) in [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   south-western France, where maize monoculture is common, conservation tillage had at best [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   no effect (Fortin, 1993; Licht and Al-Kaisi, 2005) but usually lower crop yields than those [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   with conventional tillage (Drury et al., 1999; Hussain et al., 1999). This decrease is generally [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   observed during the "transition period" of soil properties, whose duration depends on the soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   type, its initial fertility, the practices implemented and their performance (Vyn and Raimbault, [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   1993; Drury et al., 1999; Hussain et al., 1999). In some studies, however, higher soil water [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   content (SWC) under conservation tillage, especially in the topsoil (due to the presence of [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   mulch, which reduces evaporation), resulted in higher yields than those under conventional [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   tillage (Ghaffarzadeh et al., 1997; Dam et al., 2005). [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   In addition to tillage reduction, sowing cover crops (CC) during the fallow period also [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   modifies soil properties, water and mineral nitrogen (N) availabilities at sowing, and may lead [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   to contrasting agronomic performances of the following cash crop. Many studies have [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   highlighted the ability of CC to reduce nitrate leaching significantly due to their ability to [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   capture soil mineral nitrogen (SMN), thus providing i) a service of mitigating nitrate water [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   pollution (Hargrove, 1991; Justes et al., 2012) and ii) a green-manure service of N recycling [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   by releasing the residual mineral N acquired by CC to the subsequent cash crop after [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   incorporation into the soil (Constantin et al., 2011; Couëdel et al., 2018). Moreover, CC [SOURCE: Page_3]

73   stabilise soil (Hermawan and Bomke, 1997), reduce soil erosion (Kouwen et al., 1981), [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   increase soil organic carbon (C) content and, depending on the root system of the CC species, [SOURCE: Page_3]

75   enhance soil structure (Hermawan and Bomke, 1997; Hubbard et al., 2013). However, [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

76   managing the fallow period between two cash crops with CC may reduce water availability [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   for the second cash crop due to higher transpiration than that from bare soil (Unger and Vigil, [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   1998; Currie and Klocke, 2005; Qi and Helmers, 2010). A recent meta-analysis showed that [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   covering the soil with a CC during the fallow period decreases water drainage by a mean of [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   30 mm (albeit with high variability) and that termination date influences the amount of soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   plant-available water strongly, with the lowest drainage observed for late CC termination in [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   spring just before sowing (Meyer et al., 2019). In other studies, however, delaying winter CC [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   termination decreased evaporation from the soil in spring because the residues reduced [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   warming of the soil surface and increased water infiltration into the soil, which helped refill [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   soil water reserves (Clark et al., 1997a, b; Wells et al., 2014). N dynamics are also influenced [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   directly by the quality and quantity CC residues, as well as the date and method of CC [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   termination. The C:N ratio of plant residues is commonly used to estimate the N [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   mineralisation potential, varies by species and development stage (and thus termination date) [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   of the crop (Nicolardot et al., 2001; Justes et al., 2009). According to the C:N ratio, CC [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   decomposition can either release a large percentage of the acquired N, thus providing a green- [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   manure service to the next crop (Justes et al., 2012), or lead to net N immobilisation by the [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   microbial biomass through pre-emptive competition, thus reducing inorganic N availability [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   for the following crop (Clark et al., 1997a; Pantoja et al., 2015a). In addition, differences in [SOURCE: Page_4]

94   residue composition between young and old CC (mainly carbohydrates, cellulose and lignin [SOURCE: Page_4]

95   contents) lead to different N-release rates. [SOURCE: Page_4]

96   When soil tillage is reduced or stopped, agronomic performances are often lower and more [SOURCE: Page_4]

97   variable than those of conventionally tilled fields (Reicosky and Saxton, 2007). As mentioned, [SOURCE: Page_4]

98   many authors agree that a "transition period" is required for physical, chemical and biological [SOURCE: Page_4]

99   properties of soil to become modified (Reicosky and Saxton, 2007; Rühlemann et al., 2015; [SOURCE: Page_4]

100   Cooper et al., 2016). CC may play an important role in facilitating and/or accelerating this [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

101   improvement in soil properties to improve soil fertility and resistance to erosion (Magdoff [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   and van Es, 2001; Schmidt et al., 2018), but little information is available about their [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   agronomic effects during this transition period, which is a sensitive period for the profitability [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   of farming systems. [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   Experimental approaches in agronomy now increasingly supplement field experimentation [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   with modelling to analyse system functioning, which provides estimates of fluxes and [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   variables that are difficult to measure and allows for analysis over longer periods. Dynamic [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   soil-crop models are useful tools for predicting effects of agricultural practices on water and [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   N fluxes, such as evaporation, transpiration, drainage and leaching, which are difficult to [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   measure in field experiments (e.g. Brisson et al. (2003)). Moreover, it is one of the only [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   means available to date to assess interactions between factors implemented at the scale of [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   cropping systems and to evaluate long-term effects of agricultural practices by considering a [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   diversity of soil situations and climatic conditions. The STICS model is a dynamic soil-crop [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   model that simulates interactions among crop development, the climate, soil and crop [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   management at a daily time-step (Brisson et al., 1998; Brisson et al., 2002; Brisson et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   2003). STICS has been evaluated as being able to simulate agrosystem dynamics satisfactorily [SOURCE: Page_5]

117   in a wide range of soils, climates, and cropping systems (Coucheney et al., 2015). Its crop- [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   residue decomposition module is able to simulate observed dynamics well, including those of [SOURCE: Page_5]

119   fresh residues with a wide range of C:N ratios (Justes et al., 2009) and is also adapted for [SOURCE: Page_5]

120   simulating reduced or no-tillage systems (Plaza-Bonilla et al., 2018). STICS provided [SOURCE: Page_5]

121   relevant representations of effects of CC on nitrate leaching, soil C dynamics, and CO$_2$ and [SOURCE: Page_5]

122   N2O emissions, as well as on plant water and heat stress (Constantin et al., 2012; Plaza- [SOURCE: Page_5]

123   Bonilla et al., 2017). Recently, STICS was parameterised and then validated for its [SOURCE: Page_5]

124   predictions of the emergence of a wide range of CC species (Tribouillois et al., 2018) and of [SOURCE: Page_5]

125   water and N budgets for species mixtures of CC (Meyer et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

126   In the present study, by combining field experimentation with modelling with STICS, we [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   assessed effects of several species of CC with different sowing and termination dates on [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   agronomic performances of maize monoculture during a 3-year transition period from [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   conventional to no-tillage soil management. More specifically, the objectives were (i) to [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   quantify the biomass production and N uptake of different CC species introduced in maize [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   monoculture; (ii) to evaluate the effects of CC on soil water availability for maize; and (iii) to [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   estimate the effects of CC on maize productivity while evaluating some environmental [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   impacts (especially nitrate leaching and N2O emissions) by modelling. [SOURCE: Page_6]

235   2.4. Modelling approach [SOURCE: Page_10]

236   2.4.1. Overview of the STICS model [SOURCE: Page_10]

237   STICS is a soil-plant simulation model that simulates a range of processes related to plant [SOURCE: Page_10]

238   growth, water, and C and N cycles for a variety of annual and perennial crops (Brisson et al., [SOURCE: Page_10]

239   2003). Once its input variables for climate, soil and management practices are initialised, [SOURCE: Page_10]

240   STICS simulates the functioning of an average homogenous cover of crops at a daily time [SOURCE: Page_10]

241   step at the field scale. It describes crop growth based on the development of leaf area, which [SOURCE: Page_10]

242   intercepts light and converts it into biomass that is allocated to roots using a source-sink [SOURCE: Page_10]

243   approach. It also describes the flux and distribution of water and N between soil and plant [SOURCE: Page_10]

244   compartments and, consequently, related soil characteristics. For this study, STICS v^9.0 [SOURCE: Page_10]

245   version   was    used;    the   model    is   open      source   and   can   be   downloaded   at [SOURCE: Page_10]

246   https://www^6.paca.inrae.fr/stics_eng/Download. [SOURCE: Page_10]

255   2.4.2.2. Maize and cover crop calibration [SOURCE: Page_11]

256   Because STICS has often been used to simulate maize (Brisson et al., 2002; Yang et al., [SOURCE: Page_11]

257   2017), we used its default parameters for a half-early maize variety equivalent to that studied [SOURCE: Page_11]

258   experimentally; thus, no calibration was performed. Only the BS, WhiM_D$_1$, FB_D$_2$ and [SOURCE: Page_11]

259   VO_D$_1$/D$_2$ treatments were simulated, because no default parameters were available and [SOURCE: Page_11]

260   insufficient data were collected to calibrate STICS for RC_D$_1$/D$_2$, EthM_D$_1$ or TR_D$_1$. We [SOURCE: Page_11]

261   used STICS’s default parameters for WhiM and FB (Constantin et al., 2012; Tribouillois et [SOURCE: Page_11]

262   al., 2018). Because no default parameters were available for VO, we followed the procedure [SOURCE: Page_11]

263   of Meyer et al. (2020), who calibrated STICS to simulate water and N dynamics for a [SOURCE: Page_11]

264   mustard-clover CC. We used the parameters already calibrated in STICS for vetch and oat [SOURCE: Page_11]

461   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_19]

462   4.1. Cover crops can be productive in no-tillage maize monoculture [SOURCE: Page_19]

463   Introducing CC between two maize crops is often difficult, as the sowing period can be late [SOURCE: Page_19]

464   and close to winter in south-western France, which often leads to relatively low CC biomass; [SOURCE: Page_19]

465   as a result, the soil in maize monoculture usually remains bare during the fallow period leading [SOURCE: Page_19]

466   to increased soil erosion in the winter period. One of the main constraints for growing a CC after [SOURCE: Page_19]

467   maize is the availability of water at CC sowing to ensure crop establishment (Gabriel and [SOURCE: Page_19]

468   Quemada, 2011). In 2012-2013, rainfall in early autumn, especially 17 mm in the week after [SOURCE: Page_19]

19 [SOURCE: Page_19]

469   sowing, favoured the homogeneous emergence and establishment of CC, and then regular [SOURCE: Page_20]

470   rainfall and mild temperatures during the CC growing season (Fig. 2) promoted growth. CC [SOURCE: Page_20]

471   biomass was high (> 7.6 t DM ha-1), which emphasises that covering soils with a well- [SOURCE: Page_20]

472   developed CC between two spring crops is a realistic goal, although some differences [SOURCE: Page_20]

473   between species were observed. Brassicaceae developed fastest in autumn, but their biomass [SOURCE: Page_20]

474   was the lowest among the early-terminated CC; nevertheless, it remained higher than 4 t DM [SOURCE: Page_20]

475   ha-1. In 2013-2014, temperatures also favoured the growth of CC, but late sowing (mid- [SOURCE: Page_20]

476   October) had a negative impact on CC establishment, and then low rainfall during the fallow [SOURCE: Page_20]

477   period limited their development and biomass production. CC biomass decreased sharply to [SOURCE: Page_20]

478   less than half that of the previous year. Despite these differences in biomass production, CC [SOURCE: Page_20]

479   composition, especially their N concentration, was similar in both years of the experiment. [SOURCE: Page_20]

480   Differences were observed between CC treatments, however, with the lowest C:N ratio [SOURCE: Page_20]

481   measured for FB (11-12), due to N$_2$ fixation. Brassicaceae C:N ratios were relatively low in [SOURCE: Page_20]

482   our study (16-19), which could be explained by late sowing (end of September 2012 to mid- [SOURCE: Page_20]

483   October 2013) leading to lower growth and by the fact that they did not reach the flowering [SOURCE: Page_20]

484   stage. Couëdel et al. (2018) reported mean C:N ratios of 20-26 for a wide range of [SOURCE: Page_20]

485   Brassicaceae, but for sowing dates in August for plants that had reached at least the flowering [SOURCE: Page_20]

486   stage. [SOURCE: Page_20]

487   Most of the CC tested significantly reduced SMN at maize sowing compared to that under [SOURCE: Page_20]

488   BS, illustrating the well-known nitrate catch-crop effect (Constantin et al., 2011; Justes et al., [SOURCE: Page_20]

489   2012). Unlike other studies (Tonitto et al., 2006; Couëdel et al., 2018), however, we measured [SOURCE: Page_20]

490   no large nitrate catch-crop effect of the FB CC. In contrast, STICS simulations predicted a [SOURCE: Page_20]

491   significant decrease in nitrate leaching, even though it was low during the experiment (≤ 10 [SOURCE: Page_20]

492   kg N ha-1 year-1). In addition, lengthening the growing period of the CC did not decrease [SOURCE: Page_20]

493   SMN further in either the field or STICS simulations. In contrast, under most of the late- [SOURCE: Page_20]

20 [SOURCE: Page_20]

494   terminated CC, SMN contents at maize sowing were higher than those under the early- [SOURCE: Page_21]

495   terminated CC, which usually remained similar to those measured under BS. Despite their [SOURCE: Page_21]

496   lower measured biomass, Brassicaceae reduced SMN significantly. This is explained by their [SOURCE: Page_21]

497   faster increase in root depth and density than those of other species (Thorup-Kristensen, 2001; [SOURCE: Page_21]

498   Thorup-Kristensen et al., 2003), which allows them to capture more N soon after sowing, [SOURCE: Page_21]

499   decreasing nitrate transfer in soil, and thus nitrate leaching and water pollution (Thomsen and [SOURCE: Page_21]

500   Hansen, 2014). [SOURCE: Page_21]

501   CC are known to reduce soil evaporation and increase plant transpiration, leading to an [SOURCE: Page_21]

502   overall increase in actual evapotranspiration compared to that of BS (Qi et al., 2011; Nielsen [SOURCE: Page_21]

503   et al., 2015). In parallel, in no-tilled soil, crop residues on the soil surface limit evaporation [SOURCE: Page_21]

504   and thus usually allow more water to be maintained in the topsoil (Fortin, 1993; Bescansa et [SOURCE: Page_21]

505   al., 2006). While soil tillage may be an effective way to avoid excess water in the topsoil [SOURCE: Page_21]

506   before preparing the seedbed (Miyamoto et al., 2001), our results showed that managing soil [SOURCE: Page_21]

507   with CC in no-tillage systems could also limit excess water before spring crops, in case [SOURCE: Page_21]

508   rainfall is high in the fallow period or early spring before maize sowing. During the entire [SOURCE: Page_21]

509   study period, actual evapotranspiration of the CC treatments was 21-37% higher than that of [SOURCE: Page_21]

510   BS. During a dry fallow period, we observed that late-terminated CC led to dryer soil than [SOURCE: Page_21]

511   that of BS or early-terminated CC. Decision rules for the termination date of CC must thus be [SOURCE: Page_21]

512   defined clearly to limit this potential disservice to water availability in the soil profile and [SOURCE: Page_21]

513   seedbed, especially in rainfed cropping systems. Meyer et al. (2020) suggested that [SOURCE: Page_21]

514   terminating CC mechanically in late autumn and retaining the residues as mulch could be a [SOURCE: Page_21]

515   good compromise among the multiple services that a CC provides during the fallow period [SOURCE: Page_21]

516   and avoid negative impacts on plant AWC for the next cash crop. We also demonstrate that [SOURCE: Page_21]

517   early-spring termination of CC is the best compromise for reduced or no-tillage systems in the [SOURCE: Page_21]

518   climatic conditions of south-western France. This timing would avoid overly strong pre- [SOURCE: Page_21]

21 [SOURCE: Page_21]

519   emptive competition for water, which could decrease maize yield or require increasing [SOURCE: Page_22]

520   irrigation to compensate for it. According to STICS simulations, drainage after a CC [SOURCE: Page_22]

521   decreased by 9-36 mm year-1, which lies in the 15-60 mm year-1 range reported in the meta- [SOURCE: Page_22]

522   analysis of Meyer et al. (2019). [SOURCE: Page_22]

523 [SOURCE: Page_22]

524   4.2. Cover crops enhance maize performances in no-tillage systems [SOURCE: Page_22]

525   The decision rules applied to this experiment were defined to manage low-input maize. The [SOURCE: Page_22]

526   target maize yield (12 t ha-1) was thus defined to be consistent with these rules and with [SOURCE: Page_22]

527   agronomic expertise for this site and region. This target was reached in the first year of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

528   experiment (2012), when the last ploughing was performed before maize sowing, which [SOURCE: Page_22]

529   highlighted the homogeneity of the soil in the experimental field. We thus assume that [SOURCE: Page_22]

530   differences in maize performances observed in 2013 and 2014 were due to interactions [SOURCE: Page_22]

531   between fallow-period management and no-tillage. [SOURCE: Page_22]

532   Maize yield was not correlated with SMN at maize sowing or with the C:N ratio of the CC at [SOURCE: Page_22]

533   termination, indicating that water and N cycles were significantly modified by the [SOURCE: Page_22]

534   introduction of CC and the type of species or mixtures used. Although maize fertilisation in [SOURCE: Page_22]

535   our study (100-120 kg N ha-1) was lower than that of farmers in this region (ca. 200 kg N [SOURCE: Page_22]

536   ha-1), it may have been high enough to mask some of the CC effect. Previous studies with less [SOURCE: Page_22]

537   N fertilisation showed a positive correlation between SMN at maize sowing after CC and [SOURCE: Page_22]

538   maize yield, as well as a negative correlation between the C:N ratio of the CC and maize yield [SOURCE: Page_22]

539   (Vaughan and Evanylo, 1998; Restovich et al., 2012). In 2013 and 2014, higher SMN content [SOURCE: Page_22]

540   was measured under FB and late-terminated VO, but maize yields were significantly higher [SOURCE: Page_22]

541   with these leguminous CC only in 2014. It is likely that these higher yields in 2014 were [SOURCE: Page_22]

542   related to higher N uptake of maize due to N released from CC residues that were both richer [SOURCE: Page_22]

543   in N (lower C:N ratio) and contained more N (more biomass produced). While SMN [SOURCE: Page_22]

22 [SOURCE: Page_22]

544   availability at maize sowing was highest under BS (≈ 50 kg N ha-1, equivalent to that under [SOURCE: Page_23]

545   FB and VO), due to no plant uptake of SMN during the fallow period (no pre-emptive [SOURCE: Page_23]

546   competition) and little nitrate leaching in winter, maize yields after BS were always the [SOURCE: Page_23]

547   lowest. [SOURCE: Page_23]

548 [SOURCE: Page_23]

549   4.3. Maize performances depend on N availability, which is enhanced by cover crops, [SOURCE: Page_23]

550   although effects differ by species and termination date [SOURCE: Page_23]

551   No clear relationship between CC biomass and maize yields was observed. In 2013, maize [SOURCE: Page_23]

552   grown after Brassicaceae CC, especially EthM, had the highest yields, but the CC biomass [SOURCE: Page_23]

553   was among the lowest measured this year, in part because Brassicaceae species can lose [SOURCE: Page_23]

554   leaves during the winter that are not included in subsequent sampling. All Brassicaceae CC [SOURCE: Page_23]

555   were terminated early, which could have released more N for the maize during its growing [SOURCE: Page_23]

556   season (Justes et al., 2009; Constantin et al., 2011); thus, the timing between dynamic maize [SOURCE: Page_23]

557   N requirements and N release from CC residues may have been better. This hypothesis is [SOURCE: Page_23]

558   demonstrated well by the close relationship between the N acquired by the maize and its yield [SOURCE: Page_23]

559   (Fig. 4). The CC induced variability in maize N uptake explains that in maize yield. This [SOURCE: Page_23]

560   result was obtained regardless of the N content to calculate N uptake, whether shoot N content [SOURCE: Page_23]

561   or estimated shoot+root N content (assuming that roots contain 15% of the N content in [SOURCE: Page_23]

562   shoots). We also believe that the mineralization of CC, calculated in this study from the [SOURCE: Page_23]

563   equation of Justes et al. (2009), is not adapted to all the treatments studied because of the [SOURCE: Page_23]

564   diversity of species, the mixtures tested and the different destruction dates. As this estimate is [SOURCE: Page_23]

565   used in the calculation of the NUE (eq. 1), it may lead to an overestimation of the NUE for [SOURCE: Page_23]

566   some treatments (by underestimating the quantities of N released by the mineralisation of CC, [SOURCE: Page_23]

567   Table 3). Parameterisation of crop models, such as STICS, to take into account a greater [SOURCE: Page_23]

23 [SOURCE: Page_23]

568   diversity of CC, by integrating mixtures and late destruction dates, could improve this [SOURCE: Page_24]

569   estimate. [SOURCE: Page_24]

570   Furthermore, the field observations over time indicated that Brassicaceae CC yielded [SOURCE: Page_24]

571   seedbeds of good quality. Improving soil structure with their root system (i.e. “biological [SOURCE: Page_24]

572   drilling” or “biological tillage”) is one service that CC provide (Cresswell and Kirkegaard, [SOURCE: Page_24]

573   1995; Burr-Hersey et al., 2017). Crucifers have a thick tap-root that can penetrate untilled [SOURCE: Page_24]

574   (“compacted”) soils better than the fibrous roots of other species and may therefore be more [SOURCE: Page_24]

575   beneficial for biological drilling (Chen and Weil, 2010; Burr-Hersey et al., 2017). Growing [SOURCE: Page_24]

576   them may allow the biopores that their roots create to be used by the following maize as [SOURCE: Page_24]

577   preferential pathways that offer low resistance to growth. CC mixtures that included grasses [SOURCE: Page_24]

578   (oat or ryegrass) in our study led to relatively poor maize performances (except VO_D$_2$ in [SOURCE: Page_24]

579   2014), probably because their residues released less N. Indeed, several studies reported [SOURCE: Page_24]

580   negative effects of grasses on maize yield (Restovich et al., 2012) due to their “high” C:N [SOURCE: Page_24]

581   ratio, leading to pre-emptive competition for N with maize during the latter’s initial growth [SOURCE: Page_24]

582   stages (Pantoja et al., 2015b). Even if grasses are terminated early (i.e. during their vegetative [SOURCE: Page_24]

583   stage), because they have a C:N ratio < 25, soil N availability for maize during the growing [SOURCE: Page_24]

584   season will be low (Restovich et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_24]

585   In parallel, several studies reported negative effects of CC on maize growth and yield due to [SOURCE: Page_24]

586   competition for soil water. For example, maize yield in no-tillage systems was reduced by [SOURCE: Page_24]

587   33% due to dry soil conditions after CC (Gerald and Russell, 1989; Lal et al., 1991). These [SOURCE: Page_24]

588   effects were identified when annual rainfall was below normal but also above normal (Lal et [SOURCE: Page_24]

589   al., 1991). In our study, during the wet fallow period of 2012-2013, excess water was avoided [SOURCE: Page_24]

590   in the late-terminated CC treatments, resulting in higher maize yields than in BS and early- [SOURCE: Page_24]

591   terminated CC treatments, where excess water was maintained longer. During the dry fallow [SOURCE: Page_24]

592   period of 2013-2014, SWC was lower at maize sowing under the late-terminated CC but did [SOURCE: Page_24]

24 [SOURCE: Page_24]

593   not differ after the first irrigation. Thus, for irrigated systems, the risk of delaying CC [SOURCE: Page_25]

594   termination is relatively low. However, it is important not to irrigate too much due to using [SOURCE: Page_25]

595   CC, as water becomes scarce in south-western France in summer. In our study, we chose to [SOURCE: Page_25]

596   sow a semi-early maize variety, which allows water consumption to be decreased during the [SOURCE: Page_25]

597   maize growing season, while harvesting earlier than a late maize variety and at lower grain- [SOURCE: Page_25]

598   moisture levels (thus also saving money and energy for grain drying). This strategy keeps [SOURCE: Page_25]

599   irrigation at a maximum of 200 mm (in 2014) while, in reference irrigated maize systems in [SOURCE: Page_25]

600   south-western France, it ranges from 230-330 mm. Thus, the introduction of CC must be [SOURCE: Page_25]

601   carefully thought out, in particular by considering the entire cropping system and its yield [SOURCE: Page_25]

602   targets to maximise services and limit disservices, to develop a system that can also maximise [SOURCE: Page_25]

603   farmers’ profit. [SOURCE: Page_25]

604 [SOURCE: Page_25]

605   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_25]

606   Our study showed that most of the CC tested maintained maize yield, and the best ones [SOURCE: Page_25]

607   increased it, during the transition period from conventional to no-tillage. Some of them, [SOURCE: Page_25]

608   however, such as the undersown mixture of RC, still need technical improvements to provide [SOURCE: Page_25]

609   satisfactory results, even though their maize yields were similar to that after BS during the [SOURCE: Page_25]

610   fallow period. Although an undersown CC is not supposed to compete with maize, it clearly [SOURCE: Page_25]

611   decreased maize yield more than the other CC studied. It covers the soil surface rapidly after [SOURCE: Page_25]

612   maize harvest, but its biomass remains low until early spring. Among the CC tested, FB [SOURCE: Page_25]

613   provides high and relatively stable yields of the subsequent maize, and it is easy to manage [SOURCE: Page_25]

614   because it can be terminated mechanically instead of chemically. [SOURCE: Page_25]

615   The monitoring and modelling of water dynamics under the different treatments studied [SOURCE: Page_25]

616   shows that managing soil with CC in no-tillage systems could limit the risks of excess water [SOURCE: Page_25]

617   at maize sowing. It also appears that during dry fallow periods, the late-terminated CC led to [SOURCE: Page_25]

25 [SOURCE: Page_25]

618   drier soil conditions than in the case of early-terminated CC, which can have a negative [SOURCE: Page_26]

619   impact on the establishment of maize, especially in rainfed cropping systems. [SOURCE: Page_26]

620   Our results demonstrate that maize grain yield is explained mainly by fallow-period [SOURCE: Page_26]

621   management and effects of CC on N dynamics, which determined the SMN available for [SOURCE: Page_26]

622   maize uptake and thus its yield. However, care must be taken to limit SMN, as FB in our [SOURCE: Page_26]

623   study did not reduce SMN significantly at maize sowing, although STICS simulations [SOURCE: Page_26]

624   indicated a potential decrease in nitrate leaching with FB even though it is a legume. One [SOURCE: Page_26]

625   option is to sow a mixture of FB with other species, especially a Brassicaceae, to increase the [SOURCE: Page_26]

626   diversity of ecosystem services and especially to obtain a good nitrate catch-crop effect. [SOURCE: Page_26]
