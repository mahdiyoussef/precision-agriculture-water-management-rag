34   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   The frequency of droughts has been increasing worldwide in many arid and semi-arid regions (East [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   Asia, Africa, Australia, and the Mediterranean basin) and temperate regions, such as Western Europe [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   (Spinoni et al., 2013). Drought occurrences may increase in the future due to climate change (Sheffield [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   and Wood, 2008) and anthropogenic pressures on water resources (Vörösmarty et al., 2000). Droughts [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   induce severe consequences notably on hydrology and agriculture (Van Loon, 2015, Malakoff and [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   Sugden, 2020). In agricultural catchments, small reservoirs are considered by water managers and [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   farmers as a potential way to adapt agricultural practices to drought occurrences (e.g., Rodrigues et al., [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   2012; Malveira et al., 2012; Albergel et al., 2005; Essegbey et al., 2012). Previous global studies have [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   shown the often-overlooked potential of small water storage to increase water and food security. Small [SOURCE: Page_2]

44   water storage or a soft-path approach would avoid the construction of large, capital intensive, and [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   environmentally damaging water infrastructure. Previous studies estimated that irrigation with small [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   reservoirs can globally feed an additional 800 million people under current climate conditions (Rosa et [SOURCE: Page_2]

47   al., 2020a) and an additional 300 million people under a 3°C warmer climate (Rosa et al., 2020b). [SOURCE: Page_2]

48   Most of these studies are global and cannot account for many local site-specific variables. [SOURCE: Page_2]

49   Small reservoirs are reservoirs whose storage capacity does not exceed 10 6 m^3 (Habets et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_2]

50   By intercepting and storing surface, subsurface and stream runoff during high-flow periods, they [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   constitute an alternative water resource for crop irrigation purposes during drought periods. As a [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   consequence, the number of small reservoirs has multiplied in recent decades in many regions [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   worldwide, and their spatial density may exceed 5 small reservoirs per km² (Habets et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   However, the increase in small reservoirs may impose antagonistic impacts. As a positive effect, small [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   reservoirs represent an alternative resource required to maintain crop yields (Biemans et al., 2011; [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   Wisser et al., 2010). As a negative effect, each small reservoir may have a local hydrological effect on [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   its nearby environment, such as by modifying the groundwater-surface exchanges or reducing the [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   stream flow. The reservoirs taken as a whole (the reservoir network) may also induce a cumulative [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   effect. Hydrological modifications, especially local modifications, may induce ecological, [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   biogeochemical and geomorphological disturbances in catchments and the ecosystems they support [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   (Habets et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   Small reservoirs in an agricultural catchment enhance the interactions between hydrology and [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   agriculture, particularly through crop water needs and farmers' decisions on crop management and [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   water withdrawals for irrigation (Figure 1). The elucidation and prediction of the hydrological and [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   agricultural impacts of small reservoirs require the articulation of the different spatial scales involved [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   in these interactions: [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   i.      the agricultural plot, where farmers decide cropping practices, where crop growth occurs and [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   where water is partitioned between evaporation, transpiration, runoff and infiltration [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   ii.     the small reservoir, which, on the one hand, is related to its upstream hydrological drained [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   area and downstream catchment area and, on the other hand, to each irrigated plot [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   iii.    the catchment, which integrates hydrological effects, especially those on stream runoff and [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   groundwater. [SOURCE: Page_3]

73   These different scales are linked by hydrological processes (surface runoff, stream runoff, [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   groundwater recharge, etc.) and crop and agricultural water management operations (water withdrawal [SOURCE: Page_3]

75   operations from reservoirs and irrigation applications). [SOURCE: Page_3]

76   Numerical modelling is a widely adopted approach to better understand and predict small-reservoir [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   impacts. Most of the models dedicated to this aim are based on hydrological catchment models. [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   Among these hydrological models, few explicitly consider the plot scale at which management [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   operations are conducted. The spatial representation of reservoirs can be explicit (Deitch et al., 2013; [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   Nathan et al., 2005), statistical (e.g., Çetin et al., 2009; Güntner et al., 2004; Nathan et al., 2005; [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   Zhang et al., 2012) or global (e.g., Habets et al., 2014; Perrin et al., 2012; Tarboton and Schulze, [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   1991). The explicit spatial representation has the advantage of simulating both local and cumulative [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   hydrological impacts of the reservoir networks (Deitch et al., 2013; Nathan et al., 2005). Regarding the [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   representation of crop growth, very few models couple crop and hydrological models (e.g., Neitsch et [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   al., 2011; Therond et al., 2014). Most models simply represent crop growth through functional [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   parameters (e.g., leaf area and crop development coefficient) or forcing variables (transpiration [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   fluxes), while other models neglect this aspect. The interaction between crop growth and hydrological [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   processes is thus not considered. Water withdrawal from reservoirs and the irrigation amount applied [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   to crops can be modelled depending on both crop water requirements and water availability (Murgue [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   et al., 2014; Neitsch et al., 2011). However, certain models consider constant irrigation amounts over [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   given periods (e.g., Hughes and Mantel, 2010), while others do not consider crop irrigation (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   Rousseau et al., 2013). With the exception of very few catchment models (e.g., Therond et al., 2014), [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   farmer decisions on crop and agricultural water management are not represented in catchment models [SOURCE: Page_4]

94   dedicated to agricultural catchments containing small reservoirs. Finally, to our knowledge, very few, [SOURCE: Page_4]

95   if any, of these models simultaneously consider the various spatial scales (plot, reservoir, and [SOURCE: Page_4]

96   catchment), the water dynamics in small reservoirs in relation to water withdrawals and the agronomic [SOURCE: Page_4]

97   and hydrological links between these scales at which agriculture-hydrology interactions occur. [SOURCE: Page_4]

98   We developed a new distributed agro-hydrological model named MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs. This [SOURCE: Page_4]

99   model is designed for agricultural catchments containing small reservoirs dedicated to irrigation. It [SOURCE: Page_4]

100   simulates the interactions between the hydrological behaviour of the catchment, crop growth and [SOURCE: Page_4]

101   farmer decisions related to the management of crops and reservoirs. MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs is [SOURCE: Page_4]

102   based on the coupling of three already proven models: i) the catchment-scale distributed MHYDAS [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   hydrological model (Moussa et al., 2002), ii) the plot-scale crop model AqYield (Constantin et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   2015) that includes yield calculation and iii) a plot and reservoir-scale farmer’s decision model [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   (Murgue et al. 2014), without any name, that represents the farmer decisions related to crop and water [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   reservoir management. The latter two have already been used in a water management model, Maelia, [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   (Therond et al., 2014), which has been applied to water resource catchments of 1000-10,000 km^2 with [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   reservoirs dedicated to irrigation (e.g., Aveyron catchment in France, Allain et al., 2018). The spatial [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   representation used for MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs combines landscape objects (e.g., plot, reservoir) [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   at the catchment scale and is based on that of MHYDAS, fully described in Lagacherie et al (2010). [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   This representation proves to be particularly effective for simulating surface hydrology (e.g., Hallema [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   et al., ), erosion (e.g., Gumière et al., 2011) or pesticide transfer (e.g., Bouvet et al., 2010) in small to [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   medium agricultural catchments. The main novelty of this model is thus the explicit representation of [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   the relevant spatial scales (plot, reservoir, and catchment) and the links between these scales involved [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   in hydrology-agriculture interactions. MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs is intended to be used in [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   catchments of 10-100 km^2 to understand and estimate the local and cumulative effects of small [SOURCE: Page_5]

117   reservoirs on the hydrology of catchments, especially stream runoff, and on crop production. [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   The objective of this paper is fourfold. First, it describes the principles of MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_5]

119   in terms of catchment spatial segmentation and the modelling of hydrological processes, crop growth [SOURCE: Page_5]

120   and farmer management practices of crops and reservoirs. Second, it demonstrates the numerical and [SOURCE: Page_5]

121   computing consistency of the model. Third, it demonstrates the capacity of the model to represent the [SOURCE: Page_5]

122   hydrological and agricultural functioning of an agricultural catchment containing small reservoirs, via [SOURCE: Page_5]

123   an application to a real case study (the Gélon catchment, France). Fourth, it investigates the feasibility [SOURCE: Page_5]

124   of model application in the examination of the hydrological and agricultural impacts of catchment [SOURCE: Page_5]

125   situations in terms of the density of reservoirs dedicated to irrigation. [SOURCE: Page_5]

143   ●         The surface unit (SU) represents a homogeneous spatial entity in terms of its properties (soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   and land use) corresponding to one sub-part of a real plot with a uniform water flow direction. [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   Therefore, depending on the topography, a real agricultural or non-agricultural plot may be [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   represented in the model as a unique SU or several SUs. The SU boundaries are determined by [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   overlapping three geographical layers: the plot map, the flow direction map derived from the [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   topography and the soil map (Lagacherie et al., 2010). An agricultural SU is dedicated to crop [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   cultivation and can be irrigated. Non-agricultural SUs are plots with non-cultivated vegetation, such as [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   forests, moors or natural pastures, or non-vegetated plots (e.g., urban areas, bare rock areas, and sand [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   areas). [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   ●         The water reservoir unit (RE) represents a reservoir that can eventually be used for irrigation. [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   The RE directly connected upstream to a certain reach of the hydrographic network is hereafter called [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   a connected RE. This type of RE is filled by surface runoff from upstream SUs, stream runoff from [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   upstream RSs, and direct rainfall. In some countries, a minimum flow prescribed by environmental [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   regulations has to be released downstream. The RE not connected upstream to a reach is hereafter [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   called a non-connected RE. Unlike a connected RE, a non-connected RE is not filled by stream runoff [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   from upstream RSs, and a minimum flow has not been prescribed to be released downstream. [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   subsurface saturated flow following the structure of the hydrological catchment. Each GU is therefore [SOURCE: Page_7]

161   derived from the topography considered to identify the flow direction. Each GU discharges in a single [SOURCE: Page_7]

162   specific reach segment (RS). [SOURCE: Page_7]

164   sources, confluence points, and connected RE or SU boundaries. RSs are connected to comprise the [SOURCE: Page_7]

229   1.2.2 Surface and stream runoff [SOURCE: Page_11]

230   Surface and stream runoff are simulated with the diffusive wave equation solved by the Hayami kernel [SOURCE: Page_11]

231   method assuming a unidirectional flow to represent runoff routing as described by Moussa and [SOURCE: Page_11]

232   Bocquillon (1996). Surface runoff corresponds to the downslope propagation of the water excess. [SOURCE: Page_11]

233   Surface runoff flows downstream from an SU to another SU, an RS or an RE, depending on the spatial [SOURCE: Page_11]

234   segmentation. Stream runoff is simulated at every time step in every RS considering the upstream flow [SOURCE: Page_11]

235   from any connected SUs, RSs, REs and GUs to the given RS. The simulated stream runoff from an RS [SOURCE: Page_11]

236   or RE flows into either a downstream RS or downstream RE. Every RS connected RE or RS used for [SOURCE: Page_11]

237   irrigation is characterised by a user-defined parameter called minimum flow used, denoted Qmin (m^3.s- 1 238       ), to model the withdrawals (see section 1.4.2 Management of the withdrawal from water resources). [SOURCE: Page_11]

239   The minimum flow is a floor threshold introduced to represent the minimum flow imposed by water [SOURCE: Page_11]

240   regulation laws to maintain the ecological quality of the stream. Any water withdrawal can be [SOURCE: Page_11]

241   performed in the stream only if the stream runoff does not fall below this floor threshold. [SOURCE: Page_11]

242   1.2.3 Evapotranspiration [SOURCE: Page_12]

243   In regard to the agricultural SUs, the actual evapotranspiration (AET) is the sum of the actual crop [SOURCE: Page_12]

244   transpiration and actual soil evaporation. The AET is calculated at a daily time step based on the soil [SOURCE: Page_12]

245   surface water content, reference evapotranspiration (ET$_0$), and soil clay content (Constantin et al., [SOURCE: Page_12]

246   2015; Therond and Villerd, 2020). The actual crop transpiration depends on the crop growth and soil [SOURCE: Page_12]

247   water content (please refer to section 1.3, Crop growth). Regarding the non-agricultural SUs, the AET [SOURCE: Page_12]

248   is calculated at each time step according to SWAT formalisms (Neitsch et al., 2011) based on ET$_0$, [SOURCE: Page_12]

249   soil characteristics (e.g., bulk density, wilting point, and thickness), soil water content and the fixed [SOURCE: Page_12]

294   1.3 Crop growth [SOURCE: Page_14]

295   The crop growth is calculated only in agricultural SUs at daily time steps based on the principles of the [SOURCE: Page_14]

296   AqYield crop model (Constantin et al., 2015). Crop growth, both aerial and root, controls crop [SOURCE: Page_14]

297   transpiration and crop yield. [SOURCE: Page_14]

298   The crop aerial development is simulated using a crop coefficient representing foliar growth. Crop [SOURCE: Page_14]

299   coefficient dynamics are a function of crop transpiration, development stage (phenology), and various [SOURCE: Page_14]

300   parameters specific to a given species. Globally, the crop coefficient increases until the flowering [SOURCE: Page_14]

301   stage and then declines until the harvesting stage. Crop development stages, particularly the flowering [SOURCE: Page_14]

302   and maturity stages, are simulated based on the concept of growing degree days, with threshold values [SOURCE: Page_14]

303   of the growing degree days and parameters specific to each species and cultivar precocity class. [SOURCE: Page_14]

304   The actual crop transpiration is calculated with an empirical function of the maximum transpiration [SOURCE: Page_14]

305   and soil water available to roots. The maximum transpiration depends on the crop coefficient and ET$_0$ [SOURCE: Page_14]

306   minus soil evaporation. The soil water available to roots varies as a function of root growth. Root [SOURCE: Page_14]

307   growth depends on the cumulative daily effective temperature, a species root-growth coefficient and a [SOURCE: Page_14]

308   reduction coefficient linked to the soil structure. [SOURCE: Page_14]

309   The crop yield is calculated at harvest as a function of the potential yield, locally defined for a species [SOURCE: Page_14]

310   or cultivar precocity class, and the water satisfaction index defined as the ratio of the actual crop [SOURCE: Page_14]

311   transpiration to the maximum crop transpiration during the cropping season. [SOURCE: Page_14]

313   1.4.1 Crop management [SOURCE: Page_15]

314   The model simulates farmer management decisions at daily time steps and for every agricultural SU. [SOURCE: Page_15]

315   The decisions are related to several practices (tillage, sowing, harvesting and irrigation), but only one [SOURCE: Page_15]

521   level in the same real catchment as was adopted for model determinism assessment (section 2.3.2, [SOURCE: Page_24]

522   Model determinism) at the daily time step, considering that the difference between the above two [SOURCE: Page_24]

741   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_39]

742   The first application of the MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs model to the Gelon catchment gave [SOURCE: Page_39]

743   promising results (Figure 5; Tables 4 and 5). The main processes underlying the hydrological and [SOURCE: Page_39]

744   agricultural functioning of the catchment seem to be well modelled. However, considering the [SOURCE: Page_39]

745   application of the model to other catchments and other agropedoclimatic contexts requires questioning [SOURCE: Page_39]

746   (i) the availability of the data needed for its application to other real case studies and (ii) the [SOURCE: Page_39]

747   improvements to the model in terms of the processes represented. The two points are discussed [SOURCE: Page_39]

748   hereafter. [SOURCE: Page_39]

749   The first point of discussion concerns the data needed to use the MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs model, [SOURCE: Page_39]

750   either to define forcing variables to obtain the spatial representation of the flow domain to [SOURCE: Page_39]

751   parameterise it on the study area or to evaluate it. Most of the necessary data (e.g., topography, [SOURCE: Page_39]

752   hydrographic network, soil characteristics, nature of crops, and meteorological variables) may be [SOURCE: Page_39]

753   extracted or derived from generic databases, often available throughout Europe. Moreover, as this [SOURCE: Page_39]

754   model is built on already proven models and on widely used equations, some parameters can be fixed [SOURCE: Page_39]

755   from the literature. For example, this is the case for the plant growing coefficients or for the k factor [SOURCE: Page_39]

756   for converting reference evapotranspiration to reservoir evaporation. This makes it easy to envision the [SOURCE: Page_39]

757   use of the model in catchment areas other than the one we studied. [SOURCE: Page_39]

39 [SOURCE: Page_39]

40 [SOURCE: Page_40]

758   However, there is no generic database for all model inputs or for all variables used for its evaluation. [SOURCE: Page_40]

759   In such cases and when available, those data may be derived from local databases, specific surveys or [SOURCE: Page_40]

760   local expertise. This is particularly the case for data on reservoirs: there is currently no database at the [SOURCE: Page_40]

761   European or even the French level that allows a complete and high-quality description of small water [SOURCE: Page_40]

762   reservoirs. The availability and estimation of the small reservoirs properties and the water use from the [SOURCE: Page_40]

763   reservoirs have remained a real challenge regardless of the approaches used in catchment hydrological [SOURCE: Page_40]

764   modelling with reservoirs (Hughes and Mantel, 2010, Lowe et al. 2005). This has motivated the [SOURCE: Page_40]

765   development of remote sensing methods to estimate position and capacity of small reservoirs over [SOURCE: Page_40]

766   large areas (Ogilvie et al., 2016). In France, the collective water management structures recently set up [SOURCE: Page_40]

767   in deficit areas ("Organismes Uniques de Gestion Collective de l'Eau") are beginning to create a type [SOURCE: Page_40]

768   of database gathering characteristics and water uses of small reservoirs. Databases describing [SOURCE: Page_40]

769   agricultural practices are also incomplete, either in terms of geographical location or in terms of [SOURCE: Page_40]

770   content, as explained in Leenhardt et al. (2010, 2020). Therefore, this requires the implementation of [SOURCE: Page_40]

771   specific acquisition methods, for example, as presented for cropping systems by Murgue et al. (2016) [SOURCE: Page_40]

772   and Rizzo et al. (2019). It is clear that the lack of generic databases for some of the necessary variables [SOURCE: Page_40]

773   to use or evaluate the model makes using the model more difficult. However, this constraint is not [SOURCE: Page_40]

774   specific to MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs and has been encountered by other modelling approaches [SOURCE: Page_40]

775   dealing with the cumulative effect of small reservoirs. [SOURCE: Page_40]

776   The quality of the data used also guarantees the predictive quality of the model and the reliability of [SOURCE: Page_40]

777   the model assessment. The use of indirect acquisition methods necessarily introduces inaccuracies, [SOURCE: Page_40]

778   either because of the quality of the expertise (Rizzo et al., 2019) or because of the method itself. For [SOURCE: Page_40]

779   example, in our case study, we did not manage to meet all the owners of the reservoirs (absences or [SOURCE: Page_40]

780   refusals) so that the data for some reservoirs correspond to hypotheses based on our observations or on [SOURCE: Page_40]

781   the expertise of neighbours. However, the existence of generic databases does not exclude the need to [SOURCE: Page_40]

782   examine the quality of the data included in them. For example, in the present study, although we had [SOURCE: Page_40]

783   databases providing stream flow, meteorological data and crop yield values, we were only able to [SOURCE: Page_40]

40 [SOURCE: Page_40]

41 [SOURCE: Page_41]

784   access stream flow data at a nearby station located within the same basin but outside the Gélon [SOURCE: Page_41]

785   catchment, while meteorological data and crop yield values, respectively, were at a resolution that was [SOURCE: Page_41]

786   too low to obtain internal spatial variability on an 8 km² grid and averaged over the entire Gers [SOURCE: Page_41]

787   department,. These spatial discrepancies necessarily affect the quality of the data. More intensive field [SOURCE: Page_41]

788   work, for example, by monitoring flows at the Gélon outlet or by obtaining yield values from [SOURCE: Page_41]

789   agricultural cooperatives or traders who collect crops in this sector, would have enabled a better [SOURCE: Page_41]

790   evaluation of the model's performance. [SOURCE: Page_41]

791   The second point of discussion is about the way to improve the modelling of processes in MHYDAS- [SOURCE: Page_41]

792   Small-Reservoirs, in particular processes directly affecting the reservoir. Regarding this point, the [SOURCE: Page_41]

793   modular design of the MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs model under the OpenFLUID platform easily [SOURCE: Page_41]

794   allows adding or improving simulators. In the current version of the model, some processes are [SOURCE: Page_41]

795   neglected. This is the case for infiltration of water from the reservoirs to the underlying groundwater [SOURCE: Page_41]

796   or conversely for the discharge of groundwater directly to the reservoirs. Neglecting these processes [SOURCE: Page_41]

797   appeared to be acceptable in the Gélon catchment given the characteristics of the reservoirs and their [SOURCE: Page_41]

798   connection to the groundwater. However, depending on the pedological and lithological contexts and [SOURCE: Page_41]

799   the properties of the reservoirs, in particular the hydrodynamic properties of the reservoir bed, [SOURCE: Page_41]

800   exchanges between the reservoir and the groundwater can be dominant processes in the hydrological [SOURCE: Page_41]

801   dynamics of the reservoir (Bouteffeha et al., 2015). Modelling the exchanges would therefore improve [SOURCE: Page_41]

802   the model in its ability to simulate a diversity of contexts. The modelling could be done simply by [SOURCE: Page_41]

803   considering the differences in water levels between the reservoir and groundwater. This type of [SOURCE: Page_41]

804   relationship is reported to well predict the dynamics of exchanges in various contexts (Sharda et al., [SOURCE: Page_41]

805   2006). [SOURCE: Page_41]

806   Another improvement of the model is in the modelling of the water management rules of the [SOURCE: Page_41]

807   reservoirs. Indeed, the cumulative hydrological effect of reservoir networks cannot be explained solely [SOURCE: Page_41]

808   by the geometric characteristics of the network (density in terms of number of reservoirs, volume or [SOURCE: Page_41]

809   surface area). The management rules of the reservoirs, which sometimes differ from one reservoir to [SOURCE: Page_41]

41 [SOURCE: Page_41]

42 [SOURCE: Page_42]

810   another, appear to be an important factor in this effect (Habets et al., 2018; Hughes and Mantel, 2010). [SOURCE: Page_42]

811   In the present case study, the sharing of available water in a reservoir is modelled by a fairly standard [SOURCE: Page_42]

812   approach by considering that the water withdrawn is distributed to the irrigated field proportionally to [SOURCE: Page_42]

813   the water demand, but other priority rules could be considered. Priority could be given, for example, to [SOURCE: Page_42]

814   crops providing high financial incomes. We could also consider defining rules based on short-term [SOURCE: Page_42]

815   weather predictions. For actual water management rules being modelled within a specific simulator, [SOURCE: Page_42]

816   the modular design of the MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs model will be a clear asset to allow various [SOURCE: Page_42]

817   water management modalities. [SOURCE: Page_42]

818   5. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_42]

819 [SOURCE: Page_42]

820   The MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs model has been developed to understand and predict the local and [SOURCE: Page_42]

821   cumulative hydrologic and agricultural effects of a reservoir network in an agricultural catchment. [SOURCE: Page_42]

822   Hydrological models are already available to assess the cumulative impact of reservoir networks. [SOURCE: Page_42]

823   Compared to these models, the originality of MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs lies in two of its features. [SOURCE: Page_42]

824   The first is that it integrates processes related to the three major components of the catchment's agro- [SOURCE: Page_42]

825   hydrological functioning: hydrology, crop growth, and water management decisions. The second [SOURCE: Page_42]

826   feature is that it explicitly represents the main elements of the agricultural catchment - the plot, the [SOURCE: Page_42]

827   reach, the reservoir, and the water table - and the hydrological and agricultural relationships between [SOURCE: Page_42]

828   these elements. In addition, the model distinguishes between reservoirs according to their connection [SOURCE: Page_42]

829   to the hydrographic networks. In doing so, it allows the simulation of both local effects in the [SOURCE: Page_42]

830   immediate environment of each reservoir and cumulative effects on overall yields (Table 6) and flows [SOURCE: Page_42]

831   (Figure 6). [SOURCE: Page_42]

832   Numerical verification of the model was successful. The first application of the model to a 19-km² [SOURCE: Page_42]

833   catchment gave promising results in terms of stream runoff and crop yield simulations. However, the [SOURCE: Page_42]

834   evaluation and validation of the model are incomplete. The model could be improved in two [SOURCE: Page_42]

42 [SOURCE: Page_42]

43 [SOURCE: Page_43]

835   directions. The first concerns its validation, with an analysis of the model performance to simulate the [SOURCE: Page_43]

836   variables for which it was intended, such as stream runoff, crop yields and water withdrawals and [SOURCE: Page_43]

837   availability in small reservoirs. Model validation could also gain from its application to catchments [SOURCE: Page_43]

838   where comprehensive, reliable and distributed data sets, such as water tables, stream runoff and crop [SOURCE: Page_43]

839   yield data, are available based on in situ measurements and observations. The second direction would [SOURCE: Page_43]

840   be to perform a sensitivity analysis. In particular, a sensitivity analysis of the reservoir characteristics [SOURCE: Page_43]

841   and of the parameters associated with water dynamics modelling in small reservoirs could be helpful [SOURCE: Page_43]

842   when parameterizing the model in future applications. Thus, the MHYDAS-Small-Reservoirs model [SOURCE: Page_43]

843   could potentially be adopted by land use planners and water managers to assist them in their decisions [SOURCE: Page_43]

844   regarding new small-reservoir projects in catchments or management of the water stored in reservoirs. [SOURCE: Page_43]
