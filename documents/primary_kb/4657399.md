66        Karst aquifers are one of the most important water supply sources worldwide. [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   Approximately 20-25% of the world’s population consumes water obtained from karst [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   aquifers (Ford and Williams, 2007; Goldscheider et al. 2020). These aquifers are [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   distinguished from porous aquifers by the high degree of heterogeneity that can be identified [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   in both surface and underground environments (White, 1988; Bakalowicz, 2005). This [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   heterogeneous characteristic implies a high flow rate into karst systems which could reach [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   several hundred m h-1 (Mudarra et al. 2014; Marin et al. 2015). These unique hydrogeological [SOURCE: Page_3]

73   characteristics make karst aquifers particularly vulnerable to surface contamination and [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   difficult to manage (Stevanović, 2015, 2019). [SOURCE: Page_3]

75        Nitrate (NO$_3$-) is the most persistent and mobile form of nitrogen in the subsurface [SOURCE: Page_3]

76   environment. Continuous exposure to nitrate contamination can be dangerous for humans, [SOURCE: Page_3]

77   leading to several types of cancer (De Roos et al. 2003; Ward et al. 2005) and an increase of [SOURCE: Page_3]

78   methemoglobin in the blood (Johnson et al. 1987). For several decades, the increase of nitrate [SOURCE: Page_3]

79   concentrations in groundwaters has caused degradation of water quality all over the world, [SOURCE: Page_3]

80   and especially in karst environments (Nolan and Stone, 2000; Li et al. 2010; El Gaouzi et al. [SOURCE: Page_3]

81   2013; Schwientek et al. 2013; Alexia et al. 2015; Musgrove et al. 2016; Opsahl et al. 2017; [SOURCE: Page_3]

82   Bu et al. 2017; Briand et al. 2017; Yue et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2020). [SOURCE: Page_3]

83   Nitrate load export at the output of karst system can be high (Eller et al. 2017) and can imply [SOURCE: Page_3]

84   a degradation of water quality in the receiving environment. [SOURCE: Page_3]

92   origin. On the other hand, the combined approach of hydrochemistry and isotope [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   biogeochemistry could be a very relevant tool to better determine the origin of nitrogen in a [SOURCE: Page_4]

94   particularly difficult context, with complex hydrogeological relationships between several [SOURCE: Page_4]

95   karst aquifers both confined and unconfined. For several years, natural abundance stable [SOURCE: Page_4]

96   isotope measurements of nitrate (δ15N and δ18O) have been used to identify its origins, [SOURCE: Page_4]

97   including karst environment (Panno et al. 2001; Liu et al. 2006; Briand et al. 2017; Valiente [SOURCE: Page_4]

98   et al. 2020). Typically, as a function of the pedo-climatic context and linked with land-use, [SOURCE: Page_4]

100   synthetic nitrate fertilizer; (ii) secondary sources such as synthetic ammonium fertilizer [SOURCE: Page_4]

104   extent of these processes and the associated isotopic fractionation, the range of δ15N and δ18O [SOURCE: Page_4]

105   values of nitrate can vary significantly especially for 15N (Kendall et al. 2007). [SOURCE: Page_4]

107   quantify the relative contribution of a single nitrogen (N) source (Aravena et al. 1993; [SOURCE: Page_4]

108   Yakolev et al. 2015; Grimmeisen et al. 2017). In the case of a secondary source of N, where [SOURCE: Page_4]

109   nitrate is produced after transformation in the soil that modify its concentration, fractionate [SOURCE: Page_4]

110   its isotopes and which can blur the initial signature of the N sources (Böttcher et al. 1990; [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   Kendall et al. 2007; Briand et al. 2017). Besides their widespread use for determining the [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   origins of nitrate, δ15N-NO$_3$- and δ18O-NO$_3$- can also be extended as a tool to characterize the [SOURCE: Page_5]

138         The Toulon Springs have supplied drinking water to the city of Périgueux (France) [SOURCE: Page_6]

142   annual daily discharge of 450 L s-1 (Lorette et al. 2018; Lorette, 2019). [SOURCE: Page_6]

143         The geology of the area is composed of carbonated rocks from upper Cretaceous and [SOURCE: Page_6]

144   middle-upper Jurassic carbonate rocks (Fig. 1), the Cretaceous rocks are 200-250 meters in [SOURCE: Page_6]

145   thickness(Turonian, Coniacian, Santonian, Campanian) and the Jurassic rocks 300-350 [SOURCE: Page_6]

321          3.3   Hydrological approach [SOURCE: Page_15]

322 [SOURCE: Page_15]

323         The origin of the nitrate present in the Toulon Springs could be inorganic and/or [SOURCE: Page_15]

324   organic and direct or indirect. Inorganic nitrate is the nitrate itself contained in rainfall and [SOURCE: Page_15]

15 [SOURCE: Page_15]

325   inorganic fertilizers. It can also be nitrate produced from the nitrification of ammonitrate in [SOURCE: Page_16]

326   the soil. Organic nitrate arises from the production of nitrate from the ammonification and [SOURCE: Page_16]

327   nitrification of organic matter and then its nitrification (Sebilo et al. 2013). [SOURCE: Page_16]

328         The isotopic composition of nitrate in the Toulon Springs, as the main outlet of the [SOURCE: Page_16]

329   karst system, could be an integration of the isotopic signatures of nitrate for different nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_16]

330   pools within the hydrogeological catchment. According to the large δ15N and δ18O values of [SOURCE: Page_16]

331   NO$_3$- range of potential sources measured in literature (Kendall et al. 2007), it was necessary [SOURCE: Page_16]

332   to: (i) confirm that the data obtained were in agreement with those presented in the Kendall [SOURCE: Page_16]

333   plot; and (ii) better define these range of values for the studied area to precisely identify the [SOURCE: Page_16]

334   isotopic signatures of nitrate sources. These range of potential sources are usually represented [SOURCE: Page_16]

335   by rectangles in δ15Nvsδ18O diagram (Panno et al. 2001; Kendall et al. 2007; Heaton et al. [SOURCE: Page_16]

336   2012; Briand et al. 2017), theoretically integrating the set of possible isotopic signatures. In [SOURCE: Page_16]

337   this work, the redefinition of range of values aimed to reduce the rectangle size in the [SOURCE: Page_16]

338   δ15Nvsδ18O diagram. [SOURCE: Page_16]

339         The data of potential sources were plotted with data from surface waters, groundwaters [SOURCE: Page_16]

340   and the Toulon Springs in the δ15Nvsδ18O diagram. At each corner of the rectangles, arrows of [SOURCE: Page_16]

341   0.5 slope are drawn, representing the denitrification process. It is considered that any point [SOURCE: Page_16]

342   which lies between the two arrows from the same potential source (or rectangle) will have the [SOURCE: Page_16]

343   potential source as an initial isotopic signature and will undergo a denitrification process [SOURCE: Page_16]

344   causing a deviation from the initial signature. [SOURCE: Page_16]

345 [SOURCE: Page_16]

346         Once relationships are established between the parameters, the data is integrated in the [SOURCE: Page_16]

347   hydrogeological context for a better explanation of the way karst system functions and to [SOURCE: Page_16]

348   help the water management over the hydrogeological catchment. [SOURCE: Page_16]

350          3.4    δ15N-NO$_3$- and δ18O-NO$_3$- of potential sources. [SOURCE: Page_17]

351 [SOURCE: Page_17]

352         In a karstic context of the study, it is thus even more important to take into account the [SOURCE: Page_17]

353   direct sources of nitrogen, likely to be able to infiltrate directly into the groundwater, and the [SOURCE: Page_17]

354   indirect sources, likely to confer an isotopic signature of the nitrate that does not reflect the [SOURCE: Page_17]

355   mixture of the direct sources (Fig. 4). The direct inputs of nitrate nitrogen on the catchment [SOURCE: Page_17]

356   should be a mixture of atmospheric deposition and inorganic fertilizers (Fig. 4). [SOURCE: Page_17]

357         The basic idea behind the use of isotope biogeochemistry is that the measurement of [SOURCE: Page_17]

358   the isotopic composition of nitrate at a definite location and time within a catchment reflects: [SOURCE: Page_17]

359   (i) the endmembers, direct sources from which it originates, and (ii) the processes responsible [SOURCE: Page_17]

360   for variations in its concentration. [SOURCE: Page_17]

361         δ15N and δ18O values of nitrate in the unsaturated zone (USZ) integrate on the one hand [SOURCE: Page_17]

362   the isotopic composition of the potential direct sources of nitrogen (atmospheric deposition, [SOURCE: Page_17]

363   inorganic fertilizers), and on the other hand the isotopic composition of nitrate resulting from [SOURCE: Page_17]

364   biogeochemical processes in the soil which represent indirect source of nitrate nitrogen. [SOURCE: Page_17]

365         The ammonification and nitrification of nitrogen contained in organic matter (soils, [SOURCE: Page_17]

366   manure, slurry) leads to the production of nitrate whose isotopic composition does not reflect [SOURCE: Page_17]

367   the exogenous nitrogen inputs (atmospheric deposition and inorganic fertilizers), but their [SOURCE: Page_17]

368   assimilation, production and re-production of nitrate from the dynamic of soil organic matter [SOURCE: Page_17]

369   (El Gaouzi et al. 2013; Sebilo et al. 2013; Briand et al. 2017). Moreover, volatilization of [SOURCE: Page_17]

370   NH$_4$+, non-complete nitrification and denitrification in the USZ could induce a significant [SOURCE: Page_17]

371   isotopic fractioning which could impact the isotopic composition of the nitrate pool leaving [SOURCE: Page_17]

372   the unsaturated zone and reaching the saturated zone. Finally, nitrate could be a product of [SOURCE: Page_17]

373   the biological transformation of sewage, sludge spreading and more generally the [SOURCE: Page_17]

374   ammonification-nitrification of organic matter within the soils (Fig. 4). [SOURCE: Page_17]

17 [SOURCE: Page_17]

375         It has been established that nitrate diffusion and advection do not generate a significant [SOURCE: Page_18]

376   isotopic fractioning (Semaoune et al. 2012). Thus, provided that no biogeochemical processes [SOURCE: Page_18]

377   take place during the infiltration of nitrate into groundwater, the isotopic composition of [SOURCE: Page_18]

378   nitrate reaching the groundwater table should reflect that of nitrate leaving the [SOURCE: Page_18]

379   biogeochemically reactive unsaturated zone. [SOURCE: Page_18]
