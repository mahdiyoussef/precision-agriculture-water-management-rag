212   4.1 Formation and composition of spring mounds [SOURCE: Page_10]

213   Spring mounds result from an exceptional combination of geomorphological, geological, [SOURCE: Page_10]

214   tectonic and climatic conditions that follow each other in a specific order (Raddadi, 2021). [SOURCE: Page_10]

215   Many sources have documented the steps involved in their formation typical of arid or semi- [SOURCE: Page_10]

216   arid environments: the collection of rainwater to fill water tables (Kraiem et al., 2014), [SOURCE: Page_10]

217   neotectonic and faults (Swezey, 1996) with emerging artesian springs (Harris, 1981; Ponder, [SOURCE: Page_10]

218   1986), the influence of vegetation to trap fine aeolian particles (e.g. Adelsberger and Smith, [SOURCE: Page_10]

219   2010) and the presence of fine sediments layers deposited in the pond in the centre of a [SOURCE: Page_10]

220   geomorphological structure similar to a little volcano (Harris, 1981; Ponder, 1986; Clarke and [SOURCE: Page_10]

221   al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_10]

222   The mounds are composed at their base of fine centimetric horizontal sediment layers, with [SOURCE: Page_10]

223   low salt content (< 2 mS cm-1 in the 1/5 water extract), which are subjected to pedogenesis [SOURCE: Page_10]

9 [SOURCE: Page_10]

224   processes with oxido-reduction features and carbonate precipitation A thick layer of indurated [SOURCE: Page_11]

225   gypsum covers the top of the mounds (Raddadi, 2021). [SOURCE: Page_11]

226   4.2 Spring mound dimensions in the Nefzaoua region [SOURCE: Page_11]

227   The size (diameter and height) of a spring mound depends on the morphology of the basement [SOURCE: Page_11]

228   of the spring. A flat area will create a large pond whose edge can be colonised by vegetation. [SOURCE: Page_11]

229   The artesian pressure will determine the flow and the height of the mound. Chemical [SOURCE: Page_11]

230   properties of the spring water influence both the precipitation of certain salts (carbonates and [SOURCE: Page_11]

231   sulphates), which elevates the mound, and the growth of the vegetation, which traps aeolian [SOURCE: Page_11]

232   sand depending on the intensity and direction of the wind. [SOURCE: Page_11]

233   The size of 88 (70%) of the 126 mounds could be measured (Raddadi and Podwojewski, [SOURCE: Page_11]

234   2022). The mean size and variability in sizes of the mounds in the Nefzaoua region are much [SOURCE: Page_11]

235   larger than those in the Great Artesian Basin (180 m in diameter and 6 m in height) (Clarke, [SOURCE: Page_11]

236   2007) or the Dakhla oasis in Egypt (< 15 m in diameter and 7.9 m in height) (Torab and al., [SOURCE: Page_11]

237   2021). In the Nefzaoua region, the diameter ranged from 250-2 430 m and the height ranged [SOURCE: Page_11]

238   from 2-28 m. [SOURCE: Page_11]

239   The mounds had a mean slope of 2.9% ± 1.23%, which is gentle (Table 3). However, in these [SOURCE: Page_11]

240   flat areas near the Chott el Jerid, the mounds clearly emerge from the landscape. The largest [SOURCE: Page_11]

241   mounds were urbanised (657 ± 192 m radius, 19 ± 6 m in height) (Figure 6). The smallest [SOURCE: Page_11]

242   mounds were naturally levelled (274 ± 127 m radius) while the shortest mounds and those [SOURCE: Page_11]

243   with the lowest slope were anthropically levelled (8 ± 4 m in height and 2.3% ± 1.15%, [SOURCE: Page_11]

244   respectively). [SOURCE: Page_11]

245   4.3 Spring mounds, indigenous settlements and degradation processes [SOURCE: Page_11]

246      Spring mounds are particularly abundant in the Great Artesian Basin in Australia (Ponder, [SOURCE: Page_11]

247   1986; Clark and al., 2007). Even though indigenous activity is well documented around these [SOURCE: Page_11]

10 [SOURCE: Page_11]

248   springs, the poor water quality, nomadic activities of indigenous people and cultural [SOURCE: Page_12]

249   importance of these springs were always underestimated (Harris, 2020). Overuse of artesian [SOURCE: Page_12]

250   springs decreased the pressure of aquifers, which led to the loss of artesian springs at the [SOURCE: Page_12]

251   beginning of the 20th century. However, in the past 10 years, under the Great Artesian [SOURCE: Page_12]

252   Sustainability Initiative and other related bore-capping programs, considerable progress has [SOURCE: Page_12]

253   been made in restoring aquifer pressure (Powell and al., 2015, Brake et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_12]

254     In the hyper arid Libyan Desert in Egypt, the oases of Kargha and Dakhla were associated [SOURCE: Page_12]

255   with spring mounds and human settlements during the Pleistocene (Idris, 1996; Adelsberger [SOURCE: Page_12]

256   and Smith, 2010; Crepy and Callot, 2016). Archaeological studies in these areas showed that [SOURCE: Page_12]

257   the degradation and rapid demise of springs were due mainly to aridification of the climate [SOURCE: Page_12]

258   during the Holocene (Bravard and al., 2016; Torab, 2021) and current excessive pumping [SOURCE: Page_12]

259   from fossil aquifers, which is the result of modern irrigation schemes (Powell and Fensham, [SOURCE: Page_12]

260   2015). [SOURCE: Page_12]

261        Nefzaoua has likely been occupied since the Neolithic (Moreau, 1947; Monaco et al, [SOURCE: Page_12]

262   2000). Many Roman vestiges, such as in the equipped urbanised mound of Ain el Relig (TRT [SOURCE: Page_12]

263   14), north and east of Kebili, confirm the use and equipment of the springs (i.e. ayoun) since [SOURCE: Page_12]

264   Antiquity, with basins and vestiges of surface channels (i.e. sâgya) (Idoux, 1902; Moreau, [SOURCE: Page_12]

265   1947; Trousset, 1986). The oldest mounds are likely those that were naturally levelled, which [SOURCE: Page_12]

266   are concentrated in a line from Douz to Kebili. Many fragments of stone points, ceramics, [SOURCE: Page_12]

267   ostrich shells and blocks of Roman walls have been found in top of these mounds (Raddadi, [SOURCE: Page_12]

268   2021). The local population also built tunnels (i.e. khargias, similar to foggaras) through [SOURCE: Page_12]

269   these mounds to drain the water directly from the spring inside to the oasis plantations at the [SOURCE: Page_12]

270   foot of each mound (Figure S1A). [SOURCE: Page_12]

271     Currently, the irrigation systems of all spring mounds are being degraded rapidly, in three [SOURCE: Page_12]

272   ways. First, the artesian springs have not been active for 30 years due to excessive pumping [SOURCE: Page_12]

11 [SOURCE: Page_12]

273   and siltation or natural levelling affected 14.3% and 22.2% of the mounds, respectively [SOURCE: Page_13]

274   (36.5% of the total). Second, the salinisation of cultivated soils due to uncontrolled irrigation [SOURCE: Page_13]

275   without adequate drainage is frequently documented in this area (e.g. Zammouri and al., 2007, [SOURCE: Page_13]

276   Marlet and al., 2009; Kraiem et al., 2014; Haj-Amor and al., 2017). Third, because sediment [SOURCE: Page_13]

277   at the bottom of mounds has a low salt content (Raddadi, 2021), mounds have been and [SOURCE: Page_13]

278   continue to be excavated by heavy machinery: 11.1% have been completely levelled, and [SOURCE: Page_13]

279   31.0% are being destroyed (Figure S1B). Spring mounds are used as sediment “mines” to [SOURCE: Page_13]

280   expand newly cultivated areas over former unproductive areas. Counting dismantled mounds [SOURCE: Page_13]

281   and those with other anthropic degradation (i.e. levelled, urbanised or equipped), over half [SOURCE: Page_13]

282   have been deeply and permanently degraded by human activity. The mounds, which were the [SOURCE: Page_13]

283   historical basis of Nefzaoua oasis culture, have largely disappeared. Only 14 (11.1%) of the [SOURCE: Page_13]

284   126 mounds were relatively well preserved. These erosion and destruction processes have not [SOURCE: Page_13]

285   been previously observed or documented. [SOURCE: Page_13]

286   4.4 Potential for conservation [SOURCE: Page_13]

287      In the Nefzaoua region, controlling pumping (legal or illegal) and protecting ancient [SOURCE: Page_13]

288   springs, as in Australia (Brake and al., 2019), is unlikely (Brochier-Puig, 2004). The spring [SOURCE: Page_13]

289   mounds in the Nefzaoua region are a model of complex geomorphologic processes that are [SOURCE: Page_13]

290   exceptional in their density. They testify to many centuries of technological skill and [SOURCE: Page_13]

291   collective management of water and irrigation infrastructure for sustainable agriculture [SOURCE: Page_13]

292   production in highly arid conditions. [SOURCE: Page_13]

293      According to Panizza (2001), the spring mounds in the Nefzaoua region can be considered [SOURCE: Page_13]

294   geomorphosites. A geomorphological resource is a geomorphosite that society can use. The [SOURCE: Page_13]

295   attributes that may confer value are scenic, socio-economic, cultural and scientific (Reynard [SOURCE: Page_13]

296   and Panizza, 2005). When possible, the time scales of a geomorphosite should be [SOURCE: Page_13]

297   distinguished: the deep past of geology, the Quaternary Period, the distant past of prehistory [SOURCE: Page_13]

12 [SOURCE: Page_13]

298   and postglacial environments, the near past of history and recent history, and the present time [SOURCE: Page_14]

299   of active geotopes, during which the dynamic interaction of internal or external geodynamics, [SOURCE: Page_14]

300   including anthropogenic factors, has an influence (Giusti and Calvet, 2010). [SOURCE: Page_14]

301   To be protected as geomorphological heritage, the mounds cannot belong only to the [SOURCE: Page_14]

302   scientific community. The concept of this geomorphosite would be linked to “emphasisis on [SOURCE: Page_14]

303   community involvement for sustainability”, one of the four concepts in the recent synthesis of [SOURCE: Page_14]

304   Németh et al. (2021). Therefore, this heritage could be preserved successfully if the local [SOURCE: Page_14]

305   population could own it and become its guardians. Unfortunately, the local population [SOURCE: Page_14]

306   perceives the mounds as a mining resource for short-term unsustainable agricultural activity. [SOURCE: Page_14]

307   According to Reynard (2009) and Reynard et al., (2015), after a complete inventory of the [SOURCE: Page_14]

308   different sites, about ten spring mounds could be selected as potential geomorphosites to be [SOURCE: Page_14]

309   protected (Raddadi et al., 2022), with suggestions for protecting them and preventing their [SOURCE: Page_14]

310   complete destruction. [SOURCE: Page_14]

311   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_14]

312   Of the 399 spring mounds identified in 1933 in the Nefzaoua region, only 126 could be [SOURCE: Page_14]

313   identified there in 2020. Only 14 were considered not to be degraded. The irreversible [SOURCE: Page_14]

314   decrease in the level of the water table caused by intense pumping of groundwater and [SOURCE: Page_14]

315   irrigation of palm tree plantations has dried up all of the artesian springs, which are now only [SOURCE: Page_14]

316   geomorphological relics. Associated with this, the establishment of villages on top of the [SOURCE: Page_14]

317   mounds, construction of water collectors in ponds, irrigation channels and tunnels at the foot [SOURCE: Page_14]

318   of the mounds testify to ancient maintenance and water management, which comprise the [SOURCE: Page_14]

319   cultural heritage. [SOURCE: Page_14]

320   As 42% of the mounds have been or are being excavated, for example to provide a soil [SOURCE: Page_14]

321   amendment to expand new date palm plantations, they are at risk of complete destruction. The [SOURCE: Page_14]

13 [SOURCE: Page_14]

322   spring mounds in the Nefzaoua region in Tunisia can be considered as geomorphosites, with [SOURCE: Page_15]

323   geomorphological, scientific, cultural and societal value, and some of them could be protected [SOURCE: Page_15]

324   according to the required criteria. However, no protection can be sustained without the [SOURCE: Page_15]

325   acceptance and participation of the local population. [SOURCE: Page_15]
