## 20    METHODS

21    Eight diversified cropping systems were designed during multi-actor co-innovation [SOURCE: Page_1]

22    workshops. These systems were established and monitored for eight years (2010-2017) on [SOURCE: Page_1]

23    two fields on each of eight farms located in areas with different soil and climate conditions. [SOURCE: Page_1]

25    using 15 economic, environmental and social indicators. [SOURCE: Page_1]

## 26   RESULTS AND CONCLUSIONS

27   Crop diversification improved most of the environmental indicators. Pesticide use decreased [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   by 20-64% in five of the eight systems but increased in the other three, due to production [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   contracts that required systematic applications or in order to control high levels of pest or [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   weed pressure. In parallel, mean energy consumption (-30%), greenhouse gas emissions [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   (-36%) and irrigation water consumption (-43%) decreased significantly after diversification, [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   which helped the systems mitigate and adapt to climate change. The economic performance, [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   however, was more contrasted, with four of the eight farms showing a decrease in semi-net [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   margin of 10-35% compared to those of the initial systems, but the other four showing an [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   increase of 5-190%. Production of food energy also generally decreased (by up to 40%) after [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   diversification, mainly due to a decrease in the amount of cereals produced (especially maize). [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   Thus, crop diversification usually improves the environmental sustainability of cropping [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   systems; however, for certain specialised high-value cropping systems, which often have high [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   environmental impacts, it tends to reduce their economic performances. [SOURCE: Page_2]

44   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   Food production in the second half of the 20th century has increased greatly due to [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   intensification of agriculture, but it has also led to many undesirable environmental impacts, [SOURCE: Page_2]

47   such as biodiversity loss; soil degradation; and soil, water, air, and food contamination by [SOURCE: Page_2]

48   pesticides (Foley et al., 2005; MEA, 2005), which can have negative impacts on human health [SOURCE: Page_2]

49   (Mostafalou and Abdollahi, 2013; Carvalho, 2017; Kim et al., 2017). To limit or reduce these [SOURCE: Page_2]

50   negative externalities, several directives and plans have been developed and implemented in [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

51   the European Union since the 1990s, especially through the Common Agricultural Policy (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   Directive 2009/128/EC; European Green Deal). In France, the national Ecophyto plan (2008- [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   2025) aims to halve the amount of pesticides applied by developing and promoting alternative [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   pest management strategies, based mainly on integrated pest management principles [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   (Reganold et al., 2001; Barzman et al., 2015), while maintaining an overall level of [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   production compatible with food/feed demand or other industrial needs (e.g. energy, fibre) [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   (Debaeke et al., 2009; Lechenet et al., 2016; Lechenet et al., 2017a). Attempting to meet such [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   potentially contradictory objectives simultaneously may be difficult and raise questions that [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   need to be addressed collectively by farmers and stakeholders in agricultural research and [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   development (Thornton and Herrero, 2001; Vasileiadis et al., 2013; Richard et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   Among the possible strategies to reach these ambitious goals, redesigning cropping systems [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   (CS) using a diversity of agronomic mechanisms that are well-reasoned and interconnected [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   for maximum efficiency is probably one of the most promising to reduce the dependence of [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   agricultural systems on inputs (Attoumani-Ronceux et al., 2011; Lechenet et al., 2017b). [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   Redesigning of cropping systems can be combined with complementary strategies such as [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   substitution of inputs with more “environmentally friendly” practices. Many agricultural [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   research and development projects use this Efficiency-Substitution-Redesign strategy [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   (MacRae et al., 1989; Hill and MacRae, 1995) to assess the degree of change between initial [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   and alternative systems and to develop methods to analyse and compare alternative systems [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   that have been developed to decrease the use of inputs (Giuliano et al., 2016; Casagrande et [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   al., 2017; Lechenet et al., 2017b). [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   Many agronomists consider crop diversification in space and time as the most effective [SOURCE: Page_3]

73   strategy for decreasing input dependence because it can decrease pest pressure (e.g. break [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   biological cycles, reduce weed specialisation), and it is a central pillar of agroecology (Altieri [SOURCE: Page_3]

75   and Nicholls, 2005; Duru et al., 2015). However, the specialisation of farming systems and [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

76   CS in France in the past 50 years has often decreased crop diversity (Duru et al., 2015; [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   Meynard et al., 2018), which has resulted in monocultures (mainly of maize and winter [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   cereals) in several regions. Currently, reintroducing crop diversity faces technical lock-ins, [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   such as a lack of agronomic knowledge about crop species (e.g. choice, management, [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   performance), a scarcity of references on how to use this diversity to build efficient crop [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   rotations (Meynard et al., 2018) and organisational and economic lock-ins, with specialised [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   sectors (e.g. the maize production chain) that are unwilling to manage a wide range of crops [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   (Magrini et al., 2016; Magrini et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   When designing CS prototypes, two main approaches can be used: model-based design or a [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   prototyping approach that involves different stakeholders/experts (Vereijken, 1997; Lançon et [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   al., 2007; Lesur-Dumoulin et al., 2018). By using knowledge from farmers, advisers (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   from extension services, cooperatives) and scientists in a design step and then defining [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   objectives that the CS prototype needs to fulfil, this second approach can explore more [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   innovations than the former (Lesur-Dumoulin et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   In south-western France, especially in the Occitanie region, CS are highly specialised and not [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   very diversified. In alluvial valleys, on loamy soils with differing depths and degrees of stone [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   content, the CS are dominated by maize (mainly for grain), usually irrigated (> 70% of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   area) and planted in monoculture. Water consumption for irrigation is a major issue, [SOURCE: Page_4]

94   especially as effects of climate change become more severe. At the same time, although maize [SOURCE: Page_4]

95   requires few pesticide treatments, the active ingredients used, such as S-metolachlor, are [SOURCE: Page_4]

96   widely detected in the region’s groundwater and surface water. In hillside areas of the region, [SOURCE: Page_4]

97   mainly on clay soils, the CS are based on a relatively short rotation of wheat (durum or soft) [SOURCE: Page_4]

98   and sunflower. Most of these CS include deep tillage (usually mouldboard ploughing) and [SOURCE: Page_4]

99   bare soil during the fallow period. These operations often cause significant erosion, loss of [SOURCE: Page_4]

100   soil fertility and high energy consumption. [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

101   The objective of this study was to test the hypothesis that crop diversification can improve [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   agronomic, economic, environmental and social performances of CS. One of its original [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   features was to conduct these experiments on farms over a long period (8 years). To this end, [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   CS were co-designed during workshops with groups of 6-8 farmers, advisers and scientists in [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   six territories of the Occitanie region. Following these co-design workshops, eight farmers (1- [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   2 per territory) committed themselves to implement the co-designed diversified CS with [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   support from their local adviser. The performance of these CS was monitored for eight years [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   using a variety of indicators to assess their sustainability. [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   2.1. Territories of the study and main features of their initial CS [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   The study was conducted in the Occitanie region (Fig. 1), which has an oceanic climate with [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   Mediterranean influences, according to the Köppen climate classification. In 2009, six [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   agricultural territories that represented the main areas of cash-crop production of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   Occitanie region were chosen: [SOURCE: Page_5]

116       -   three were located in alluvial corridors of the Garonne, Ariège and Adour Rivers (Fig. [SOURCE: Page_5]

117           1). Soil types there are mainly silty (55-60%, clay content 15-20%), with a relatively [SOURCE: Page_5]

118           low organic carbon content (mean < 1.0%) and sometimes stony (especially in the [SOURCE: Page_5]

119           Ariège corridor). The main CS is based on a grain maize (or maize seed) monoculture, [SOURCE: Page_5]

120           mostly irrigated and cultivated after deep tillage (mouldboard ploughing) in the spring. [SOURCE: Page_5]

121       -   the other three were located on hillsides of the region (Lauragais, Tarn and Causses du [SOURCE: Page_5]

122           Lot) (Fig. 1). Soils there are mainly calcareous clay (clay content > 30%). The CS, [SOURCE: Page_5]

123           usually rainfed, are based mainly on a durum wheat-sunflower rotation, but are [SOURCE: Page_5]

124           sometimes more diverse, with rapeseed or barley as winter crops and sorghum as a [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

125          summer crop. Soil tillage is generally reduced for winter crops but deeper [SOURCE: Page_6]

126          (mouldboard ploughing or other deep tillage) for spring crops. [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   In each territory, a group of 6-8 farmers, all exclusively field-crop producers with no [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   livestock, was assembled according to local advisers' knowledge of their potential interest in [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   redesigning more sustainable CS. These farmers were involved in the co-design workshops, [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   which started at the end of 2009 or beginning of 2010. [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   2.2. Method to co-design, test and evaluate diversified CS [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   Co-designing of the CS was based on a participative prototyping approach that involved [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   farmers, advisers and scientists (Vereijken, 1997; Lançon et al., 2007; Lesur-Dumoulin et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   2018). The farmers were identified by local advisers from the Chamber of Agriculture [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   according to (i) their farming systems, which had to be representative of the territory, and (ii) [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   their motivation for the project. Most of the farmers selected could be considered as “adviser [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   farmers” (Richard et al., 2020): aware of the need to improve agricultural production methods [SOURCE: Page_6]

139   and with a high level of expertise and knowledge about agronomic mechanisms alternative to [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   the use of inputs. A two-day group workshop was then organised in each territory. The [SOURCE: Page_6]

141   workshop was divided into two main steps (Fig. 2), which aimed to (i) analyse advantages and [SOURCE: Page_6]

142   disadvantages of the initial CS and stakeholders in the territory in order to (ii) co-design and [SOURCE: Page_6]

143   evaluate CS prototypes. [SOURCE: Page_6]

144 [SOURCE: Page_6]

145          2.2.1. Step 1 – Systems analysis and farmers' perceptions [SOURCE: Page_6]

146   The main objectives of step 1 were to identify the main issues of the territory in order to [SOURCE: Page_6]

147   improve the sustainability of farming systems and to analyse their existing CS by defining the [SOURCE: Page_6]

148   one that would serve as a reference. In most territories, farmers spontaneously mentioned [SOURCE: Page_6]

149   environmental problems, mainly degradation of water quality (surface or ground water) due to [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

150   the presence of nitrate and/or pesticides. In this first phase of step 1, the advisers and [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   scientists let the farmers express themselves so they could exchange practical knowledge and [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   suggestions. Next, the group analysed strengths, weaknesses, opportunities of and threats to [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   (SWOT) agronomic, economic, environmental and social aspects of the reference system. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   next phase aimed to have the group share and adhere to a systems approach to be able to co- [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   design the CS prototypes and then define and prioritise their main objectives. [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   During this phase, the advisers and scientists present identified 1-2 farmers who were likely to [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   experiment with, on their own farms, the CS outlined. Farmers were chosen by considering [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   their motivations, open-mindedness and ability to exchange with other farmers and the [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   advisers. Two farmers accepted doing so in two of the territories (Fig. 1), while one farmer [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   accepted doing so in the other four (Fig. 2). [SOURCE: Page_7]

161 [SOURCE: Page_7]

162          2.2.2. Step 2 – Co-design and ex-ante assessment of the CS prototypes [SOURCE: Page_7]

163   An iterative method, based on that developed by the national network on innovative CS [SOURCE: Page_7]

164   (Attoumani-Ronceux et al., 2011), was used to combine the participative co-design of CS [SOURCE: Page_7]

165   with multi-criteria ex-ante assessment of the CS prototypes designed (Fig. 2). For the co- [SOURCE: Page_7]

166   design phase, the objectives previously defined were used and supplemented by a variety of [SOURCE: Page_7]

167   constraints (e.g. keeping certain crops in the rotation, such as durum wheat or maize). The [SOURCE: Page_7]

168   objectives and constraints could vary among territories, but some of them were the same. In [SOURCE: Page_7]

169   particular, a common objective was to use less pesticides and fertilisers than in the initial CS, [SOURCE: Page_7]

170   using both crop diversification and other mechanisms of integrated crop protection. [SOURCE: Page_7]

171   The initial group of 6-8 farmers, 2-3 advisers and 1-2 scientists was divided into two equal- [SOURCE: Page_7]

172   sized subgroups, and a prototype version of the serious game “Mission Ecophyt’eau®” [SOURCE: Page_7]

173   (https://www.civam.org/accompagner-le-changement/mission-ecophyteau/)            was   used   to [SOURCE: Page_7]

174   facilitate interactions and knowledge sharing in a playful atmosphere. The principle of this [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

175   collaborative game is to generate interactions between participants in order to co-design CS [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   that meet the objectives and constraints set. One of the scientists or advisers acted as the [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   “facilitator”, who was familiar with the CS co-design method. Maintaining a neutral position [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   during the workshop, the facilitator stimulated discussion and thoughts in the groups. The [SOURCE: Page_8]

179   participants first identified which cash crops to include in the CS and then discussed where [SOURCE: Page_8]

180   place these crops in the CS. These exchanges led them to reflect on how to manage fallow [SOURCE: Page_8]

181   periods, sometimes by adding multiservice cover crops or maintaining periods of bare soil [SOURCE: Page_8]

182   (e.g. “false sowing” for mechanical weed control). Once the CS was finalised, which usually [SOURCE: Page_8]

183   required many iterations, the group defined the technical operations and their associated [SOURCE: Page_8]

184   decision rules. These rules specified the reasoning behind management decisions, such as [SOURCE: Page_8]

185   tillage operations, destruction dates of multiservice cover crops and pest thresholds at which [SOURCE: Page_8]

186   pesticide treatments begin. [SOURCE: Page_8]

187   When 2-3 prototypes had been designed in each group, ex-ante assessment of their [SOURCE: Page_8]

188   performance was performed using the multi-criteria assessment method MASC 2.0® (Sadok [SOURCE: Page_8]

189   et al., 2009) to verify whether the prototypes met the objectives set. If the performances [SOURCE: Page_8]

190   assessed using the selected indicators did not meet the farmers’ objectives and constraints [SOURCE: Page_8]

191   sufficiently well, adjustments could be made. At the end of this stage, the farmer(s) who were [SOURCE: Page_8]

192   going to experiment with the co-designed CS on their farm chose from among the prototypes [SOURCE: Page_8]

193   developed, depending on their specific objectives and constraints. Because the CS developed [SOURCE: Page_8]

194   were no longer prototypes after the farmers’ choice, we refer to them as "diversified CS". [SOURCE: Page_8]

195 [SOURCE: Page_8]

227          2.3.1. Economic indicators [SOURCE: Page_10]

228   #1 Mean semi net-margin at the rotation level [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   The semi-net margin (SNM, €/ha/year), an indicator of the economic profitability of the crop [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   or CS, was calculated as follows: [SOURCE: Page_10]

231   SNM = (Gross product + Subsidies – Operational costs – Mechanical costs) / Rotation [SOURCE: Page_10]

232   duration [SOURCE: Page_10]

233   with Operational costs = Seed costs + Pesticide costs + Fertiliser costs + Irrigation costs n [SOURCE: Page_10]

Mechanical costs= ∑ Areai × (Tractor costi + Equipment costi + Fuel costs) i=1 [SOURCE: Page_10]

234   where n is the number of mechanical operations on a field, and Areai is the area worked (in [SOURCE: Page_10]

235   ha) per operation. The costs of the tractor, equipment and fuel (in €.ha-1) per operation include [SOURCE: Page_10]

236   wear, maintenance and depreciation. [SOURCE: Page_10]

237   Irrigation costs include the irrigation equipment used (e.g. pivot, hose reel, sprinkler) and [SOURCE: Page_10]

238   associated costs (e.g. electricity or fuel for pumps). Mechanical costs are estimated using [SOURCE: Page_10]

239   reference values for technical operations based on the type of equipment, traction power and [SOURCE: Page_10]

240   working width used (Chambres d'agriculture de France, 2018). [SOURCE: Page_10]

241   Eight crop-price scenarios were chosen for the period 2007-2014 to test the robustness of the [SOURCE: Page_10]

242   SNM of the CS (Massot et al., 2016) (Table 3). Economic performances of the initial CS and [SOURCE: Page_10]

243   diversified CS were compared. [SOURCE: Page_10]

245   #2 Weed pressure in the field [SOURCE: Page_11]

246   Weed pressure was considered an economic indicator because poor weed management can [SOURCE: Page_11]

247   have severe economic consequences for farmers due to primary harmfulness (competition) in [SOURCE: Page_11]

248   the short term or secondary harmfulness (seed bank) in the long term. Weed abundance and [SOURCE: Page_11]

249   phenological stage were determined using qualitative scales at the flowering stage of each [SOURCE: Page_11]

250   cash crop of the rotation. Measurements were based on counting weeds in 0.5 m^2 quadrats (5 [SOURCE: Page_11]

251   per field) to estimate weed density (plants.m-2) and on crossing each field in a “W” shape (i.e. [SOURCE: Page_11]

252   four transects) to assess ca. 2000 m^2 per field. The scales used for monitoring were as follows: [SOURCE: Page_11]

- 253       Seven classes for abundance, based on weed density (plants.m-2) (adapted from Barralis [SOURCE: Page_11]

254       (1976)): ε: less than 10 individual weeds in the entire area assessed; Class 0: < 0.1, Class [SOURCE: Page_11]

255       1: 0.1-1, Class 2: 1-3, Class 3: 4-20, Class 4: 21-50, Class 5: 51-500, Class 6: > 500 [SOURCE: Page_11]

256   -   Five classes for phenological stage: A: Cotyledons to 1-3 leaves (grasses: 1-3 leaves); B: [SOURCE: Page_11]

257       Young plant (4-6 leaves); C: Adult plant (grasses: tillering); D: Flowering; E: Seeds [SOURCE: Page_11]

258       disseminated, senescent plant. [SOURCE: Page_11]

259   The observer records the weed species identified and their minimum, maximum and dominant [SOURCE: Page_11]

260   phenological stages. To estimate overall weed pressure in the field, a score from 1-10 is then [SOURCE: Page_11]

261   attributed to each of the four transects according to the dominant class of abundance and [SOURCE: Page_11]

262   phenological stage of the weed community (without distinguishing species), and the four [SOURCE: Page_11]

263   scores are averaged for the field (Table 4). When designing this scoring system, the [SOURCE: Page_11]

264   agricultural advisers in the project considered that a mean score > 7 corresponds to well- [SOURCE: Page_11]

265   managed weeds, whereas a mean score > 8 corresponds to a low risk of increasing the weed [SOURCE: Page_11]

266   seed bank. [SOURCE: Page_11]

## 303   #6 I-PHY

304   Environmental impacts of pesticides were estimated using the cumulative I-PHY index [SOURCE: Page_13]

305   (formerly IPEST), an expert system for calculating indicators that reflect potential [SOURCE: Page_13]

306   environmental impacts of applying a pesticide to a field crop (van der Werf and Zimmer, [SOURCE: Page_13]

307   1998; Lindahl and Bockstaller, 2012; Lechenet et al., 2014). I-PHY estimates the risk of [SOURCE: Page_13]

308   pesticide application to three compartments of the environment: air, surface water and ground [SOURCE: Page_13]

309   water. I-PHY is calculated for each active ingredient application and ranges from 0 (high risk) [SOURCE: Page_13]

310   to 10 (no risk), with an acceptable risk defined at a score of 7. I-PHY is based on a field’s [SOURCE: Page_13]

311   inherent sensitivity to pesticide transfer toward these three compartments; characteristics of [SOURCE: Page_13]

312   the active ingredient (e.g. sorption properties, solubility, ecotoxicity, mobility, degradation [SOURCE: Page_13]

313   half-life); and characteristics of the application (e.g. number of active ingredients, canopy [SOURCE: Page_13]

314   cover at application) to calculate three impact factors, one for each compartment. I-PHY is [SOURCE: Page_13]

13 [SOURCE: Page_13]

315   obtained using fuzzy decision trees that aggregate the three impact factors into one summary [SOURCE: Page_14]

316   indicator. [SOURCE: Page_14]

528   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_22]

529   4.1. Crop diversification as a mechanism to improve environmental performances [SOURCE: Page_22]

530   The degree to which the environmental performance of a CS can be improved depends on the [SOURCE: Page_22]

531   former’s initial level and thus on the reference value used (Viguier et al., 2021). Nevertheless, [SOURCE: Page_22]

532   in this eight-year study, conducted with farmers who were ready to question their technical [SOURCE: Page_22]

533   operations, diversifying CS with cash crops and/or service plants improved most of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

534   environmental indicators selected. These results, obtained under "real" farming conditions and [SOURCE: Page_22]

22 [SOURCE: Page_22]

535   not on research fields, are particularly original and encouraging and clearly highlight the [SOURCE: Page_23]

536   value of diversifying CS to reduce their environmental impacts. [SOURCE: Page_23]

537   Concerning pesticide use, a decrease of 50%, as targeted by the Ecophyto plan, appears to be [SOURCE: Page_23]

538   realistic and achievable, including in CS without livestock, but decreases differed greatly [SOURCE: Page_23]

539   among farmers. These differences were related mainly to (i) farmers’ technical skills, in [SOURCE: Page_23]

540   particular their knowledge about the new species introduced into the CS (Meynard et al., [SOURCE: Page_23]

541   2018); (ii) their freedom to make decisions about pesticide applications, as contracts for crops [SOURCE: Page_23]

542   usually require systematic applications and are thus less compatible with reduction of [SOURCE: Page_23]

543   pesticide use; (iii) initial pest and weed pressures in the CS and (iv) the number of [SOURCE: Page_23]

544   agroecological mechanisms that can be used to manage pest and weed pressures (Gaba et al., [SOURCE: Page_23]

545   2014; Deguine et al., 2021); for example, a lack of tillage in conservation agriculture systems [SOURCE: Page_23]

546   greatly complicates weed management without herbicides, especially glyphosate (Adeux et [SOURCE: Page_23]

547   al., 2017). The decrease in pesticide use observed was also related to regular monitoring of [SOURCE: Page_23]

548   pest and weed pressures in fields by the local advisers in the project (12-14 days/year/farm), [SOURCE: Page_23]

549   in direct relation to the regional network for monitoring pest and weed pressures (Cros et al., [SOURCE: Page_23]

550   2021). Along with the decrease in the amounts of pesticide used, decreasing the risk of [SOURCE: Page_23]

551   transfer to the environment seems possible, either directly because of this decreased use or [SOURCE: Page_23]

552   indirectly through a more appropriate choice of active ingredients applied or their formulation [SOURCE: Page_23]

553   (Li et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_23]

554   In southern France, adapting CS to climate change requires, in particular, minimizing reliance [SOURCE: Page_23]

555   on irrigation water. Diversifying crops with species that consume less water (than maize, in [SOURCE: Page_23]

556   particular) has positive effects, in addition to decreasing the sensitivity of farms to climate [SOURCE: Page_23]

557   hazards (Lin, 2011; Roesch-McNally et al., 2018; Hufnagel et al., 2020). In addition, [SOURCE: Page_23]

558   increasing the duration of soil cover by plants (or mulch) can decrease negative effects of [SOURCE: Page_23]

559   extreme rainfall events (Baumhardt and Jones, 2002; Palm et al., 2014), whose frequency is [SOURCE: Page_23]

23 [SOURCE: Page_23]

560   increasing significantly. Here again, co-design of CS, their diversification, especially by [SOURCE: Page_24]

561   service plants, and better organisation of crop rotations can increase the duration of soil cover, [SOURCE: Page_24]

562   as highlighted during the on-farm monitoring. However, these effects are not always [SOURCE: Page_24]

563   sufficient to increase soil carbon sequestration (estimated here by the OMI) (Viguier et al., [SOURCE: Page_24]

564   2021), or in particular to return the amount of OM to the soil observed in irrigated maize [SOURCE: Page_24]

565   monocultures, even without soil cover during the fallow period. Achieving objectives of the [SOURCE: Page_24]

567   increasing the OM returned to the soil, especially by service plants (Poeplau and Don, 2015; [SOURCE: Page_24]

568   Rumpel et al., 2020; Rodrigues et al., 2021). In this study, most of the cover crops planted [SOURCE: Page_24]

569   were faba bean, mostly as a pure crop, as it is inexpensive and easy to plant and establish, but [SOURCE: Page_24]

570   it does not fit well into all possible periods between two cash crops. To optimise carbon [SOURCE: Page_24]

571   sequestration, the species and varieties of cover crops and the composition of mixtures must [SOURCE: Page_24]

573   Couëdel et al., 2018; Bybee-Finley et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_24]

574   In this study, crop diversification decreased energy consumption and GHG emissions, which [SOURCE: Page_24]

575   are strongly correlated, by nearly 40%; thus, CS diversification is an effective mechanism for [SOURCE: Page_24]

576   decreasing them, as highlighted in other studies (Pellerin et al., 2013; Viguier et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_24]

577   The main items that consume energy are fertilisers, fuel and, if present, irrigation (Colnenne- [SOURCE: Page_24]

578   David and Dore, 2015; Pellerin et al., 2017). Nevertheless, the CS studied appeared less [SOURCE: Page_24]

579   effective at mitigating effects of climate change through carbon sequestration, as only the CS [SOURCE: Page_24]

580   of Farm 4, under conservation agriculture, showed an increase in OMI. However, this [SOURCE: Page_24]

581   indicator remains relatively inaccurate and, as highlighted by other studies, should be [SOURCE: Page_24]

582   combined with measurements of soil carbon content after several rotations (Islam and Weil, [SOURCE: Page_24]

583   2000; Scopel et al., 2013; Viguier et al., 2021). As mentioned, cover crops are important for [SOURCE: Page_24]

584   increasing soil carbon content in cereal CS significantly (Poeplau and Don, 2015). In south- [SOURCE: Page_24]

24 [SOURCE: Page_24]

585   western France, one challenge is to successfully exploit the summer, during which potential [SOURCE: Page_25]

586   photosynthetic activity is highest but water to produce OM that will be returned to the soil is [SOURCE: Page_25]

587   scarce. By redesigning diversified CS whose cash crops use less water, some of the water [SOURCE: Page_25]

588   saved could be used to produce ecosystem services besides provisioning services, particularly [SOURCE: Page_25]

589   climate-regulation services; however, doing so requires a real paradigm shift in crop [SOURCE: Page_25]

590   production and potentially providing inputs (such as water) for supporting or regulating [SOURCE: Page_25]

591   ecosystem services, not only provisioning services. [SOURCE: Page_25]

592 [SOURCE: Page_25]

593   4.2. Crop diversification as a mechanism to improve economic and social performances [SOURCE: Page_25]

594   The economic performance of the CS was assessed using four indicators, particularly the [SOURCE: Page_25]

595   SNM, which includes mechanisation costs. The eight-year monitoring of the eight farms [SOURCE: Page_25]

596   shows that crop diversification can increase the SNM significantly if the decrease in input [SOURCE: Page_25]

597   costs is combined with a strict control of mechanisation costs. Another important conclusion [SOURCE: Page_25]

598   is that crop diversification and input reduction do not always increase economic profitability, [SOURCE: Page_25]

599   despite good crop productivity. They can even lead, as for four of the farms in this study, to [SOURCE: Page_25]

600   lower economic performance, which is consistent with the economic performance of low- [SOURCE: Page_25]

601   input CS tested in experimental fields (Bonnet et al., 2021; Viguier et al., 2021). In initial CS [SOURCE: Page_25]

602   with high added value, such as the maize-seed monoculture studied, diversification always [SOURCE: Page_25]

603   decreased economic profitability. The farmers were satisfied by this improvement in [SOURCE: Page_25]

604   environmental performance, but it was not enough to compensate for the loss of more than [SOURCE: Page_25]

605   30% of SNM per ha. The reorientation of Common Agricultural Policy subsidies towards an [SOURCE: Page_25]

606   agroecological transition of systems is an incentive (Solazzo et al., 2016; Scown et al., 2020), [SOURCE: Page_25]

607   but it remains insufficient to make deep changes to these CS, which are oriented towards [SOURCE: Page_25]

608   economic optimisation, without any real consideration for the negative externalities [SOURCE: Page_25]

609   generated. In contrast, for irrigated grain-maize monocultures, introducing soya bean into the [SOURCE: Page_25]

25 [SOURCE: Page_25]

610   rotation (as in four farms in this study) is a powerful mechanism for increasing SNM (Jouffret [SOURCE: Page_26]

611   et al., 2015). Soya beans are relatively simple to grow, with few pest pressures, and are [SOURCE: Page_26]

612   suitable for low-input management (25% of soya bean area in France is in organic farming). [SOURCE: Page_26]

613   For two of the farms studied, initially irrigated maize monocultures, diversifying the CS [SOURCE: Page_26]

614   followed a similar strategy of introducing soya bean and a straw cereal. Nearly the same [SOURCE: Page_26]

615   rotation, tested at an experimental station to diversify a maize monoculture, showed [SOURCE: Page_26]

616   satisfactory economic performance and a large decrease in environmental impacts, [SOURCE: Page_26]

617   particularly pesticide transfers (Giuliano et al., 2016; Giuliano et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_26]

618   Finally, another positive effect of diversifying CS was better distribution of labour time [SOURCE: Page_26]

619   throughout the year, especially for CS that were initially specialised (e.g. monocultures). This [SOURCE: Page_26]

620   result must be tempered, however, because introduction of new crops into the CS was [SOURCE: Page_26]

621   accompanied by regular technical monitoring by advisers in the project, rather than by the [SOURCE: Page_26]

622   farmers themselves. [SOURCE: Page_26]

624   4.3. Limits and perspectives of the on-farm study [SOURCE: Page_26]

625   The multi-criteria analysis highlights that, to date, one or more CS cannot be identified that [SOURCE: Page_26]

626   simultaneously satisfy all the sustainability objectives targeted and that trade-offs must [SOURCE: Page_26]

627   therefore be accepted, for example according to the priorities of a territory. This observation [SOURCE: Page_26]

628   is similar to that made by other studies based on long-term experiments on CS at experimental [SOURCE: Page_26]

629   stations or based on monitoring of farmers' fields (Giuliano et al., 2016; Angevin et al., 2017; [SOURCE: Page_26]

630   Colnenne-David et al., 2017; Viguier et al., 2021). Furthermore, the diversity of stakeholders [SOURCE: Page_26]

631   in the co-design workshops of this study was probably too low, which did not allow for a [SOURCE: Page_26]

632   wide range of diversification strategies to be explored. Ultimately, despite strong [SOURCE: Page_26]

633   diversification of the initial CS, participants chose mainly crops that were already common in [SOURCE: Page_26]

634   the territories concerned, probably because organised value chains with a guaranteed a market [SOURCE: Page_26]

26 [SOURCE: Page_26]

635   already existed, but also perhaps due to farmers’ and advisers’ fears and lack of technical [SOURCE: Page_27]

636   knowledge about less common crops (Meynard et al., 2018). A recommendation for a future [SOURCE: Page_27]

637   study would therefore be to broaden the panel of stakeholders involved in co-design [SOURCE: Page_27]

638   workshops to (i) collectively choose and prioritise diversification objectives and strategies [SOURCE: Page_27]

639   that support the agroecological transition of territories and (ii) allow for a wide range of [SOURCE: Page_27]

640   possibilities that could, in some cases, favour the emergence of new value chains. [SOURCE: Page_27]

641   On this point, contracting within certain value chains is often proposed as a mechanism for [SOURCE: Page_27]

642   economic stability and as a way to encourage diversification of production (Huh and Lall, [SOURCE: Page_27]

643   2013; de Roest et al., 2018). However, the study shows that some contracts, particularly those [SOURCE: Page_27]

644   for seed production (e.g. maize and carrots), restrict technical operations and input levels. [SOURCE: Page_27]

645   This limits the ability to implement alternative strategies, particularly pesticide applications, [SOURCE: Page_27]

646   and farmers lose some of their decision-making capacity. [SOURCE: Page_27]

647   Finally, as mentioned, the results of this on-farm study are encouraging and suggest effective [SOURCE: Page_27]

648   strategies for decreasing environmental impacts of farming activities. Although performing [SOURCE: Page_27]

649   research directly on farms is increasingly encouraged to promote interactions and facilitate [SOURCE: Page_27]

650   transitions (Lacoste et al., 2021), few on-farm studies that evaluate the performance of [SOURCE: Page_27]

651   innovative CS have been published, and in this respect the results of this study are original. In [SOURCE: Page_27]

652   this study, supporting the farms required a large amount of time of the agricultural advisers, [SOURCE: Page_27]

653   up to 15 days/year/farm. One of the initially unidentified benefits of this investment was that [SOURCE: Page_27]

654   it increased the local advisers’ skill with systemic agronomy approaches, which they were [SOURCE: Page_27]

655   able to use later, particularly with the farm networks of the national Ecophyto plan. However, [SOURCE: Page_27]

656   this degree of human investment suggests that it will be difficult to disseminate equivalent [SOURCE: Page_27]

657   approaches to a larger scale if the same level of support is provided. Despite this large [SOURCE: Page_27]

658   investment in monitoring and accompanying farmers, it was not possible to have all crops of a [SOURCE: Page_27]

659   CS present on each farm each year, which is recommended to identify effects of annual [SOURCE: Page_27]

27 [SOURCE: Page_27]

660   weather conditions on crop performance (Bonnet et al., 2021). Moreover, this kind of [SOURCE: Page_28]

661   approach cannot specifically assess conflicts between technical operations, which remain [SOURCE: Page_28]

662   manageable when only two fields are involved but could become problematic if the entire [SOURCE: Page_28]

663   farm is involved. Nevertheless, to partially overcome this limitation, the results were [SOURCE: Page_28]

664   analysed, discussed and compared to those of other farmers in each territory at each annual [SOURCE: Page_28]

665   review. [SOURCE: Page_28]

667   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_28]

668   This study summarised eight years of monitoring and data collection from initial and [SOURCE: Page_28]

669   diversified CS on eight farms in south-western France. The initial hypothesis was that crop [SOURCE: Page_28]

670   diversification was an important mechanism for improving the overall sustainability of CS. [SOURCE: Page_28]

671   The multi-criteria analysis performed, using a variety of indicators, indicates that crop [SOURCE: Page_28]

672   diversification improves the environmental sustainability of CS and some aspects of the social [SOURCE: Page_28]

673   dimension. Several farms were able to meet objectives for reducing inputs, particularly [SOURCE: Page_28]

674   pesticides and nitrogen fertilisers. Diversified CS were generally more climate-friendly as [SOURCE: Page_28]

675   they consumed less energy and emitted less GHGs. In irrigated CS, which were often [SOURCE: Page_28]

676   monocultures, diversification also decreased the amount of irrigation water greatly. Compared [SOURCE: Page_28]

677   to the initial CS, the diversification strategies used reduced farmers’ workload and distributed [SOURCE: Page_28]

678   it better throughout the year. However, the economic performance of the diversified CS [SOURCE: Page_28]

679   varied, with no guarantee of increasing or even maintaining farmers' income. Promoting the [SOURCE: Page_28]

680   agroecological transition of CS and, more broadly, of food systems, thus implies providing [SOURCE: Page_28]

681   economic support to “virtuous” systems that help restore ecosystem functions and services. [SOURCE: Page_28]
