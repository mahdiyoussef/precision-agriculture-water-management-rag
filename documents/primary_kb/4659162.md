178   Glyphosate adsorption process: Influence of soil properties [SOURCE: Page_7]

179   Glyphosate adsorption is primarily determined by its molecular structure, dissociation constants, and water [SOURCE: Page_7]

180   solubility. The molecule has three distinct polar functional groups: one carboxylic, one amine, and one [SOURCE: Page_7]

181   phosphonic (Figure 1). As a result, its adsorption behavior is complex and can vary according to soil properties. [SOURCE: Page_7]

182   Indeed, as detailed in the following paragraphs, the literature emphasizes the cumulative role of pH, soil clay [SOURCE: Page_7]

183   content, exchangeable phase, and OM in the retention behavior of GPS. Variations in these latter parameters [SOURCE: Page_7]

184   (texture, pH, OM content) explain the large range of soil sorption coefficient constants (K d) varying from 5 to [SOURCE: Page_7]

185   811 l kg-1 (European Food Safety Agency 2015). GPS was once described as “slightly mobile”, with a mean Kd [SOURCE: Page_7]

186   value of 209.4 l kg-1 in the PPDB database (Lewis et al. 2016). Although qualified as “slightly mobile”, GPS [SOURCE: Page_7]

187   and AMPA are frequently quantified in water bodies (see paragraph: Glyphosate in surface water and transport [SOURCE: Page_7]

188   by runoff) because of the high application rates and frequency of usage of GPS and the large range of retention [SOURCE: Page_7]

189   coefficients. Effect of pH on glyphosate retention in soil [SOURCE: Page_7]

190   Usually, when the pH of the soil is high, the adsorption of most ionizable molecules decreases (Kah and Brown [SOURCE: Page_7]

191   2007; Barrett and McBride 2007; ). Indeed, an increase in pH resulted in a reduction in GPS soil retention [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

192   capacity (de Jonge 1999). When the pH decreases, soil adsorption becomes easier because certain minerals or [SOURCE: Page_8]

193   iron oxides facilitate the binding of negatively charged GPS (Morillo et al. 2000; Mamy et al. 2005; Gimsing and [SOURCE: Page_8]

194   Borggaard 2007; Tèvez and Afonso 2015). Most Mediterranean soils exhibit a high pH (pH~8), which is in [SOURCE: Page_8]

195   accordance with the influence of calcite dissolution equilibria (Torrent 2005). As mentioned previously, this [SOURCE: Page_8]

196   characteristic is prone to a lower soil adsorption coefficient, favoring runoff contamination. In particular, De [SOURCE: Page_8]

197   Gerónimo et al. (2022) studied GPS adsorption in the soils of Argentina and reported that it reached a maximum [SOURCE: Page_8]

198   at a pH equal to 6 and then decreased as the pH increased or decreased. Competition between GPS and [SOURCE: Page_8]

199   phosphate at adsorption sites may change retention, as the GPS Freundlich coefficient K f decreased to 65% with [SOURCE: Page_8]

200   the addition of 0.5 mM inorganic phosphate. [SOURCE: Page_8]

201   Effect of soil clay content on GPS retention [SOURCE: Page_8]

202   Sprankle et al. (1975) highlighted the ability of GPS to fix more on clay soils than on sandy soils. The adsorption [SOURCE: Page_8]

203   intensity depends on the clay content, cation exchange capacity (CEC), nature of the clay, and surface area, [SOURCE: Page_8]

204   ranging from bentonite (220 m^2 g-1) to montmorillonite (10 m^2 g-1) (Glass 1987; Hiera et al. 2007). The distances [SOURCE: Page_8]

205   between anionic and cationic clay sheets are correlated with GPS adsorption (Li et al. 2004; Damonte et al. [SOURCE: Page_8]

206   2007). Indeed, the adsorption constant is strongly correlated with the clay content and CEC because GPS is [SOURCE: Page_8]

207   bound to cations released on the surface of clay through a cation exchange reaction (Okada et al. 2016). Various [SOURCE: Page_8]

208   adsorption studies were performed using clay-based soils to determine the sorption affinity of GPS and AMPA [SOURCE: Page_8]

209   to kaolinite, Montmorillonite, and illite (e.g. Borggaard and Gimsing 2007, 2008). A previous study investigated [SOURCE: Page_8]

210   the effect of soil characteristics on GPS and AMPA persistence in soils and revealed that GPS was present at [SOURCE: Page_8]

211   significantly greater levels in soils with clay textures than in soils with loam and sandy loam textures. In [SOURCE: Page_8]

212   contrast, AMPA showed no significant difference between textures. Nevertheless, it was, on average, more [SOURCE: Page_8]

213   present in clay textures (91%) than in loam textures (67%) and sandy loam textures (50%) (Maccario et al. [SOURCE: Page_8]

214   2022). [SOURCE: Page_8]

215   Effect of exchangeable ions and soil solution ions [SOURCE: Page_8]

216   Several studies have described the influence of specific exchangeable ions, such as copper, aluminum, and [SOURCE: Page_8]

217   phosphate, on the adsorption of GPS. Morillo et al. (2000) showed that the presence of Cu$_2$+ ions in three [SOURCE: Page_8]

218   Spanish soils favor GPS adsorption and the formation of copper-GPS complexes. For phosphate, Gimsing et al. [SOURCE: Page_8]

219   (2004) demonstrated that when confronted with sorption sites occupied by phosphate, GPS is more easily [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

220   leached. Consequently, phosphates generated by chemical fertilizers interfere with GPS behavior. When [SOURCE: Page_9]

221   phosphate concentrations are high, GPS adsorption decreases (de Jonge et al. 2001). [SOURCE: Page_9]

222   Influence of organic matter [SOURCE: Page_9]

223   In the literature, the results concerning the impact of OM on GPS adsorption are contradictory, revealing [SOURCE: Page_9]

224   different causal mechanisms. Sprankle et al. (1975) explained the binding of GPS via its phosphonic (acid) group [SOURCE: Page_9]

225   to fulvic acid complexes by a metal, usually in the form of a trivalent cation (Fe 3+ or Al$_3$+). A second mechanism [SOURCE: Page_9]

226   was identified by Piccolo et al. (1994), where GPS was adsorbed to humic substances via hydrogen bonds, [SOURCE: Page_9]

227   resulting in fragile interactions sensitive to soil leaching. In contrast, Mamy and Barriuso (2005) reported no [SOURCE: Page_9]

228   correlation between soil OM content and GPS adsorption but highlighted high variability in Koc values (1152– [SOURCE: Page_9]

229   44630 L kg-1). A more recent study (Ololade et al. 2014) was unable to clarify the effect of OM on GPS [SOURCE: Page_9]

230   absorption. OM may have an indirect effect by hiding GPS adsorption sites on amorphous oxides, even if GPS [SOURCE: Page_9]

231   appears to be adsorbed on humic compounds in soil (Albers et al. 2009). Ghafoor et al. (2013) recommended [SOURCE: Page_9]

232   researching on soils with low levels of OM (<2%) to distinguish between the effects of the organic and mineral [SOURCE: Page_9]

233   phases. Sidoli et al. (2016) followed this recommendation and concluded that strong adsorption occurred on [SOURCE: Page_9]

234   mineral components and was unrelated to low OM contents. Data on the behavior of AMPA, the main metabolite [SOURCE: Page_9]

235   of GPS, are still limited or lacking (Báez et al. 2015). AMPA was found in soils at the same concentration levels [SOURCE: Page_9]

236   as glyphosate (GPS), and both were abundant in suspended particulate matter and sediment (Aparicio et al. [SOURCE: Page_9]

237   2013). [SOURCE: Page_9]

252   Biodegradation of glyphosate [SOURCE: Page_10]

253   Microbial degradation is considered the most important pathway for GPS breakdown (Mercurio et al. 2014; [SOURCE: Page_10]

254   Wang et al. 2016; Al-Rajab 2007). GPS can be degraded by bacteria or fungi (Sprankle et al. 1975; Bronstad and [SOURCE: Page_10]

255   Friestad 1985; Rueppel et al. 1977), while GPS degradation can occur under anaerobic conditions, higher [SOURCE: Page_10]

256   degradation rates occur in aerobic environments (Kah et al. 2007). Two major pathways that cause GPS [SOURCE: Page_10]

257   degradation have been identified under water-sediment conditions (Wang et al. 2016) and in soil (Muskus et al. [SOURCE: Page_10]

258   2019). [SOURCE: Page_10]

259   The first degradation pathway results in the production of AMPA (with C-N bond cleavage) (Fig. 1) and [SOURCE: Page_10]

260   glyoxylic acid by GPS oxidoreductase (Levering et al. 1984). Glyoxylate is included in the tricarboxylic acid [SOURCE: Page_10]

261   cycle and produces energy for bacteria (Sviridov et al. 2015). The first bacterial strain involved in this pathway [SOURCE: Page_10]

262   (from the Flavobacterium genus) was discovered by Balthazor and Hallas (1986). AMPA is mineralized via [SOURCE: Page_10]

263   phosphonate hydrolysis to generate phosphate and ammonium ions that can be consumed by bacteria. Jacob et [SOURCE: Page_10]

264   al. (1988) isolated another bacterial strain (Pseudomonas sp. strain LBr) that can transform up to 20 mM GPS [SOURCE: Page_10]

265   into AMPA. The presence of phosphate does not inhibit the degradation of GPS into AMPA (Kishore and Jacob [SOURCE: Page_10]

266   1987). [SOURCE: Page_10]

267   The second degradation pathway is the mineralization of GPS to sarcosine (CH NHCH COOH ) and^3      2       2 [SOURCE: Page_10]

268   orthophosphate through C-P bond cleavage induced by C-P lyase (Hove-Jensen et al. 2014; Firdous et al. 2017; [SOURCE: Page_10]

269   Kishore and Jacob 1987). Two strains of bacteria are involved (Kishore and Jacob 1987; Pipke et al. 1987). [SOURCE: Page_10]

270   These bacteria use GPS as a phosphorus source. Indeed, the addition of phosphate-based fertilizers to soils may [SOURCE: Page_10]

271   inhibit the degradation of GPS by these strains since bacterial species are a direct source of phosphate, in [SOURCE: Page_10]

272   contrast to what is observed in the first degradation pathway. Other bacterial species can metabolize phosphate [SOURCE: Page_10]

273   (Sviridov et al. 2012; Feng et al. 2020), and sarcosine may be converted to formaldehyde and glycine and used [SOURCE: Page_10]

274   as a growth nutrient (a carbon and nitrogen source) for microorganisms (Durand 2007). [SOURCE: Page_10]
