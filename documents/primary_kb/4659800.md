Abstract [SOURCE: Page_7]

Permanent ponds are valuable freshwater systems and biodiversity hotspots. They [SOURCE: Page_7]

provide diverse ecosystem services (ES), including water quality improvement and [SOURCE: Page_7]

supply, food provisioning and biodiversity support. This is despite being under [SOURCE: Page_7]

significant pressure from multiple anthropogenic stressors and the impacts of ongoing [SOURCE: Page_7]

global change. However, ponds are largely overlooked in management plans and [SOURCE: Page_7]

legislation, and ecological research has focused on large freshwater ecosystems, such as [SOURCE: Page_7]

rivers or lakes. Protection of ponds is often insufficient or indirectly provided via [SOURCE: Page_7]

associated habitats such as wetlands. This phenomenon is likely exacerbated due to [SOURCE: Page_7]

lacking a full-scale understanding of the importance of ponds. In this review, we [SOURCE: Page_7]

provided a detailed overview of permanent ponds across Europe, including their usages [SOURCE: Page_7]

and the biodiversity they support. By discussing the concepts of pondscape and [SOURCE: Page_7]

metacommunity theory, we highlighted the importance of connectivity among and [SOURCE: Page_7]

between ponds and identified fluxes of emerging insects as another ES of ponds. Those [SOURCE: Page_7]

insects are rich in essential nutrients such as polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), which [SOURCE: Page_7]

are delivered through them to the terrestrial environment, however the extent and [SOURCE: Page_7]

impact of this ES remains largely unexplored. Several potential stressors, especially [SOURCE: Page_7]

related to ongoing global change, which influence pond diversity and integrity were [SOURCE: Page_7]

discussed. To conclude this review, we provided our insights on future pond [SOURCE: Page_7]

management. Adaptive measures, taking into account the pond system per se within the [SOURCE: Page_7]

pondscape, were found to be the most promising to mitigate the loss of natural ponds [SOURCE: Page_7]

and restore and conserve natural small water bodies as refuges and diversity hotspots in [SOURCE: Page_7]

increasingly urbanized landscapes. [SOURCE: Page_7]

### 1. Introduction

Ponds are found across multiple landscape types, often occurring in high numbers [SOURCE: Page_8]

which surpass those of lakes or rivers. Despite this, they have been historically [SOURCE: Page_8]

overlooked by research focusing on larger aquatic ecosystems. Since ponds share many [SOURCE: Page_8]

characteristics with shallow lakes, wetlands, or more region-specific ecosystems (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_8]

vernal pools, billabongs), their structural distinction is often unclear. However, certain [SOURCE: Page_8]

features can help to distinguish ponds from other standing water bodies (Søndergaard et [SOURCE: Page_8]

al. 2005; Richardson et al. 2022). There is a long history of debate around the definition [SOURCE: Page_8]

of ponds which continues to the present (Richardson et al. 2022). Ponds were frequently [SOURCE: Page_8]

defined as small and shallow, natural or artificial standing water bodies, which can [SOURCE: Page_8]

contain water permanently or fall dry occasionally (Biggs et al. 2005; De Meester et al. [SOURCE: Page_8]

2005). Some much larger water bodies were also sometimes considered as ponds, for [SOURCE: Page_8]

instance, many man-made and shallow fish ponds in some European countries (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_8]

Austria or the Czech Republic) cover many hectares (Lhotský 2010). These should, [SOURCE: Page_8]

according to a recent publication, not be referred to as ponds but as shallow lakes [SOURCE: Page_8]

(Richardson et al. 2022). A maximum depth of 5 meters and a surface area of no more [SOURCE: Page_8]

than 5 hectares were proposed to classify a pond. Characteristics that were also included [SOURCE: Page_8]

within this study are the aphotic zone, which is typically absent in ponds unless they are [SOURCE: Page_8]

very turbid, and macrophytes, which can grow throughout the entire water column [SOURCE: Page_8]

(Biggs et al. 2005) should cover no more than 30% of the water body (Richardson et al. [SOURCE: Page_8]

2022). [SOURCE: Page_8]

Ponds are not isolated systems. They are strongly linked to other aquatic systems and [SOURCE: Page_8]

their surrounding terrestrial environment. One of the most important linkages between [SOURCE: Page_8]

ponds and their terrestrial surroundings is the export of dietary energy from ponds to the [SOURCE: Page_9]

associated terrestrial environment via emerging insects. Within this review, we [SOURCE: Page_9]

incorporate both the historical background of ponds in the landscape and novel insights [SOURCE: Page_9]

on their contributions to export of energy and essential biomolecules, investigating: the [SOURCE: Page_9]

publication bias despite the abundance and ecological importance of ponds (Section 2); [SOURCE: Page_9]

the importance of pond biodiversity in relation to pond connectivity within the [SOURCE: Page_9]

pondscape (Section 3); the influence of stressors on the local and regional scale of [SOURCE: Page_9]

ponds in the pondscape (Section 4); the effect of emerging insects on the terrestrial [SOURCE: Page_9]

environment and the role of this ES (Section 5); and perspectives and future challenges [SOURCE: Page_9]

especially regarding ongoing global change (Section 6). [SOURCE: Page_9]

### 2. Ponds - ubiquitous but often overlooked systems

Present in all biogeographical regions, ponds are heterogeneously distributed in the [SOURCE: Page_9]

landscape, with densities that decrease from arctic and boreal regions towards the [SOURCE: Page_9]

tropics (Verpoorter et al. 2014). A global assessment suggested that the number of [SOURCE: Page_9]

natural ponds with a surface area of 0.1-1 ha exceeds 277 million and a total area of [SOURCE: Page_9]

692,600 km^2 is covered, representing 91% of standing freshwater bodies worldwide and [SOURCE: Page_9]

16% of their surface area (Downing et al. 2006). In Europe, estimates are lacking for [SOURCE: Page_9]

most countries. However, there are some data for ponds between 0.01 ha and 5 ha in [SOURCE: Page_9]

size in Switzerland (32,000 ponds; Oertli et al. 2005), Denmark (120,000 ponds; [SOURCE: Page_9]

Søndergaard et al. 2005) and Great Britain (size: 0.0025 ha - 5 ha; 400,000 ponds; [SOURCE: Page_9]

Biggs et al. 2005). For Austria (>25,000 standing waters larger than 0.025 ha; BMLRT [SOURCE: Page_9]

2021) and Ireland (12,206 freshwater lakes, mostly smaller than 1 ha; Kristensen and [SOURCE: Page_9]

Globevnik 2014), studies on small water body abundances have not exclusively focused [SOURCE: Page_9]

on ponds. The reliability of these high estimates has been questioned (Biggs et al. [SOURCE: Page_9]

2017), considering changes in the number of small water bodies over time (Pekel et al. [SOURCE: Page_9]

2016). [SOURCE: Page_10]

Despite their wide distribution, high abundance, and distinctiveness, smaller-scale [SOURCE: Page_10]

ecosystems, such as ponds, brooks or springs are underrepresented in the scientific [SOURCE: Page_10]

literature (Oertli et al. 2002; De Meester et al. 2005; Cantonati et al. 2021) and excluded [SOURCE: Page_10]

from management plans (Biggs et al. 2017). A literature search for “ponds” returns [SOURCE: Page_10]

three times fewer results than “lakes” and six times fewer results than “rivers/streams” [SOURCE: Page_10]

(Figure 1). A potential confounding factor might be the inclusion of “ponds” in other [SOURCE: Page_10]

classifications, due to the aforementioned overlaps in definitions, thus leading to [SOURCE: Page_10]

insufficient clarity regarding the contents of the research. This is problematic since [SOURCE: Page_10]

research on ponds is hindered if varying definitions complicate literature searches for [SOURCE: Page_10]

previous studies. A straightforward approach during the development of protection [SOURCE: Page_10]

policies would certainly also benefit from a common definition, which very recently has [SOURCE: Page_10]

been proposed in the aforementioned Richardson et al. (2022) publication. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Lacking a full-scale understanding of the ecological value of ponds might be one of the [SOURCE: Page_10]

reasons for the continuous degradation and decline of ponds in anthropogenic [SOURCE: Page_10]

landscapes (Oertli 2018). Recent studies on pond management and protection have [SOURCE: Page_10]

improved their conservation status in some European countries (Oertli et al. 2005; Boix [SOURCE: Page_10]

et al. 2012; Labat et al. 2022). Awareness of the ecological value of ponds has greatly [SOURCE: Page_10]

increased in recent years (Oertli et al. 2009; Oertli and Parris 2019), as pointed out by [SOURCE: Page_10]

many studies on different pond types, such as urban ponds (Hassall 2014; Hill et al. [SOURCE: Page_10]

2017), farm ponds (Lewis-Phillips et al. 2020) or even garden ponds (Hill et al. 2015). [SOURCE: Page_10]

However, ponds remain excluded from key freshwater management strategies, both at [SOURCE: Page_10]

national and continental levels. For example, the European Water Framework Directive [SOURCE: Page_10]

(WFD; Council of the European Communities 2000) only considers standing water [SOURCE: Page_10]

bodies larger than 50 ha, whereas the European Habitats Directive (Council of the [SOURCE: Page_10]

European Communities 1992) only defines two types of ponds that should be protected, [SOURCE: Page_11]

namely “Mediterranean temporary ponds” and Irish “Turloughs” (Hill et al. 2018; Oertli [SOURCE: Page_11]

2018). In some cases, ponds are indirectly protected by being included in association [SOURCE: Page_11]

with wetlands under the Ramsar convention (Kingsford et al. 2021), which leads to [SOURCE: Page_11]

enhanced protection despite not being properly named in conservation plans. We argue [SOURCE: Page_11]

that the WFD and Habitats Directive should be adapted to include all permanent and [SOURCE: Page_11]

temporary ponds rather than relying on indirect protection from associated habitats. [SOURCE: Page_11]

Thus, their ecological importance in the rapidly changing landscapes of Europe would [SOURCE: Page_11]

be better acknowledged, and monitoring of their status in response to ongoing global [SOURCE: Page_11]

change improved. [SOURCE: Page_11]

### 3. Intrinsic biodiversity of ponds and "the pondscape"

Despite their small size in comparison to other water bodies, ponds constitute [SOURCE: Page_11]

biodiversity hotspots, both in terms of community composition and functional traits, [SOURCE: Page_11]

and are vital for a wide range of rare and protected species (e.g. Oertli et al. 2002; [SOURCE: Page_11]

Williams et al. 2004; EPCN 2008; Céréghino et al. 2014). These small but valuable [SOURCE: Page_11]

ecosystems host many critically endangered taxa, including emblematic "flagship [SOURCE: Page_11]

groups" of wetland biodiversity, such as amphibians, dragonflies and aquatic plants [SOURCE: Page_11]

(Sahlén et al. 2004; Semlitsch et al. 2015; Law et al. 2019). The high biodiversity [SOURCE: Page_11]

generally associated with ponds is related to their particular structural and biotic [SOURCE: Page_11]

features, such as rich ecotone zones and complex interactions with the surrounding [SOURCE: Page_11]

terrestrial environments (Céréghino et al. 2014; Bolpagni et al. 2019). A large part of [SOURCE: Page_11]

these cross-ecosystem interactions can be attributed to species which require both [SOURCE: Page_11]

environments to complete their life-cycle, such as emerging aquatic insects (Schriever [SOURCE: Page_11]

et al. 2014; see Section 4). These act as a vector for dietary energy (as measured in [SOURCE: Page_11]

lipids), exporting essential molecules from the aquatic into the terrestrial environment [SOURCE: Page_11]

(see Mathieu-Resuge et al. 2021). [SOURCE: Page_12]

3.1 Characterization of "the pondscape" and its implications for biodiversity [SOURCE: Page_12]

Pond size is often highlighted as one of the main determinants of pond species diversity [SOURCE: Page_12]

(Oertli et al. 2002; Kadoya et al. 2004; Hill et al. 2015). Like other aquatic habitats, [SOURCE: Page_12]

ponds might also be subject to the species-area effect in this regard (Dodson et al. [SOURCE: Page_12]

2000). Likewise, pondscapes are mainly characterized by the size and density of ponds, [SOURCE: Page_12]

along with surrounding land use and pond connectivity (Boothby 1997; Fang 2011; [SOURCE: Page_12]

Jooste et al. 2020). However, the role of pond size in shaping biodiversity can be [SOURCE: Page_12]

difficult to assess as several other environmental (e.g., depth, hydroperiod, and [SOURCE: Page_12]

connectivity; Carchini et al. 2007; Hamer et al. 2012; Tornero et al. 2016) and biotic [SOURCE: Page_12]

factors (e.g., fish presence and predation, presence of macrophytes; Pearman 1995; [SOURCE: Page_12]

Semlitsch et al. 2015; Kolar and Boukal 2020) may also influence species richness and [SOURCE: Page_12]

the community composition of ponds within the pondscape. The biodiversity within [SOURCE: Page_12]

pondscapes increases with the diversity of environmental factors and landscape [SOURCE: Page_12]

heterogeneity by enhancing the number of ecological niches available to species and [SOURCE: Page_12]

metapopulations (Williams et al. 2004; Boix et al. 2012; Oertli and Parris 2019). A [SOURCE: Page_12]

diverse mosaic of ponds provides a good variety of characteristics at the landscape [SOURCE: Page_12]

scale, and is crucial to promote and maintain biodiversity at the regional level (Oertli et [SOURCE: Page_12]

al. 2002; Goertzen and Suhling 2013; Oertli and Parris 2019). In urban environments, [SOURCE: Page_12]

species richness is mainly affected by artificial barriers (e.g., roads, buildings), [SOURCE: Page_12]

especially in densely populated areas, and by the reduction of, and connection to, [SOURCE: Page_12]

natural areas (Oertli and Parris 2019). [SOURCE: Page_12]

Since metacommunities largely depend on the potential to move between [SOURCE: Page_12]

interconnected aquatic habitat patches, enhancing connectivity within the pondscape [SOURCE: Page_12]

will promote species dispersal and ultimately may increase its biodiversity (Oertli and [SOURCE: Page_13]

Parris 2019). The idea of pondscapes comes hand in hand with the metacommunity [SOURCE: Page_13]

concept. Metacommunity refers to a set of local communities connected through spatial [SOURCE: Page_13]

dispersal. The metacommunity concept incorporates the importance of local processes [SOURCE: Page_13]

and regional dispersal effects into four main mechanisms that act alone or in interaction [SOURCE: Page_13]

within a habitat: species sorting, mass effects, patch dynamics, and neutral processes [SOURCE: Page_13]

(Leibold et al. 2004). The need for pond connectivity poses unique management [SOURCE: Page_13]

challenges at the landscape scale: the aim should be an integration of small water bodies [SOURCE: Page_13]

into densely human-populated landscapes. [SOURCE: Page_13]

3.2 Dispersal abilities and life-histories: how life in the pond interconnects with the pondscape [SOURCE: Page_13]

Ponds are inhabited and used by a large set of organisms with widely different life [SOURCE: Page_13]

histories and dispersal abilities that define their interactions with the pondscape (Rundle [SOURCE: Page_13]

et al. 2002; Massol et al. 2017; Sarremejane et al. 2020). The ability of organisms to [SOURCE: Page_13]

disperse is crucial for finding new refuges or more suitable conditions within the [SOURCE: Page_13]

pondscape when facing challenges that threaten their individual fitness (e.g., heightened [SOURCE: Page_13]

predation pressure, habitat instability, or harsh competition; Baguette et al. 2013). [SOURCE: Page_13]

Dispersal can be passive (i.e., the organism relies on a vector to disperse) or active (i.e., [SOURCE: Page_13]

the organism can move and disperse by itself) (Bilton et al. 2001; De Bie et al. 2012). [SOURCE: Page_13]

Dispersal strategies can change dramatically within the life cycle. Emerging insects are [SOURCE: Page_13]

actively dispersing organisms in their adult stage (Bilton et al. 2001). How frequently [SOURCE: Page_13]

and how far organisms move within the pondscape depends mainly on individual [SOURCE: Page_13]

(physiological) needs, their sensory organs and environmental conditions. Certain [SOURCE: Page_13]

dragonfly individuals (Odonata) have been found to cover large distances from their [SOURCE: Page_13]

natal ponds (Conrad et al. 1999), with dragonflies generally being known as rather [SOURCE: Page_13]

strong flyers. For instance, as an exceptional case, the globe skimmer (Pantala [SOURCE: Page_14]

flavescens) takes advantage of the wind to cross the Indian Ocean (Hobson et al. 2012; [SOURCE: Page_14]

Bomphrey et al. 2016). Around 2 km dispersal distances have been recorded for some [SOURCE: Page_14]

caddisfly (Trichoptera) species, depending on body size, with larger species dispersing [SOURCE: Page_14]

further (Kovats et al. 1996). Particularly for rare species with low dispersal abilities [SOURCE: Page_14]

such as certain freshwater beetles (Iversen et al. 2017), connectivity is a major driver [SOURCE: Page_14]

ensuring the functioning of the pondscape at metacommunity level (Chase et al. 2020). [SOURCE: Page_14]

3.3 Local and regional diversity [SOURCE: Page_14]

With high variability in their physicochemical characteristics and an intrinsically [SOURCE: Page_14]

changing spatiotemporal distribution within the terrestrial landscape, ponds are good [SOURCE: Page_14]

examples of systems hosting metacommunities which are connected, in part, via food [SOURCE: Page_14]

webs (Wilbur 1997; De Meester et al. 2005). Spread across a wide range of geographic [SOURCE: Page_14]

and environmental gradients with generally small catchment areas, ponds typically [SOURCE: Page_14]

reflect local variation in geology, hydrology, climate and vegetation and can harbor a [SOURCE: Page_14]

proportionally higher species richness than larger catchments of lakes and rivers, where [SOURCE: Page_14]

environmental conditions are more uniform (Biggs et al. 2005; Davies et al. 2008). [SOURCE: Page_14]

Previous studies from Great Britain indicated that when comparing the biodiversity of [SOURCE: Page_14]

### 2. Future research (field studies, mesocosm experiments and modeling) should

habitats. Pond attributes are strongly correlated to the landscape features where [SOURCE: Page_27]

farm ponds, small reservoirs, stormwater ponds, gravel pit ponds and fish ponds) [SOURCE: Page_27]

### 3. To achieve these goals, we propose to develop specific recommendations and

interventions for different ponds based on their type (e.g., permanent or [SOURCE: Page_28]

temporary), successional stages and their connectivity to other aquatic [SOURCE: Page_28]

conservation. This pondscape-based approach, which preserves heterogeneity [SOURCE: Page_28]

Water quality and supply: improvement of runoff quality (Krivtsov et al. 2022), removal of sediments and heavy metals via sediments and macrophytes (Fiener et al. 2005), carbon sequestration in sediments and plants (Moore and Hunt 2012), water reserves for livestock, irrigation and fire protection (Oertli 2018); 8. Biodiversity: enhance landscape biodiversity (γ-diversity), essential for migration, dispersal and genetic exchange among wild species (Oertli and Parris 2019), biodiversity hotspot especially in anthropogenic areas (Hill et al. 2017), refuges for rare and endemic species, support of semi-aquatic and terrestrial flora and fauna (Usio et al. 2017); 9.
