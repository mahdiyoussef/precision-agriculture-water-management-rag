Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

Agricultural ditches are frequently included in the panel of landscape elements to be managed to [SOURCE: Page_2]

minimize the negative impacts of agriculture on the environment, particularly water contamination. [SOURCE: Page_2]

A new mechanistic model simulating pesticide transfer in ditch networks during flood events was [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   developed for help in designing ditch management. The model considers pesticide sorption [SOURCE: Page_2]

like ditch networks, with a reach resolution. The model was evaluated with pulse tracer experiments [SOURCE: Page_2]

conducted on two vegetated and litter-rich ditches and with two contrasting pesticides, namely, [SOURCE: Page_2]

diuron and diflufenican. It appears necessary to consider exchange of only a small proportion of the [SOURCE: Page_2]

intermediate between the theoretical transport distance by diffusion and the thicknesses usually [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   considered in mixing models for pesticide remobilization by field runoff. The numerical exploration [SOURCE: Page_2]

1- Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

Agricultural ditches are present in many agricultural landscapes and are frequently included in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

panel of elements to be managed to minimize the negative impacts of agriculture on the [SOURCE: Page_3]

environment, including water contamination and biodiversity erosion (e.g., Dollinger et al., 2015, [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   Herzon and Helenius, 2008). However, few studies and tools are currently available for developing [SOURCE: Page_3]

ditch management strategies. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Farmed ditch networks are human-made infrastructures originally designed to control the [SOURCE: Page_3]

waterlogging of arable lands and prevent soil erosion by collecting and routing excess shallow [SOURCE: Page_3]

groundwater or surface overland ﬂow (Dollinger et al., 2015; Levavasseur et al., 2012). Depending [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   on the depth and fluctuation levels of the water table, the flows in the ditches can be intermittent. [SOURCE: Page_3]

This is typically the case in arid and semiarid contexts where shallow permanent water tables are [SOURCE: Page_3]

rare and flow in the ditches is often limited to short periods of heavy rainfall that generate overland [SOURCE: Page_3]

flows on upstream hillslopes. Infiltration losses in the network can vary greatly between flow events [SOURCE: Page_3]

and along a ditch network according to spatial and temporal fluctuations in water table depths, soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   water content and soil hydraulic conductivity (e.g., Marofi, 1999). These can be very substantial [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

and accentuate the intermittence of the flows (Hughes and Sami, 1992; Dagès et al., 2009). In [SOURCE: Page_4]

cropped landscapes where overland flow waters collected by ditches may be loaded with pesticides [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Louchart et al., 2001; Tang et al., 2012; Voltz and Louchart, 2001), ditch networks can represent a [SOURCE: Page_4]

major pathway of rapid pesticide transfer towards surface water or groundwater bodies or both. [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   Moreover, ditches are also reputed to attenuate water contamination due to their buffering capacities [SOURCE: Page_4]

(e.g., Dousset et al., 2010, Dollinger et al., 2015, 2016, Dages et al., 2015). Otto et al. (2016) [SOURCE: Page_4]

suggested that ditches are the best buffer zones to immediately and locally limit pesticide dispersion [SOURCE: Page_4]

from field runoff. As reviewed by Dollinger et al. (2015), the main processes involved in the [SOURCE: Page_4]

buffering capacity of ditches include adsorption, degradation, and leaching for infiltrating ditches. [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   Several studies have aimed to elucidate the factors controlling pesticide retention in ditches, mainly [SOURCE: Page_4]

for near-permanent flow conditions, by statistical analysis of experimental data (Bundschuh et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

2016), use of simplified modelling based on fugacity models (Otto et al., 2016, Dollinger et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

2016) and mechanistic modelling (Adriaanse et al., 2022). The presence of vegetation and litter in [SOURCE: Page_4]

ditches was often suggested to improve pesticide retention by increasing both the hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   retention time and the sorption capacity of ditches (e.g., Cooper et al., 2004, Dollinger et al, 2015, [SOURCE: Page_4]

Bundschuh et al., 2016, Otto et al., 2016). However, these case studies were specific, and the [SOURCE: Page_4]

diversity and range of the tested factors do not allow a generic conclusion about the key factors [SOURCE: Page_4]

explaining the variability in pesticide retention. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Ditches exhibit great spatial and temporal diversity in their properties and characteristics (geometry, [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   vegetation and litter type and abundance, etc.). Therefore, retention capacity is expected to vary [SOURCE: Page_4]

along a network. Considering the spatial and temporal variability in pesticide loads in ditches [SOURCE: Page_4]

induced by agricultural practices, the overall mitigation provided by a ditch network is also [SOURCE: Page_4]

expected to be highly variable. Thus, management strategies to optimize pesticide mitigation need [SOURCE: Page_4]

to be planned at the network scale and relative to the season (Dollinger et al., 2015; Herzon and [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   Helenius, 2008). The design of management strategies is still hampered by the lack of knowledge [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

about the hierarchy of mechanisms and factors explaining pesticide mitigation capacity (Bundschuh [SOURCE: Page_5]

et al., 2016). This could be addressed with mechanistic modelling, but models must properly [SOURCE: Page_5]

simulate the transient flows that prevail during floods and the associated pesticide transport. They [SOURCE: Page_5]

must also consider the main processes involved in pesticide retention and the properties or factors [SOURCE: Page_5]

80    controlling them. Finally, to assess ecological risk, the models must be able to evaluate both mean [SOURCE: Page_5]

and maximal pesticide concentrations (Bundschuh et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_5]

There are several modelling approaches to simulate pesticide transfer in ditches, whether they are [SOURCE: Page_5]

integrated within catchment hydrological modelling or not. They all assume a well-mixed layer of [SOURCE: Page_5]

water overlying a layer of sediment, inducing a homogenous concentration of the water column. [SOURCE: Page_5]

85    They differ mainly in the following three aspects. [SOURCE: Page_5]

First, they have adopted contrasting types of formalisms to calculate the exchange of pesticides [SOURCE: Page_5]

between the water column and the soil in a ditch. The most widespread formalism represents [SOURCE: Page_5]

exchanges according to a diffusion flow and is implemented in ditch-scale models suitable for [SOURCE: Page_5]

surface flows with low velocities and without transmission losses (no infiltration) (e.g., TOXSWA, [SOURCE: Page_5]

90    Adriaanse, 1996) and in watershed hydrological models for higher flow in larger hydrographic [SOURCE: Page_5]

networks, such as rivers (e.g., SWAT (Neitsch et al., 2011), Mike-She (DHI, 2020)). Another [SOURCE: Page_5]

formalism, similar to fugacity approaches used to calculate retention indicators (e.g., Dollinger et [SOURCE: Page_5]

al., 2016, Otto et al., 2016), was adopted in some models, such as SPIDER (Renaud et al., 2008) or [SOURCE: Page_5]

PESHMELBA (Rouzies et al., 2019). It is based on the concept of a mixing zone, as already used in [SOURCE: Page_5]

95    many models, such as SWAT or PRZM (Young and Fry, 2014), to simulate pesticide washoff at the [SOURCE: Page_5]

field scale by surface runoff. Finally, an empirical relationship developed from a database of [SOURCE: Page_5]

transfer in grass strips is used, to our knowledge, only in VSFMOD (Muñoz-Carpena et al., 2010, [SOURCE: Page_5]

Philips et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Second, models differ in the consideration of the diversity of materials likely to interact with [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   pesticides. Most models only consider the possibility of sorption onto sediments, whether they are [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

deposited as a layer below the water column or remain in suspension. To our knowledge, only [SOURCE: Page_6]

TOXSWA and VSFMOD consider sorption onto other materials, with explicit consideration of [SOURCE: Page_6]

sorption onto macrophytes in TOXSWA and implicit and undifferentiated consideration of material [SOURCE: Page_6]

contribution by fitting an overall sorption coefficient for the entire ditch in VSFMOD (Phillips et [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_6]

Third, models differ in their ability to simulate transient flow along a heterogeneous tree-like ditch [SOURCE: Page_6]

network. Several models simulate pesticide transfer at the network scale. Hydrological models, such [SOURCE: Page_6]

as SPIDER, Mike-She, and SWAT, can simulate rapid and transient flows that occur during flood [SOURCE: Page_6]

events. However, they do not account for ditch diversity, which is considered a shortcoming by [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   Wang et al. (2019) in their review of pesticide fate modelling using SWAT. Thus, these models can [SOURCE: Page_6]

only simulate the overall buffering effect of ditches without considering its spatial modulation and [SOURCE: Page_6]

can hardly be used to analyse the effect of temporal and spatial arrangement scenarios of [SOURCE: Page_6]

management operations likely to control the retention capacity of ditches. TOXSWA can consider [SOURCE: Page_6]

nonconfluent and linear networks with a weir at their outlet (Adriaanse and Beltman, 2009). Finally, [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   no model can simulate the transfer of pesticides in infiltrating and heterogeneous ditch networks [SOURCE: Page_6]

during flood events with the above detailed specifications. Such situations are often observed in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

Mediterranean environment (see, for example, Moussa et al., 2002, Dagès et al., 2009) and lead to [SOURCE: Page_6]

water contamination by pesticides (e.g., Louchart et al., 2001). [SOURCE: Page_6]

In this paper, we present a new model, PITCH (Pesticides in dITCH networks), that fills this gap. [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   The aims of this study were to i) develop a pesticide transfer model adapted to infiltrating floods [SOURCE: Page_6]

and heterogeneous tree-like ditch networks and ii) demonstrate its usefulness for management [SOURCE: Page_6]

design purposes. The ditches in the network are described according to topological (position in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

network), geometrical (e.g., width, slope) and compositional (e.g., soil type, living vegetation and [SOURCE: Page_6]

litter composition) characteristics. The paper describes first the concepts of the model, then [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   evaluates the model against reactive transfer experiments in ditches, and analyses the sensitivity of [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

its outputs to pesticide and ditch properties and finally discusses its ability to simulate the effect of [SOURCE: Page_7]

ditch management on the mitigation of pesticide pollution. [SOURCE: Page_7]

2- PITCH model principles [SOURCE: Page_7]

2.1- Main concepts [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   PITCH was developed to simulate pesticide transport and fate in ditch networks during flood [SOURCE: Page_7]

events. Its formalism was kept simple and robust enough to allow easy integration into a [SOURCE: Page_7]

hydrological model. Only the most relevant processes at the time scale of a flood event, namely, [SOURCE: Page_7]

from a few hours to a few days, were included in the model. Thus, flood water routing and [SOURCE: Page_7]

infiltration into ditches and sorption and transport of pesticides are considered, whereas [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   volatilization and degradation are neglected given the small atmosphere‒water interface and the [SOURCE: Page_7]

usual short duration of flow events. Sorption is assumed to operate on soil but also on living [SOURCE: Page_7]

vegetation and on most of the solid materials frequently laying over the ditch bed, such as litter [SOURCE: Page_7]

materials. [SOURCE: Page_7]

To represent exchanges between pesticides in flowing water and in ditch material, three layers are [SOURCE: Page_8]

155   distinguished within the water column (see Figure 1b) with i) a layer interacting with the bank and [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   distributed within the layer and to be at equilibrium with the compounds adsorbed on the materials [SOURCE: Page_8]

in contact with this zone. The pesticide concentration of the water infiltrating the ditch bed and [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   namely, soil and litter in the wetted zone and submerged vegetation in the vegetation zone. [SOURCE: Page_9]

380   The evaluation of PITCH was conducted to mimic as much as possible the application of PITCH in [SOURCE: Page_19]

5.1 Ability of PITCH to reproduce pesticide transfer within ditches. [SOURCE: Page_33]

The ability of PITCH to simulate pesticide transfer in ditches during flood events is evidenced by [SOURCE: Page_33]

### 5.3. Effect of ditch management operations on pesticide mitigation

790   Despite the above-cited restriction, our simulations show that two types of management levers may [SOURCE: Page_38]

improve the mitigation capacity for most compounds. The first type of lever concerns the [SOURCE: Page_38]

composition of the ditches and the management practices that regulate this composition. Promoting [SOURCE: Page_38]

the emergence of vegetated ditches and controlling the dynamics of vegetation may indeed be [SOURCE: Page_38]

favourable to surface removal. Indeed, vegetation destruction practices, such as mowing or burning, [SOURCE: Page_38]

795   increase the mass of litter and in turn improve surface removal, at least for hydrophobic [SOURCE: Page_38]

compounds, as already discussed by Dollinger et al. (2016, 2017). Moreover, another positive effect [SOURCE: Page_38]

of revegetation would be the increase in soil infiltrability due to the creation of macroposity in [SOURCE: Page_38]

connection with root development (e.g., Gould et al. 2016) or with more abundant soil mesofauna in [SOURCE: Page_38]

vegetated soils (Herzon and Helenius, 2008). However, increasing infiltrability needs to be carefully [SOURCE: Page_38]

800   considered according to environmental conditions since it is beneficial in limiting the contamination [SOURCE: Page_38]

of surface water bodies, but in turn it increases the risk of groundwater contamination. A second [SOURCE: Page_38]

type of lever concerns the geometry of the ditch, in particular its width, and should be implemented [SOURCE: Page_38]

when the network is created or dredged. The contact surface with the soil, for a given water volume [SOURCE: Page_38]

but also potentially the vegetative biomass, increases with width. However, these two levers must [SOURCE: Page_38]

805   be evaluated according to the agricultural context. When they are at the edge of a plot, ditches [SOURCE: Page_38]

overlap with the cultivated area, and farmers try to minimize their footprint. Increasing ditch width [SOURCE: Page_38]

may therefore not be acceptable by farmers. In the same way, increasing the vegetation density in [SOURCE: Page_38]

all or part of a ditch network may be seen, in some contexts like in Mediterranean areas, as contrary [SOURCE: Page_38]

to the primary purpose of the ditches, which were built to evacuate floods. Indeed, the increase in [SOURCE: Page_38]

810   roughness due to vegetation increases the risk of overflowing (Dollinger et al., 2017). The [SOURCE: Page_38]

37 [SOURCE: Page_38]

integration of PITCH in a hydrological model would allow to evaluate these controls in terms of [SOURCE: Page_39]

both contamination mitigation and flood prevention. [SOURCE: Page_39]

6- Concluding remarks [SOURCE: Page_39]

815   The purpose of this study was to present and evaluate PITCH, a new mechanistic model simulating [SOURCE: Page_39]

pesticide transfer in ditch networks at the flood event time scale. Compared to other models, it is [SOURCE: Page_39]

adapted to infiltrating ditches and rapid and transient flows and simulates transfers at the tree-like [SOURCE: Page_39]

network scale with a reach resolution. PITCH was validated with experimental chemograms of two [SOURCE: Page_39]

compounds with contrasting properties, diuron and diflufenican, and for vegetated ditches with [SOURCE: Page_39]

820   litter. None of the parameterizations corresponding to an exchange of the entire water column with [SOURCE: Page_39]

the ditch materials yields a good reproduction of the chemogram. The numerical exploration of [SOURCE: Page_39]

PITCH demonstrates both its consistent behaviour in predicting pesticide mitigation and its ability [SOURCE: Page_39]

for deployment to evaluate ditch management modalities. [SOURCE: Page_39]

The pesticide mitigation potential of ditches is highly variable, ranging from null to extremely high. [SOURCE: Page_39]

825   Favouring wide vegetated ditches and adopting a vegetation control method that enriches the ditch [SOURCE: Page_39]

bottom with litter and favours infiltration would improve the mitigation of pesticide pollution, at [SOURCE: Page_39]

least for hydrophobic compounds. [SOURCE: Page_39]

PITCH was designed for easy completion. The next steps will be an extension from a flood event [SOURCE: Page_39]

model to a continuous model and its integration in a watershed hydrological model. This step [SOURCE: Page_39]

830   requires integrating new processes, such as degradation. To extend its application domain to [SOURCE: Page_39]

semiarid contexts where flows are strongly intermittent, it is envisaged to integrate a potential [SOURCE: Page_39]

infiltration model that varies according to the initial humidity conditions. [SOURCE: Page_39]
