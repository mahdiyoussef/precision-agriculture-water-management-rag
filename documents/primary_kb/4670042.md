Production choices with water markets and risk aversion: The role of initial allocations and forward trading Philippe Bontems∗               Céline Nauges† [SOURCE: Page_1]

July 12, 2018 [SOURCE: Page_1]

Abstract We develop a theoretical model that describes risk-averse farmers’ decisions when facing production risk due to uncertain weather conditions and when irrigation water can be traded on a market. We focus on the role of initial water allocations granted to irrigated farms at the start of the season. The presence of water markets makes the future water price uncertain and hence the value of initial water allocations uncertain. We analyse the properties of this background risk and study how initial water allocations impact farmers’ land allocation decisions between an irrigated crop and a non-irrigated crop, both characterised by random expected net returns. We then extend the model by permitting irrigation water to be traded ex-ante at a known price (forward market). Finally, we illustrate our main theoretical ﬁndings using simulations. We calibrate distributions of the random variables based on observed data from the Murray-Darling Basin in Australia where a water market has been in place for several decades. [SOURCE: Page_1]

1    Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

In regions where water is scarce and rainfall is erratic, irrigated farms face production risk due [SOURCE: Page_2]

to the uncertainty of future water availability. To help farmers manage this uncertainty, some [SOURCE: Page_2]

countries (e.g., Australia, Chile, the United States) have developed water trading schemes. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Water markets have been shown to increase overall welfare by permitting the reallocation of [SOURCE: Page_2]

water between low-value and high-value uses (e.g., Vaux and Howitt, 1984; Garrido, 1998) [SOURCE: Page_2]

and to improve eﬃciency in risk sharing (e.g., Lefebvre, Gangadharan and Thoyer, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_2]

We develop a theoretical model to study the (short-term) production decisions of a rep- [SOURCE: Page_2]

resentative, risk-averse, farmer who is using irrigation water as an input in production and [SOURCE: Page_2]

has the possibility to trade water allocations on a competitive market. Studies modeling [SOURCE: Page_2]

farmers’ behaviour in the presence of water institutions such as water markets are still rare [SOURCE: Page_2]

(Li, Xu and Rosegrant, 2017). We are primarily interested in understanding how the level [SOURCE: Page_2]

of initial water allocations granted to the farmer aﬀects his/her production decisions. Initial [SOURCE: Page_2]

allocations, which have been shown to impact market outcomes in the presence of uncertainty [SOURCE: Page_2]

and risk aversion (Baldursson and von der Fehr, 2004), are one important ingredient in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

design of quota systems which has not been studied in the case of water markets, as far as [SOURCE: Page_2]

we know. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Irrigated farms typically face production risk due to uncertainty of future weather and [SOURCE: Page_2]

hence of the availability of water over the growing season. When water allocations can be [SOURCE: Page_2]

traded on a market, the price of water depends on both demand for, and supply of, irrigation [SOURCE: Page_2]

water, and is thus volatile. Price volatility not only adds to the uncertainty of future water [SOURCE: Page_2]

availability but also makes the value of farmers’ water rights uncertain. More precisely, the [SOURCE: Page_2]

uncertain value of water allocations granted to farmers generates a background risk that [SOURCE: Page_2]

is going to impact their production choices if farmers are risk-averse (Eichner and Wagener, [SOURCE: Page_2]

2012). Our model accounts for these various sources of uncertainty and describes their impact [SOURCE: Page_2]

on the production decisions of a single and representative risk-averse farmer. The modelling [SOURCE: Page_2]

The main features of our model are the following: we consider a risk-averse farmer who [SOURCE: Page_3]

receives an initial allocation of water that can be physically used for the production of an [SOURCE: Page_3]

irrigated crop or sold on a competitive market. The price of water, which depends on supply [SOURCE: Page_3]

(uncertain) wealth and having the possibility of investing in two (possibly correlated) risky [SOURCE: Page_3]

initial allocations impacts farmers’ allocation of land between the irrigated crop and the non- [SOURCE: Page_3]

irrigated crop. Next, we extend EW’s analysis by studying the implementation of a forward [SOURCE: Page_3]

market for water allocations. The possibility of trading water forward at a certain price oﬀers [SOURCE: Page_3]

farmers a new option for managing the risk brought about by the uncertainty about the price [SOURCE: Page_3]

of water and hence can modify farmers’ land allocation decisions. [SOURCE: Page_3]

3     The farmer’s decision model 3.1    A portfolio problem [SOURCE: Page_7]

We consider a farmer who has the possibility of allocating his land of size l that we normalise to [SOURCE: Page_7]

unity, to either an irrigated crop or a non-irrigated crop. We denote the share of land devoted [SOURCE: Page_7]

to the irrigated production by x and the share assigned to the non-irrigated production by [SOURCE: Page_7]

(1 − x). Irrigation water can be bought or sold on a competitive market at price r. From an [SOURCE: Page_7]

ex-ante perspective, the water price r is random with mean r̄ because it is the outcome of [SOURCE: Page_7]

supply and demand for irrigation water. [SOURCE: Page_7]

The (per hectare) net beneﬁt of both the irrigated crop and the non-irrigated crop are [SOURCE: Page_7]

random and denoted R and ε respectively, with means R̄ and ε̄. Randomness in the return [SOURCE: Page_7]

of the irrigated crop is mainly driven by the uncertain water price r. Implicitly, the ex-post [SOURCE: Page_7]

net return R of the irrigated crop can be deﬁned as: R = maxz (pf (z, θ) − rz) x with z, the [SOURCE: Page_7]

quantity of water supplied through irrigation and sold on a competitive market at price r; [SOURCE: Page_7]

f , a production function that is assumed to be increasing and concave in z; θ, a random [SOURCE: Page_7]

production shock, and p, the crop price. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Randomness in the return of the non-irrigated crop might be driven by variability in [SOURCE: Page_7]

weather conditions through its impact on crop yield and/or by output price variability. Fi- [SOURCE: Page_7]

nally, we denote e^0 ≥ 0 as the initial level of water allocations which is made available to [SOURCE: Page_7]

the farmer at the beginning of the growing season. The value of this initial endowment is [SOURCE: Page_7]

unknown ex-ante due to the uncertainty of the water price. [SOURCE: Page_7]

The timing of farmers’ decisions is the following: at the start of the growing season, initial [SOURCE: Page_7]

water allocations e^0 granted to the farmers are announced and the farmers decide on the land [SOURCE: Page_7]

area that will be planted with the irrigated crop (x). At that time, the quantity of irrigation [SOURCE: Page_7]

water that will be available in the summer (when the irrigated crop is in greatest need of [SOURCE: Page_7]

water) and the price at which water will be sold, are not known with certainty. The decision [SOURCE: Page_7]

made in the spring about the share of land to be irrigated (x) can thus be seen as an ex-ante [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

decision, before the uncertainty is resolved. In the summer, weather conditions have been [SOURCE: Page_8]

observed and information on water availability is fully revealed, so farmers no longer face [SOURCE: Page_8]

any uncertainty. They have full information when deciding on the quantity of water used [SOURCE: Page_8]

to produce the irrigated crop and on the quantity of water traded on the market (ex-post [SOURCE: Page_8]

decisions). In the following we focus only on the farmer’s optimal (ex-ante) decision in terms [SOURCE: Page_8]

of land allocation (x). [SOURCE: Page_8]

The farmer’s ex-post (ﬁnal) wealth, which corresponds to the sum of the net returns of [SOURCE: Page_8]

the irrigated and non-irrigated productions, plus his/her initial (certain) wealth w^0 , and the [SOURCE: Page_8]

value of the initial water allocations e^0 , is written as follows: [SOURCE: Page_8]

w = w^0 + xR + (1 − x)ε + re^0 . [SOURCE: Page_8]

The decision problem of the farmer, i.e. how to allocate land between two risky assets [SOURCE: Page_8]

possibility of trading water on a market allows ex-post ﬂexibility for the producer but a [SOURCE: Page_8]

strictly positive initial endowment also generates a cost in terms of risk exposure through [SOURCE: Page_8]

the introduction of an (additive) background risk potentially correlated with the two risky [SOURCE: Page_8]

activities, R and ε. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Since water is used as an input in the production of the irrigated crop, we expect a higher [SOURCE: Page_8]

water price r to induce a lower net beneﬁt R for the irrigated production. Also, because [SOURCE: Page_8]

higher water prices are more likely to be observed in dry conditions, we expect a negative [SOURCE: Page_8]

correlation between r and ε, the net beneﬁt of the non-irrigated crop. Hence we assume in the [SOURCE: Page_8]

7     Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_29]

This article provides some new insights into risk-averse farmers’ behaviour when farmers face [SOURCE: Page_29]

production risk due to the uncertainty of future weather and when irrigation water can be [SOURCE: Page_29]

traded on a market. Water markets have been shown to improve the eﬃciency of resource [SOURCE: Page_29]

and risk sharing when water is scarce but in this article we focus instead on the role of initial [SOURCE: Page_29]

water allocations as a background risk (induced by the uncertainty about the market price [SOURCE: Page_29]

of water). We consider a risk-averse farmer who grows an irrigated crop and a non-irrigated [SOURCE: Page_29]

crop and receives an initial water endowment at the start of the season. In this context, the [SOURCE: Page_29]

expected net returns of the two crops are unknown ex-ante because of the uncertain amount [SOURCE: Page_29]

of irrigation water and rainfall that will be available over the season; these random net returns [SOURCE: Page_29]

are also likely to be correlated with the background risk. We show that risk-averse farmers’ [SOURCE: Page_29]

decisions about land allocation between the irrigated and non-irrigated activities depend on [SOURCE: Page_29]

whether the background risk is decreasing or increasing at the optimum, and on the relative [SOURCE: Page_29]

magnitude of three eﬀects (labelled mean, variance, and correlation eﬀects). These ﬁndings [SOURCE: Page_29]

are similar to those established by EW for a risk-averse agent owning a portfolio with two [SOURCE: Page_29]

risky assets in the presence of a background risk. However the background risk is fair in our [SOURCE: Page_29]

setting, which diﬀers from EW’s framework. We then extend the model to allow farmers to [SOURCE: Page_29]

trade water ex-ante (at the start of the season) at a known price.
