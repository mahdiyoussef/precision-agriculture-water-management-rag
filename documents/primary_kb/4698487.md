Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

This paper improves our understanding of how rainwater impacts economic growth by inves- tigating the effects of overlooked properties of the water cycle. First, we consider the natural separation of rainwater into flows of blue water (i.e., the water that runs off towards rivers) and green water (i.e., that remaining in the soil). Second, we account for the presence of surface and groundwater stocks. These considerations allow us to comprehensively address the whole partition of rainwater, which, upon reaching the ground, splits into distinct water resources that determine water availability inland. Our analyses on a global panel coupling sub-national economic and hydrological data show that rainwater does increase growth, but do so differently depending on its partition. Specifically, blue water leads to more economic growth than green water at the margin, but, because two thirds of terrestrial water is green, the latter contributes more to growth in total. By missing this crucial partition, we find that commonly used rain- water measurements overstate rainwater’s contribution to growth (by about two). Our analyses further indicate that, although groundwater reserves always mitigate the impacts of rainwater reduction on growth, surface water reserves sometimes amplify regional dependence to rainwater (depending on sector, income and reserve types). 1        Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

Water is a critical resource for various economic activities, whose availability primarily depends on rainfall. As a result, many locations suffer from permanent or occasional lack of water, which can lead to conflicts of use between sectors and hinder economic growth. The IPCC (2022) estimated that half of the world currently experiences severe water scarcity issues for at least some part of the year, and that such issues are expected to be exacerbated by climate change. In this context, it is crucial to investigate the effects of (rain)water availability on economic development. Despite the growing body of research on the assessment of temperature impacts on growth at the global level (e.g., Barrios et al., 2010; Dell et al., 2012; Burke et al., 2015; Burke and Tanutama, 2019; Kalkuhl and Wenz, 2020; Linsenmeier, 2023),1 consistent estimates of the economic impacts of rainwater have been more elusive (Damania et al., 2020; Russ, 2020; Kotz et al., 2022; Marbler, 2024). We argue that this lack of consensus may be due to specific properties of water. Indeed, while temperature is a pure public good, (rain)water presents some degrees of rivalry and exclusivity in its use, which could affect the assessment of its contribution to growth. Specifically, once rainwater touches the ground, the water cycle naturally partitions it into various exclusive terrestrial water resources (McColl et al., 2022), which determine the real availability of water for agents to undertake their economic activities (rather than rainwater directly; Hoekstra and Mekonnen, 2012). The aim of this paper is to account for the natural partition of rainwater throughout the water cycle to enhance the econometric assessment of its impacts on economic growth. We specifically account for two major properties of the water cycle. First, we split the flows of rainwater into two distinct categories based on their position in the very first stage of the water cycle – the flows of blue water vs. green water – and measure their respective impacts on growth. Specifically, blue water corresponds to the portion of rainwater that quickly runs off towards rivers, while green water corresponds to water that remains in the soil. Second, we account for the presence of water stocks in addition to water flows. Indeed, in the longer run, water stocks can form throughout the water cycle as flows accumulate. We account here for the diversity of terrestrial water stocks, by considering both the reserves naturally located under the ground – i.e. groundwater – and those located at the surface, either naturally or artificially. The partition between the variety of water resources that we describe above reflects the various forms under which water is really available inland. It results from what hydrologists call the “terrestrial water cycle” (McColl et al., 2022), that is, all the water cycle partition processes consecutive to the precipitation stage – when rainwater falls – and that precede the evaporation stage (when liquid water is transformed into vapour, before forming clouds and perpetuating the cycle). Given that these processes involve a series of percolation and runoff mechanisms, the result of water partition is largely governed by the local pedoclimatic characteristics (Falkenmark and Rockström, 2006), which – because they vary largely over space – lead to an extremely uneven distribution of the various terrestrial water resources across regions. To measure the impacts of rainwater on growth while accounting for the water cycle partition, we built a sub-national panel using several global economic and hydrological datasets with high spatial resolution. Our final panel dataset consists of 31,000 sub-national regions located in 77 countries observed between 1990 and 2015, for which we have annual measurements of (i) economic growth, (ii) green and blue water flows and (iii) dummy variables reflecting the relative accessibility of the regions to surface and groundwater stocks in our sample. Our identification strategy follows the literature standards for assessing weather impacts on economic outcomes (Dell et al., 2014; Hsiang, 2016). Specifically, our benchmark model measures the impacts of region-specific annual deviations of temperature and rainwater from regional averages – referred to as shocks hereafter – on corresponding abnormal growth deviations. We extend this standard model in two directions by, in a first step, partitioning rainwater into green and blue water flows, and, in a second step, interacting these flows with the dummies relating to surface and groundwater reserves. The exploitation of water flow shocks as the primary source of variation for identification allows us to account for the effect of location- specific, time-invariant characteristics that may be correlated with growth (Hsiang, 2016). With this in mind, we particularly use the additional within-sample variations in green and blue water flows (compared to rainwater) to improve our identification of the impacts of rainwater on growth. Finally, the interactions of the water stock dummies with water flow shocks in the second set of models allow us to exploit the between- sample variation of the (strictly or largely time-invariant) regional access to water reserves to identify their role in spite of region fixed effects. Common in the literature (Carleton et al., 2022; Mérel et al., 2024), these interactions here reflect the heterogeneous impacts of water flows on growth depending on regional access to surface or groundwater stocks. A particular problem is that, since some of the surface reserves are artificial, they may not be randomly located. Our analyses yield two primary sets of results. First, the impact of rainwater on growth depends on whether it is retained in the soil – i.e., green water – or whether it runs off to rivers (i.e., blue water). Our preferred analyses including water flows only – that is, without the interactions with water stocks – indicate that an extra centimeter of blue water per month increases growth by 0.49%, whereas an extra centimeter of green water per month increases growth by only 0.33%. Statistically significant, this difference shows that blue water contributes (marginally) more than green water to growth. However, since green water accounts for two thirds of terrestrial water, we show that it actually contributes more in total to growth than blue water. Previous studies relying on traditional “non-partitioned” rainwater measurements were unable to detect such differentiated impacts.3 In fact, our analyses show that the use of non-partitioned measurements in these studies may have even been detrimental to consistently assessing rainwater’s contribution to growth. Indeed, without partitioning rainwater, we find that an extra centimeter of rainwater per month increases growth by 0.63% – about an additional 0.03 percentage points (p.p.) in absolute terms in our sample. However, when we account for the proportion of blue and green water in our economies, the weighted effect of an extra centimeter of “partitioned” rainwater per month on growth is only 0.38%. Robust to several checks, this statistically significant difference suggests that traditional rainwater metrics overestimate rainwater’s contribution to growth by a factor of about two. Second, we find that the impacts of water flows on growth depend on the endowment of water stocks within the region. Our preferred analyses including these water stocks on top of water flows indicate that, while a one centimeter reduction in partitioned rainwater per month reduces growth by 0.45% in regions with both scarce surface and hard-to-reach groundwater reserves, regions with both abundant surface and easy-to- reach groundwater reserves only suffer from a 0.29% growth loss for a similar shock. However, surface and groundwater reserves do not act in the same way: a one centimeter reduction of rainwater per month reduces growth by 0.41% in regions with abundant surface water reserves (but hard-to-reach groundwater reserves), and by 0.33% in regions with easy-to-reach groundwater reserves (but scarce surface water reserves). Finally, we find that the water stored in these reserves primarily mitigates the impacts of rainwater reduction on growth via their substitution role for green water. In addition to these two sets of primary results, we identify several secondary results regarding the het- erogeneity of (rain)water impacts on growth. For example, we show that the impacts vary across sectors, with the manufacturing and services sectors benefiting more from blue water and agriculture benefiting more from green water (both at the margin and in total). We show that rich countries may only benefit from rain- water in regions with hard-to-reach groundwater reserves. We also identify heterogeneous mitigating effects of reserves depending on their nature. For example, we find that although natural surface reserves mitigate rainwater impacts on growth, artificial surface reserves tend to amplify rainwater impacts in the regions where they are built. We finally document large distributional effects of water reserves on mitigating rain- water impacts. Our research contributes to three bodies of literature. First, we contribute to the literature assessing weather impacts on growth by joining recent efforts that proposed methodological developments to better reflect rainwater’s specificities (Damania et al., 2020; Russ, 2020; Kotz et al., 2022; Marbler, 2024). Our main argument in this paper is that rainwater poorly approximates the real availability of water in our economies. Indeed, because agents do not directly use rainwater to undertake their economic activities but, instead, use the various terrestrial water resources resulting from its partition, we argue that it is necessary to capture these various water flows and stocks to adequately assess rainwater impacts on growth. Our results show that, indeed, accounting for the various terrestrial water resources resulting from water partition does matter. On the one hand, we show that, since regions can substitute water flows with pre- existing stocks, rainwater primarily benefits regions with few reserves. To our knowledge, such results have not been previously highlighted in the literature, as studies have mainly focused on rainwater impacts, with no emphasis on the role of water stocks. On the other hand, we show that differentiating rainwater into blue and green water flows affects the assessment of rainwater impacts on growth. Specifically, we show that the use of traditional rainwater measurements leads to an approximately two-fold overestimation of rainwater’s contribution to growth compared to our partitioned approach. This result aligns with Russ (2020), who argued that rainwater poorly reflects water availability for non-agricultural activities, and that variations in blue water flows – i.e., “water runoff” – should rather be exploited to value water’s contribution to growth. While our results confirm Russ’s intuition that the manufacturing and service sectors primarily benefit from blue water, they unequivocally indicate that green water also benefits the economy. Actually, because green water is twice as abundant as blue water, we show that accounting for blue water only, as in Russ (2020), leads to overestimating water’s contribution to growth by half. Second, we contribute to the water economics literature by measuring the economic impacts of the various terrestrial water resources using panel econometric methods instead of commonly used computable general equilibrium (CGE) models (Hertel and Liu, 2019). While useful in assessing water impacts in integrated markets, CGE results hinge to a great extent on model structure assumptions. As these assumptions remain untestable, they raise legitimate concerns about potential mis-specifications (Damania, 2020). In contrast, econometric studies in water economics generally restrict their scope of analysis to specific regions or sectors (e.g., studies on agriculture in a specific region such as Mendelsohn and Dinar, 2003), which does not allow the results to be generalized. To our knowledge, the only study that has ever conducted an econometric assessment of the impact of various water resources on global economic outcomes is Candau et al. (2022), who used structural econometrics on cross-sectional data to measure the impact of green and blue water flows and groundwater stocks on agricultural production worldwide. Structural econometrics share some of CGE’s limitations as the estimates depend on the constraints imposed by the model structure (Timmins and Schlenker, 2009). By comparison, our reduced-form panel approach is flexible enough to capture water’s impacts on growth using exogenous within-sample variations only, without further assumptions about the model structure. Third, we contribute to the literature on the impacts of infrastructure on economic growth (e.g., Asher and Novosad, 2020), looking at the particular role of water reserves in coping with rainwater reduction. 2     Background: water cycle partition [SOURCE: Page_7]

At first glance, water may seem like a homogeneous resource whose availability could be adequately repre- sented by rainwater quantity. However, this simplistic view overlooks the wide diversity of resources through which rainwater actually partitions and circulates inland (McColl et al., 2022). In this section, we begin by a straightforward overview of the water cycle and its “collection stage”, before classifying the various terrestrial water resources resulting from this process. For simplicity, we categorize these water resources via two interwoven classifications commonly used in hydrology (i.e., green vs. blue water and flows vs. stocks), which we adapt for the purposes of our paper. [SOURCE: Page_7]

2.1    Overview of the water cycle [SOURCE: Page_7]

Water is neither created nor destroyed but, instead, undergoes a continuous cycle of transformations in terms of state (gas, liquid or solid), location (atmosphere, ocean or inland) and duration (from a few days to thousands of years). These transformations are governed by the water cycle. Figure 1A provides a simple overview of the water cycle with four main stages (Bradley, 2017). Starting from the evaporation stage, water rises from the oceans – and, to a lesser extent, from terrestrial water bodies – to become water vapour in the atmosphere. The vapour then accumulates into clouds in the condensation stage, before falling to the ground during the precipitation stage. Rainwater is then partitioned inland to form exclusive terrestrial water resources during the collection stage, before returning to the oceans and perpetuating the cycle. The collection stage splits rainwater into the various terrestrial water flows and stocks depicted in Figure 1B. This is thus the only stage where water is directly available for economic activities (Hoekstra and [SOURCE: Page_7]

Mekonnen, 2012; Debaere and Kurzendoerfer, 2017). Indeed, most activities need liquid water to undertake any form of production, such that water in the evaporation and condensation stages cannot be directly used. Similarly, the precipitation stage only determines the flows of water within a region, but does not characterize water availability inland (Falkenmark and Rockström, 2006).4 Studying the rainwater impacts on growth thus implies focusing on the many transformations it undergoes during the collection stage (also called the “terrestrial water cycle”), after rainwater actually falls on the ground. This is what we do in this paper. The partition of rainwater into green and blue water occurs at two different points: (i) the upper partitioning point at the land surface between surface runoff and soil infiltration, and (ii) the lower, between water uptake by plant roots and percolation for groundwater recharge (Falkenmark and Rockström, 2006). As such, the immediate partition of rainwater into green or blue water upon contact with the surface does not dictate its subsequent state within the water cycle. The cycle itself encompasses both spatial and temporal dynamics, creating interactions between green and blue water resources. These spatiotemporal dynamics notably occur at the lower partitioning point, where temporal interconnections between green and blue water resources can emerge from this gradual partition, on top of potential spatial interconnections between the two in large groundwater reserves. The spatiotemperal dynamics of these processes are influenced by the unique pedoclimatic attributes of each region (Falkenmark and Rockström, 2006), such that, for a similar amount of rainwater, different regions will have different proportions of green and blue water. For example, for the same amount of rainwater, hilly regions with calcareous soil face higher water runoff in the short term and, ultimately, have more blue water than plains with clay soils (which, conversely, have more green water). Similarly, in the long term, regions with sandy soils present higher water percolation into groundwater than regions with impermeable rocks (which, conversely, present larger surface reserves). Because the pedoclimatic characteristics are heterogeneous across space, this results in largely uneven distribution of the terrestrial water resources across space.7 [SOURCE: Page_9]

2.3    The flow vs. stock classification [SOURCE: Page_11]

Definitions. Another way to classify terrestrial water resources is to distinguish water flows from stocks. Hydrologists commonly distinguish water flows from stocks by considering that the former accumulates in the latter over time. Accordingly, they typically classify rainwater, runoff and evaporation as flows, and soil moisture and groundwater as stocks. We however slightly adjust this classification to ease the presentation of our identification strategy (see Section 4). Specifically, we adapt it according to the typical renewal rate of the resources considered. We classify water resources as flows as long as their renewal rate occurs within a time frame of less than a year and, [SOURCE: Page_11]

11 [SOURCE: Page_11]

conversely, classify them as stocks when their renewal period spans more than one year. These stocks typically correspond to water resources that accumulate over time for which only a small quantity is subject to within- year variations.11 Accordingly, we classify soil moisture and runoff as water flows, and consider as stocks the water stagnating at the surface or infiltrating deep into the ground – whose renewal rate can typically extend over centuries.12 That is, we consider that there are only flows of green water, while there are both flows and stocks of blue water. Our definition of the water stocks allows us to clearly highlight the role of regions’ endowment in terms of water reserves when assessing the contribution of (rain)water flows to economic growth. Indeed, given that agents can substitute (rain)water flows with stored water (Blakeslee et al., 2020), reserves may act as a buffer in cases of rainwater reduction. In this article, we follow the literature and consider two main types of water stocks: surface and groundwater reserves. Although the latter constitute the largest portion of blue water globally (Falkenmark and Rockström, 2006), the former are more easily accessible to agents and thus represent a larger proportion of water consumption worldwide (Debaere and Kurzendoerfer, 2017). The specificity of these surface stocks is that they encompass both natural and artificial reserves (e.g., lakes vs. dams), the former arising naturally depending on pedoclimatic conditions, while the latter are man- made infrastructure designed for specific purposes (e.g., hydroelectric production, irrigation or urban water supply). [SOURCE: Page_12]

Measurements. Our distinctive definitions of water flows and stocks directly translate into the way we measure them empirically. Specifically, while our measurements of water flows are made of location-specific time-varying continuous variables of annual water balance hydrometrics (equal to the average of monthly soil moisture and runoff over the year), we measure water stocks as binary variables describing their relative location-specific but (largely) time-invariant accessibility in comparison to other locations (compared to the median of our sample in our preferred analyses – see Section 4). We however believe that this flow vs. stock classification improves the clarity of our messages, and thus use it in the remaining of the paper. 12 While water in the upper soil layers constitutes green water (available to plants), groundwater consists typically of blue water (rarely available for plants). The strict separation between green and blue water in the soil depends on local pedoclimatic characteristics but generally ranges between one and two meters below the surface (Jahn et al., 2006). Water infiltration from the former towards the latter depends on soil moisture levels, notably whether or not they exceed the field capacity (where water and air contents of the soil are ideal for crop growth). 13 This distinction between water flows (measured using time-varying continuous variables) and water stocks (mea- sured using – strictly or largely – time-invariant dummy variables) is particularly useful for the presentation of our identification strategy (see Section 4). For this reason, our distinction between water flows and stocks differs slightly from those found in hydrology. For example, hydrologists commonly consider soil moisture as a “green water stock”, while “green water flows” usually refer to (evapo)transpiration. Because of the existence of continuous monthly mea- surements, we refer here to soil moisture as “green water flows”, and this paper ignores the role of transpiration since it is unavailable to agents. Similarly, hydrologists usually refer to “blue water flows” for both surface water runoff (as we do), but also for groundwater discharge and seepage. Due to the difficulty of measuring water quantity in groundwater, we instead used time-invariant variables to reflect relative accessibility to groundwater. Surface water reserves are considered as water stocks in both hydrologists’ terminology and ours. [SOURCE: Page_12]

12 [SOURCE: Page_12]

3.2    Spatial and temporal resolution [SOURCE: Page_16]

The above datasets being provided at different spatial and temporal resolutions, we had to define common units of analysis to aggregate them in a consistent way. We adopted the annual basis of our economic data as our preferred temporal unit of analysis, aggregating the monthly weather and water flow variable measurements to a yearly resolution based on simple arithmetic means.16 For the spatial resolution, we chose the sub-national administrative level 2 – eq. to US counties – as our preferred analytical unit. The main reason is that (rain)water availability is highly spatially heterogeneous, so that it is important to keep a sufficiently high degree of spatial resolution to capture it (Harari and Ferrara, 2018; Damania et al., 2020).17 To downscale DoSE to administrative level 2, we simply project the administrative level 1 GRP/cap data using version 3.6 of the Global Administrative Areas database – which maps six levels of administrative boundaries worldwide (level 0 for countries, level 1 for states/provinces, and so forth until level 5 for municipalities). This procedure leads in total to 31,000 administrative level 2 regions with GRP/cap measurements (27,560 for the sectoral analyses), where those located within the same administrative level 1 region all present similar GRP/cap levels. Because weather and water data are available at a finer resolution, we have to further aggregate them at the administrative level 2 region. Following the literature (e.g., Damania et al., 2020), we first upscale these variables available at the 0.5 and 2.5 arcminute cells to 5 arcminute cells with a simple arithmetic mean, before aggregating them at the administrative level 2 region by weighting them by the corresponding 5 arcminute resolution population count from Kummu et al. (2018). For each administrative level 2 region, this procedure ends up with time-invariant average groundwater depths and annual averages of monthly water heights for all water flows. Finally, we compute the maximum water height of surface water reserves as the total volume of water stored in each administrative level 2 region divided by its surface area. Such temporal and spatial aggregation at regional and annual resolutions is commonly performed in hydrological (e.g., Tuninetti et al., 2019) and economic studies in relation to the topic (e.g., Damania et al., 2020; Russ, 2020; Marbler, 2024). 16 Our results remain robust to the use of alternative temporal resolutions (e.g., growing season; see Section 5.2). 17 Damania et al. (2020) showed that even more granular data could improve the assessment of the rainwater impacts on growth. We show in Section 5.2 that our main results are robust to this choice, as they hold at finer – either at 5 or 30 arcminutes – and coarser spatial resolutions (administrative level 1). An issue with this latter spatial resolution is that the impacts of reserves are not significant, highlighting their very local nature. In line with Harari and Ferrara (2018) and Damania et al. (2020), surface and groundwater reserves thus seem more accurately represented at administrative level 2 than administrative level 1. [SOURCE: Page_16]

Water stocks.         Our identification strategy in models (3) and (4) additionally uses variation of surface and groundwater reserves. Although these reserves show considerably heterogeneity over space (see Figures 4 and 5 respectively), a challenge when analyzing the economic impacts of such reserves is to deal with potential endogeneity issues that could arise from the fact that water reserves can both impact and be impacted by economic development. This simultaneity issue has been largely recognized as a source of bias when estimating the relationship between economic growth and surface water reserves, notably artificial ones (Duflo and Pande, 2007; Strobl and Strobl, 2011; Lipscomb et al., 2013). The common approach to address this issue is to instrument these infrastructures using regional gradients and national water storage capacities (Duflo and Pande, 2007). This is what we do in this paper. Appendix A$_5$ specifically develops the methodology and first-stage results of our instrumental variable (IV) strategy, that we adapt from Duflo and Pande (2007) to better match the nature of our variable (the quantity of water potentially stored within the region instead of the presence of dams). Our preferred approach then couples these predicted stored water quantities within the artificial reserves with those potentially stored in natural water surfaces. Because the latter are computed using simulated heights of the reserves (that is, without human extraction; see Section 3.1), they are orthogonal to economic growth and do not suffer from potential endogeneity.19 The problem is slightly different for the simultaneity between growth and groundwater. Like fossil fuels, the quantity of water in groundwater is generally inversely proportional to the amount extracted. There- fore, current observations of deep aquifers may reflect over-exploited groundwater, reflecting a relationship between water depth and growth that is opposite to what we intend to analyze. Hopefully, our groundwater measurements reflect the simulated groundwater depths without human extraction (Section 3.1), which are orthogonal to economic development and, thus, enable the identification of plausible causal effects on growth (and not the other way around). 18 Using a sample similar to ours, Damania et al. (2020) showed that such remaining deviations in rainwater were sufficient to consistently estimate their impacts on growth. Similarly, Russ (2020) showed that the remaining deviations in blue water flows after controlling for region and year fixed effects were sufficient to identify causal impacts of water runoff on growth. To our knowledge, we are the first to estimate the impacts of green water on growth, such that similar comparisons are excluded. However, Figure 2 shows similar spatial variations of green water flows as those for rain and blue water depicted in Figures A$_1$ and 3, such that we can assume that remaining variations in green water after controlling for region and year fixed effects are sufficient to consistently estimate our parameters of interest. 19 Sensitivity analyses in Section 5.2 show that our results are robust to alternative IV strategies where the quantity of water potentially stored in natural reserves are also instrumented. An issue with our measurements of water reserves is that they are largely time-invariant,20 such that the region fixed effects are likely to purge all of their direct effects on growth. For this reason, we identify the economic impacts of surface and groundwater reserves in models (3) and (4) indirectly, by interacting their dummy measurements with the water flow shocks.21 Such a strategy of interacting regional time- invariant binary characteristics with time-varying regional continuous shocks has notably been used by Dell et al. (2012), Carleton et al. (2022) or Mérel et al. (2024) to identify the heterogeneous economic impacts of weather shocks depending on regional time-invariant characteristics, despite the inclusion of the region fixed effects. Here, these interactions reflect the indirect economic impacts of water reserves through their mitigating/amplifying role in how water flow shocks impact growth. [SOURCE: Page_21]

5.2       Sensitivity analyses [SOURCE: Page_26]

The results above show that (i) additional rainwater contributes to economic growth but that it does so differently depending on (ii) whether rainwater remains in the soil (green water) or whether it runs off towards rivers (blue water), (iii) whether regions present easy- or hard-to-reach groundwater reserves and (iv) whether they have access to abundant or scarce surface water reserves. Above all, our results indicate that, compared to the use of partitioned water metrics, relying on traditional rainwater measurements overestimates the contribution of rainwater to growth. These insights provide an important step towards our better understanding of the impacts of rainwater on growth. However, one can wonder whether these results are driven by true relationships between rainwater and growth, or whether they are due to some of our empirical choices. To test their sensitivity, we estimate the impacts of rainwater on growth while accounting for the water cycle partition with alternative empirical choices. 5.4    Additional results on the role of reserves in coping with rainwater impacts [SOURCE: Page_31]

So far, our results have shown that both terrestrial water flows and stocks resulting from rainwater partition contribute to growth. While we cannot easily deepen our analyses regarding the economic impacts of water flows, our data allows us to gain much more information on the nature of the water reserves. We can notably distinguish them by their size, nature and purpose, allowing us to investigate their potential distributional and heterogeneous effects in coping with rainwater impacts on growth. Such analyses follow the tradition of the literature investigating the impacts of water infrastructure on growth (e.g., Duflo and Pande, 2007; Strobl and Strobl, 2011; Lipscomb et al., 2013), but extend it to all types of water reserves – not only artificial, but also natural, either at the surface or under the ground. In the following paragraphs, we thus perform several different decompositions of our binary variables representing surface and groundwater reserves and re-estimate models (3) and (4) accordingly on our sample. [SOURCE: Page_31]

Distributional effects. We first document distributional effects of surface and groundwater reserves. Specifically, we separate the regions into decile groups according to their position within the global distri- bution of surface water reserves on the one hand, and groundwater reserves on the other. 6    Concluding remarks [SOURCE: Page_35]

The assessment of the value of climate for our economies has been the subject of much research in recent years (Dell et al., 2012; Burke et al., 2015; Kalkuhl and Wenz, 2020; Linsenmeier, 2023). While the majority of studies have found robust positive concave impacts of temperature on growth, the identification of consistent rainwater impacts has been more difficult. The lack of consensus in this regard can be attributed to various factors, ranging from econometric considerations (Damania et al., 2020), to more conceptual issues (Russ, 2020). Our main argument in this paper is that rainwater poorly approximates the real availability of water in our economies. Instead, because rainwater naturally partitions into various terrestrial water resources (that determine the real water availability inland), we argue that it is necessary to capture these various water flows and stocks to adequately assess rainwater impacts on growth. This is what we do in this paper, by accounting for both (i) the partition of rainwater into green and blue water flows and (ii) the presence of surface and groundwater reserves. Using a global sub-national panel between 1990 and 2015, we show how these two elements influence the assessment of (rain)water’s contribution to growth. Specifically, we find that overlooking the partition of rainwater flows leads to significant aggregation bias. Indeed, using traditional rainwater measurements suggests that an extra centimeter of rainwater per month increases growth by 0.63%, while, by comparison, weighting our estimates by the average rainwater partition worldwide suggests that an extra centimeter of partitioned rainwater per month increases growth by only 0.38%. That is, relying on rainwater measurements commonly used in the literature overstate rainwater’s contribution to growth (by a factor of about two). In detail, our analyses reveal that blue water flows benefit growth to a greater extent than green water at the margin. However, given that green water accounts for two thirds of water availability inland, green water flows have a greater overall impact on economic growth than blue water ones. Secondary results indicate that these larger effects of green water are notably driven by poor countries via its beneficial effects on agriculture. This emphasizes the importance of regional pedoclimatic characteristics in affecting rainwater partition and subsequently shaping structural changes towards regional specialization (Barrett et al., 2023). Our analyses additionally reveal that surface and groundwater reserves are important drivers of rainwater impacts on growth. Regions with scarce surface water reserves and hard-to-reach groundwater suffer about twice as much from rainwater reduction than regions with easy-to-reach groundwater and abundant surface water reserves. Secondary results indicate that water reserves reduce the dependency of growth on rainwater in different ways. While surface water reserves have heterogeneous effects on growth in the regions where they are built, groundwater reserves always mitigate rainwater impacts on growth (regardless of the sector and country). These results emphasize the importance of accounting for regional pedoclimatic characteristics in affecting the formation of water stocks and subsequently determining the regional economic dependence to rainwater. [SOURCE: Page_35]

35 [SOURCE: Page_35]

All of our results reflect the fact that rainwater is crucial for local and global economic development. They do, however, point out that similar rainwater shocks affect regions differently depending on the resulting partition of rainwater throughout the terrestrial water cycle (both in terms of blue and green water flows and stocks), and they unequivocally indicate that the assessment of the impacts of water availability on growth cannot solely rely on traditional rainwater measurements. This conclusion is central for our better understanding of the costs of climate change. However, several limitations of our study warrant further refinement in future research before being able to make full sense of these costs. For instance, our dataset is biased towards rich countries, with Africa having only limited representation. This could impede the external validity of our findings (Barrios et al., 2010), although this concern may dissipate as more comprehensive data becomes available across a greater number of countries. Similarly, we have disregarded the role and quality of water institutions in promoting optimal distribution of water across sectors, whereas these institutions are considered to be crucial in ensuring sustainable growth in a context of increased competition to access water (Zhao et al., 2024). Finally, we have followed the common practice in hydrology of aggregating water flows at consistent spatial and temporal resolution, but have not accounted for the specific effects linked to the spatiotemporal dynamics of the water cycle (e.g., strategic upstream/downstream effects along international rivers; Kahn et al., 2015). While the full characterization of such dynamics remains the subject of debates among hydrologists, we hope that our study will facilitate future (perhaps more interdisciplinary) work on the effect of the water cycle on economic growth. A$_5$        Instrumental variable strategy for surface water reserves [SOURCE: Page_50]

In this appendix, we summarize the procedure and the main results of our instrumental variable strategy to approximate the regional accessibility to surface water reserves. [SOURCE: Page_50]

Methodology. The methodology is largely based on Duflo and Pande (2007) and Strobl and Strobl (2011), with some minor modifications. Indeed, our ultimate objective is to instrument the dummy variable sw Dr(c)t in models (3) and (4) of Table 2 of the main text, indicating whether the region is among the half of all regions with the most abundant surface water reserves (Dr(c)t sw = 1; or Dr(c)t sw = 0 otherwise). Because we do not aim to predict the number of dams in each region (but the quantity of water that can be stored on the surface instead – in the related man-made reservoirs), we indeed adapt their approach at the margin. To construct an instrument for this dummy variable, we predict in a first stage the total quantity of water that can be stored on the surface in region r and year t following Duflo and Pande (2007)’s and Strobl and Strobl (2011)’s strategy. A$_6$       Comparison of in- and out-of-sample forecast performance [SOURCE: Page_56]

Because our preferred estimates – see Table 3 of the main text – can be typically used to make projections of the impacts of future climate change on growth, it is common in the literature to not only look at the explanatory power of the estimated models, but also at their predictive power. Even if this is not the primary purpose of our paper, we develop this analysis in this appendix. Specifically, we investigate here both the in-sample and out-of-sample forecast performance of the three new estimated models – models (2), (3) and (4) – compared to the standard model from the literature – model (1). The commonly-used criterion to evaluate such performances is to compute the root-mean-square error (RMS) of the estimated models, that is, the sum of the absolute differences between predicted and observed data divided by the number of observations. For the in-sample part, we thus estimate our models on our whole sample and compute the RMS by taking the differences between predicted and observed growth for all of our data. The approach is more technical for the out-of-sample part. Indeed, the commonly-used method for assessing out-of-sample forecast performance is to drop part of the data and to compare their observed and predicted values using the remaining observations. In the case of a panel dataset such as ours, the commonly-used strategy is to estimate the model after dropping a particular subset of years before comparing the observed growth values to their predictions. This is what we do here, by estimating our four specifications after randomly dropping two years of data (out of 25), and testing their respective ability to predict the growth values for these two years. Table A$_7$ shows, for our three original models, the change in RMS of the comparison to the standard model from the literature. One difference between our estimated models in this section and the remainder of the paper is that, because we cannot use year fixed effects to perform out-of-sample predictions on non- observed years, we drop year fixed effects from the four estimated models. Table A$_7$ indicates that all three original models improve the quality of in-sample prediction, though to a very limited extent. Indeed, accounting for water partition and water reserves in models (2) to (4) improves the quality of prediction by 0.01% to 0.06%. We find even smaller differences for the out-of-sample performance. Thought limited in amplitude, these results indicate that our new models slightly improve the projections of rainwater impacts on growth. At least, they mean that we do not decrease the quality of projections of the standard model from the literature. [SOURCE: Page_56]

A$_8$      Sensitivity analysis: external validity [SOURCE: Page_64]

Here, we test the external validity of our results. Specifically, we re-perform our analyses by using two alternative databases for the measurement of regional economic growth per inhabitant. Table A14 reports the obtained estimates when we use data from Kummu et al. (2018). Such data has been used by Damania et al. (2020) for example. On the one hand, it presents the advantage of covering a larger number of regions. On the other hand, it presents the disadvantage of being interpolated from 1990 to 2000 (contrary to DoSE). The results constantly show beneficial effects of water flows (whether for rainwater, green or blue water flows) in all specifications. The main difference with regard to our results in Section 5.1 is that blue water seems to complement water reserves. Such a result may suggest that blue water particularly benefits regional development when the region presents abundant and easy-to-reach water reserves. Table A15 reports the estimates obtained when we use data from the G-ECON database. Such data has been used by Conte et al. (2021) for example, but presents the disadvantage of being available only by steps of 5 years (from 1990 to 2015). Table A15 constantly shows beneficial effects of rainwater, green and blue water flows in all specifications. The main difference with regard to our results in Section 5.1 is that, although access to water reserves tends to reduce the dependency of growth on water flows, their effects are mostly statistically non-significant. Overall, we confirm in this appendix that rainwater benefits economic growth, whether accounting for rainwater partition throughout the terrestrial water cycle or not. This is valid in all of our economic databases. We also confirm that the weighted effect of green and blue water provides smaller estimates of the benefits to economic growth than those obtained when using rainwater measurement only. However, the results on water reserves seem less robust with these alternative databases, as most interaction terms between water flows and reserves become non-significant. Some results suggest in particular that blue water is a complement to surface water reserves (i.e., that blue water particularly benefits regional development when the region has surface reserves to store water runoff), while our preferred results indicate the opposite. Given that other robustness checks on this aspect return insignificant results, our previous results for the differentiated impacts of blue water flows on economic growth should be interpreted cautiously.A$_5$ [SOURCE: Page_64]

A$_9$       Sensitivity analyses: measurement errors [SOURCE: Page_67]

This appendix tests the robustness of our preferred results to possible measurement errors in our preferred green and blue water flow metrics (as available in TerraClimate). For this purpose, we use alternative hydrometrics from three databases that generate measurements of soil moisture and locally-generated water runoff at the same spatial and temporal resolution as TerraClimate. The first two databases rely on soil moisture and water runoff outcomes generated by two commonly used water balance models – resp. ERA$_5$ and WaterGAP – that differ with TerraClimate with regard to the calibration of rainwater partition between green and blue water.A$_6$ The third relates to direct soil moisture measurements from the ESA Climate Change Initiative Plus Soil Moisture, for which we couple the provided soil moisture measurements with the water runoff from TerraClimate. We aggregate all of these monthly hydrometrics available at the 5 arcminute resolution to the annual and regional scale by averaging them as we did for TerraClimate – see Section 3.2 of the main text. We then estimate our models with details on green and blue water flows – specifications (2) and (4) – using these alternative aggregated measurements. Because there is no reason to assume that the potential measurement errors would be the same in these alternative datasets than in TerraClimate (given the use of alternative assumptions on rainwater partition or the use of direct measurements), the results from these estimations on alternative water databases would indicate whether our preferred hydrometrics suffer from critical measurement errors. Table A16 shows the estimates obtained from these alternative estimations, and Table A17 displays the corresponding marginal effects recomputed from equations 2 to 4 in the main text. Table A16 shows similar temperature effects as those estimated in Table 3 of the main text. It indicates similarly that the green and blue water effects on growth are consistent with those identified previously, with the two benefiting growth but blue water flows increasing growth more than green water flows at the margin. The estimates consistently indicate that the weighted effects of rainwater after partition affect about two to six times less growth than the traditional rainwater metrics. The estimates consistently indicate that groundwater access reduce the economic dependence of the region on rainwater (in four out of six of the estimated interactions), as in Table 3. However, as in the other sensitivity analyses (see Appendices A$_7$ and A$_8$), we find that access to surface water reserves does not necessarily work in the same way, but could instead amplify the economic dependence on rainwater in the regions where they are built (as indicated for four out of six of the estimated interactions). These heterogeneous estimates of the effects of water reserves in affecting (rain)water impacts on growth justifies our deeper distributional and heterogeneous analyses of these reserves in Section 5.4 of the main text. For the present analysis, results in Table A16 suggest that our conclusions in the paper are robust to the use of hydrometrics generated by other water balance models than TerraClimate or to the use of direct soil moisture observations. That is, results in Table A16 suggest limited measurement errors in our preferred green and blue water flow metrics (as available in TerraClimate). [SOURCE: Page_67]
