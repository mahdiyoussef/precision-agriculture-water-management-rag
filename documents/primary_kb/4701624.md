38   Abstract 39   Lack of floral resources is suspected to be one of the factors involved in flower-visiting insect [SOURCE: Page_4]

40   declines. Because agricultural landscapes are often poor in flowers, it seems crucial to assess [SOURCE: Page_4]

41   weeds as floral resources to feed flower-visiting insects and to identify the factors that drive [SOURCE: Page_4]

42   floral productivity, defined as floral biomass produced by the weed community. We monitored [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   floral presence, productivity and traits in 16 olive groves from September 2021 to June 2022. [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   The objectives were to understand to which extinct abiotic factors, among agricultural practices, [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   pedoclimate and weather, determine floral productivity and to analyse the relationships between [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   floral traits, floral presence and productivity. We found mowing frequency (2 to 3 per year on [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   average) increased mean floral area and height, advanced flowering onset, and increased floral [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   functional diversity and flowering species richness, which in turn increased floral presence and [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   productivity. [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   Keywords 51   Floral productivity; floral resources; floral traits; woody agroecosystem; mowing 52 [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   1. Introduction 54   Since nectar and pollen are the main food resources for pollinating insects (Roulston and [SOURCE: Page_5]

55   Goodell, 2011), the lack of floral resources is one of the major causes of decreasing pollinating [SOURCE: Page_5]

56   insect populations (Goulson et al., 2015; Potts et al., 2010; Scheper et al., 2014). In [SOURCE: Page_5]

57   agroecosystems, species-rich weed communities support populations of insect natural enemies [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   and pollinators (Aviron et al., 2023), thereby contributing to biodiversity conservation, [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   biological control and entomophilous pollination. Insect visitation depends on traits linked to [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   floral resources (nectar, pollen) or morphology (flower area height, number of flowers etc.) , [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   driving the quality of plants as floral resources (Fornoff et al., 2017; Hatt et al., 2019; Hegland [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   & Totland, 2005; Rowe et al., 2020). Besides, weed communities with high taxonomic and [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   functional diversity enhance diversity across trophic levels (Lefcheck et al. 2015). High [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   functional diversity in weed communities should support more populous and diverse insect [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   communities by multiplying ecological niches for the first trophic level of consumers with [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   various ecological requirements (Potts et al., 2010). However, intensive agricultural practices [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   in Europe since 1945 curtailed weed diversity and abundance (Andreasen et al., 1996; Baessler [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   & Klotz, 2006; Meyer et al., 2013), and hence floral resources in landscapes (Bretagnolle & [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   Gaba, 2015; Richner et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_5]

70          In woody agroecosystems, weeds are a biodiversity component contributing to inter- [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   row ground cover and can be managed in a biodiversity-friendly way by cover cropping or [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   mowing, which maintain higher plant and insect richness and abundance than tillage or [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   herbicide spraying (Carpio et al., 2019 ; Kratschmer et al., 2019 ; Kazakou et al., 2016). Low- [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   intensity management such as moderate mowing (once or twice a year) maintain soil cover and [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   insect-pollinated weeds throughout the year (Tarifa et al., 2021), especially when fewer flowers [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   are available in surrounding semi-natural environments and insects need food resources [SOURCE: Page_6]

77   (Rundlöf et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_6]

78          Few studies have evaluated the potential of weeds for providing floral resources to [SOURCE: Page_6]

79   insects in woody agroecosystems, by assessing their floral traits or their floral productivity, that [SOURCE: Page_6]

80   we defined as floral biomass produced by the weed community, and even fewer have monitored [SOURCE: Page_6]

81   weed floral resources diachronically over the year. Weeds are unstable communities mostly [SOURCE: Page_6]

82   composed of annual species, and change considerably within one year, both taxonomically and [SOURCE: Page_6]

83   functionally. Because floral traits vary significantly among weed species, floral resources in [SOURCE: Page_6]

84   agroecosystems are very diverse. Common weed species can be very attractive to insects, such [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   as Picris hieracioides L., Taraxacum sp or Echium vulgare L. (Balfour & Ratnieks, 2022; [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   Hernández-Villa et al., 2020; Kuppler et al., 2023), unlike others such as Chenopodium album [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   L., Amaranthus spp. or Rumex spp., (Kuppler et al. 2023). In addition, the timing of flowering [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   is crucial for many insects, whose requirements peak at specific periods of the year depending [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   on their life cycle. Previous studies have shown that anthesis occurs earlier and lasts longer in [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   weeds (Bourgeois et al., 2019). However, the impacts of agricultural practices on the floral [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   traits and productivity of weeds are poorly known because weed studies have mostly focused [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   on resource-use ecological strategies and effects on crops. [SOURCE: Page_6]

93          Floral traits are mostly studied as determinants of insect presence as they drive insect [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   visitation and richness at the community scale (Fornoff et al., 2017; Rowe et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   However, their community-scale responses to environmental factors are rarely investigated [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   (Vojtko et al., 2020), and only in grasslands or semi-natural environments., A previous study [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   showed that floral functional diversity responds to soil characteristics in French grasslands [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   (Goulnik et al. 2021). Another recent work investigates the effects of water deficit on floral [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   traits, which reduces flower size and number, and nectar volume (Kuppler & Kotowska, 2021). [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   Dry Mediterranean conditions select for mostly small or short-lived flowers (Teixido and [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   Valladares, 2014) so as to maintain a positive water balance at the flower scale (Roddy et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

102   2023). However, weed floral traits response to the environment, pedoclimatic conditions or [SOURCE: Page_7]

103   agricultural practices, are almost unknown. At the species level, weed floral traits are linked to [SOURCE: Page_7]

104   Grime’s CSR strategies (Genty et al., 2023), suggesting that they could be sensitive to [SOURCE: Page_7]

105   management and disturbance. One recent study showed that organic farming increases [SOURCE: Page_7]

106   functional richness and the number of red and zygomorphic flowers in weed communities [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   (Rotchés-Ribalta et al., 2023), but how other floral traits linked to insects respond to agricultural [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   practices is not known. [SOURCE: Page_7]

109           Flower abundance, usually measured as the flower cover, is another factor of insect [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   visitation (Hegland and Boeke, 2006; Wray et al., 2014). However, which practices determine [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   floral resources abundance, composition, and above all, dynamics, in agroecosystems is [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   scarcely studied. We suppose that low-disturbance practices should allow greater weed flower [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   production than intensive practices (Kratschmer et al., 2019). In addition, species richness, [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   especially of flowering species, should increase flower abundance and productivity. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   relationships between plant traits (Pontes et al., 2007) and productivity of vegetative biomass, [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   as well as those between diversity and -productivity (Lehman and Tillman, 2000) are well [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   studied. They indicate that more diverse plant communities with specific values for vegetative [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   traits are also more productive, however, these relationships this remains largely unexplored [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   for floral productivity. [SOURCE: Page_7]

120           In this study, we explored how agricultural practices, weather and pedoclimatic [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   conditions affected weed communities and flowers over one agricultural year in extensive olive [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   groves in southern France, focusing on optimising weed floral resources for insects. Our [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   hypotheses were that species richness and functional structure of weed communities (1) are [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   affected both by agricultural and pedoclimatic variables, in particular that low-disturbance [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   inter-row management practices (e.g. mowing), water availability and soil fertility would [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   enhance species richness and floral trait diversity, and (2) have a positive effect on floral [SOURCE: Page_8]

127   productivity via their effects on traits and richness that could drive temporal niche partitioning. [SOURCE: Page_8]

128   Weeds flowering earlier can be visited by different insects than those flowering later, thus [SOURCE: Page_8]

129   decreasing competition to attract pollinators and improving flower production. Greater diversity [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   in morphological floral traits in the community could increase flower-visiting insect richness [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   because different floral morphologies attract different insects. More flowers per plant on [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   average and longer mean flowering duration should increase floral productivity over the entire [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   year by extending the period during which flowers are present. [SOURCE: Page_8]

164   Five permanent quadrats of 0.25 m^2 were randomly placed in the inter-rows of each olive grove [SOURCE: Page_9]

170   ground covered by open flowers and flower buds in each quadrat. We also recorded the number [SOURCE: Page_10]

171   of species at the flowering stage (‘floral richness’) in each quadrat, and the total species richness [SOURCE: Page_10]

339   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_25]

340   4.1. Weed floral traits and richness are affected by pedoclimate and [SOURCE: Page_25]

341   agricultural practices [SOURCE: Page_25]

342   Our results indicate that agricultural practices affect the floral functional structure of weed [SOURCE: Page_25]

343   communities in Mediterranean olive groves. More mowing interventions (min : 0, max : 5 in [SOURCE: Page_25]

344   the network) increased floral richness (Table 1), functional heterogeneity (i.e. wider community [SOURCE: Page_25]

345   weighted variances) of floral area, nectar sugar content and flowering onset, postponed mean [SOURCE: Page_25]

346   flowering onset, and resulted in communities with larger and taller flowers (Table 2), [SOURCE: Page_25]

347   supposedly more attractive to insects (Lundin et al., 2019; Rowe et al., 2020). The diversifying [SOURCE: Page_25]

348   effect can be explained by the positive effect of low-to-intermediate disturbance of mowing on [SOURCE: Page_25]

349   species diversity and trait variance in the community, as predicted by the intermediate [SOURCE: Page_25]

350   disturbance hypothesis (Wilkinson et al., 1999). The number of mowing interventions ranged [SOURCE: Page_25]

351   from 1 to 5 per year, with a mean of 2.19, which is considered fairly low but disturbing enough [SOURCE: Page_25]

352   to curb the dominance of the most competitive species. However, this relationship may not hold [SOURCE: Page_25]

353   when mowing is more frequent. [SOURCE: Page_25]

354          In contrast, irrigation and fertilisation had a homogenising effect, reducing community [SOURCE: Page_25]

355   weighted variances of floral area and flowering onset (Table 2), perhaps because high levels of [SOURCE: Page_25]

356   resources benefited the most competitive species. For example, flowering onset was earlier in [SOURCE: Page_25]

357   more frequently mown, fertilised but unirrigated olive groves, which is logical because more [SOURCE: Page_25]

358   disturbed communities are traditionally composed of species flowering earlier (Fried et al., [SOURCE: Page_25]

359   2012).Higher levels of resources (N fertilization, irrigation) also selected for species producing [SOURCE: Page_25]

360   more floral units that flower for a longer period, which is allowed by the available resources, [SOURCE: Page_25]

361   as showed in vinetards by Guerra et al. (2021). Managing the regularity of mowing seems [SOURCE: Page_25]

24 [SOURCE: Page_25]

362   efficient to regulate phenology so as to provide more diversified and abundant floral resources [SOURCE: Page_26]

363   for flower-visiting insects (Yvoz et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_26]

364           Even though agricultural practices are considered the main driver of trait variability in [SOURCE: Page_26]

365   weed communities at the regional scale (Bourgeois et al., 2021), pedoclimatic and weather [SOURCE: Page_26]

366   variables also matter, especially for trait variance (Poinas et al., 2023). In our study, the [SOURCE: Page_26]

367   homogenising effect of greater soil fertility, e.g. higher levels of organic matter and clay [SOURCE: Page_26]

368   contents (pedoclim^1), may be due to selection of the most competitive species (Fried et al., [SOURCE: Page_26]

369   2022) that develop earlier and exclude later species by pre-empting light resources (Grime, [SOURCE: Page_26]

370   1974). Higher temperatures allowed some cold-tolerant and sensitive species to survive, thus [SOURCE: Page_26]

371   diversifying functional structure, but also decreased community weighted mean of flowering [SOURCE: Page_26]

372   onset, advantaging competitive species that flower early. Rainfall also had a diversifying effect [SOURCE: Page_26]

373   leading to greater FCWVs of total floral unit number and pollen: water deficit may force weeds [SOURCE: Page_26]

374   to allocate less resources to flowers or pollen production (Kuppler & Kotowska, 2021). As [SOURCE: Page_26]

375   found by Bourgeois et al. (2021) for weeds in annual agroecosystems, pedoclimate affected the [SOURCE: Page_26]

376   variance more than the mean of floral traits. [SOURCE: Page_26]

377   4.2. Floral productivity is linked to community floral richness and traits [SOURCE: Page_26]

378   Not all floral traits were linked to floral productivity, indicating that communities with any [SOURCE: Page_26]

379   floral strategy could potentially provide floral resources. However four floral traits were found [SOURCE: Page_26]

380   to be positively correlated with floral productivity: mean nectar sugar content (Table 4), number [SOURCE: Page_26]

381   of flowers, onset of flowering (Figures 5 and 6) and floral richness (Table 3), the last increasing [SOURCE: Page_26]

382   all the indicators of floral productivity. [SOURCE: Page_26]

383           Weed richness enhanced floral productivity and thereby provided a diversity of food [SOURCE: Page_26]

384   resources for insects (Balfour & Ratnieks, 2022). The most abundant species, e.g. M. minima [SOURCE: Page_26]

385   and S. arvensis, had ruderal floral strategies and produced many small flowers (Genty et al., [SOURCE: Page_26]

25 [SOURCE: Page_26]

386   2023b) excluded by wild bees (Kuppler et al., 2023) but preferentially visited by other important [SOURCE: Page_27]

387   pollinators such as Diptera and Coleoptera (Lanuza et al., 2023). However, the great floral [SOURCE: Page_27]

388   richness found in the olive groves (99 flowering species) enhanced their potential for hosting [SOURCE: Page_27]

389   plants visited by all types of pollinator, among which E. vulgare and P. hieracioides, favoured [SOURCE: Page_27]

390   by wild bees (Kuppler et al., 2023). Regarding year-round flower presence, which is important [SOURCE: Page_27]

391   for flower-visiting insects, we found that floral resources in olive groves peaked in mid-April [SOURCE: Page_27]

392   overall, with local variations from March to June. This means that flowers were present early [SOURCE: Page_27]

393   in the season, supplying food at a critical time for insects (Pelletier and MacNeil, 2003), [SOURCE: Page_27]

394   however very few resources were available late in the season, which is another critical period [SOURCE: Page_27]

395   for foraging. [SOURCE: Page_27]

396   4.3. Mowing is a promising practice for enhancing floral productivity [SOURCE: Page_27]

397   Since floral richness, mean traits and trait variances were directly affected by agricultural [SOURCE: Page_27]

398   practices (Tables 1 and 2), and were linked to floral resources productivity, we conclude that [SOURCE: Page_27]

399   agricultural practices affect floral productivity in olive groves. Indeed, the most regularly mown [SOURCE: Page_27]

400   communities were composed of species flowering earlier and with more flowers per individual [SOURCE: Page_27]

401   produced flowers for a longer period (Fig. 6, 7). The intermediate level of disturbance caused [SOURCE: Page_27]

402   by two or three mowing interventions per year increased weed floral richness and trait diversity, [SOURCE: Page_27]

403   as in grasslands (Piseddu et al., 2021). This finding suggests that the impact of agricultural [SOURCE: Page_27]

404   practices on floral richness can rival that of environmental drivers (Pittarello et al., 2020). This [SOURCE: Page_27]

405   may be due to the greater instability of weed communities compared with grasslands: because [SOURCE: Page_27]

406   weeds are mostly annual species, they are more responsive to intra-annual events like [SOURCE: Page_27]

407   management practices than communities of perennial species. [SOURCE: Page_27]

408          Mowing two to three times a year appears to be a beneficial practice for weed species [SOURCE: Page_27]

409   richness and related ecosystem services in woody agroecosystems (Winter et al., 2020). In our [SOURCE: Page_27]

410   work, we found that regular mowing frequency favors higher floral productivity over the year [SOURCE: Page_27]

26 [SOURCE: Page_27]

411   in olive groves. We conclude as other studies for different services (Bopp et al., 2022b; [SOURCE: Page_28]

412   Kavvadias and Koubouris, 2019), that mowing is a biodiversity-friendly weed management [SOURCE: Page_28]

413   practice able to both deliverecosystem services and ensure satisfactory yield in Mediterranean [SOURCE: Page_28]

414   woody agroecosystems (Guerra et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_28]
