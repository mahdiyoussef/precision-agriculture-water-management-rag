2   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_5]

3   Introduction: While the popularity of aquatic physical activities continues to grow among [SOURCE: Page_5]

4   women, the effects on energy expenditure and appetite control remain unknown. The objective [SOURCE: Page_5]

5   of this study was to examine the effect of water temperature during aqua-cycling session on [SOURCE: Page_5]

6   energy expenditure, rate of perceived exertion, energy intake, appetite sensations, and food [SOURCE: Page_5]

7   reward in healthy premenopausal women. Methods: Participants completed three experimental [SOURCE: Page_5]

8   session, in the postprandial condition, in a randomized order: a land control session (CON); an [SOURCE: Page_5]

9   aqua-cycling session in 18°C (EXO18); an aqua-cycling session in 27°C (EXO27). Energy [SOURCE: Page_5]

10   expenditure, food intake, appetite sensations, and food reward were investigated for each [SOURCE: Page_5]

11   conditions. Results: EXO18 induced a significant increase in energy expenditure (p<0.001) [SOURCE: Page_5]

12   and oxygen consumption (p<0.01) compared to EXO27. CHO oxidation was higher in EXO18 [SOURCE: Page_5]

13   session compared to EXO27 and CON (p<0.05 and p<0.001, respectively). While fat oxidation [SOURCE: Page_5]

14   was higher in exercise sessions compared with CONT (p<0.01), no difference was observed [SOURCE: Page_5]

15   between EXO18 and EXO27. Exercise sessions did not alter absolute energy intake session but [SOURCE: Page_5]

16   induced a decrease in relative energy intake (p<0.001) and in hunger, desire to eat, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

17   prospective food consumption compared with CON (p<0.001). We also show here that cold [SOURCE: Page_5]

18   water exposure can increase EE while RPE is lower at the end of exercise session compared to [SOURCE: Page_5]

19   same exercise at 27°C (p<0.05). [SOURCE: Page_5]

20   Conclusion: An exposure to a moderately cold water during aqua-cycling is an efficient [SOURCE: Page_5]

21   strategy to promote increased energy expenditure and decreased hunger, which may be effective [SOURCE: Page_5]

22   for energy balance management in healthy premenopausal women. [SOURCE: Page_5]

## 26   INTRODUCTION

27   Regular exercise is well-known to have significant health benefits including the decrease in [SOURCE: Page_6]

28   cardiometabolic risks (Janiszewski & Ross, 2009). It is thus essential to develop appropriate [SOURCE: Page_6]

29   and achievable exercise programs to promote adherence, maximize engagement and favor [SOURCE: Page_6]

30   health improvements. In the past few years, aquatic physical activities such as aqua-cycling, [SOURCE: Page_6]

31   have gained popularity. This activity consists of pedaling against the water resistance using [SOURCE: Page_6]

32   stationary immersible bicycle. Due to the physical properties and advantages of immersion (i.e., [SOURCE: Page_6]

33   non-weight-bearing activity and low joint impact), aqua- cycling seems appropriate for [SOURCE: Page_6]

34   individuals wishing to begin or resume physical activity (Bergamin, Ermolao, Matten, [SOURCE: Page_6]

35   Sieverdes, & Zaccaria, 2015; Schaun, Pinto, Praia, & Alberton, 2018; Yazigi et al., 2013). Most [SOURCE: Page_6]

36   of the available investigations are feasibility studies (Morlock & Dressendorfer, 1974; Shapiro, [SOURCE: Page_6]

37   Avellini, Toner, & Pandolf, 1981), or compare cardiorespiratory parameters in response to [SOURCE: Page_6]

38   exercises performed in water versus dryland conditions (Ayme, Gavarry, Rossi, Desruelle, et [SOURCE: Page_6]

39   al., 2014; Ayme, Gavarry, Rossi, Guieu, & Boussuges, 2014; Ayme, Rossi, Gavarry, Chaumet, [SOURCE: Page_6]

40   & Boussuges, 2015; Bréchat et al., 1999; Garzon et al., 2017; Garzon et al., 2015; Sosner et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

41   2016). While the effects of aqua-cycling on cardiorespiratory responses and energy expenditure [SOURCE: Page_6]

42   have been investigated (Barbosa, Garrido, & Bragada, 2007; Pendergast, Moon, Krasney, Held, [SOURCE: Page_6]

43   & Zamparo, 2015), little is known regarding its influence on energy balance (EB) despite its [SOURCE: Page_6]

44   increasing popularity especially with women wishing to improve their body composition. [SOURCE: Page_6]

45   Although exercise induces increased energy expenditure (EE), it is now well-established that it [SOURCE: Page_6]

46   can also affect energy intake (EI), appetite and food reward, depending on the exercise [SOURCE: Page_6]

47   parameters (intensity, duration, induced EE, etc.) and on the individuals ‘characteristics [SOURCE: Page_6]

48   (Blundell, Gibbons, Caudwell, Finlayson, & Hopkins, 2015; Howe, Hand, & Manore, 2014; [SOURCE: Page_6]

49   Miguet et al., 2018; Pomerleau, Imbeault, Parker, & Doucet, 2004; Rocha, Paxman, Dalton, [SOURCE: Page_6]

50   Winter, & Broom, 2015). While few studies suggest that cold water exposure during exercise [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_7]

51   can increase both EE (McArdle, Toner, Magel, Spina, & Pandolf, 1992; Sheldahl, Buskirk, [SOURCE: Page_7]

52   Loomis, Hodgson, & Mendez, 1982) and EI (Crabtree & Blannin, 2015; White, Dressendorfer, [SOURCE: Page_7]

53   Holland, McCoy, & Ferguson, 2005), the tested temperatures may not be generalizable to [SOURCE: Page_7]

54   exercises common in classical exercise programs to promote health. The use of moderately cold [SOURCE: Page_7]

55   water temperatures (18°C - 20°C) and their effect on overall EB needs to be investigated. [SOURCE: Page_7]

56   Considering that the interplay between EE and EI is a fundamental feature of the long-term [SOURCE: Page_7]

57   regulation of body weight, the physiological modulations in response to immersed cycling need [SOURCE: Page_7]

58   to be explored to inform appropriate exercise programs. Thus, the objective of this study was [SOURCE: Page_7]

59   to examine the effect of water temperature during immersed cycling on energy expenditure, [SOURCE: Page_7]

60   energy intake, appetite sensations, and food reward in healthy, premenopausal women. We [SOURCE: Page_7]

61   hypothesized that cycling in cold water would induce a negative energy balance compared with [SOURCE: Page_7]

62   exercising with a warmer water temperature, by increasing energy expenditure and decreasing [SOURCE: Page_7]

63   appetite sensation after exercise in cold condition. [SOURCE: Page_7]

## 65   METHODS

66   Population [SOURCE: Page_7]

67   This study was conducted on eleven women aged 21.2 ± 0.6 y, recruited through [SOURCE: Page_7]

68   advertisements. Their mean body mass was 58.2 ± 5.3 kg, with a BMI of 21.7 ± 2.9 kg.m-2, [SOURCE: Page_7]

69   percentage of fat mass of 22.2 ± 4.5 %, and FFM of 42.5 ± 2.8 kg. All women had normal [SOURCE: Page_7]

70   menstruation cycles (length of cycles: 29 ± 1 days) for at least 1 year and had not taken any oral [SOURCE: Page_7]

71   contraceptives for more than 1 year prior to the beginning of the study. Smokers, dieters, [SOURCE: Page_7]

72   aquaphobic individuals, and individuals taking medication were excluded. Volunteers were not [SOURCE: Page_7]

73   engaged in regular intense physical activities. This study was conducted in accordance with the [SOURCE: Page_7]

74   Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the ethical authorities (Human Ethical Committee: [SOURCE: Page_7]

75   CPP; authorization reference: 2019-A00353-54). This work has been registered as a Clinical [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_8]

76   Trial (NCT03978975). Of note, the present analysis covers only data regarding the main [SOURCE: Page_8]

77   objective of the overall project (Temperature effect), among normal weight participant only. [SOURCE: Page_8]

78   All participants gave written informed consent. [SOURCE: Page_8]

79 [SOURCE: Page_8]

80   Experimental design [SOURCE: Page_8]

81   All visit took place in a medical center. After a full medical examination to assess eligibility, [SOURCE: Page_8]

82   the included participants were asked to complete a food preference questionnaire (which was [SOURCE: Page_8]

83   used to compose the buffet meals presented during the experimental sessions). Each participant [SOURCE: Page_8]

84   came to the laboratory on four separate occasions. The first visit aimed to assess anthropometry [SOURCE: Page_8]

85   and body composition. Then, participants completed three experimental visits, in a postprandial [SOURCE: Page_8]

86   state, in a randomized order during their luteal phase and separated by at least 7 days. The first [SOURCE: Page_8]

87   was a control condition (CON); the second was a water-based cycling session at a temperature [SOURCE: Page_8]

88   of 18°C (EXO18); the third was a water-based cycling session at a temperature of 27°C [SOURCE: Page_8]

89   (EXO27). Subjects were informed if they were allocated to the control session or an exercise [SOURCE: Page_8]

90   session but they didn’t know which one of the two exercise sessions was planned. Thirty [SOURCE: Page_8]

91   minutes after each session, an ad libitum lunch meal was provided and energy intake measured. [SOURCE: Page_8]

92   Cardiorespiratory parameters, and rate of perceived exertion were assessed during the session. [SOURCE: Page_8]

93   Before and after each session, appetite sensations, food preference and reward were assessed at [SOURCE: Page_8]

94   different times. [SOURCE: Page_8]

95 [SOURCE: Page_8]

96   Visit 1: Anthropometry and body composition [SOURCE: Page_8]

97   Height and body mass were determined using a standard wall-mounted stadiometer and digital [SOURCE: Page_8]

98   scale (SECA, Les Mureaux, France), respectively. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated as [SOURCE: Page_8]

99   body mass (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Fat mass (FM) and fat-free mass (FFM) were [SOURCE: Page_8]

7 [SOURCE: Page_9]

100   assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) (QDR4500A scanner, Hologic, [SOURCE: Page_9]

101   Waltham, MA, USA). [SOURCE: Page_9]

102 [SOURCE: Page_9]

103   Visits 2, 3, and 4: Experimental sessions [SOURCE: Page_9]

104   Sessions [SOURCE: Page_9]

105   Subjects were submitted to three experimental session in a randomized order. Participants [SOURCE: Page_9]

106   arrived at the laboratory at 11:00 am, three hours after a standardized breakfast      which [SOURCE: Page_9]

107   represented 9.5 to 10 kcal per kg of body mass (55% CHO, 30% lipids and 15% protein) (Isacco, [SOURCE: Page_9]

108   Duché, & Boisseau, 2012). The participants were instructed to abstain from stimulants (coffee, [SOURCE: Page_9]

109   tea) and from moderate-to-vigorous physical activity for 24 h prior to each session. [SOURCE: Page_9]

110   During the CON session, on land, women were asked to sit on a chair and to remain quiet and [SOURCE: Page_9]

111   at rest during 40 min in a stable environmental temperature (22 ± 0.5°C). [SOURCE: Page_9]

112   During the two exercise trials (EXO18; EXO27), participants performed exercise on a cycle [SOURCE: Page_9]

113   ergometer, immersed in water at the waist level in an individual cabin, with trunk and head [SOURCE: Page_9]

114   exposed to ambient air (aquabikecabine; Aquafit Technologie®, SIREM, Saint Maurice s/ [SOURCE: Page_9]

115   Beynost, France). They cycled for 40 min (~11:20am-12:00am) at 70% of their theoretical [SOURCE: Page_9]

116   maximal heart rate (220-age) corresponding to similar intensity used in other studies (McArdle [SOURCE: Page_9]

117   et al., 1992). [SOURCE: Page_9]

118   For each exercise session, the water temperature was continuously monitored by a thermometer, [SOURCE: Page_9]

119   and a water renewal system maintained a constant water temperature. Similarly to the CON [SOURCE: Page_9]

120   condition, ambient air temperature was stable (22 ± 0.5°C). [SOURCE: Page_9]

## 221   DISCUSSION

222   This is, to our knowledge, the first study investigating the impact of different water temperature [SOURCE: Page_14]

223   of aqua-cycling on EE, EI, appetite sensations, and food reward in healthy premenopausal [SOURCE: Page_14]

224   women. Our results, suggest that cold water exposure (18°C) during aqua-cycling leads to a [SOURCE: Page_14]

225   higher EE compared with a 27°C temperature. Importantly, both aqua-cycling sessions induced [SOURCE: Page_14]

226   a decrease in REI and appetite sensations, compared with a control session, suggesting that [SOURCE: Page_14]

227   practice of aqua-cycling could be a promising weight management strategy. [SOURCE: Page_14]

228   While exercising, heat is produced during skeletal muscle contraction thus, a water temperature [SOURCE: Page_14]

229   of 27°C is classically used in aquatic center for swimming use rather than thermoneutral [SOURCE: Page_14]

230   temperature (i.e., 33-35°C) (Barbosa, Marinho, Reis, Silva, & Bragada, 2009). Many studies [SOURCE: Page_14]

231   have investigated the effect of cold exposure on physiological outcomes using climatic chamber [SOURCE: Page_14]

232   or water immersion (Crabtree & Blannin, 2015; Gagnon et al., 2020; Gagnon et al., 2013; [SOURCE: Page_14]

233   McArdle, Magel, Lesmes, & Pechar, 1976; McArdle et al., 1992; Shorten, Wallman, & Guelfi, [SOURCE: Page_14]

234   2009). Nevertheless, the range of studied temperatures remains wide (from -10°C to 36°C) with [SOURCE: Page_14]

235   significant methodological heterogeneity, which makes any comparison difficult. The available [SOURCE: Page_14]

236   evidence seems to indicate an increase in EE and lipid oxidation during cold exposure (Gagnon [SOURCE: Page_14]

237   et al., 2020; Gagnon et al., 2013; McArdle et al., 1976; Timmons, Araujo, & Thomas, 1985), [SOURCE: Page_14]

238   with few studies examining the acute responses to exercise in cold water on EB (McArdle et [SOURCE: Page_14]

239   al., 1976; McArdle et al., 1992; White et al., 2005). McArdle et al. (McArdle et al., 1992) [SOURCE: Page_14]

240   showed in men and women, that oxygen consumption was higher in a cold water condition [SOURCE: Page_14]

241   (20°C) at rest and during low intensity exercise (≤35% VO2max) compared with warmer water [SOURCE: Page_14]

242   temperature (28°C). However, the difference in oxygen consumption was no longer significant [SOURCE: Page_14]

243   between 20°C and 28°C during moderate intensity exercise (i.e., 40% to 66% VO2max). In young [SOURCE: Page_14]

244   healthy men, White et al. (2005) found that 45 min of aqua-cycling at 60% of VO2max at 20°C [SOURCE: Page_14]

245   did not influence EE compared to 33°C, which is not in line with the present results where cold [SOURCE: Page_14]

13 [SOURCE: Page_15]

246   condition is associated with higher EE at a similar relative exercise intensity (White et al., [SOURCE: Page_15]

247   2005). This was concomitant with a higher VO$_2$ and a decrease in RPE at the end of exercise. [SOURCE: Page_15]

248   In addition, in the present study, subjects were asked to pedal at a constant pace to maintain the [SOURCE: Page_15]

249   fixed HR. An explanation for our increased EE could be that they exercised at a higher speed [SOURCE: Page_15]

250   during the cold compared to warm temperature session. Indeed, cold exposure during exercise [SOURCE: Page_15]

251   can induce a significant decrease in HR (Gagnon et al., 2013) which could partly explain the [SOURCE: Page_15]

252   increased oxygen consumption and respiratory demand in the cold compared with the aqua- [SOURCE: Page_15]

253   cycling session at 27°C matched for HR. In fact, cold exposure have been shown to induce [SOURCE: Page_15]

254   increased central blood volume and activation of the baroreceptor reflex, both mechanisms [SOURCE: Page_15]

255   associated with decreased HR (Gagnon et al., 2013). We also found here that the increase of [SOURCE: Page_15]

256   EE during the 18°C condition was concomitant with an increase in carbohydrate oxidation and [SOURCE: Page_15]

257   no change in lipid oxidation, which is different (Gagnon et al., 2020; Gagnon et al., 2013; [SOURCE: Page_15]

258   Timmons et al., 1985) or in accordance (Haman et al., 2005; White et al., 2005) with others [SOURCE: Page_15]

259   studies. Some methodological differences may explain the variability of results found in [SOURCE: Page_15]

260   literature like the condition of cold exposure (i.e., climatic chamber vs. water immersion), the [SOURCE: Page_15]

261   large range of temperature (from -10°C to 22°C) and the nutritional status of individuals (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_15]

262   fasting vs. post-prandial) which is essential when investigating substrate utilization. Finally, we [SOURCE: Page_15]

263   show here that cold water exposure can increase EE while RPE is lower at the end of exercise [SOURCE: Page_15]

264   compared to session at 27°C. It is worth noting that while RPE values were similar at 20 min [SOURCE: Page_15]

265   of exercise, the RPE discrepancy emerging at 40 min occurred in parallel with a drop in VO$_2$ [SOURCE: Page_15]

266   values from 30 to 40 min of exercise at 18°C (no more significant difference in VO$_2$ between [SOURCE: Page_15]

267   the two exercise sessions). It would have been relevant to report RPE values at each 10 min of [SOURCE: Page_15]

268   exercise to decipher if similar RPE pattern was observed with exercise duration and better [SOURCE: Page_15]

269   understand these adaptations. [SOURCE: Page_15]

14 [SOURCE: Page_16]

270   While cold water exposure may be an attractive strategy to increase EE, it has also been shown [SOURCE: Page_16]

271   to increase EI (Crabtree, Chambers, Hardwick, & Blannin, 2014; Shorten et al., 2009; White et [SOURCE: Page_16]

272   al., 2005) leading to no significant modification in EB. The present results indicate that [SOURCE: Page_16]

273   compared to the dryland resting, both bouts of aqua-cycling induced a significant decrease in [SOURCE: Page_16]

274   REI and AUC for appetite feelings (i.e., hunger, prospective food consumption, and desire to [SOURCE: Page_16]

275   eat). There was no difference due to the water temperature. Interestingly, this result indicates [SOURCE: Page_16]

276   that cold immersed exercise does not necessarily increase subsequent food intake, as it has been [SOURCE: Page_16]

277   previously suggested (White et al., 2005). Appetite feelings were significantly higher leading [SOURCE: Page_16]

278   up to the sedentary period during the dryland control session compared with ratings leading up [SOURCE: Page_16]

279   to the immersed exercise sessions. It could reflect an anticipatory effect, indicating that subjects [SOURCE: Page_16]

280   who knew that they were going to exercise rated appetite sensations at a lower level rather than [SOURCE: Page_16]

281   the influence of the immersed exercise per se (Barutcu, Witcomb, & James, 2019). In addition, [SOURCE: Page_16]

282   no difference in hedonic preference for high-fat foods was observed between conditions which [SOURCE: Page_16]

283   may back-up the non-compensation in EI subsequent to exercise. Future studies should thus [SOURCE: Page_16]

284   investigate EI, appetite feelings and hedonic responses for the rest of the day and subsequent [SOURCE: Page_16]

285   days to see if any compensation appears. [SOURCE: Page_16]

286   We have to note several limitations in our study. First we focused on a theoretical percentage [SOURCE: Page_16]

287   of HR that could be influenced by immersion (Alberton, Antunes, et al., 2013; Alberton, Kanitz, [SOURCE: Page_16]

288   et al., 2013). We could not rule out that the intensity was slightly lower than 70% as aquatic [SOURCE: Page_16]

289   immersion can induce a decrease in HR at rest and during exercise. Secondly we used a [SOURCE: Page_16]

290   standardized breakfast and gave recommendation for the day before each session. However we [SOURCE: Page_16]

291   did not control and calibrate the food intake on the day before the experimentation, we cannot [SOURCE: Page_16]

292   exclude that it could have influenced our results on EB. Finally those who were randomly [SOURCE: Page_16]

293   assigned to the two exercise session consecutively knew beforehand that their next visit would [SOURCE: Page_16]

15 [SOURCE: Page_17]

294   be the control session. This last point could have indirectly influenced some subjective appetite [SOURCE: Page_17]

295   rating. [SOURCE: Page_17]

296   To conclude, this study is the first to our knowledge to show that cold, aqua-cycling exercise [SOURCE: Page_17]

297   can be a strategy to increase EE without increasing absolute food intake in healthy young [SOURCE: Page_17]

298   women. The use of the aquatic environment for exercise, and more specifically aqua-cycling, [SOURCE: Page_17]

299   could be considered in future health management programs. Future studies should thus focus [SOURCE: Page_17]

300   on chronic effects of different aqua-cycling modalities and EI responses to determine [SOURCE: Page_17]

301   appropriate programs to induce long term control and/or improvement in body composition and [SOURCE: Page_17]

302   health in women. [SOURCE: Page_17]
