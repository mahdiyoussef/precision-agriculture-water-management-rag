49   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_4]

50         Predators of similar size often compete over prey. In semi-arid ecosystems [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   where water is a limiting resource, prey availability can be affected by water [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   distribution, which further increases resource competition and exacerbate conflict [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   among predators. This can have implications for carnivore dietary competition. [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   Hence, we evaluated the dynamics of food resource competition between African [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   wild dogs and four competing predators (cheetahs, leopards, lions and spotted [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   hyaenas) in different seasons and across areas with different waterhole densities [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   in Hwange National Park, Zimbabwe. We used the frequency of occurrence of prey [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   items found in predators’ scats to analyse diet composition, overlap and prey [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   preference. For most predators, kudu was most frequently consumed and [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   preferred. Low and medium water-dependent prey (medium and small-sized) were [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   mostly consumed by wild dogs, leopards and cheetahs. Wild dog diet overlap was [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   high with all predators, particularly with hyaenas and lions. There were no seasonal [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   differences in the predators diet. The diet overlap of wild dogs with lions was [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   highest in the low waterhole density area, and wild dog diet composition did not [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   differ significantly from the diet of lions and hyaenas. In the low waterhole density [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   area, wild dogs and hyaenas broadened their niche breadth, and predators diet [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   had a higher proportion of low water-dependent prey. A low density of waterholes [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   increased food resource competition. However, high density of waterholes, where [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   there is more prey availability, can increase the aggregation and density of [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   predators, and hence, increase the risks involved in interspecific competition on [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   wild dogs. To reduce food resource competition on wild dogs, we propose to [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   conserve larger-bodied prey that are less dependent on water (e.g. kudu, [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   reedbuck, eland, gemsbok). As the use of water pumping is common practice, we [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   propose maintaining water management heterogeneity where prey which is less [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   dependent on water can also thrive. [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_6]

81         Large predators help maintain stable ecological processes as they exert top- [SOURCE: Page_6]

82   down effects (Dalerum et al. 2008). Thus, their conservation is crucial to maintain [SOURCE: Page_6]

83   healthy ecosystem functioning (Martinez-del-Rio et al. 2001). Among mammals, [SOURCE: Page_6]

84   large predators (> 21 kg) are a distinct functional group on top of the food chain [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   that can feed on a wide range of prey sizes (Radloff and Du Toit 2004). As large [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   predators belong to the same carnivorous guild, their ecological niches overlap, [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   which results in competition (Amarasekare 2003; Radloff and Du Toit 2004). [SOURCE: Page_6]

88         To promote coexistence among competing species, a form of niche [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   separation is necessary; this can be temporal, spatial or through diet partitioning [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   (Amarasekare 2003). Diet separation can reduce exploitative competition, which is [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   when species indirectly compete for common resources through depletion of these [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   resources (Tilman 1982; Ghoddousi et al. 2017). Thus, species need to adapt their [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   foraging strategies to maximize their fitness (Schoener 1971). As predators of [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   similar size compete over prey (Cupples et al. 2011; Harihar et al. 2011), a [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   subordinate predator (smaller in size) may change its diet due to the presence of a [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   dominant predator (larger in size), especially when food is scarce (Hayward and [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   Kerley 2008; Mbizah et al. 2012). In such a situation, prey selection could depend [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   more heavily on competition among predators than on predator-prey [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   characteristics (Jones and Barmuta 1998; Radloff and Du Toit 2004). [SOURCE: Page_6]

100         Diet overlap serves as an indication of resource competition (Du Preez et al. [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   2017). A high degree of diet overlap, which can indicate the potential for a high [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   level of resource competition, can exacerbate conflict among predators (Fedriani et [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   al. 2000; Donadio and Buskirk 2006; Du Preez et al. 2017). One way to reduce [SOURCE: Page_7]

104   interspecific competition is through diet segregation, particularly when competing [SOURCE: Page_7]

105   species overlap spatio-temporally (De Almeida Jácomo et al. 2004; Gerber et al. [SOURCE: Page_7]

106   2012; Balme et al. 2017). Subordinate predators can reduce interspecific [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   competition by feeding on different prey groups (e.g. prey water dependency, or [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   prey size) (Hayward and Kerley 2005; Davis et al. 2018); as well as, through [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   segregating their diet seasonally (Jones and Barmuta 1998; Carvalho and Gomes [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   2004; Azevedo et al. 2006) and spatially (different habitats and areas) (Jones and [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   Barmuta 2000; Tsunoda et al. 2019). However, it is possible that the options for [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   diet segregation are reduced when prey abundance decreases, in which case [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   subordinate predators will be affected more heavily than dominant ones (Schoener [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   1971; Creel et al. 2018; Ferretti et al. 2020; Steinmetz et al. 2020). [SOURCE: Page_7]

115          The African wild dog (Lycaon pictus) (~22 kg) (referred to as wild dog [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   throughout the manuscript) is an endangered, social and subordinate carnivore [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   within Africa’s large carnivore guild (IUCN 2023). It suffers from interspecific [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   competition with lions (Panthera leo) (150-250 kg) and spotted hyaenas (referred [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   to as hyaenas throughout the manuscript) (Crocuta crocuta) (~70 kg). These two [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   dominant predators affect wild dogs through direct killing, exploitative competition, [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   exclusion from prey rich areas, and kleptoparasitism (Creel 2001; Van der Meer et [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   al. 2011, 2013b; Vanak et al. 2013). Wild dog diet overlaps not only with the diet of [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   lions and hyaenas, but also with the diet of leopards (Panthera pardus) (23-31 kg) [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   and cheetahs (Acinonyx jubatus) (34-64 kg) (Hayward and Kerley 2008; Mbizah et [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   al. 2012). [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

126          In arid and semi-arid ecosystems, water becomes a limiting resource in the [SOURCE: Page_8]

127   dry season and is therefore, in some areas, actively pumped to provide water to [SOURCE: Page_8]

128   animals (Owen-Smith 1996). Variation in water availability affects the abundance [SOURCE: Page_8]

129   and distribution of herbivores (Redfern et al. 2003; Valeix 2011), which in turn [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   affects the abundance and distribution of predators (Valeix et al. 2010, 2012), and [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   ultimately the level of intraguild competition between predators (Périquet et al. [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   2021). The widespread use of artificially supplied water in African savannahs [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   (Owen-Smith 1996; Edwards et al. 2015; Sutherland et al. 2018) can impact [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   carnivore interactions and potentially affect the fate of endangered species such as [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   wild dogs. Although food competition between large African predators has been [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   widely studied (Hayward and Kerley 2008; Mbizah et al. 2012; Creel et al. 2018), [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   the role of water on the dynamics of this competition has never been assessed. [SOURCE: Page_8]

138          Here, we aim to identify the level of food competition (diet composition, diet [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   overlap, and prey preference) between wild dogs and the four competitive [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   predators (cheetahs, leopards, lions and spotted hyaenas) in different seasons [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   (weather seasons: wet-early dry [November-June] / late dry [July-October]; wild [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   dogs’ behavioural seasons: nomadic (non-breeding) [September-April] / breeding [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   (restricted in movement due to denning) [May-August]) across areas characterized [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   by contrasting water availability to assess the role of water availability, and hence [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   provisioning, on the potential exploitative competition between wild dogs and other [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   large carnivores. As water can have an impact on prey species distribution and [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   abundance, our main hypothesis is that food resource competition between wild [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   dogs with larger predators would differ between seasons and between areas with [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   different waterhole densities due to differences in prey distribution and availability. 7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   We predict that the potential for resource competition, i.e. diet overlap, is higher [SOURCE: Page_9]

151   between wild dogs and the other predators during the dry and breeding season [SOURCE: Page_9]

152   and in areas with a lower density of waterholes, as prey would likely be less [SOURCE: Page_9]

153   available there. [SOURCE: Page_9]

155   Methods [SOURCE: Page_9]

156   Study site [SOURCE: Page_9]

157          The study site is situated in Hwange National Park (HNP), an unfenced [SOURCE: Page_9]

158   protected area without human settlements or paved roads and used for [SOURCE: Page_9]

159   photographic tourism. The park covers ca. 15,000 km^2 in western Zimbabwe [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   (19:00’S, 26:30’E) (Fig. 1), with altitudes between 800 m and 1100 m. The habitat [SOURCE: Page_9]

161   comprises of woodland, bushland and open areas of grassland mainly associated [SOURCE: Page_9]

162   with waterholes (Arraut et al. 2018). HNP does not have natural perennial water [SOURCE: Page_9]

163   sources; thus, in the dry season, animals depend on artificially provisioned [SOURCE: Page_9]

164   waterholes. The wet-early dry season (November-June) has a mean rainfall of [SOURCE: Page_9]

165   ~540 mm, and the late dry season (July-October) has a mean rainfall of ~12 mm [SOURCE: Page_9]

166   (Wilderness Safaris Zimbabwe, unpublished data for 2010-2017). During the late [SOURCE: Page_9]

167   dry season, deciduous trees lose their foliage, and pasture is of the lowest quality. [SOURCE: Page_9]

168   Waterholes are mainly found in the northern area of HNP (both in the North West [SOURCE: Page_9]

169   (NW) and North East (NE) areas - Fig. 1 - where waterhole density is 2.0 and 4.6 [SOURCE: Page_9]

170   per 100 km^2, respectively – Table 1). The NW area has the most fertile soil (basalt [SOURCE: Page_9]

171   soil) and is characterized by woodland and bushland; while woodland and open [SOURCE: Page_9]

172   grassland in Kalahari sands characterize the NE area. Moreover, in the NW area [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

173   there are rivers that carry water during the wet season (Chamaillé-Jammes et al. [SOURCE: Page_10]

174   2007). The South West (SW) area is the driest part of the park (waterhole density [SOURCE: Page_10]

175   is 0.5 per 100 km^2; Table 1), and characterized by bushy grassland on Kalahari [SOURCE: Page_10]

176   sand soil with almost no water provisioning during the dry season (Rogers 1993; [SOURCE: Page_10]

177   Arraut et al. 2018). Areas adjacent to HNP include human settlements, trophy [SOURCE: Page_10]

178   hunting areas and photographic safari areas (Loveridge et al. 2017). During the [SOURCE: Page_10]

179   study period in HNP (2013-2019), the density of dominant predators was estimated [SOURCE: Page_10]

180   to be: ~2.9 (±2.2 sd) lions/100 km^2, and ~10.7 (±6.1 sd) hyaenas/100 km^2 (Table 1; [SOURCE: Page_10]

181   Loveridge et al. 2022). [SOURCE: Page_10]

182   Data collection [SOURCE: Page_10]

183          Faecal samples of cheetahs, leopards, lions and hyaenas were collected [SOURCE: Page_10]

184   opportunistically along roads, trails, kills, and latrines from 2012 until 2015. Faecal [SOURCE: Page_10]

185   samples for wild dogs were collected while following packs from 2012 to 2020. The [SOURCE: Page_10]

186   identification of the faeces of predators was based on morphology, colour, odour [SOURCE: Page_10]

187   and associated tracks (Mbizah et al. 2012) or by directly observing defecation. [SOURCE: Page_10]

188   Following Mbizah et al. (2012), samples were photographed, washed in acetone, [SOURCE: Page_10]

189   dehydrated in 100% ethanol, and dried on filter paper. For prey species [SOURCE: Page_10]

190   identification, 6 to 8 hairs from the washed hair sample were selected from different [SOURCE: Page_10]

191   parts of the prey species’ pelage. Hair cross-sections and scale pattern imprints [SOURCE: Page_10]

192   made on wood glue were photographed through a microscope and each hair was [SOURCE: Page_10]

193   identified to species level using photographic reference guides (Buys and Keogh [SOURCE: Page_10]

194   1984; Kent 2004; Seiler 2010; Taru and Backwell 2014). To limit the probability of [SOURCE: Page_10]

195   pseudo-replication, for lion and hyaena scats, we considered only one sample [SOURCE: Page_10]

196   collected per 24 hours per location (location was considered the same if ≤ 1 km 9 [SOURCE: Page_10]

197   apart). For wild dog’s scats, we considered only one sample collected per 24 hours [SOURCE: Page_11]

198   from the same pack, regardless of location. We acknowledge that there could still [SOURCE: Page_11]

199   be some pseudo-replication by only considering 24h, as complete digestion can [SOURCE: Page_11]

200   take longer, however, in order to keep a good sample size we used 24h based on [SOURCE: Page_11]

201   Mbizah et al. 2012. [SOURCE: Page_11]

202   To calculate prey abundance, we used data from line-transect surveys carried out [SOURCE: Page_11]

203   in the north of HNP in September/October (late dry season) and in May/June (early [SOURCE: Page_11]

204   dry season) each year from 2012 until 2019. Further details of line-transect survey [SOURCE: Page_11]

431          Artificial water provisioning in arid and semi-arid ecosystems is a common [SOURCE: Page_21]

432   practice throughout Africa (Owen-Smith 1996; Edwards et al. 2015; Sutherland et [SOURCE: Page_21]

433   al. 2018). As water distribution can affect herbivores availability and abundance [SOURCE: Page_21]

434   (Redfern et al. 2003; Valeix 2011), the competition level of predators over prey can [SOURCE: Page_21]

435   also be affected. In HNP, competition over prey was high between wild dogs and [SOURCE: Page_21]

436   larger predators (cheetahs, leopards, lions, hyaenas), and the levels of food [SOURCE: Page_21]

437   resource competition between wild dogs with dominant predators (lions and [SOURCE: Page_21]

438   hyaenas) differed between areas with different waterhole densities associated to [SOURCE: Page_21]

439   different levels of resource availability. [SOURCE: Page_21]

440   Diet composition [SOURCE: Page_21]

441          Wild dog diet overlap was high with all predators. Overall, it was highest with [SOURCE: Page_21]

442   hyaenas, similar to findings from Breuer and Breuer (2005) and Mbizah et al. [SOURCE: Page_21]

443   (2012), and lowest with leopard and cheetah diet. This contrasts with other studies [SOURCE: Page_21]

444   where wild dog diet overlapped more with cheetah and leopard diet than with lion [SOURCE: Page_21]

445   and hyaena diet (Hayward and Kerley 2008; Vogel et al. 2019). This might indicate [SOURCE: Page_21]

446   that wild dogs are subjected to higher levels of dietary competition with the two [SOURCE: Page_21]

447   most dominant predators (lions and hyaenas) in HNP than in other parts of Africa. [SOURCE: Page_21]

448   This could add competition pressure of dominant predators to wild dogs in HNP. [SOURCE: Page_21]

449          Wild dogs, cheetahs and leopards preyed more upon less water-dependent [SOURCE: Page_21]

450   species (which are mixed, browser, medium and smaller species). While, lions and [SOURCE: Page_21]

451   hyaenas preyed more frequently upon high water-dependent species (which are [SOURCE: Page_21]

452   grazers and larger sized species). These differences in prey categories could [SOURCE: Page_21]

453   facilitate coexistence. [SOURCE: Page_21]

20 [SOURCE: Page_21]

454         Contrary to our predictions that food resource competition was going to be [SOURCE: Page_22]

455   different between seasons, season did not influence diet composition of predators [SOURCE: Page_22]

456   nor the level of resource competition between wild dogs with lions and hyaenas. In [SOURCE: Page_22]

457   HNP, Davidson et al. (2013), also found that overall there were no seasonal [SOURCE: Page_22]

458   differences in lion diet. In other carnivores and ecosystems, there were seasonal [SOURCE: Page_22]

459   foraging differences that depended on seasonal food availability (Padial et al. [SOURCE: Page_22]

460   2002; Lanszki et al. 2020; Vissia et a. 2022). Our results demonstrate that the level [SOURCE: Page_22]

461   of resource competition did not depend on prey fluctuation due to seasonality, as [SOURCE: Page_22]

462   waterholes are also pumped in the dry season. Perhaps, instead of seeking [SOURCE: Page_22]

463   different prey in different seasons, wild dogs focused on different age and sex [SOURCE: Page_22]

464   classes of the same species (Pole 2000), depending on the breeding season of [SOURCE: Page_22]

465   their main prey. [SOURCE: Page_22]

466         The main prey of wild dogs were impala, kudu, duiker and bushbuck. These [SOURCE: Page_22]

467   results are consistent with other studies in HNP (Childes 1988; Van der Meer et al. [SOURCE: Page_22]

468   2013b) and other areas (Pole et al. 2004; Hayward et al. 2006; Mbizah et al. 2012). [SOURCE: Page_22]

469   However, impala was not always preferred by wild dogs, but consumed according [SOURCE: Page_22]

470   to availability. Prey size tends to increase with wild dog pack size (Creel and Creel [SOURCE: Page_22]

471   1995), but we did not find a correlation with pack size and prey size. This result [SOURCE: Page_22]

472   could be because we did not consider prey age classes, or because even only one [SOURCE: Page_22]

473   individual wild dog can kill large prey species such as a kudu (PDC unpublished [SOURCE: Page_22]

474   data). In densely vegetated areas, wild dogs hunting success is higher (Creel and [SOURCE: Page_22]

475   Creel 2002), and the risk of kleptoparasitism is lower (Creel and Creel 1996, 1998). [SOURCE: Page_22]

476   Therefore wild dogs may prefer to make their kills in dense vegetation where [SOURCE: Page_22]

21 [SOURCE: Page_22]

477   kudus, bushbucks and duikers (as strict browsers) are commonly found (Valeix et [SOURCE: Page_23]

478   al. 2009). [SOURCE: Page_23]

480          In the low waterhole density area, the level of resource competition of wild [SOURCE: Page_23]

481   dogs with lions and hyaenas was higher. This was mainly due to both dominant [SOURCE: Page_23]

482   predators shifting their diet (lion and hyaena diet had a higher proportion of wild [SOURCE: Page_23]

483   dogs preferred prey species in this area). Only in this area, wild dogs diet did not [SOURCE: Page_23]

484   differ from lion and hyaena diet, nor did it differ in terms of prey diet and size with [SOURCE: Page_23]

485   leopard diet (browsers were consumed more by the four predators). Browsers are [SOURCE: Page_23]

486   less dependent on water than grazers (Redfern et al. 2003; Valeix 2011), which [SOURCE: Page_23]

487   could also explain why browsers were consumed more in the low waterhole density [SOURCE: Page_23]

488   area. In this area, wild dogs and leopards had a higher proportion of duiker and [SOURCE: Page_23]

489   steenbok in their diet; hyaenas consumed proportionally more duikers compared to [SOURCE: Page_23]

490   other areas; and lions consumed a significantly higher proportion of low water- [SOURCE: Page_23]

491   dependent species (kudu, duiker, steenbok) than in other areas of HNP. For both [SOURCE: Page_23]

492   dominant predators, in the driest area, there is less abundance of preferred prey, [SOURCE: Page_23]

493   which are mainly large sized, grazers, and high water-dependent prey; this can [SOURCE: Page_23]

494   explain why lions and hyaenas consume more prey preferred by wild dogs (prey [SOURCE: Page_23]

495   smaller in size and less water dependent) in that area. All this potentially indicates [SOURCE: Page_23]

496   a high level of resource competition between wild dogs with both dominant [SOURCE: Page_23]

497   predators (especially with lions) in the area with the lowest waterhole density. [SOURCE: Page_23]

498          Wild dogs had the largest niche breadth in the low waterhole density area. In [SOURCE: Page_23]

499   this area, wild dogs preferred prey included a wider range of prey species (i.e. [SOURCE: Page_23]

500   sable, reedbuck, gemsbok [Oryx gazelle] and baboon [Papio ursinus]), which were 22 [SOURCE: Page_23]

501   avoided in the high and maximum density areas, possibly because these species [SOURCE: Page_24]

502   are more dangerous to hunt (Van der Meer et al. 2019). Wild dogs would tend to [SOURCE: Page_24]

503   evade hunting these species to avoid any potential fitness costs imposed by [SOURCE: Page_24]

504   hunting dangerous prey. Carnivores need to become more generalist when there is [SOURCE: Page_24]

505   lower prey abundance (Macarthur and Levins 1967; Lanszki et al. 2019), especially [SOURCE: Page_24]

506   subordinate carnivores competing with dominant ones for food (Dröge et al. 2017; [SOURCE: Page_24]

507   Petroelje et al. 2021). Consequently, wild dogs might have had to broaden their [SOURCE: Page_24]

508   niche breadth in this area to compensate for a high diet overlap with the dominant [SOURCE: Page_24]

509   predators, as well as, due to a lower relative prey abundance. [SOURCE: Page_24]

510   Conservation implications [SOURCE: Page_24]

511         It is important to conserve complete predator guilds to preserve ecological [SOURCE: Page_24]

512   processes (Dalerum et al. 2008). Although wild dogs are adapted to coexist with [SOURCE: Page_24]

513   other predators as they have evolved with them for millennia (Turner 1990), water [SOURCE: Page_24]

514   provisioning could potentially aggravate the interspecific competition of wild dogs [SOURCE: Page_24]

515   by reducing areas to escape competition inside protected areas with high dominant [SOURCE: Page_24]

516   predator densities and outside protected areas with high anthropogenic threats [SOURCE: Page_24]

517   (Van der Meer et al. 2013a). [SOURCE: Page_24]

518         Wild dogs might not necessarily need an exclusive prey species to survive, [SOURCE: Page_24]

519   as kudu, impala and duiker are also important prey for the four other predators, [SOURCE: Page_24]

520   and bushbuck is a species also preferred by leopards and cheetahs. However, a [SOURCE: Page_24]

521   reduction of prey abundance can increase food resource competition (Karanth and [SOURCE: Page_24]

522   Sunquist 1995; Creel et al. 2018; Sévêque et al. 2020), which is what seems to be [SOURCE: Page_24]

523   happening in the low waterhole density area in HNP. [SOURCE: Page_24]

23 [SOURCE: Page_24]

524         Although wild dogs do not seem to be limited by prey availability (Creel and [SOURCE: Page_25]

525   Creel 1998; Woodroffe et al. 2007), reducing prey availability can affect wild dogs [SOURCE: Page_25]

526   by increasing intraguild competition (Creel et al. 2018). Moreover, low prey [SOURCE: Page_25]

527   abundance could affect wild dogs’ reproduction (Marneweck et al. 2019b), [SOURCE: Page_25]

528   potentially increase intraspecific competition between African wild dog packs [SOURCE: Page_25]

529   (Marneweck et al. 2019a), and increase the probability of packs consuming [SOURCE: Page_25]

530   livestock herewith provoking conflict with humans (Woodroffe et al. 2005). [SOURCE: Page_25]

531         It is crucial to conserve both density and diversity of prey, especially prey [SOURCE: Page_25]

532   preferred by threatened predators (Hayward and Kerley 2008; Davidson et al. [SOURCE: Page_25]

533   2019). Kudu is an important species because it was preferred, and had frequency [SOURCE: Page_25]

534   and high biomass contribution in the diet of most predators (especially in wild dog [SOURCE: Page_25]

535   diet). In the low waterhole density area with high resource competition, and where [SOURCE: Page_25]

536   lions and hyaenas were consuming a higher proportion of smaller prey less [SOURCE: Page_25]

537   dependent on water (possibly because there was less abundance of large bodied [SOURCE: Page_25]

538   prey), conserving large sized prey preferred, like eland and gemsbok, by lions and [SOURCE: Page_25]

539   hyaenas, would most likely decrease the food competition on wild dogs. Hence, we [SOURCE: Page_25]

540   emphasise not only to prioritize the conservation of kudu, but also the conservation [SOURCE: Page_25]

541   of other large prey species moderately dependent on water, such as reedbuck, [SOURCE: Page_25]

542   eland and gemsbok, mainly in areas with low waterhole density. This is consistent [SOURCE: Page_25]

543   with Creel el al. 2018 who found that a lack of large bodied prey leads to more [SOURCE: Page_25]

544   dietary competition. Hence, we propose to conserve these prey species by keeping [SOURCE: Page_25]

545   their populations stable but not necessarily increasing their abundance. To [SOURCE: Page_25]

546   prioritize the conservation of these prey species we recommend to avoid the culling [SOURCE: Page_25]

547   of them, as well as to have enough spaces without too many waterholes: either by 24 [SOURCE: Page_25]

548   closing waterholes in the maximum waterhole density area, or by not creating more [SOURCE: Page_26]

549   waterholes in areas with high waterhole densities. [SOURCE: Page_26]

550         In high waterhole density areas, there is a higher density of dominant [SOURCE: Page_26]

551   predators (Loveridge et al. 2022), which means that there is a higher number of [SOURCE: Page_26]

552   competitors for food in those areas. Moreover, if prey abundance is high (such as [SOURCE: Page_26]

553   in high waterhole density areas), dominant carnivores’ density can increase [SOURCE: Page_26]

554   (Carbone and Gittleman 2002; Hayward et al. 2007), and have negative effects on [SOURCE: Page_26]

555   wild dogs (Creel and Creel 1996, 1998), such as excluding them from prey rich [SOURCE: Page_26]

556   areas (Creel 2001), or even through direct mortality (Prugh and Sivy 2020). When [SOURCE: Page_26]

557   food resource competition is high, diet partitioning might not play a major role in [SOURCE: Page_26]

558   predators’ niche segregation for coexistence. Instead, in those cases, [SOURCE: Page_26]

559   spatiotemporal dimensions might be the main mechanisms allowing coexistence, [SOURCE: Page_26]

560   such as wild dogs hunting in crepuscular times and dominant predators hunting at [SOURCE: Page_26]

561   night, or by wild dogs avoiding areas highly used by lions (Bruno et al. 2003; Dröge [SOURCE: Page_26]

562   et al. 2017; Tsunoda et al. 2019; Vissia et al. 2022). [SOURCE: Page_26]

563         Low waterhole density in the ecosystem increases food resource [SOURCE: Page_26]

564   competition (especially with lions); but high waterhole density in the ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_26]

565   (where there is more prey availability), can increase the density of predators [SOURCE: Page_26]

566   (Macdonald 2016), and hence, increase the risks involved in interspecific [SOURCE: Page_26]

567   competition on wild dogs (Creel 2001). Thus, we emphasize the need to maintain [SOURCE: Page_26]

568   heterogeneity in water management actions. [SOURCE: Page_26]

569   Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_26]

570   Resource competition between wild dogs with larger predators, driven by [SOURCE: Page_26]

571   fluctuations of prey availability and abundance, differed between areas with 25 [SOURCE: Page_26]

572   different waterhole densities, but not between seasons. Dietary competition of wild [SOURCE: Page_27]

573   dogs with dominant predators (especially with lions) was highest in the low [SOURCE: Page_27]

574   waterhole density area. To reduce food resource competition (exploitative [SOURCE: Page_27]

575   competition) on wild dogs, we propose to conserve larger-bodied prey that are less [SOURCE: Page_27]

576   dependent on water. As food resource competition was high between wild dogs [SOURCE: Page_27]

577   with the four larger predators, spatiotemporal partitioning might be playing a major [SOURCE: Page_27]

578   role to allow coexistence. [SOURCE: Page_27]
