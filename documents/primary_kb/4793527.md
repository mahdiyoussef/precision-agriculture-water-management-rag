Tolosan, France *Correspondence: [EMAIL_REDACTED] Abstract Growing a second crop in a year, either following an early summer harvest (double crop) or by sowing into the previous autumn-sown crop (relay crop), enables additional production, whether for feed, food or energy purposes. This practice of double cropping can generate additional income while providing support and regulation services. As such, it can be seen as an ecologically intensive form of agriculture, but also as an opportunity offered by climate change. The decision-making process leading to double cropping is based on a number of factors linked to soil and climatic conditions, but also to the expectation of gain and the perception of risk. The CASDAR project "3C2A: Three crops in two years" (2019-2023), which gathered 15 partners (farmers, advisors from chambers of agriculture, engineers from technical institutes, researchers), was set up to create useful references for double cropping in south-western France, encompassing the regions of Nouvelle-Aquitaine and Occitanie. The aim of this paper is to illustrate the potential interest of soybean and sunflower as second crops in south-western France by means of a qualitative analysis of farmers' perceptions of the risks and opportunities of this practice, enriched by a 4- year on-farm assessment of the agronomic and economic performances of double cropping (130 fields) and at the experimental station, where a range of varieties were compared over a 3-year period. With the support of agronomic simulation (SPA$_1$, STICS$_2$), it was possible to assess the robustness of the conclusions (feasibility, productivity) over longer climatic sequences, for unexperimented soils, climates and cropping practices, and for climate change scenarios. It was also possible to assess the environmental impacts (water requirements, drainage, nitrate leaching) of double crops in the South-West of France. Keywords: yield, semi-net margin, climate change, irrigation, on-farm experiments [SOURCE: Page_2]

1. Introduction Global warming, soil degradation and the depletion of natural resources jointly threaten crop productivity and the environmental sustainability of agriculture. It is against this backdrop that the concepts of 'ecologically intensive' agriculture and 'biodiversity-based' resilient farming systems have been developed, with a view to helping to (re)generate ecosystem services and limit the negative externalities associated with agricultural production (Bonny, 2010; Ghali et al., 2014; Tittonell, 2014). To achieve this, it is necessary to increase cultivated biodiversity at field, farm and landscape level, for example by diversifying crops in space and time (Lithourgidis et al., 2011; Duru et al., 2015). This would increase [SOURCE: Page_2]

Debaeke et al, [SOURCE: Page_3]

land-use efficiency and, in the case of cash crops, yield per unit area cultivated (Caviglia et al., 2004; Heaton et al., 2013; Bowles et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020). In addition, crop diversification can provide additional income for the farmer while also providing support and regulatory services such as i) soil cover during the fallow period, which helps to increase soil carbon storage and reduce nutrient loss, ii) symbiotic nitrogen fixation through the introduction of legumes, or iii) reducing the impact of weeds, pathogens and pests through a cycle-breaking effect (Schipanski et al., 2014; Bulan et al., 2015; Blesh, 2018; Wauters et al., 2021; Guinet et al., 2023). Growing a second food crop, forage or biomass for energy purposes (CIVE) during the fallow period (interval between two crops) is commonly known as "double cropping" (DC). This practice involves harvesting two crops in the same year, whether they are planted successively (DC = sequential double crop) or whether the second crop is planted during the cycle of the first (DC = relay crop), which implies that their growth periods overlap (Tanveer et al., 2017; Lamichhane et al., 2023). In temperate and Mediterranean countries, this corresponds to a summer crop (e.g. maize, soybean, sorghum, sunflower, etc.) sown immediately after the early harvest of a winter-sown crop (e.g. barley, peas, oilseed rape, etc.) (Jacquin, 1992; Yamane et al., 2016). These two practices, which are not new, are widespread throughout the world, but in regions where there is sufficient temperature and water (Zhang et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2012; Borchers et al., 2014; Andrade and Satorre, 2015; Schnitkey, 2018; Kawasaki, 2019; Page et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2021; Simon-Miquel et al., 2023). Climate change could create new opportunities for these practices in more temperate regions, but only if the water deficit induced by high temperatures and low rainfall can be mitigated by supplemental irrigation (Seifert and Lobell, 2015; Gammans and Mérel, 2019; Gao et al., 2019; Kawasaki, 2019). In France (and Europe), double cropping is mainly used to produce a second crop for fodder or bioenergy (Brochier et al., 2011; Graß et al., 2013; Morand et al., 2013). However, as autumn soil cover is now mandatory on land classified as vulnerable zones in accordance with the European Nitrates Directive, this has led some farmers to test double cropping as an opportunistic replacement for intermediate crops (Callewaert, 2016). The decision-making process regarding the adoption of double cropping is based on many factors related to soil and climatic conditions, but also on the expectation of financial gain and ecosystem services, and the perception of risk (Shapiro et al., 1992). Oilseeds such as soybean and sunflower have the potential to improve crop productivity and profitability in a wide range of cropping systems (Le Gall et al., 2022). These spring crops make it possible to diversify crop rotations based on winter cereals and have an advantage over other crops in terms of price, ease of adoption and satisfactory production under sub-optimal environmental conditions (Debaeke et al., 1997; Reddy and Suresh, 2009). Soybean and sunflower, already grown as main spring-sown crops, could also be considered good candidates for double cropping (summer-sown) in south-west France, mainly due to the selection of very early cultivars allowing a short growing period (100-120 days) (Jacquin, 1992; Debaeke et al., 1997; Egli and Bruening, 2000; Lecomte, 2009; Vlachostergios et al., 2011; Debaeke et al., 2021). The performance of both species as double crops has been reported in many countries (Borchers et al., 2014; Andrade and Satorre, 2015; Yamane et al., 2016; Hansel et al., 2019). In addition, the soybean and sunflower sectors are well developed in south-western France, as these crops are two pillars of rainfed and irrigated cropping systems, respectively. However, very few references have been produced in Europe on the performance of these two oilseeds grown as double crops. In France, to our knowledge, no statistical data is available to assess the contribution of double cropping to harvested area or arable land production. For this reason, the CASDAR project "3C2A: Three crops in two years" (2019-2023) aimed to produce references on double cropping in south-western France, which encompasses the Nouvelle-Aquitaine and Occitanie regions (Pitchers et al., 2023). The project focused mainly on crops harvested as grains (cereals and oilseeds) rather than fodder or bioenergy crops, which have been more widely referenced in France to date (Brochier et al., 2011; Morand et al., 2013). ### 2. Farmers' perceptions of double cropping

2.1. Method Data collection was carried out in 2020 and 2021 by means of an online survey (29 responses) and direct interviews (14) with farmers participating or not in the project (Pitchers et al., 2023). The aim was to identify the current double cropping practices of farmers in New Aquitaine (Charente, Charente-Maritime, Vienne, Pyrénées-Atlantiques) and Occitanie (Aude, Gers, Haute-Garonne, Hautes-Pyrénées, Tarn, Tarn-et-Garonne). The questionnaire was designed to gather farmers' motivations and the main factors perceived as contributing to the success or failure of these practices. Within the group, 35 farmers already had experience of double cropping, 7 had tried relay cropping and 6 had never tried either of these practices. 13% of the farmers were involved in organic production. [SOURCE: Page_4]

2.2. Results Due to the small number of farmers surveyed, we opted for a qualitative analysis of the responses. Most farmers practised sequential double cropping (mainly soybean and sunflower). In fact, relay cropping is perceived as technical and risky, and is rarely practised, as weed control is uncertain in the absence of herbicides applicable to the crop sown under the cereal stand (Lamichhane et al., 2023). The interviews revealed that farmers have both economic and agronomic reasons for growing double crops. They are looking to increase their income per hectare, to replace intermediate crops in regions where these are compulsory in the autumn, or to compensate for poor results from the previous winter crop. They also aim to protect the soil against the risks of water and wind erosion, and to diversify their crop sequence. ### 3. On-farm experimentation

3.1. Method The aim of these 'on-farm' trials was to assess the feasibility and performance of different options for double cropping (Pitchers et al., 2023). Thus, from 2019 to 2022, 130 trials spread between New Aquitaine (North-West and South) and Occitanie (Centre) (Figure 1) were set up by volunteer farmers, 107 of whom were followed through to harvest with yield measurements (combine harvester, manual sampling in the event of harvesting deemed unprofitable). Each farmer was responsible for deciding on the species grown, tillage, weed control (chemical or mechanical), fertilisation and irrigation. Causes of failure were reported. The semi-net margin, defined as the difference between the gross product (actual yield x product price) and production costs, calculated as the sum of applied inputs (seeds, fertilisation, plant protection products), irrigation costs and machinery costs (tillage, sowing, harvesting), was estimated using the SYSTERRE® tool (Jouy and Tournier, 2011). 3.2. Crop practices Soybean and sunflower were grown on 25% and 23% of plots respectively, followed by buckwheat (14%) and sorghum (11%). Other double crops (27%) were: grain maize, energy crops (including silage maize), fodder crops (including feed maize, millet and various mixtures of grasses and legumes), camelina, sweetcorn and chia. The main previous crops were winter cereals (barley, soft wheat and durum wheat), oilseed rape (for oil or seed) and field peas. Consequently, the most frequent 'winter crop - second crop' sequences were as follows: (1) rape followed by soybean, sorghum or sunflower: 24%, (2) barley followed by soybean or maize: 20%, (3) wheat followed by soybean, sunflower or buckwheat: 19%. The most common practices for double cropping were as follows: mechanical operations to crush and shallowy bury the residue from the previous crop (55%), no application of herbicides (58%) or fertilisers (77%) and supplementary irrigation (54%). The absence of nitrogen fertilisers is linked to the high proportion of soybean in the panel (25%) and to farmers' desire to limit the costs of growing these second crops. The soybean varieties most commonly used are ES Comandor (000), Herta PZO (000), Solena (000) and ES Mentor (00). For sunflowers, very early varieties such as SY Arco and ES Baltic or early varieties such as MAS 82OL and Durban CS were frequently sown. [SOURCE: Page_6]

3.3. Successes and failures of double crops The summers of 2019 and 2020 were not favourable for double cropping in the South West. Low rainfall after sowing delayed seedling emergence and hampered crop growth. High temperatures in July and August (e.g. in Toulouse: 34 days with a maximum temperature Tmax > 30°C in 2019 and 25 days in 2020) with little or no rainfall had a dramatic impact on yields. Then, from mid-October 2019, excessive rainfall hampered harvests. In 2021, the late harvest of winter crops due to frequent rainfall in June delayed the sowing of double crops, but soil moisture was optimal for emergence. The summer of 2021 was relatively dry and more favourable in terms of temperature (e.g. in Toulouse: only 16 days with Tmax > 30°C), but conditions became very wet towards the end of the crop year, which delayed grain ripening and hampered harvesting operations. Between May and September, the 2022 season was exceptionally dry and warm (e.g. in Toulouse: 40 days with Tmax > 30°C in July and August), which allowed very early harvesting of the previous winter crops from mid-June onwards and therefore early sowing of second crops, which is a potential factor for success. However, the amounts of irrigation required to ensure summer crops were extremely high for double cropping in 2022. As a result, of the 130 plots monitored over the four years of agricultural trials, 39% were not harvested due to unfavourable weather conditions, with some variations depending on the species: 21% for sunflower, 22% for soybean, 43% for sorghum and 50% for buckwheat (Figure 2). In some cases, crop maturity was delayed due to the delayed establishment of seedlings in dry conditions. In other cases, harvesting was impossible due to uncontrolled weediness or weather conditions that were too wet in autumn. However, most of these failures can be attributed to a severe water deficit at sowing, responsible for low plant populations and reduced biomass at harvest. However, 52% of non-irrigated crops were not harvested, compared with 21% of irrigated crops. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Contribution of modelling Two types of model were used in this project: SPA (Simple Photothermal Algorithm, Schoving et al., 2019; 2020), a phenology model to assess the feasibility of crops, i.e. the possibility of completing the cycle; STICS (Simulateur mulTIdisciplinaire pour les Cultures Standard, Brisson et al., 2008), a crop model to assess the accessible yield, water requirements and environmental impacts (drainage, nitrate leaching) of double crops. One of the advantages of these models is that they can explore soil conditions, climates and cropping practices that go beyond the situations under experimentation, take account of the future climate (RCP scenarios from IPCC), and simulate variables that are difficult to estimate (e.g. water and nitrogen dynamics in the soil) on extended spatial and temporal scales, within the framework of virtual experiments. 5.3. Environmental impact of double crops Simulation using the STICS model shows that the introduction of double crops can have positive effects on reducing drainage and nitrate leaching. 6. Conclusion and outlook Based on on-farm and on-station experiments, agronomic modelling and a survey of farmers, the 3C2A study suggests that oilseed double cropping is feasible in the agro-ecological conditions of south-west France and can be a profitable opportunity under some conditions. Soybean has emerged as the best candidate for double crops in the south of New Aquitaine and the west of Western France, with a low economic risk. However, to minimise this risk, this practice requires irrigation, except in the south-west of New Aquitaine where rainfall is well distributed and sufficiently regular in summer. Sunflower is another potential candidate, which was massively adopted in 2022, a very dry year, but with previous crops harvested exceptionally early in June. The favourable sales price context in 2022 also strongly influenced farmers' choice. Nevertheless, the economic risk seems higher for this crop, particularly in rainy conditions. Sunflower as a double crop should not be concentrated in the Occitanie region, where it is a major component of cropping systems, in order to prevent pathogen problems resulting from short rotations (e.g. mildew, phomopsis, phoma) (Mestries et al., 2011). However, the temperature requirements for these two crops are not currently met with sufficient frequency in the north of New Aquitaine, despite very early varietal types, and buckwheat remains the best potential candidate in this region (Ferrand, 2018). This could change with climate change, as shown by simulations using the SPA and STICS models, which suggest that these crops are feasible to a greater extent (earlier sowing and harvesting dates) in northern areas (northern New Aquitaine), provided that suitable varieties are chosen. Work is underway to reduce the uncertainty of yield predictions for a wide range of varieties and cropping practices and to test the potential of double crops for higher latitudes in France and Europe.
