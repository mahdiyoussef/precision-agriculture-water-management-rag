## ABSTRACT

STICS-MACRO is a process-based model simulating the fate of pesticides in the soil-plant system as a function [SOURCE: Page_2]

of agricultural practices and pedoclimatic conditions. The objective of this work was to evaluate the influence of [SOURCE: Page_2]

crop management practices on water and pesticide flows in contrasted environmental conditions. We used the [SOURCE: Page_2]

Morris screening sensitivity analysis method to identify the most influential cropping practices. Crop residues [SOURCE: Page_2]

management and tillage practices were shown to have strong effects on water percolation and pesticide leaching. [SOURCE: Page_2]

In particular, the amount of organic residues added to soil was found to be the most influential input. The presence [SOURCE: Page_2]

of a mulch could increase soil water content so water percolation and pesticide leaching. Conventional tillage was [SOURCE: Page_2]

also found to decrease pesticide leaching, compared to no-till, which is consistent with many field observations. [SOURCE: Page_2]

The effects of the soil, crop and climate conditions tested in this work were less important than those of cropping [SOURCE: Page_2]

practices. STICS-MACRO allows an ex ante evaluation of cropping systems and agricultural practices and of the [SOURCE: Page_2]

related pesticides environmental impacts. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Keywords: STICS-MACRO model, Morris sensitivity analysis, conservation agriculture, sustainable cropping [SOURCE: Page_2]

practices, water percolation, pesticide leaching [SOURCE: Page_2]

### 1. Introduction

One of the current challenges of sustainable agriculture is to reduce the environmental impacts due to pesticides [SOURCE: Page_3]

and to protect the environmental resources such as soil and water. A way to achieve these objectives is to introduce [SOURCE: Page_3]

new cropping systems relying on integrated pest management or low input systems (Hossard et al., 2016; [SOURCE: Page_3]

Mortensen et al., 2000). However, because of the wide diversity of possible combinations of crops, agricultural [SOURCE: Page_3]

practices (tillage, organic matter management, mulch…), soils, and climates, it is difficult and costly to carry out [SOURCE: Page_3]

comprehensive in situ experiments to study the sustainability of each potential new system. An alternative is to [SOURCE: Page_3]

develop and use ex ante simulation tools to assess numerous options and to identify the best systems. [SOURCE: Page_3]

There are few modelling tools able to quantify the environmental impacts of pesticides (i.e. concentrations [SOURCE: Page_3]

in soil and water) taking into account the effects of pedoclimatic conditions, agricultural practices and cropping [SOURCE: Page_3]

systems: examples are STICS-MACRO (Lammoglia et al., 2016), RZWQM (Malone et al., 2004) and STICS-Pest [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Queyrel et al., 2016). The performance of RZWQM to simulate pesticide fate and transport in cropping systems [SOURCE: Page_3]

was shown to be good but calibration is often necessary (Malone et al., 2004). STICS-Pest was found to be efficient [SOURCE: Page_3]

to predict pesticides leaching in some contexts however it has a simplified formalism for water transfer, and the [SOURCE: Page_3]

non-linear sorption is not considered despite it can be decisive to simulate the fate of pesticides in the environment, [SOURCE: Page_3]

and in particular in groundwater (Beltman et al., 2008; Queyrel et al., 2016). The STICS-MACRO model combines [SOURCE: Page_3]

the performances of a crop model (STICS) and of a pesticide fate model (MACRO). STICS-MACRO is thus able [SOURCE: Page_3]

to simulate crop growth and development, and water and pesticides leaching, and the predictions are satisfactory [SOURCE: Page_3]

despite they vary with the pedoclimatic context (Lammoglia et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_3]

This study aims at identifying the most influential agricultural practices to reduce pesticides losses by [SOURCE: Page_3]

leaching based on a sensitivity analysis of the STICS-MACRO model. Sensitivity analysis investigates the [SOURCE: Page_3]

relationship between model input and output and helps to identify the parameters which require the greatest [SOURCE: Page_3]

accuracy in their determination and which require the most (or least) attention when parameterizing models (Dubus [SOURCE: Page_3]

et al., 2003; Saltelli et al., 2008). In this context, this analysis will help to identify the most influential STICS- [SOURCE: Page_3]

MACRO management practices related inputs on pesticides losses. Among the different existing methods of [SOURCE: Page_3]

sensitivity analysis, the Morris screening method proposed by Morris (1991), and then improved by Campolongo [SOURCE: Page_3]

et al. (2007), can be applied to complex models in order to rank a large number of inputs with a relatively small [SOURCE: Page_3]

number of simulations (Saltelli et al., 2008). [SOURCE: Page_3]

No sensitivity analysis was previously performed to STICS-MACRO but several sensitivity analyses were [SOURCE: Page_3]

conducted to MACRO and STICS separately. Those conducted on the MACRO model showed that the most [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

important parameter affecting the predictions of percolated water volumes was the water content defining the [SOURCE: Page_4]

boundary between micropores and macropores (Dubus et al., 2003). These studies also revealed that pesticide [SOURCE: Page_4]

losses were influenced by pesticides properties (sorption and degradation) and by the hydrological properties of [SOURCE: Page_4]

the soil (Dubus et al., 2003; Larsbo et al., 2005; Lindahl et al., 2005). On the contrary, few global sensitivity [SOURCE: Page_4]

analyses had previously been conducted on the STICS model. Ruget et al. (2002) showed that the sensitivity of [SOURCE: Page_4]

STICS to the various input parameters depends on the different conditions of climate, crops and soils, and that no [SOURCE: Page_4]

parameter was systematically influential. However, the crop development module of STICS was most sensitive to [SOURCE: Page_4]

juvenile stage duration and filling stage duration, while the leaf area index module of STICS was most sensitive [SOURCE: Page_4]

to crop density. [SOURCE: Page_4]

The effect of crop management on pesticide losses in the environment has never been studied. Therefore, [SOURCE: Page_4]

in this study, we explored the effects of changes in model inputs related to crop management on the water [SOURCE: Page_4]

percolation and pesticide leaching values simulated by STICS-MACRO for different soil-crop-climate conditions. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Results were used to identify the most relevant cropping practices allowing reduction in pesticides losses in the [SOURCE: Page_4]

environment. [SOURCE: Page_4]

## 2.1. STICS-MACRO

STICS (Simulateur mulTI disciplinaire pour les Cultures Standards, Brisson et al., 1998) is a dynamic crop model [SOURCE: Page_4]

designed for site-scale operational applications, which describes in detail the soil and crop processes associated [SOURCE: Page_4]

with numerous crop varieties and management practices. STICS predicts more than 200 variables, on a daily basis, [SOURCE: Page_4]

related to the crop production (LAI, quantity and quality of yield), to the environment (water, carbon, and nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_4]

fluxes), and to the evolution of soil water and nitrate contents. STICS has been widely tested and it performance [SOURCE: Page_4]

was shown to be good to simulate many outputs such as soil water content or crop development, under various [SOURCE: Page_4]

agricultural practices (Brisson et al., 2003; Coucheney et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_4]

MACRO (Larsbo et al., 2005) is a one-dimensional dual-permeability model of water flow and solute [SOURCE: Page_4]

transport in macroporous soil. The water and solute are partitioned into two domains: micropores, where [SOURCE: Page_4]

equilibrium flow and transport occur, represented by the Richards and the convection-dispersion equations, and [SOURCE: Page_4]

macropores, where non-equilibrium gravity-driven flow occurs. Pesticide sorption is described using the [SOURCE: Page_4]

Freundlich isotherm, while degradation is described using first-order kinetics and depends on soil temperature and [SOURCE: Page_4]

moisture content. The representation of crop development is simply based on crops’ emergence and harvest dates, [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

maximum LAI, maximum root depth and maximum crop height. No agricultural practices such as mulching or [SOURCE: Page_5]

tillage can be considered. The performance of MACRO is known to be good to predict the fate of pesticides under [SOURCE: Page_5]

conventional crop management (e.g. Marín-Benito et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_5]

STICS-MACRO results in the combined use of STICS and MACRO in order to simulate crop growth and [SOURCE: Page_5]

pesticide fate in complex cropping systems (Fig. 1) (Lammoglia et al., 2016). Dedicated R packages (R [SOURCE: Page_5]

Development Core Team, 2016) were developed to automate the forcing of MACRO inputs with some STICS [SOURCE: Page_5]

output variables. The packages allow to sequentially (1) import predefined STICS and MACRO parameterization [SOURCE: Page_5]

sets (one for each model), (2) simulate the crop rotation, crop development, water, and nitrogen requirements under [SOURCE: Page_5]

agricultural practices such as fertilization or crop residues management with STICS model, (3) extract from STICS [SOURCE: Page_5]

output files the estimated potential evapotranspiration, as well as the estimated green and total leaf area index, the [SOURCE: Page_5]

crop height and the maximum root depth (for each time step), (4) convert these to MACRO input file format and [SOURCE: Page_5]

adapt MACRO parameterization, (5) re-estimate the fraction of the spayed pesticide intercepted by the crop based [SOURCE: Page_5]

on STICS total LAI when the pesticide is sprayed, (6) run the MACRO model with crop shape variables and [SOURCE: Page_5]

potential evapotranspiration coming from STICS, and finally (7) import MACRO simulation results, i.e. water [SOURCE: Page_5]

balance and pesticides concentrations in soils and water as a function of time. The results can either be analyzed [SOURCE: Page_5]

and visualized in R or exported. Internally, the tools use existing command line modes of STICS and MACRO, [SOURCE: Page_5]

but do not use the graphical user interfaces of the two models. As indicated above, the performance of STICS- [SOURCE: Page_5]

MACRO to simulate the fate of pesticides under different cropping systems was shown to be good despite they [SOURCE: Page_5]

vary with the pedoclimatic context (Lammoglia et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_5]

horizon - Hinit^1-5, and nitrate content in each horizon - Ninit^1-5) were considered in the sensitivity analysis because [SOURCE: Page_6]

### 2.4. Statistical analysis

Significant differences among the rankings obtained for the eight scenarios were determined with the non- [SOURCE: Page_12]

parametric test of Kruskal-Wallis. Data normality was first tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test at P < 0.05 but the [SOURCE: Page_12]

distribution of the data was found not to present the homogeneity of variance and the normal distribution required [SOURCE: Page_12]

to do an ANOVA. The Kruskal-Wallis analyses were performed at the significance level P = 0.05 with the R [SOURCE: Page_12]

software (R Development Core Team, 2016). [SOURCE: Page_12]

The test of Kruskal-Wallis was also used to study the effect of different values of each influential input [SOURCE: Page_12]

on the model outputs. Indeed, each input value is simulated several times thus providing a set of values for the [SOURCE: Page_12]

considered output. This set of values or group is compared to the groups obtained for the other values of the same [SOURCE: Page_12]

input. This comparison will allow us to determine the relative importance of each input, then to identify how each [SOURCE: Page_12]

input influences the considered output. [SOURCE: Page_12]

### 3.2.3. Sowing practices

The results of the sensitivity analysis of STICS-MACRO also indicated that the sowing depth (profsem), in relation [SOURCE: Page_24]

to the sowing date (iplt^0), could affect water and pesticide flows: under crops sowed at an extreme depth of 10 cm [SOURCE: Page_24]

(Arvalis, 2014), they were significantly higher than under shallow sowed crops (Fig. 5 and Supplementary data [SOURCE: Page_24]

Fig. A$_1$ to A$_3$). Indeed, the effect of the sowing depth on water and pesticide flows occurs through the variation [SOURCE: Page_24]

of the duration of crop emergence which is related to the soil temperature and moisture in the seedbed (Brisson et [SOURCE: Page_24]

al., 2008). The sowing depth influences not only the duration of emergence but also the number of emerged plants. [SOURCE: Page_24]

Shallow sowed seeds may germinate from a small rain while deep sowing provokes a significant delay in [SOURCE: Page_24]

germination dates and seeds may die unless enough rain falls to moisten it. Germination initiates the growth of [SOURCE: Page_24]

root and of shoot which may slow down with unsuitable soil moisture. If the duration between germination and [SOURCE: Page_24]

emergence is too long (emergence occurs when elongation is greater than the sowing depth), there may be a [SOURCE: Page_24]

reduction in plant density which can reduce the foliage production (Brisson et al., 2008). High plant density [SOURCE: Page_24]

produces more foliage and quicker loss of soil moisture through transpiration leaving little water available for deep [SOURCE: Page_24]

percolation. Our results seem to indicate that a sowing depth of 7 cm would help to reduce pesticide losses (Fig. [SOURCE: Page_24]

5) compared to a 4 cm depth as usually recommended (Arvalis, 2014; Arvalis, 2016). Deep sowing may also [SOURCE: Page_24]

protect seeds from desiccation, rain splashing, and seed consumption by birds or rodents (Brisson et al., 1998; [SOURCE: Page_24]

Pascual et al., 1999). A balance of benefits and risks would help to select the most appropriate sowing depth. [SOURCE: Page_24]

The sowing date (iplt^0) was also shown to significantly affect water and pesticide flows (Fig. 5). The [SOURCE: Page_24]

early start of the growing season generated less water and pesticide while the late start of the growing season [SOURCE: Page_24]

generated higher water and pesticide. In STICS, crops development depends on the thermal index (degree.day) [SOURCE: Page_24]

### 3.2.4. Levels of satisfaction of the crops water requirements

Irrigation is known to affect pesticide leaching (Flury, 1996). Using the automatic irrigation option, STICS [SOURCE: Page_25]

calculates the water required to maintain the crops water stress index above the given threshold (ratiol). The model [SOURCE: Page_25]

then determines the irrigation schedule (the date and the amount of water applied) (Brisson et al., 2008). For a low [SOURCE: Page_25]

water satisfaction level, the number of irrigations is reduced compared to a high water satisfaction level. This water [SOURCE: Page_25]

satisfaction level describing irrigation calendar had a significant effect on water percolation (Fig. 5). Cropping [SOURCE: Page_25]

systems which received more often irrigation water (ratiol = 0.73 and 0.99, Fig. 5) had significantly higher water [SOURCE: Page_25]

percolation (P < 0.05) than all the other water-limited cropping systems. [SOURCE: Page_25]

When the crops water requirements are satisfied at a level of 0.21, water and pesticide leaching were [SOURCE: Page_25]

found to be minimal (Fig. 5). This value corresponds to an extreme reduction in irrigation water that will induce [SOURCE: Page_25]

severe crops water deficit stress. It might prevent possible pesticide losses but will greatly affect the crop yield. [SOURCE: Page_25]

The percolated water increases when the satisfaction of the crops water requirements increases from 0.47 to 0.99 [SOURCE: Page_25]

(Fig. 5). When ratiol is 0.47, the amount of pesticide is maximum (Fig. 5). Although there was no significant [SOURCE: Page_25]

difference between 0.73 and 0.99 levels, there was a trend toward higher water and pesticide losses from the 0.99 [SOURCE: Page_25]

ratiol. In terms of minimizing water and pesticide losses, while maintaining a good irrigation input (to get closer [SOURCE: Page_25]

to the recommended ratio of 0.90), the optimum irrigation threshold would be 0.73 (Fig. 5). [SOURCE: Page_25]

Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_25]

A Morris sensitivity analysis of the STICS-MACRO model was done to identify crop management practices that [SOURCE: Page_25]

could help to decrease pesticides losses in groundwater. The results showed that water and pesticide transport [SOURCE: Page_25]

could be highly affected by cropping practices, and in particular by organic residues management and by tillage. [SOURCE: Page_25]

Concentrations of pesticide in leachates could decrease when there is no mulch on soil surface because the presence [SOURCE: Page_25]

of mulch increases soil water content. This decrease in pesticide leaching in conventional tillage is consistent with [SOURCE: Page_25]

many field observations showing that no-till favours pesticide leaching. It was also shown that the effects of [SOURCE: Page_25]

cropping practices on pesticide losses could be more important than those of soil, crop or climate conditions in [SOURCE: Page_25]

some situations. However, STICS-MACRO cannot simulate the changes of soil structure and of water holding [SOURCE: Page_25]

25 [SOURCE: Page_25]

capacity of the soil following organic residues addition, and the effect of the change in soil organic carbon content [SOURCE: Page_26]

on pesticide sorption and degradation. The consideration of these processes would help to improve the assessment [SOURCE: Page_26]

of pesticide fate in complex cropping systems. Finally, the use of longer climatic series and of different crop [SOURCE: Page_26]

rotations and soils would help to identify cropping systems allowing a decrease in the risk of pesticide leaching in [SOURCE: Page_26]

various conditions. [SOURCE: Page_26]
