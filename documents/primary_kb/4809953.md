Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

The current challenge in sustainable agriculture is to introduce new cropping systems to reduce pesticides use in [SOURCE: Page_2]

order to reduce ground- and surface-water contamination. However, it is difficult to carry out in situ experiments [SOURCE: Page_2]

to assess the environmental impacts of pesticides use for all possible combinations of climate, crop and soils, [SOURCE: Page_2]

therefore in silico tools are necessary. The objective of this work was to assess pesticides leaching in cropping [SOURCE: Page_2]

systems coupling the performances of a crop model (STICS) and of a pesticide fate model (MACRO). STICS- [SOURCE: Page_2]

MACRO has the advantage of being able to simulate pesticides fate in complex cropping systems and to consider [SOURCE: Page_2]

some agricultural practices such as fertilization, mulch or crop residues management, which cannot be accounted [SOURCE: Page_2]

for with MACRO. The performance of STICS-MACRO was tested, without calibration, from measurements done [SOURCE: Page_2]

in two French experimental sites with contrasted soil and climate properties. The prediction of water percolation [SOURCE: Page_2]

and pesticides concentrations with STICS-MACRO was satisfactory, but it varied with the pedoclimatic context. [SOURCE: Page_2]

The performance of STICS-MACRO was shown to be similar or better than that of MACRO. The improvement [SOURCE: Page_2]

of the simulation of crop growth allowed better estimate of crop transpiration therefore of water balance. It also [SOURCE: Page_2]

allowed better estimate of pesticide interception by the crop which was found to be crucial for the prediction of [SOURCE: Page_2]

pesticides concentrations in water. STICS-MACRO is a new promising tool to improve the assessment of the [SOURCE: Page_2]

environmental risks of pesticides used in cropping systems. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

In line with European regulation (Directive 2009/128/EC), there is a need for a reduction of pesticide usage in [SOURCE: Page_2]

order to reduce non-point contaminations, especially to ground- and surface-water, while maintaining the yields [SOURCE: Page_2]

and quality of agricultural productions (Butault et al. 2010). This objective requires to change the production [SOURCE: Page_2]

system and to design and introduce new innovative cropping systems which decrease the needs for chemical [SOURCE: Page_2]

protection and mitigate the risks of water contamination (Mortensen et al. 2000). [SOURCE: Page_2]

A large number of new cropping systems can be designed combining various agronomical techniques [SOURCE: Page_2]

such as those from IPM (Integrated Pest Management) or conservation agriculture (Aubertot et al. 2005; Lichtfouse [SOURCE: Page_2]

et al. 2009; Ricci et al. 2011). The diversity of possible combinations makes it difficult to carry out in situ [SOURCE: Page_2]

experiments that would be required to study the sustainability of each new system. A signiﬁcant constraint in [SOURCE: Page_2]

estimating the risk of detrimental environmental effects of pesticides used in these cropping systems is the large [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

variation in agricultural practices, soils, and climates that have a strong influence on the fate of pesticides (Mottes [SOURCE: Page_3]

et al. 2014). [SOURCE: Page_3]

Therefore, ex-ante evaluation tools that are able to represent and to assess numerous options and to [SOURCE: Page_3]

identify alternative systems were developed (Meynard 2008). These tools, based on multi-criteria decision-aid [SOURCE: Page_3]

approach, can be used to qualitatively assess economic, social and environmental sustainability of production [SOURCE: Page_3]

systems at the field scale. They provide the opportunity to assess a high number of innovative options across a [SOURCE: Page_3]

large range of situations (e.g. Bockstaller et al. 2009; Bohanec et al. 2008; Dogliotti et al. 2004; Sadok et al. 2009). [SOURCE: Page_3]

However they do not take into account direct and indirect effects of climate, pesticide and soil characteristics and [SOURCE: Page_3]

they do not allow the assessment of pesticide flows and concentrations in the environment (soil, water, air). [SOURCE: Page_3]

One efficient way of assessing pesticides environmental impacts, i.e. their transfer to water, soil, and [SOURCE: Page_3]

atmosphere, is to use process-based numerical models such as pesticides fate models (Vanclooster et al. 2000). [SOURCE: Page_3]

Several models exist which account for the effects of both pedologic and climatic conditions on the transfer of [SOURCE: Page_3]

each considered pesticide, however the representation of agricultural practices (tillage, organic matter [SOURCE: Page_3]

management, mulch…) remains incomplete (Mottes et al. 2014). Recently, to better integrate crop management [SOURCE: Page_3]

and crop growth in the simulation of agricultural pollutions, Queyrel et al. (2016) introduced a simplified pesticide [SOURCE: Page_3]

fate module in the STICS crop model (Brisson et al. 1998). STICS can simulate the growth of a large range of [SOURCE: Page_3]

crops under different agricultural practices (rotation, mixed crops, management residues, tillage…) and different [SOURCE: Page_3]

soil and climatic conditions (Coucheney et al. 2015). The resulting STICS-Pest model therefore allowed to [SOURCE: Page_3]

consider the diversity of agricultural practices. It was found to be as efficient as capacity pesticide fate models to [SOURCE: Page_3]

predict pesticides leaching in some contexts. However, as underlined by the authors, some of the limits of STICS- [SOURCE: Page_3]

Pest is the simplified formalism for water transfer, and the absence of non-linear sorption which can be decisive [SOURCE: Page_3]

to simulate the fate of pesticides in the environment, and in particular in groundwater (Beltman et al. 2008; Queyrel [SOURCE: Page_3]

et al. 2016). To address this problem, another innovative strategy is to combine the use of a pesticide fate model [SOURCE: Page_3]

and of a crop model. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Among the pesticide fate models, MACRO (Larsbo et al. 2005) simulates water and pesticides flows in [SOURCE: Page_3]

both soil micropores and macropores, and its performance is known to be good to predict the fate of pesticides [SOURCE: Page_3]

under conventional crop management (e.g. Marín-Benito et al. 2014). In addition, MACRO is one of the models [SOURCE: Page_3]

used in Europe for the assessment of pesticides leaching within the scope of their registration (FOCUS, 2000). [SOURCE: Page_3]

However, although water and pesticides flows are rigorously described in MACRO, the description of the crop is [SOURCE: Page_3]

maybe too simple as it only considers the maximum leaf area index (LAI), the maximum rooting depth and the [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

maximum crop height. Thus, the combination of MACRO and of a crop model such as STICS can be used to test [SOURCE: Page_4]

whether a better modelling of crop growth can improve simulation of pesticides leaching for a wide variety of crop [SOURCE: Page_4]

and management practices. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Therefore, the objectives of this work were (1) to develop a new modeling approach to assess pesticides [SOURCE: Page_4]

leaching in cropping systems, based on a sequential use of the STICS model (to estimate variables such as the crop [SOURCE: Page_4]

evapotranspiration) and of the MACRO model (forced with those variables as inputs), and (2) to test the [SOURCE: Page_4]

performance of the STICS-MACRO resulting model, without calibration, by comparing its results to those [SOURCE: Page_4]

provided by MACRO, parameterized for similar conditions, and then to compare the outputs of both models with [SOURCE: Page_4]

measurements of pesticides concentrations in groundwater done in two French experimental sites. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Materials and methods [SOURCE: Page_4]

Models [SOURCE: Page_4]

MACRO pesticide fate model MACRO (Larsbo et al. 2005) is a one-dimensional dual-permeability model of [SOURCE: Page_4]

water flow and solute transport in macroporous soil. The water and solute are partitioned between two domains: [SOURCE: Page_4]

micropores, where equilibrium flow and transport occur, represented by the Richards and the convection- [SOURCE: Page_4]

dispersion equations; and macropores where non-equilibrium gravity-driven flow occurs. The later is represented [SOURCE: Page_4]

by a kinematic wave equation, with two hydraulic parameters: (1) the saturated hydraulic conductivity of [SOURCE: Page_4]

macropores, and (2) an exponent reflecting macropores tortuosity and connectivity and how the conductivity [SOURCE: Page_4]

increases with the degree of saturation. The water retention is described by a modified form of the van Genuchten [SOURCE: Page_4]

function. Water exchange between micropores and macropores is considered as an instantaneous “discharge” when [SOURCE: Page_4]

the matrix becomes over-saturated, while exchange in the other direction is modelled as a diffusive process [SOURCE: Page_4]

controlled by an effective diffusion pathlength, as a surrogate parameter for the geometry of soil structure (Gerke [SOURCE: Page_4]

and van Genuchten 1996). Pesticide sorption is described using the Freundlich isotherm, while degradation is [SOURCE: Page_4]

described using first-order kinetics and depends on soil temperature and moisture content. [SOURCE: Page_4]

The representation of crop development is simply based on crops’ emergence and harvest dates, maximum [SOURCE: Page_4]

LAI, maximum root depth and maximum crop height. While root depth and crop height are assumed to increase [SOURCE: Page_4]

linearly until the crop reaches its maximum development, the increase of the LAI is divided in two phases, a slow [SOURCE: Page_4]

linear growth phase (for describing autumn crop overwintering) and a fast non-linear growth phase until the LAI [SOURCE: Page_4]

reaches its full extent. When the maximal development is reached, root depth, crop height and total LAI are kept [SOURCE: Page_4]

constant until harvest, but the green LAI decreases (non-linearly) until the harvest date (where it reaches the “LAI [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

at harvest”, another model parameter). Overall, the crop description is quite flexible, but it does not allow any [SOURCE: Page_5]

physiological or climatic feedback-effect on the crop development: the crop growth is fully defined by the user [SOURCE: Page_5]

and independent of other processes. Potential evapotranspiration can be calculated with the Penmann-Monteith [SOURCE: Page_5]

equation, as a function of climate variables (minimum and maximum air temperature, wind speed, solar radiation [SOURCE: Page_5]

and vapor pressure) and crop development characteristics (crop height and LAI) at each time step. Potential [SOURCE: Page_5]

evapotranspiration can also be provided as an input variable (along with minimum and maximum air temperature). [SOURCE: Page_5]

The actual plant transpiration is limited by water availability in each soil layer. The MACRO model also includes [SOURCE: Page_5]

a mechanism of compensatory root water uptake that partly balance the effect of water availability. An important [SOURCE: Page_5]

difference between STICS and MACRO is the description of soil evaporation. The latter calculates transpiration [SOURCE: Page_5]

at the soil surface as a function of soil matric potential and bounded by the atmospheric demand. Below the soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

surface, the effect of soil evaporation is simply driven by gradients in soil hydraulic head (Richards equation). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Rainfall can be provided as input variable at a daily or an hourly time step. When the rainfall is provided at a daily [SOURCE: Page_5]

time step, it is internally disaggregated at an hourly time step (at which the model runs). Pesticide dynamic can be [SOURCE: Page_5]

very sensitive to the rainfall time step (and to the disaggregation parameter: the average rainfall intensity), [SOURCE: Page_5]

especially when the soil is susceptible to macropore flow. [SOURCE: Page_5]

We used for this work a development version of the MACRO model (aiming at replacing the official [SOURCE: Page_5]

release MACRO 5.2 in the future). For this work, the MACRO model was modified to use variable crop [SOURCE: Page_5]

characteristics (green and total LAI, crop height and root depth) as forcing. The profile of root density is calculated [SOURCE: Page_5]

internally in MACRO from the maximum root depth (and a shape parameter), both when the root depth is [SOURCE: Page_5]

calculated internally and when supplied as forcing. [SOURCE: Page_5]

STICS crop model STICS (Simulateur mulTI disciplinaire pour les Cultures Standards) is a generic and dynamic [SOURCE: Page_5]

crop model, with a daily time step, able to simulate the growth and yield of various crops but also the carbon, [SOURCE: Page_5]

water and nitrogen balances of the soil-crop system by taking into account the effects of climate, soil, crop, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

management practices (e.g. nutrient and organic fertilization, irrigation, soil tillage, and residues management) [SOURCE: Page_5]

(Brisson et al. 1998). The crop is characterized by its aboveground biomass (carbon and nitrogen), LAI, as well as [SOURCE: Page_5]

the number and biomass (carbon and nitrogen) of harvested crop organs. It follows that vegetative organs (leaves, [SOURCE: Page_5]

ramifications or stems) are viewed as a whole. The plant’s carbon metabolism drives crop growth with solar [SOURCE: Page_5]

radiation intercepted by the canopy converted into shoot biomass. Crop development is driven by the accumulation [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

of degree days. The model simulates the behaviour of an average plant (i.e. not accounting for within field [SOURCE: Page_6]

variability). [SOURCE: Page_6]

The soil is divided into five horizons, and each horizon is characterized by its bulk density, and by water [SOURCE: Page_6]

contents at field capacity and at wilting point. Water transport in the soil is calculated daily for each 1-cm layer [SOURCE: Page_6]

using a capacity approach. Plant roots, which enable the interactions between the soil and the crop, are defined [SOURCE: Page_6]

with their length distribution in the soil profile. STICS responds to management practices by determining stress [SOURCE: Page_6]

indices depending on the water input from irrigation and nitrogen input from fertilization. These indices can reduce [SOURCE: Page_6]

leaf growth, root growth and biomass accumulation under water- and nutrient-limited growing conditions. [SOURCE: Page_6]

To simulate a cropping system, STICS requires inputs such as the seeding date, depth and density, the [SOURCE: Page_6]

date and rate of mineral and organic fertilization, the date and amount of irrigation, and the date and method of [SOURCE: Page_6]

soil tillage. This includes a description of crop residues, and organic products ploughed under since the STICS [SOURCE: Page_6]

model also accounts for the decomposition of crop residues. If measured data are not available, STICS is also able [SOURCE: Page_6]

to compute irrigation and fertilizer amounts according to plant needs. STICS was parameterized and evaluated for [SOURCE: Page_6]

many crops such as wheat, maize, oilseed rape, sunflower, soybean, flax, tomato, sorghum, lettuce crops, mustard, [SOURCE: Page_6]

sugarbeet, barley, and potato (Brisson et al. 2003). It can predict more than 200 output variables, on a daily basis, [SOURCE: Page_6]

related to the crop production (LAI, quantity and quality of yield), to the environment (water, carbon, and nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_6]

fluxes), and to the evolution of soil water and nitrate contents. The 8.4 version of STICS was used in this work. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Sequential use of STICS and MACRO Dedicated R packages (R Development Core Team 2015) were [SOURCE: Page_6]

developed to automate the forcing of MACRO inputs with some STICS output variables (Fig. 1). The packages [SOURCE: Page_6]

allow to sequentially (1) import predefined STICS and MACRO parameterization sets (one for each model), (2) [SOURCE: Page_6]

simulate the crop rotation with STICS model, (3) extract from STICS output files the estimated potential [SOURCE: Page_6]

evapotranspiration, as well as the estimated green and total leaf area index, the crop height and the maximum root [SOURCE: Page_6]

depth (for each time step), (4) convert these to MACRO input file format and adapt MACRO parameterization, [SOURCE: Page_6]

(5) optionally re-estimate the fraction of the spayed pesticide intercepted by the crop (based on STICS total LAI [SOURCE: Page_6]

when the pesticide is sprayed), (6) run the MACRO model (with adapted parameterization, crop shape variables [SOURCE: Page_6]

and potential evapotranspiration; Fig. 1), and finally (7) import MACRO simulation results. It is possible to choose [SOURCE: Page_6]

whether both crop shape variables and potential evapotranspiration are “forced”, or only one of the two. The results [SOURCE: Page_6]

can either be analyzed and visualized in R or exported. Internally, the tools use existing command line modes of [SOURCE: Page_6]

STICS and MACRO, but do not use the graphical user interfaces of the two models. [SOURCE: Page_6]

STICS and a good representation of water and pesticides leaching in soils through MACRO as water transfer in [SOURCE: Page_22]

STICS is not accurate enough (Queyrel et al. 2016). As underlined by Jacobsen et al. (2008), considering [SOURCE: Page_22]

uncertainty related to (1) converting soil physical data to soil hydraulic properties by curve fitting or pedotransfer [SOURCE: Page_22]

functions, (2) what extend locally measured samples represent the spatial scale of interest, (3) the ability of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

model to capture major controls of leaching, and (4) the absence of calibration, then differences between observed [SOURCE: Page_22]

and simulated concentrations by up to one order of magnitude at most may not be so significant. Therefore, STICS- [SOURCE: Page_22]

MACRO was found efficient to simulate pesticide concentration and offer the advantage to allow parameterization [SOURCE: Page_22]

of complex agricultural systems and to simulate the crop growth as a function of climate, soil and agricultural [SOURCE: Page_22]

practices (fertilization, irrigation…). [SOURCE: Page_22]

Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_22]

The objective of this work was to develop a new tool based on the sequential use of the STICS model and of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

MACRO model to assess pesticides leaching in cropping systems. STICS-MACRO was tested from measurements [SOURCE: Page_22]

of the concentrations of two herbicides having similar physico-chemical properties, imazamox and S-metolachlor, [SOURCE: Page_22]

in two different soil and climate contexts. The results were also compared to simulations done with MACRO. Both [SOURCE: Page_22]

models were parameterized using pedotransfer functions, guidance documents and literature, and no calibration [SOURCE: Page_22]

22 [SOURCE: Page_22]

was done. In this stringent conditions, the performance of STICS-MACRO to simulate water percolation and [SOURCE: Page_23]

pesticides concentrations was acceptable, but it depended on the pedoclimatic context, and it was similar or better [SOURCE: Page_23]

than that of MACRO. The improvement of the simulation of crop growth allowed better estimate of crop [SOURCE: Page_23]

transpiration therefore of water balance. It also allowed better estimate of pesticide interception by the crop which [SOURCE: Page_23]

was found to be crucial for the prediction of pesticides concentrations in water. STICS-MACRO is a credible [SOURCE: Page_23]

alternative to improve MACRO to study agronomic practices scenarios avoiding prior calibration phase. It offers [SOURCE: Page_23]

the advantage of being able to simulate complex cropping systems and to consider some agricultural practices such [SOURCE: Page_23]

as fertilization, mulch, crop residues management, etc. Further tests are needed to ensure STICS-MACRO can be [SOURCE: Page_23]

used to assess pesticides flows in a large range of situations. In particular, agro-pedoclimatic conditions involving [SOURCE: Page_23]

sandy or heavy clay soils, cold and warm climates, and various crops and cropping systems have to be studied. [SOURCE: Page_23]

These conditions will consequently allow the test of different pesticides (for example, pesticides more sorbed and [SOURCE: Page_23]

more persistent than imazamox and S-metolachlor). Tests are also needed on long term time series as it is usually [SOURCE: Page_23]

done for probabilistic risk assessment. However the STICS-MACRO modelling approach is promising to improve [SOURCE: Page_23]

the assessment of the environmental risks of pesticides used in cropping systems. [SOURCE: Page_23]
