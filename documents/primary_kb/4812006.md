12   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

13         The main goals of conservation agriculture are to enhance soil fertility and reduce soil [SOURCE: Page_1]

14   degradation, especially through erosion. However, conservation agriculture practices can [SOURCE: Page_1]

15   increase the risk of contamination by pesticides, mainly through vertical transfer via water flow. [SOURCE: Page_1]

16   Better understanding of their sorption and degradation processes is thus needed in conservation [SOURCE: Page_1]

17   agriculture as they control the amount of pesticide available for vertical transfer. The purpose [SOURCE: Page_1]

18   of our study was to investigate the sorption and degradation processes of nicosulfuron in soil [SOURCE: Page_1]

19   profiles (up to 90 cm deep) of a Vermic Umbrisol and a Stagnic Luvisol managed either in [SOURCE: Page_1]

20   conventional or in conservation agriculture. Two laboratory sorption and incubation [SOURCE: Page_1]

21   experiments were performed. Low sorption was observed regardless of the soil type, [SOURCE: Page_1]

22   agricultural management or depth, with a maximum value of 1.3 ± 2.0 L.kg-1. By the end of the [SOURCE: Page_1]

23   experiment (91 days), nicosulfuron mineralisation in the Vermic Umbrisol was similar for the [SOURCE: Page_1]

24   two types of agricultural management and rather depended on soil depth (29.0 ± 2.3% in the 0- [SOURCE: Page_1]

25   60 cm layers against 7.5 ± 1.4% in the 60-90 cm). In the Stagnic Luvisol, nicosulfuron [SOURCE: Page_1]

1 [SOURCE: Page_1]

26   mineralisation reach similar value in every layer of the conservation agriculture plot (26.5% ± [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   0.7%). On the conventional tillage plot, mineralisation decreased in the deepest layer (25-60 [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   cm) reaching only 18.4% ± 6.9% of the applied nicosulfuron. Regardless of the soil type or [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   agricultural management, non-extractable residue formation was identified as the main [SOURCE: Page_2]

37      1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

38      To enhance the sustainability of agricultural systems and decrease the impacts of [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   conventional agriculture, alternative cultivation practices such as conservation agriculture have [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   been developed. Conservation agriculture is defined as a combination of three main [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   interconnected soil-conservation techniques: (i) little or no soil disturbance, (ii) permanent soil [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   cover by crop residues and/or cover crops and (iii) diversification of plant species in the crop [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   rotation (FAO, 2016). The main goals of conservation agriculture are to reduce soil degradation [SOURCE: Page_2]

44   due to erosion but also the contamination of surface water by runoff or transfer of sorbed [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   agrochemicals on eroded soil particles (Holland, 2004; Hobbs et al., 2008). [SOURCE: Page_2]

46      Compared to conventional agriculture that includes soil tillage (sometimes with inversion [SOURCE: Page_2]

47   of topsoil layers), conservation agriculture enhances faunal activity, especially earthworm [SOURCE: Page_2]

48   activity, which results in a dense biomacropore network (Shipitalo et al., 2000). Moreover, [SOURCE: Page_2]

49   because the topsoil is disturbed less, it usually contains a greater continuity of vertically [SOURCE: Page_2]

50   oriented macropores, which leads to higher hydraulic conductivity than that of tilled soils (Wahl [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

51   et al., 2004). Increasing vertical transfer is one way to reduce runoff and thus surface water [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   contamination, but in return it increases the risk of deep vertical transfer to groundwater. This [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   is a major drawback of conservation agriculture, especially as reducing tillage intensity [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   generally increases weed pressure, which often leads to an increase in herbicide use (Alletto et [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   al., 2010). Better understanding of sorption and degradation processes in such agricultural [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   systems is thus critically needed as they control the amount of pesticide available for vertical [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   transfer. [SOURCE: Page_3]

58       One of the main changes of not inverting topsoil layers, as in conservation agriculture, is [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   the accumulation of dead residues or mulch at the soil surface, which enriches the first few cm [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   of soil in organic carbon. The carbon content then decreases with soil depth, reaching contents [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   less than or equal to those of conventionally tilled soil (Doran, 1980; Kay and VandenBygaart, [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   2002). For many pesticides, sorption can be related to soil organic matter content (Gaston et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   2007; Đurović et al., 2009; Sadegh-Zadeh et al., 2017). Thus, at an equivalent depth in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   topsoil, pesticide sorption is generally higher under conservation agriculture than conventional [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   agriculture because of the former’s higher carbon content (Gaston et al., 2003; Locke et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   2005). Deeper in the soil, sorption decreases because the carbon content is lower, and it tends [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   to follow the decrease in carbon content (Clay and Koskinen, 2003). In addition, the [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   accumulation of organic matter (and fertilisers) under conservation agriculture generally [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   acidifies the topsoil (Thompson and Whitney, 2000; Thomas et al., 2007; Obour et al., 2017;). [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   Weak acids and ionisable molecules dissociate to their anionic form as soil pH increases, which [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   lowers adsorption (Dyson et al., 2002). More acidic conditions in the topsoil may then increase [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   pesticide adsorption under conservation agriculture. For instance, Alletto et al. (2013) observed [SOURCE: Page_3]

73   a negative correlation between pH and S-metolachlor sorption in soils under conservation [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   agriculture. [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

75      Because of soil organic matter accumulation and less soil disturbance by agricultural [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   operations, microbial biomass and microbial activity in the topsoil is generally greater under [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   conservation agriculture than conventional agriculture (Kandeler et al., 1999). This increase in [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   microbial activity can sometimes increase pesticide degradation (Locke et al., 2005; [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   Zablotowicz et al., 2007), but pesticide degradation does not always reflect soil microbial [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   activity (Mahía et al., 2007; Okada et al., 2019). Decrease in substrate availability due to higher [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   adsorption is often mentioned as an explanation for low degradation under conservation [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   agriculture (Zablotowicz et al., 2000). Adaptation of the microbial community can also increase [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   pesticide degradation (Barriuso et al., 1996). Both may explain the lack of correlation between [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   total microbial activity and pesticide mineralisation (Alletto et al., 2013). On the other hand, [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   microbial activity and biomass are often correlated with soil pH, which strongly influences [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   pesticide degradation in soils (Kah et al., 2007). Acidification of the topsoil under conservation [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   agriculture may thus modify pesticide degradation. In the subsoil (> 25-30 cm deep), pesticide [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   degradation is generally lower, generally due to less microbial activity and/or biomass related [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   to lower carbon content, temperature and oxygenation (Fomsgaard, 1995; Bending and [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   Rodríguez-Cruz, 2007). [SOURCE: Page_4]

91      We studied the sorption and degradation of nicosulfuron in soil profiles (up to 90 cm deep) [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   of two soils, a Stagnic Luvisol and a Vermic Umbrisol, managed for more than 10 years under [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   two types of agricultural management: conventional agriculture (tillage, bare soil and [SOURCE: Page_4]

94   monoculture) and conservation agriculture (no tillage, cover crops and crop rotations). [SOURCE: Page_4]

95   Nicosulfuron is a post-emergence herbicide used to control grass species in maize. It is [SOURCE: Page_4]

96   considered environmentally safe because of low application rates (usually < 100 g.ha-1) [SOURCE: Page_4]

97   (Regitano and Koskinen, 2008). Nicosulfuron is however generally in anionic form in soils and [SOURCE: Page_4]

98   is considered to be highly mobile, which could result in groundwater contamination (Gonzalez [SOURCE: Page_4]

99   and Ukrainczyk, 1996). In addition, nicosulfuron generally has low sorption and fast [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

100   degradation rates (Olivera et al., 2001; Poppell et al., 2002; Azcarate et al., 2018; PPDB, 2020). [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   Recently, Cueff et al. (2020) reported high nicosulfuron leaching and a high degree of [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   preferential flow in undisturbed columns sampled in the same soils as in the present study. [SOURCE: Page_5]

104         2. Materials and methods [SOURCE: Page_5]

105         2.1 Sites and types of agricultural management [SOURCE: Page_5]

106         The two sites of this study are located in south-western France, in the Pyrénées-Atlantiques [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   (43.516532N, -0.260186W) and Gers (43.410980N, 0.284574E) departments. Both sites are [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   subjected to an altered oceanic climate. [SOURCE: Page_5]

109         The soil at the Pyrénées-Atlantiques site is a thick humic acid soil developed from [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   Quaternary silty alluvial deposits that is classified as a Vermic Umbrisol according to the World [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   Reference Base for Soil Resources (IUSS Working Group WRB, 2007) and as a veracrisol [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   according to the French Soil Classification (AFES, 2008) (locally called “Touyas”). It has a [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   rich, deep (50-80 cm deep) and well-structured organic horizon (Table 1). Its texture is [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   composed mainly of fine silt (> 450-500 g.kg-1), with a low proportion of sand (< 100-120 g.kg- 1 115       ). Clay minerals are mainly kaolinite, illite and vermiculite (Arrouays et al., 1992). It has a [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   high agronomic potential, especially for maize (mean yield ≈ 13-15 t.ha-1) and soya bean (mean [SOURCE: Page_5]

117   yield ≈ 3.7-4.2 t.ha-1) (most not irrigated). Hereafter, this site is referred to as the VER [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   (veracrisol) site. [SOURCE: Page_5]

119         The soil at the Gers site is classified as a Stagnic Luvisol (IUSS Working Group WRB, [SOURCE: Page_5]

120   2007) and a luvisol redoxisol (AFES, 2008) (locally called “Boulbènes”), with a loamy topsoil [SOURCE: Page_5]

121   layer (fine + coarse silt ≈ 450-500 g.kg-1) and an illuvial clay horizon that appears at ca. 45-55 [SOURCE: Page_5]

122   cm (Table 1). Clay minerals are mainly illite and smectite (Michel et al., 2003). The substratum [SOURCE: Page_5]

123   is an alluvial pebbly layer with low permeability that appears at ca. 60-80 cm. With low organic [SOURCE: Page_5]

124   carbon contents (usually < 7-8 g.kg-1) in the topsoil and high silt contents, this soil is strongly [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

125   sensitive to crusting. Because of these properties (especially hydromorphic conditions at low [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   depth), it has a moderate agronomic potential for mainly irrigated crops (mean maize yields ≈ [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   10-12 t.ha-1). Hereafter, this site is referred to as the LUV (luvisol) site. [SOURCE: Page_6]

128       Each site consists of two adjacent plots managed under conventional (TILL) and [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   conservation (CONS) practices. The conventional plots have been cultivated under maize [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   monoculture for more than 40 years, and the soil is left bare in winter. Tillage depth with a [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   mouldboard plough is ca. 22-23 cm at the VER site and 30 cm at the LUV site. In both [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   conservation plots (VER and LUV), agricultural management consists of (i) no-tillage [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   practices, (ii) permanent soil surface cover by mulch and cover crop mixtures (phacelia and [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   faba bean at the VER site, two cereals and faba bean at the LUV site) sown during fallow [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   periods and (iii) a diversified crop rotation (maize, soya bean, cereals and sometimes intercrops [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   of cereals and legumes). These conservation agriculture practices have been performed since [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   2006 and 2000 at the VER and LUV sites, respectively. [SOURCE: Page_6]

144   each profile at the VER and LUV site, respectively. Soils were air-dried and sieved at 5 mm [SOURCE: Page_6]

179   sealed incubation jar that was large enough to prevent formation of anoxic conditions in the [SOURCE: Page_8]

245      3.1 Soil organic carbon content and mineralisation [SOURCE: Page_11]

394      4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_17]

395          4.1.Nicosulfuron behaviour in soil [SOURCE: Page_17]

396      Adsorption of nicosulfuron was low regardless of the agricultural management or depth, [SOURCE: Page_17]

397   never exceeding 1.3 ± 2.0 L.kg-1 (Table 2). The few studies on nicosulfuron available also [SOURCE: Page_17]

398   generally reported low sorption, with sorption coefficients (Kd or Kf, with n usually close to 1) [SOURCE: Page_17]

399   ranging from 0.02-1.8 L.kg-1 in soils from the United States, Brazil, Argentina and Europe [SOURCE: Page_17]

400   (EFSA, 2007; Regitano and Koskinen, 2008; Azcarate et al., 2015). To our knowledge, only [SOURCE: Page_17]

401   two studies reported relatively high values of nicosulfuron Kf : up to 8.8 in a silt-loam soil in [SOURCE: Page_17]

402   Iowa, United States (Gonzalez and Ukrainczyk, 1996) and up to 16.1, in volcanic ash-derived [SOURCE: Page_17]

403   soils, in Chile (Caceres-Jensen et al., 2020). Nicosulfuron is a weak acid (pKa of 4.78 and 7.58; [SOURCE: Page_17]

404   EFSA, 2007), which results in a mostly ionic form in most agricultural soils and thus little [SOURCE: Page_17]

405   interaction with soil components, which can explain the low sorption. Nicosulfuron sorption [SOURCE: Page_17]

406   was correlated with soil organic carbon content (r = -0.03) in either soil type, as suggested by [SOURCE: Page_17]

407   the KOC values; this result agrees with a previous study (Olivera et al., 2001). Ukrainczyk and [SOURCE: Page_17]

408   Rashid (1995), however, reported that nicosulfuron sorption may depend instead on clay [SOURCE: Page_17]

409   content, especially smectites, on which it could be rapidly and irreversibly sorbed. Such high [SOURCE: Page_17]

410   sorption of nicosulfuron could strongly decrease leaching to groundwater (Gonzalez and [SOURCE: Page_17]

411   Ukrainczyk, 1999). In our study, however, nicosulfuron sorption was not correlated with clay [SOURCE: Page_17]

412   content (r = 0.25). Caceres-Jensen et al. (2020) also reported irreversibility of the nicosulfuron [SOURCE: Page_17]

413   sorption and this could be related to sorption mechanisms, specific to volcanic soils. In the same [SOURCE: Page_17]

414   soil type as in the present study, we recently showed a high potential for nicosulfuron leaching [SOURCE: Page_17]

415   up to 30 cm in undisturbed soil columns (Cueff et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_17]

17 [SOURCE: Page_17]

416      Large amounts of nicosulfuron residues were still available in the water-soluble fraction at [SOURCE: Page_18]

417   the end of incubation, especially in the deep soil layers, which probably resulted from the [SOURCE: Page_18]

418   combination of low sorption and low mineralisation. Based on previous studies, nicosulfuron [SOURCE: Page_18]

419   often mineralised more than other pesticides, such as acifluorfen, bentazon and chlorimuron, at [SOURCE: Page_18]

420   an equivalent depth and incubation time (Reddy et al., 1995; Gaston et al., 1996; Gaston and [SOURCE: Page_18]

421   Locke, 2000). Boivin et al. (2004) reported that only 11% of applied bentazon (with low [SOURCE: Page_18]

422   adsorption and a half-life similar to that of nicosulfuron) mineralised after 160 days in a Stagnic [SOURCE: Page_18]

423   Luvisol (north-eastern France). Thus, in a similar soil type and with a longer incubation period, [SOURCE: Page_18]

424   the mineralised fraction of bentazon was one-third that of nicosulfuron. We also identified [SOURCE: Page_18]

425   strong and rapid formation of NER in both soils, which represented the main dissipation [SOURCE: Page_18]

426   pathway for nicosulfuron. NER likely originated from biodegradation of nicosulfuron rather [SOURCE: Page_18]

427   than from physical or chemical bonding given the low interaction of nicosulfuron with the soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

428   matrix. It has been suggested for several pesticides that most NER have a biogenic origin [SOURCE: Page_18]

429   (Nowak et al., 2011, 2013), as suggested by the relatively strong correlation (r = 0.62) between [SOURCE: Page_18]

430   the first-order rate constant of nicosulfuron mineralisation (𝑏𝑁 ) and the modelled final [SOURCE: Page_18]

431   cumulative NER fraction (𝑎𝑁𝐸𝑅 ). Despite uncertainties about the reversibility of this dissipation [SOURCE: Page_18]

432   process, NER formation is generally assumed to decrease the bioavailability of pesticides for a [SOURCE: Page_18]

433   long time (Barriuso et al., 2008; Schäffer et al., 2018). Several studies indicated that most [SOURCE: Page_18]

434   sulfonylurea herbicides had high potential to contaminate groundwater (Martins and Mermoud, [SOURCE: Page_18]

435   1999; Sondhia, 2009; Azcarate et al., 2015). Nicosulfuron’s relatively low application rate (< [SOURCE: Page_18]

436   100 g.ha-1) and relatively rapid dissipation by NER formation and mineralisation in surface [SOURCE: Page_18]

437   layers could indicate little risk of transfer to groundwater. However, the low sorption in surface [SOURCE: Page_18]

438   layers and slower dissipation in deeper layers suggest that groundwater contamination cannot [SOURCE: Page_18]

439   be excluded, especially in situations of preferential transport. [SOURCE: Page_18]

440          4.2. Influence of soil type on nicosulfuron behaviour [SOURCE: Page_18]

18 [SOURCE: Page_18]

441       We studied the fate of nicosulfuron at several depths in two soil types. Despite a strong [SOURCE: Page_19]

442   decrease in microbial activity and biomass with depth, nicosulfuron mineralisation varied little [SOURCE: Page_19]

443   in the 0-60 cm layer but was lower below 60 cm. Observing mineralisation in the 25-60 cm [SOURCE: Page_19]

444   layer similar to that in surface layers in both soil types was surprising, as mineralisation of [SOURCE: Page_19]

445   several pesticides has been observed to decrease with depth in agricultural soils, in particular [SOURCE: Page_19]

446   under the plough layer (0-30 cm) (Larsen et al., 2000; Rodríguez Cruz et al., 2008). Pesticide [SOURCE: Page_19]

447   mineralisation is generally assumed to be a function of soil organic carbon content, which [SOURCE: Page_19]

448   influences microbial activity and biomass (Holden and Fierer, 2005). However, in our study [SOURCE: Page_19]

449   nicosulfuron mineralisation was not correlated with microbial biomass (r = 0.09) or microbial [SOURCE: Page_19]

450   activity (r = 0.02). [SOURCE: Page_19]

451       Differences between microbial activity and pesticide mineralisation can be due to [SOURCE: Page_19]

452   development of a microbial community that specialises in degrading a regularly applied [SOURCE: Page_19]

453   pesticide. Several microbial strains that can degrade nicosulfuron directly have been identified [SOURCE: Page_19]

454   in the literature (Lu et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2012; Song et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2015; Carles [SOURCE: Page_19]

455   et al., 2017). Higher mineralisation potential in soil previously exposed to a given pesticide has [SOURCE: Page_19]

456   already been observed for several pesticides (Sørensen and Aamand, 2003; Zablotowicz et al., [SOURCE: Page_19]

457   2006). Although this phenomenon has yet to be observed for nicosulfuron, specific degradation [SOURCE: Page_19]

458   is a likely hypothesis to explain the nicosulfuron mineralisation in our study, since it was [SOURCE: Page_19]

459   applied regularly to the studied fields. [SOURCE: Page_19]

460       4.3. Influence of agricultural management [SOURCE: Page_19]

461       In both soil types, higher microbial activity was measured in the topsoil layer (0-10 cm) in [SOURCE: Page_19]

462   the CONS plot than in the TILL plot and in the deeper soil layers. As mentioned by several [SOURCE: Page_19]

463   authors, this difference in microbial activity probably resulted from accumulation of organic [SOURCE: Page_19]

464   carbon at the soil surface in untilled soils (Kandeler et al., 1999). Despite much higher carbon [SOURCE: Page_19]

465   content at the VER site and similar microbial biomass at both sites, microbial activity [SOURCE: Page_19]

19 [SOURCE: Page_19]

466   (illustrated here by soil carbon mineralisation) appeared to be much higher at the LUV site. At [SOURCE: Page_20]

467   the LUV site, total mineralisation was strongly correlated with organic carbon (r = 0.90) and [SOURCE: Page_20]

468   microbial biomass (r = 0.93). At the VER site, however, the correlations were much weaker [SOURCE: Page_20]

469   (non-significant for organic carbon and r = 0.66 for microbial biomass), which may indicate [SOURCE: Page_20]

470   high organo-mineral interactions at the VER site that limited microbial access to carbon for [SOURCE: Page_20]

471   mineralisation (Dungait et al., 2012). Conservation agriculture generally favours these [SOURCE: Page_20]

472   interactions (Veloso et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_20]

473      Although surface layers had higher microbial activity and biomass, nicosulfuron [SOURCE: Page_20]

474   mineralisation in them was no higher than that in the 25-60 cm layer in the CONS plot at both [SOURCE: Page_20]

475   sites. Lower pesticide mineralisation in the topsoil under conservation agriculture than in deeper [SOURCE: Page_20]

476   layers, or than in topsoil layers under conventional agriculture, is generally attributed to a [SOURCE: Page_20]

477   decrease in bioavailability. This decrease is related to higher sorption of pesticides due to an [SOURCE: Page_20]

478   increase in organic carbon content at the soil surface and the presence of crop residues that can [SOURCE: Page_20]

479   intercept pesticides and reduce their availability to degrading microorganisms (Reddy and [SOURCE: Page_20]

480   Locke, 1998; Zablotowicz et al., 2000; Rodríguez Cruz et al., 2006; Alletto et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_20]

481   Although we observed low sorption of nicosulfuron in the soil layers studied, the higher [SOURCE: Page_20]

482   methanol-extractable fraction in the surface layers in CONS plots than in TILL plots from the [SOURCE: Page_20]

483   beginning of incubation could indicate a lower nicosulfuron availability in the soil solution, [SOURCE: Page_20]

484   thus limiting its mineralisation by microorganisms. [SOURCE: Page_20]

485      The NER fraction was always the largest dissipation pathway for nicosulfuron, especially [SOURCE: Page_20]

486   under conservation agriculture, while the water-extractable fraction was always lower than that [SOURCE: Page_20]

487   under conventional agriculture. In particular, a large water-extractable fraction was observed in [SOURCE: Page_20]

488   the 25-60 cm layer in the TILL plot at the LUV site. This suggested low dissipation (through [SOURCE: Page_20]

489   mineralisation and NER formation) of nicosulfuron, even three months after application. If [SOURCE: Page_20]

490   nicosulfuron reaches this depth under field conditions, the TILL plot is likely to leach more of [SOURCE: Page_20]

499      5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_21]

500   We studied sorption and degradation processes of nicosulfuron in two soil types from south- [SOURCE: Page_21]

501   western France managed under either conservation or conventional agriculture. Sorption and [SOURCE: Page_21]

502   incubation experiments were performed under controlled laboratory conditions using 14C- [SOURCE: Page_21]

503   nicosulfuron and used soil samples collected at several depths in the two soil types under both [SOURCE: Page_21]

504   types of agricultural management. Little was known about the environmental behaviour of [SOURCE: Page_21]

505   nicosulfuron, especially under contrasting types of agricultural management. Our results [SOURCE: Page_21]

506   confirmed the low sorption affinity of nicosulfuron for soil components, which resulted in no [SOURCE: Page_21]

507   significant difference in its sorption behaviour between the two types of agricultural [SOURCE: Page_21]

508   management studied. Nicosulfuron mineralisation was influenced by mainly soil depth. For [SOURCE: Page_21]

509   both soil types and types of agricultural management, the 25-60 cm layer had the same [SOURCE: Page_21]

510   mineralisation as that in the topsoil layers but much lower microbial activity, suggesting [SOURCE: Page_21]

511   development of a specific nicosulfuron-degrading microbial community. Formation of non- [SOURCE: Page_21]

512   extractable residues is one of the main nicosulfuron dissipation pathways, especially for [SOURCE: Page_21]

513   conservation agriculture systems. For these reasons, little nicosulfuron may be transferred to [SOURCE: Page_21]

514   groundwater under conservation agriculture, especially in a Stagnic Luvisol. However, due to [SOURCE: Page_21]

21 [SOURCE: Page_21]

515   a generally higher proportion of macropores in these systems, the risk of preferential flow [SOURCE: Page_22]

516   cannot be ignored. [SOURCE: Page_22]
