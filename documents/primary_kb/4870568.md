Abstract [SOURCE: Page_4]

This study focuses on the vulnerability of fishery resources in three lakes—Hourtin, Lacanau, and Cazaux-Sanguinet—through habitat modeling.Macrophytes, which play a crucial role in the ecological services of shallow lakes, are sensitive to both anthropogenic and natural disturbances. Eutrophication and changes in hydrological regimes affect their distribution by altering water trans- parency and levels, reducing light availability at the bottom. We utilized an integrated dataset (2011–2023) to explore the factors influencing the distribution of 13 macrophyte species. Species distribution models (SDMs)—specifically GBM, GAM, MAXENT, and ANN—were applied using 16 environmental variables to predict current distribution patterns, which are essential for guiding ecosystem management strategies. Additionally, habitat models encapsulate the ecological niches of species based on various environ- mental variables. We projected these models under a climate warming scenario to identify winning and losing species, as well as regions experiencing changes in species assemblages. Our findings indicate that some species will restrict their distribution due to a 2°C temperature increase, while others will expand theirs. The models generated under current conditions identified new areas likely to harbor undiscovered populations in the three lakes. These results have significant conservation implications, particularly for species threatened with extinction, which are expected to lose suitable habitats in a climate change scenario. Les modèles de distribution des espèces (SDM) - en particulier GBM, GAM, MAXENT et ANN - ont été appliqués en utilisant 16 variables environnementales pour prédire les schémas de distribution actuels, qui sont essentiels pour guider les stratégies de gestion des écosystèmes. En outre, les modèles d’habitat encapsulent les niches écologiques des espèces en fonction de diverses variables environnementales. Nous avons projeté ces modèles dans le cadre d’un scénario de réchauffement climatique afin d’identifier les espèces gagnantes et perdantes, ainsi que les régions connaissant des changements dans les assemblages d’espèces. Nos résultats indiquent que certaines espèces restreindront leur distribution en raison d’une augmentation de la température de 2°C, tandis que d’autres l’étendront. Les modèles générés dans les conditions actuelles ont permis d’identifier de nouvelles zones susceptibles d’abriter des populations inconnues dans les trois lacs. Ces résultats ont des implications importantes en matière de conservation, en particulier pour les espèces menacées d’extinction, qui devraient perdre des habitats adéquats dans un scénario de changement climatique. 1 Introduction [SOURCE: Page_12]

1.1      Contexte As the human population continues to expand, the impact of human activities on ecosystems is anticipated to escalate. This includes local pressures such as exploitation, pollution, habitat fragmentation, loss, and the introduction of non-native species. Whose pressures are projected to increase, especially if current practices persist without significant changes. (Bellard et al., 2012; Leadley et al., 2010; Shin et al., 2019; Steffen et al., 2018). Associated with climate change, these pressures are likely to exacerbate the already observed detrimental impacts on ecosystems, potentially leading to important consequences for human societies (Díaz et al., 2019; Lotze et al., 2019; Thiault et al., 2019; Tittensor et al., 2019). Decades of research have provided crucial insights into how species might respond to climate change in the near future (Parmesan et al., 2015;Sinervo et al., 2010; Tingley et al., 2012;Visser et al., 1998). These responses include adaptation, shifts in geographic range, or local extinction, which can lead to significant changes in species composition, abundance, and biotic interactions, such as food webs and ecosystem functioning (Weiskopf et al., 2020; Pereira et al., 2010; Román-Palacios and Wiens, 2020). Furthermore, the findings of the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment underscore the vulnerability of aquatic ecosystems to anthropogenic activities and environmental shifts. Their degradation and decline are progressing at an alarming pace, oftentimes outpacing that of marine and terrestrial ecosystems (Millennium Ecosystem Assessment, 2005; Dudgeon et al., 2006; Heino et al., 2009). Macrophytes are a key functional component of continental hydrosystems, playing a crucial role in shaping the structure and functioning of freshwater ecosystems (Gurnell et al., 2012). They provide numerous ecological services, including enhancing water transparency, reducing nutrient levels, stabilizing sediments, suppressing phytoplankton growth, and offering food and habitats for fish and zooplankton (Anda et al., 2016; Bakker et al., 2012; Carr et al., 2010; Quilliam et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_12]

1 [SOURCE: Page_12]

However, multiple factors, such as land use changes, climate change, and the introduction of invasive species, are increasingly threatening macrophyte communities (Sala et al., 2000). In particular, climate change is expected to have profound effects on freshwater ecosystems, potentially altering the dynamics of macrophyte populations and their role in maintaining ecosystem balance. However, with increasing eutrophication, anthropogenic activities, and global climate change, macrophytes abundance in shallow lakes has undergone a continuous global reduction, with accelerating decline (Ersoy et al., 2020; Phillips et al., 2016; Xia et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2017). Macrophytes are sensitive to various environmental factors, including nutrient enrichment, algal blooms, and extreme climate changes (Luo et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2008; Sand-Jensen et al., 2000; Wang et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019). However, the driving forces of macrophyte dynamics vary significantly due to the diversity of anthropogenic and natural disturbances, making it challenging to estimate the recovery potential of declined macrophytes in shallow lakes. Macrophyte distribution, growth, and reproduction are highly dependent on the availability of underwater light (Ersoy et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020). Macrophytes exhibit varying sensitivities to various natural and human pressures, making them good indicators of the ecological status of a river (Li et al.,2010, Lewin et al., 2012). Aquitaine’s lakes are an integral part of the region’s natural and landscape heritage (Jamoneau et al., 2019). They play an essential role in local ecosystems and are a key part of the region’s identity (Jamoneau et al., 2019). These lakes represent France’s largest lowland lake ecosystems (jamoneau et al., 2019). However, human activities such as eutrophication, pollution, and development have had a significant negative impact on the composition and abundance of macrophyte communities in these lakes (Jamoneau et al., 2019). In this context, it is essential to develop the idea of conservation to meet these challenges proactively. Species conservation involves a holistic approach aimed at protecting and restoring populations of endangered species (Reid et al 2019). To evaluate the impacts of climate change on biodiversity and ecosystem goods and services, species distribution models (SDMs) have emerged as relevant tools (Cheung et al., 2009; Robinson et al., 2017). These models, also known as environmental niche models (ENMs), are empirical statistical approaches that relate species distribution data (occurrences or abundances) to environmental descriptors. By doing so, they predict the potential presence of species in unsampled locations or time periods. SDMs are based on the ecological niche concept introduced by Hutchinson (1957). As such, distribution models have [SOURCE: Page_13]

2 [SOURCE: Page_13]

provided researchers with an innovative tool to explore diverse questions in ecology, evolution, and conservation. For example, SDMs have gained popularity in projecting past, present, and future species distributions under various climate change scenarios (Bellard et al., 2012; Gavish et al., 2017; Guisan and Thuiller, 2005). They have been used to study relationships between environmental parameters and species (Mac Nally and Fleishman 2004), characteristics and spatial configuration of habitats that allow persistence of species in landscapes (Arau´ jo and Williams 2000, Ferrier et al. 2002a, Scotts and Drielsma 2003), invasive potential of non-native species (Peterson 2003, Goolsby 2004), species’ distributions in past (Hugall et al. 2002,Peterson et al. 2004) or future climates (Bakkenes et al. 2002, Skov and Svenning 2004, Arau´ jo et al. 2004, Thomas et al. 2004, Thuiller et al. 2005), and ecological and geographic differentiation of the distributions of closely-related species (Cicero 2004, Graham et al. 2004) SDMs have gained popularity in projecting past, present, and future species distributions under various climate change scenarios (Bellard et al., 2012; Gavish et al., 2017; Guisan and Thuiller,2005). They provide valuable insights into how climate change may affect species distributions and the services provided by ecosystems. These models have been applied to various environments, including terrestrial, freshwater, and marine, and have been used to study diverse biological groups. Furthermore, SDMs are multifunctional tools that can inform conservation decisions by identifying priority areas, assessing the impacts of climate change, predicting biological invasions, planning ecological restoration, and evaluating migration corridors. They provide crucial information for conservation planning and ecosystem management (Guisan and Zimmermann, 2000; Fleishman et al., 2001; Elith et al., 2006; Austin, 2007; Padalia et al., 2014,margules et pressey 2000). In spatial ecology, the choice of study area scale, expressed as resolution (grain size) and extent, is crucial for achieving more accurate conservation outcomes (Franklin et al 2013). Modelling a large area does not necessarily imply considering a coarse resolution. As Wiens (2002) emphasized, addressing any spatial ecology question requires a thorough consideration of the specific components at which data are gathered or examined. Given the importance of Aquitaine’s lakes as productive areas, in terms of natural resources, biodiversity, and ecosystem services, a key question arises: how macrophytes will be distributed in response to climate change? 2.1     Study area Fifteen natural lakes are situated along the western French Atlantic coast, from the Gironde estuary to the Pyrenees Mountains in the south. 2.3.2    Wind Exposure Computation [SOURCE: Page_20]

The wind exposure (WEX) was computed from raster data provided by the Copernicus Climate Change Data Store, which contained the mean monthly horizontal air movement speeds (u and v components). Assuming constant speed and direction across each single lake, the two components were re-computed as weighted averages at the lake centroids based on the inverse great circle distances to the individual raster grid cells. Subsequently, mean monthly speeds and angles were computed for each lake (i.e., centroid). Having the mean monthly, angles of direction the mean monthly fetch, representing the continuous [SOURCE: Page_20]

9 [SOURCE: Page_20]

length of the water surface passed over by the wind before reaching the opposite shore, was individually determined for each site. In short, this involved delineating the section of the shoreline within a given site constructing a straight line in the appropriate angle from each individual point making up that section of the shoreline to the opposite shore and measuring its length while accounting for complex geometries and islands. The median fetch value within each site was then computed and assigned to that site. Finally, monthly wind exposure (WEX) for each site was computed by multiplying monthly fetch with the corresponding mean monthly wind speed. These monthly WEX values were aggregated to annual WEX for each study site by summation. [SOURCE: Page_21]

4 Discussion This study was a triple-purpose analysis of a large dataset reflecting the spatial distribution of macrophytes in Aquitaine Lakes in southwestern France. The first aim was to predict the potential and evaluate the variability in projections of climate change impacts on native and invasive macrophytes species. Secondly, we attempted to describe the ecological responses of 13 macrophytes species to several environmental and non-environmental variables using a single model. The third goal was to determine hotspots to identify which sites need protection for the Isoetes group, a heritage group of macrophytes. [SOURCE: Page_71]

4.1      Prediction quality We demonstrated the ability to reliably predict current and future macrophyte distributions using species distribution models based on various environmental variables, such as physical, hydrody- namic, and landscape factors. All the models applied (GBM, GAM, Maxent, ANN) produced accurate predictions, showing similar performance with a moderate AUC of 0.65 (Figure^2). This led us to adopt a consensus method that combines the predictions of these models for climate change projections, specifically using an ensemble approach with different AUC values (Marmion et al. 2009). The GBM model implemented, in addition to making predictions, has allowed us to understand the importance of three key environmental variables (WAW _PCT _PERM, MEE, and TPI) in the spatial structuring of species. These variables were identified as the most significant after analyzing multiple variables with the GBM model. However, their influence varies depending on the species. For example, the model identifies WAW _PCT _PERM as a major environmental determinant for exotic species, whereas native species seem to be primarily affected by MEE. Although WAW _PCT _PERM and TPI are also limiting factors for native species, they are considered secondary. [SOURCE: Page_71]

60 [SOURCE: Page_71]

4.2     Distribution of native species Current distribution maps of native species in the lakes of Médoc (HOU and LAC) and Landes (CAZ) reveal a heterogeneous distribution depending on the species (Figure 3.5,3.6,3.7). Species such as C. verticillato-inundata, C. fragifera, J. bulbosus, L. uniflora, L. dortmanna, P. polygonifolius, and S. pungens are present in all the lakes, although C. fragifera is absent from certain sites in the north-east and south-west. In contrast, N. lutea, N. hyalina, and L. vulgaris are found in a few sites in the north-west, east, and south-east, respectively. Vanden Berghen (1964, 1968, 1969) reported the presence of C. fragifera on the shores of the two Médoc lakes, and more recent studies also confirm the presence of this species (Clément and Aidoud, 2009). The heterogeneous distribution of J. bulbosus (Figure 6) might be explained by local variations in environmental conditions, such as lake depth and hydrodynamics, which restrict its presence to the northeast and southwest regions. Previous studies indicate that this species has specific habitat preferences, including gently sloping areas, intense hydrodynamics, and a high proportion of coarse sand (Bertrin, 2018; Saltmarsh, Mauchamp and Rambal, 2006). N. lutea is found in specific areas, such as the north-west, and shows well-defined ecological prefer- ences. It favors biotopes characterized by a marked slope, low hydrodynamics, and a rich organic substrate (Bertrin, 2018; Ribaudo et al., 2015). These preferences are supported by NMDS analysis results (Figure), which reveal that N. lutea is primarily present in cultivated areas. The distribution map of the isoetid community, represented here by L. dortmanna, L. uniflora, and C. verticillato-inundata, shows that this community is distributed regardless of bank orientation. It is present in both wave-exposed and wave-protected sectors. This community, including L.dortmanna and L. uniflora, consists of plants with short, rigid leaf rosettes and deep roots, enabling them to survive in areas of the lake with strong hydrodynamics (Bertrin, 2018). Additionally, the western shores of these lakes, sheltered from ocean winds by the littoral dune, experience lower hydrodynamics, which favors the colonization of these species (Bertrin, 2018; Dutartre and Sarat, 2016). To predict future changes and enhance our understanding of native species dynamics in these lakes, we developed maps forecasting potential shifts in their distribution under a climate change scenario, specifically a 2°C temperature rise. The projections indicate that J. bulbousus and P. polygonifolius [SOURCE: Page_72]

61 [SOURCE: Page_72]

are expected to be present on the eastern shore of HOU and LAC lakes, while N. lutea will migrate towards the east coast. This suggests that these species are adapting to a more favorable environment by moving to cooler areas that better support their development. Numerous studies have shown that climate model predictions can accurately forecast species move- ments (Morin and Thuiller 2009; Anderson 2013). Similarly, patterns observed in fish communities indicate that thermophilic species benefit from rising water temperatures and may accelerate the extirpation of endemic species (Olden et al. 2006). This risk is further increased by the growing spatial overlap between exotic and endemic species, as documented in the Mediterranean region Mouillot 2008. The relationship between the Topographic Position Index (TPI) and species distribution reveals distinct patterns. The first group of species, such as C. verticillato-inundata, shows a uniform probability of presence across different TPI values. This indicates that these species are generalists, thriving equally well in both depressed (negative TPI) and elevated (positive TPI) areas. They are not confined to specific topographic conditions and can adapt to a wide range of ecological environments. In contrast, the second group, including species like J. bulbosus and L. dortmanna, prefers flat areas (positive TPI). These species are better suited to slightly elevated or flat environments where conditions such as soil moisture and nutrient availability are more stable and uniform. Their habitat preferences are characterized by gentle slopes, intense hydrodynamics, and high fractions of coarse sand (Bertrin 2018; Saltmarsh, Mauchamp, and Rambal 200). Isoetid vegetation that develops in areas with low pressure of variations in Minimum Energy Ex- penditure (MEE). In contrast, L. grandiflora is widespread throughout Lake Cazaux but is absent from the western part of Lake Lacanau. In Lake Hourtin, it is sparsely present, primarily in the northwestern region, while in Lake Lacanau, its distribution is more heterogeneous, with a predominant concentration in the northwest. The presence of L. grandiflora in the Médoc lakes remains low, but this plant thrives in mesotrophic and eutrophic waters (Dutartre and Rebillard 2015, Pieret, Delbart, and Mahy 2009). Therefore, its expansion could increase if the water quality in the studied lakes deteriorates. The limited presence of E. densa and L. major can be attributed to depth-related factors. Our study focuses on species distribution at a depth of 1 meter, while Bertrin et al. (2007) noted thatE. densa typically occurs between 2.5 and 3.5 meters, and L. major between 2 and 4 meters. Since our observations are restricted to shallow waters, it is likely that these hydrophytes were not detected because they grow at greater depths. Thus, the results indicating a decline may not accurately reflect the actual dynamics of E. densa and L. major. To anticipate future developments and enhance our understanding of the dynamics of exotic species in these lakes, we have created maps projecting potential changes in their distribution under a climate change scenario, specifically with a 2°C increase in temperature. These maps reveal varied responses (Figure 3.24). For example, E. densa is projected to disappear from Lakes CAZ and LAC, and will only be present at a single site in the eastern part of Lake Hourtin. L. major is expected to vanish from all the lakes. In contrast, L. grandiflora is anticipated to remain present along the entire shoreline of Lake CAZ and to migrate towards the western shores of the Médoc lakes, particularly Lakes HOU and LAC. The apparent absence of E. densa and L. major under future conditions may be attributed to an initial lack of sufficient data. Early in the study, the available data might not have fully captured all possible future environmental conditions, leading to incomplete or inaccurate predictions of species [SOURCE: Page_74]

63 [SOURCE: Page_74]

distribution. This absence does not necessarily mean that these species cannot survive or adapt to future conditions but highlights the need for additional data to improve the accuracy of future projections. Collecting more comprehensive data will help refine predictions and better understand the potential presence of these species in future scenarios. Higher temperatures could indeed promote the colonization and winter survival of certain invasive aquatic plant species, especially those originating from tropical regions whose distribution was limited by minimum winter temperatures (Mckee et al., 2002; Alahuhta et al., 2011; Peeters et al., 2013; Li et al., 2016). Studies showed that perennial submerged macrophytes and floating macrophytes could outcompete annual submerged species due to climate warming (Netten et al., 2010, 2011). Our results confirmed this hypothesis, revealing that rising temperatures could not only expand the distribution of L. major across all lake shores but also lead to the disappearance of E. densa in some lakes in response to altered thermal and ecological constraints. The distribution of plant species is influenced by the Topographic Position Index (TPI). L. major and E. densa are typically found in areas with negative TPI, favoring low-lying environments like valleys, where water and nutrients accumulate, creating ideal conditions for growth. In contrast, L. grandiflora thrives in flat areas with zero TPI, benefiting from stable soil and moisture conditions that promote its colonization. Additionally, these species tend to inhabit deeper zones between 2 and 4 meters (Bertrin et al. 2017), which are less exposed to wave action and characterized by sediment rich in organic matter (Bertrin 2018). Extended response curves indicate that these species are capable of surviving and thriving across a broad range of environmental conditions, including significant variations in Minimum Energy Expenditure (MEE). In other words, they demonstrate a high level of flexibility with respect to soil conditions affected by trampling. These species possess high ecological plasticity, allowing them to adjust to various levels of disturbance and stress. This flexibility enables them to colonize diverse habitats and to spread across a wide geographical range. [SOURCE: Page_75]

4.5     Scopes Although the models used were able to make robust predictions of the spatial distribution of macrophytes, their long-term reliability remains difficult to assess. Even with excellent predictions, the uncertainties in the projections are highly dependent on the uncertainties associated with the climate models used. Indeed, using a single scenario from a single climate model to determine the sensitivity of many species means ignoring a significant proportion of the climate uncertainty. In addition, current models project areas where combinations of environmental variables will be suitable for organisms in the future, thus defining potential habitats. However, as long as substrate parameters and biotic interactions are not integrated, these potential habitats cannot be assimilated into the actual future distribution of species. Dispersal and colonization capacity, which are essential if species are to reach these new habitats, are not currently considered due to persistent knowledge gaps. Additionally, our models did not account for local factors such as light, water slope, or water quality (pH, nutrients, turbidity, etc.), which can significantly impact the suitability of aquatic environments for the taxa under study (Feijoo et al. 2002; Hussner et al. 2009; Hussner 2010; Bornette and Puijalon 2010). Finally, abiotic factors in aquatic habitats play a crucial role in the structuring of macrophyte communities. However, in the context of Aquitaine’s coastal lakes, the simultaneous presence of heritage species and exotic species along the shores of the same lake raises questions about competition and spatial sharing between these species. While studies have extensively explored competition and interspecific interactions involving native species, primarily in lakes in northern Europe and North (Spierenburgetal. 2010) America, (Borman et al. 2009)it is also important to [SOURCE: Page_76]

65 [SOURCE: Page_76]

consider biotic factors. Specifically, examining the potential competition between native and exotic species is crucial. Therefore, a more comprehensive analysis should integrate abiotic influences while also addressing the specific biotic interactions within these aquatic environments. Conclusion This study represents an attempt at spatial modeling of macrophytes in Aquitaine lakes, with the aim of assessing their vulnerability in the face of climatic, hydrodynamic, and landscape changes. As part of the conservation of this area, we explored tools enabling managers to assess the state of the ecosystem and predict its future evolution. Species distribution models were used to visualize the current distribution of macrophytes and to estimate the future evolution of species as a function of different environmental parameters. Our results showed that endemic species are highly vulnerable to climate change, due to environmental changes and land use. Projections indicate that the coldest areas of lakes could serve as refuges for cold-water species. At the same time, warmer waters will lead to a change in macrophyte assemblages, with losses and gains of species that will alter community composition. Endemic species are subject to two types of pressure: biotic pressure from exotic species and abiotic pressure from warming waters. These two factors are likely to add up, increasing the vulnerability of local species. In addition, human activities could exacerbate this situation by accelerating habitat fragmentation. For future management, we propose that conservation plans be drawn up, considering the preservation of groups of isoetids and heritage species, as well as a management plan for invasive species. In order to strengthen this approach into a genuine management tool, future research should couple habitat models with macrophyte community dynamics, to analyze the impacts of global change on macrophyte composition, their ecological interactions, and the ecosystem services they provide.
