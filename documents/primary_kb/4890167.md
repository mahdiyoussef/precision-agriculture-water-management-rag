1   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

2   Warming trends in Patagonia and severe droughts in recent decades are still poorly [SOURCE: Page_2]

3   understood in terms of their hydrological effects. The effects of climate change on water [SOURCE: Page_2]

4   dynamics in addition to human water management could generate a future water scarcity [SOURCE: Page_2]

5   scenario in one of the regions with the most abundant water resources of Chile. The aim of [SOURCE: Page_2]

6   this work is to focus on assessing the impacts of warming trends on water dynamics in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

7   Patagonian Simpson River watershed during the last two decades. We estimated anomalies in [SOURCE: Page_2]

8   the main components of water balance such as precipitation (P), snow cover (SC), [SOURCE: Page_2]

9   evapotranspiration (ET) and streamflows (Q) as well as surface variables and meteorological [SOURCE: Page_2]

10   forcing (i.e. air temperature - Ta, solar radiation - RS, land surface temperature - LST). The [SOURCE: Page_2]

11   processed data were obtained from remote sensing, reanalysis and in-situ data. We [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   implemented a trend analysis for each variable in the period 2000-2019 at monthly, seasonal [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   and annual scale. Results showed a warming trend in Ta and LST of about 1.2°C and 2.1°C, [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   respectively, concentrated mainly in the autumn and winter seasons. Although P showed non- [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   significant trends, Q diminished significantly at rates of more than 9.1 m^3/s/decade, [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   representing 36% of its historical mean. However, the decreases in Q are seen only in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   maximum (spring) and minimum (summer) seasonal flows. These decreases are explained by [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   significant increases in ET, led by a positive feedback of its drivers (LST, Ta and RS), which [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   is directly linked to the impact of warming and an associated vegetation greenness in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   watershed, as well as a decrease in SC during winter that feeds the Simpson River during [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   spring and summer. The decrease in Q is reinforced by the intensification of water [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   withdrawals in recent decades, as shown by an accelerated increase in water rights for [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   agricultural and drinking uses. In a context of water scarcity and increasing and extreme [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   droughts, this work contributes to further understanding water dynamics in western [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   Patagonia, providing support for policy and decision-making when defining sustainable [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   productive practices at watershed scale. [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   1   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

32   The impacts of rising temperatures due to climate change could have unpredictable [SOURCE: Page_4]

33   consequences on sub-Antarctic ecosystems. Precipitation distribution, glacier melting rate [SOURCE: Page_4]

34   and future water stress scenario might generate unknown consequences over marine and earth [SOURCE: Page_4]

35   ecosystems deserving of special attention. In fact, Cabré et al. (2016) developed several [SOURCE: Page_4]

36   future simulations on climate change scenarios for South America, in particular for the [SOURCE: Page_4]

37   Patagonia region. They projected that by the end of the 21st century, the severe changes in [SOURCE: Page_4]

38   temperature (i.e. increases ranging from 1 up to 5.5 °C) and the dramatic decrease in [SOURCE: Page_4]

39   precipitation (i.e. 60% for summer and spring seasons) will directly impact the water resource [SOURCE: Page_4]

40   abundance mainly located in ice fields. These trends could lead to increases in the melting [SOURCE: Page_4]

41   rate of glaciers, and also bring about a stressful ecophysiological pattern on native forest, [SOURCE: Page_4]

42   generating losses on water ecosystem services that affect the water footprint. However, [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   climate change is not the only factor having an effect on the water footprint in Patagonia. [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   During the last century, several repercussions of the Anthropocene on land cover dynamics [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   have devastated more than 6 million hectares due to bad forestry and agricultural practices [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   (Caldentey et al., 2009; Fajardo and Gundale, 2015; Lusk, 1996; Promis and Allen, 2017; [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   Veblen et al., 1989; Zamorano-Elgueta et al., 2012, 2014). Cattle ranching in forests forest [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   fires, alien invasive species and selective logging, are currently the main anthropogenic [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   threats to Patagonian forest ecosystems, for example on forest resilience in the face of global [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   warming and water scarcity scenarios. (Zamorano-Elgueta et al., 2012, 2014). [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   The impacts of global warming have also been revealed in extreme southern heat waves [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   events (Jacques-Coper et al., 2016). This is basically related to the interaction between the [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   westerly flow from the Pacific Ocean and the southern Andes, which leads to a temperate and [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   hyper-humid climate. Thus, spatial and temporal variations of temperature contribute to [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   increase/decrease the humidity content and affects the water dynamics. Furthermore, the [SOURCE: Page_5]

56   projections in western Patagonia tend to be warmer and characterized by extreme heat waves [SOURCE: Page_5]

57   (Feron et al., 2019) causing a variety of effects such as increased risk of flooding as the [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   affected Villa Santalucía a few years ago (Duhart et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   To understand the water dyncamics in Patagonia in further scenarios, it is necessary to use [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   several information sources. However, the western Patagonian watershed is poorly monitored [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   by meteorological stations networks. Remote sensing fulfills the critical role of estimating [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   various land surface biophysical patterns, such as water demand and its relation to extreme [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   climate and drought events based on its historical data sets (Jiménez-Muñoz et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) products have been mostly used [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   in cases where long term records or scarcity meteorological networks are lacking and optical [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   data are available (Jiménez-Muñoz et al., 2013). In addition, remote sensing is capable of [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   providing historical records of vegetation indicators such as the Normalized Difference [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   Vegetation Index (NDVI) or the Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) or thermal parameters [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   such as Land Surface Temperature (LST), which can be combined using energy balance [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   models in order to estimate the vegetation and surface water status                    such as [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   evapotranspiration and water scarcity indexes (Jimenez et al., 2018; Olivera-Guerra et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   2017). These surface variables are related to different processes of water and energy flows [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   between soil and atmosphere, which have made it possible to better understand the [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   hydrological cycle at remote regions and larger scales, such as at catchment level (Lara et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   2018; Olivares-Contreras et al., 2019) [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   The aim of this work is to provide a comprehensive assessment of the water dynamics of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   Patagonian watershed by combining remote sensing and meteorological data in order to [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   disentangle the impact of climate change and the anthropogenic contribution to water [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   dynamics. [SOURCE: Page_5]

137   3   Methodology [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   Trend analysis was implemented on variables closely linked to the water balance dynamics of [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   the basin (P, ET, SC and Q). To better understand the water dynamics, we also analyzed key [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   surface variables in the exchange of water fluxes between the surface and atmosphere (LST [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   and NDVI) as well as key meteorological forcing (Ta and RS) in this process. [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   Prior to temporal analysis, in situ measurements, MODIS and reanalysis data were averaged [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   to monthly time series and then averaged at both annual and seasonal scale. The austral [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   seasons are the mean between: March–May (autumn), June–August (winter), September– [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   November (spring) and December–February (summer). The time series were analyzed in [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   terms of standardized anomalies (analogous to z-score) using the differences of each period [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   (month, season and year) and its historical average and standard deviation based on the full [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   time series (2000-2019). The use of standardized anomalies reduces the autocorrelation and [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   seasonality of the time series and allows the comparison between variables that may be [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   related by the coupled water-energy balance. [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   The trend analysis on the time series of standardized anomalies was performed at pixel and [SOURCE: Page_9]

152   basin scale for seasonal and yearly periods. This trend analysis was based on the Theil-Sen [SOURCE: Page_9]

153   slope method (Theil, 1950; Sen, 1968) for determining the rate at which the variable is [SOURCE: Page_9]

154   changing over time. The Theil-Sen slope method is a robust non-parametric test represented [SOURCE: Page_9]

155   by the median of the slopes among all the combinations of ordered pairs. The significance of [SOURCE: Page_9]

156   trends is then verified by the non-parametric Mann-Kendall test (Mann, 1945; Kendall, 1975) [SOURCE: Page_9]

157   with a significance level of 0.05. [SOURCE: Page_9]

158   In addition, the standardized anomalies were used to perform a correlation matrix between [SOURCE: Page_9]

159   the studied variables at different temporal scales. For this purpose, the Pearson coefficient [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   was estimated from the time series for the 2000-2019 period at monthly, seasonal and annual [SOURCE: Page_9]

161   scale in order to assess the interaction and coupling between variables. Even though [SOURCE: Page_9]

162   correlations cannot prove links of causality, they can be used as indirect estimations of [SOURCE: Page_9]

163   coupling for relationships for which causality has been established (Seneviratne et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_9]

164   In this vein, correlations allow for a better understanding of the variability and possible trends [SOURCE: Page_9]

165   detected in the variables studied. Finally, in order to assess the impact of water withdrawals [SOURCE: Page_9]

166   in the decrease of streamflows in the Simpson catchment, we analyzed the water use rights [SOURCE: Page_9]

167   granted over the last decades. The concept of water use rights was established by the Chilean [SOURCE: Page_9]

168   legislation on water resources in 1981 (Water Code Decree-Law N°1122), which strengthen [SOURCE: Page_9]

169   private autonomy in water use. The water rights are divided into those referring to actual [SOURCE: Page_9]

170   water consumption (consumptive uses) and those in which the water is simply used to [SOURCE: Page_9]

171   produce something else (non-consumptive uses) (Valdés-Pineda et al., 2014). [SOURCE: Page_9]

212   In terms of ET, another important water outflow at basin scale, we observe a significant [SOURCE: Page_11]

213   positive trend (0.77 σ/decade) that can be explained by a positive feedback of its main drivers [SOURCE: Page_11]

214   (NDVI, LST, Ta and RS), showing similarly significant positive trends. It should be noted [SOURCE: Page_11]

215   that the watershed is mainly characterized by an energy-limited evapotranspiration regime. [SOURCE: Page_11]

216   That means that energy (radiation) provides a first-order constraint on ET and evaporative [SOURCE: Page_11]

217   fraction (i.e. the ratio of ET to available energy) is independent of the soil moisture content [SOURCE: Page_11]

218   since it is often above a critical threshold at which vegetation undergoes water stress [SOURCE: Page_11]

219   (Seneviratne et al. 2010). Thus, the positive trend in RS (0.60 σ/decade) and the related [SOURCE: Page_11]

220   warming trend (i.e. significant positive trend in Ta and LST both with a rate greater than 0.50 [SOURCE: Page_11]

221   σ/decade) leads to a significant increase in ET in the Simpson basin due to the radiation [SOURCE: Page_11]

11 [SOURCE: Page_11]

222   limitation regime. The warming trend represented a significant increase of 1.2 °C and 2.1 °C [SOURCE: Page_12]

223   over the las two decades in Ta and LST, respectively, on the Simpson catchment scale. These [SOURCE: Page_12]

224   results are in agreement with significant warming trends detected in Ta over the last two [SOURCE: Page_12]

225   decades at downstream and upstream in situ stations of 0.78 and 1.32 °C, respectively. In [SOURCE: Page_12]

226   addition, the warming trend is consistent with a recent study carried out at larger extent over [SOURCE: Page_12]

227   the Patagonian sub-Antarctic forest by Olivares-Contreras et al. (2019), who provided [SOURCE: Page_12]

228   evidence of a trend of 0.78 °C/decade in LST. [SOURCE: Page_12]

229   In particular, the ET-Ta feedback is explained by the fact that an increase in Ta leads to more [SOURCE: Page_12]

230   evaporative demand by means of a higher vapor pressure deficit and hence an increase in ET. [SOURCE: Page_12]

231   Accordingly, a recent study by Green et al. (2020) carried out in the Amazonian rainforest [SOURCE: Page_12]

232   (humid biome) found increases in photosynthesis, and hence in ET and NDVI, in response to [SOURCE: Page_12]

233   an increased atmospheric dryness (i.e. higher vapor pressure deficit) as a result of global [SOURCE: Page_12]

234   warming. In addition to the control of Ta on ET, the contrary effect can be expected in cold [SOURCE: Page_12]

235   biomes like Patagonian watersheds, where Ta increases are dampened by ET through [SOURCE: Page_12]

236   evaporative cooling and thus reducing the warming effect. Given that temperature is the [SOURCE: Page_12]

237   major constraint on plant development in cold regions, the positive radiation and temperature [SOURCE: Page_12]

238   (Ta and LST) trends also lead to a significant positive trend in NDVI, such as the significant [SOURCE: Page_12]

239   rate of 0.60 σ/decade found in the Simpson watershed for the 2000-2019 period. [SOURCE: Page_12]

367   4.3   Vegetation responses to climatic variations [SOURCE: Page_18]

368   Vegetation responses in terms of indicators such as NDVI or EVI to climatic variations are [SOURCE: Page_18]

369   often complex and not straightforward (Fabricante et al., 2009). The influence of air [SOURCE: Page_18]

370   temperature and precipitation on NDVI often depends on biome characteristics, the [SOURCE: Page_18]

371   functional types of dominant species and the phenological stages of the vegetation (Mao et [SOURCE: Page_18]

372   al., 2012; Mohamed et al., 2004). For instance, Bianchi et al. (2020) reported relationships of [SOURCE: Page_18]

373   NDVI with Ta and P over northern Patagonia, finding significant positive correlations in late [SOURCE: Page_18]

374   winter/spring over temperate forests in western North Patagonia (Chilean Patagonia). In our [SOURCE: Page_18]

375   study area, the strong NDVI-Ta relationship is revealed at monthly (R = 0.60) and annual (R [SOURCE: Page_18]

376   = 0.79) scales, while at seasonal scale it is seen in autumn (R = 0.69) and particularly in [SOURCE: Page_18]

377   winter and spring (R = 0.8). This positive relationship is partially explained by the effect of [SOURCE: Page_18]

378   temperature on the onset and length of the growing season, which modulates variations in [SOURCE: Page_18]

379   NDVI as reported over cold grassland, Patagonian steppes and temperate forests (Bianchi et [SOURCE: Page_18]

380   al., 2020; Buermann et al., 2003; Jobbágy et al., 2002). In terms of the NDV-P relationship, [SOURCE: Page_18]

381   we found weaker and negative relationships as other studies have reported for cold grassland [SOURCE: Page_18]

382   and temperate forests (Bianchi et al., 2020; Mao et al., 2012). [SOURCE: Page_18]

383   In the study area, the pasture management and forest plantations of exotic species may [SOURCE: Page_18]

384   increase the water demand at landscape scale, which could cause water scarcity in a scenario [SOURCE: Page_18]

385   of climate change. Previous studies in the central and southern zone of Chile have [SOURCE: Page_18]

386   demonstrated the impact of industrial forest plantations, increasing the water requirements at [SOURCE: Page_18]

387   landscape scale (Alvarez-Garreton et al., 2019; Little et al., 2009; Olivera-Guerra et al., [SOURCE: Page_18]

388   2014). These changes could have an unknown impact on the functional properties of the [SOURCE: Page_18]

389   ecosystem and its resilience, indicating that further research is needed to fully understand the [SOURCE: Page_18]

390   multiple factors affecting forest ecosystem functions after human-induced disturbances. For [SOURCE: Page_18]

18 [SOURCE: Page_18]

391   instance, the case of water withdrawals introduced and additional anthropogenic forcing, [SOURCE: Page_19]

392   which have unknown consequences on ecosystem services and landscape resilience. [SOURCE: Page_19]

419    4.5   Human impact on water dynamics [SOURCE: Page_20]

420   The increase of human water demand, closely linked to water use rights granted in Simpson [SOURCE: Page_20]

421   basin, is a clear anthropogenic pressure on the streamflow, particularly the consumptive uses [SOURCE: Page_20]

422   extracted almost entirely from surface waters. Indeed, during 2020, the total volume granted [SOURCE: Page_20]

423   as consumptive uses represented the 22.6 % of the historical annual mean streamflow at [SOURCE: Page_20]

424   catchment scale (55.5 m^3/s) and its consequences on ecosystem services and water reservoirs [SOURCE: Page_20]

425   are still unknown. However, this water management scheme deserves more analysis in terms [SOURCE: Page_20]

426   of those ecological, economic and social impacts of an excessive human water consumption. [SOURCE: Page_20]

427   For instance, Barria et al. (2021) recently proposed an index of anthropic intervention (IAI) at [SOURCE: Page_20]

428   basin scale as a diagnosis of water allocation system in Chile. This IAI index is a theoretical [SOURCE: Page_20]

429   approach to assess adequately the water management system to satisfy human and ecological [SOURCE: Page_20]

430   demands for water based in the ratio between the total granted consumptive water volume [SOURCE: Page_20]

431   plus the ecosystem requirements based on the ecological streamflow to the water availability [SOURCE: Page_20]

432   within the basin. The water availability correspond to theoretical volumes of water to be [SOURCE: Page_20]

433   granted as permanently exercisable surface and subsurface (groundwater) water rights on an [SOURCE: Page_20]

434   annual basis. The water availability is calculated at monthly basis as Q with probability of [SOURCE: Page_20]

435   exceedance greater than 85% (Q85%), while the ecological streamflow is calculated between [SOURCE: Page_20]

436   the 20 % of historical mean Q and the 50 % of the Q95% (Barria et al., 2021; DGA, 2008). [SOURCE: Page_20]

437   Barria et al. (2021) also defined two IAI thresholds to assess the allocation water in the [SOURCE: Page_20]

438   catchment, where IAI over 0.75 means a risk of future overallocation and IAI over 1 means [SOURCE: Page_20]

439   that the basin is already overallocated, therefore, under large risk of water scarcity. [SOURCE: Page_20]

20 [SOURCE: Page_20]

440   In order to assess the level of the human impact within the Simpson basin, we implement the [SOURCE: Page_21]

441   IAI during the last decades over Simpson catchment. The consumptive water use rights [SOURCE: Page_21]

442   reached to 11.4 m^3/s in 2020, the ecological streamflow and water availability are 7.6 m^3/s [SOURCE: Page_21]

443   and 28.8 m^3/s, respectively, which results in an IAI value of 0.66. This IAI index is very [SOURCE: Page_21]

444   close to the critical value generating a future risk for water allocation in Simpson River. [SOURCE: Page_21]

445   Indeed, the water use rights granted until 2020 (11.4 m^3/s) are close to reach a critical value [SOURCE: Page_21]

446   of 14 m^3/s that represents an IAI threshold (0.75) of risk of future overallocation. The latter [SOURCE: Page_21]

447   estimation is even assuming that the water availability (based on the historical streamflows) [SOURCE: Page_21]

448   remains constant. In another hand, this IAI threshold could also be reached if the mean [SOURCE: Page_21]

449   streamflow (i.e. water availability) decreases to 44.3 m^3/s, which is calculated using the water [SOURCE: Page_21]

450   rights granted until year 2020 and by assuming that there are not further water rights granted [SOURCE: Page_21]

451   in Simpson catchment. Therefore, it is very likely that this critical IAI value of 0.75 will be [SOURCE: Page_21]

452   reached during over the next few years due to the decreasing trend in Q (13.3 m^3/s/decade) [SOURCE: Page_21]

453   and the steady increase of water consumption during the last two decades (i.e. consumptive [SOURCE: Page_21]

454   water rights with a trend of 4.0 m^3/s/decade). Both trends in Q and consumptive water rights [SOURCE: Page_21]

455   decrease the water availability and reinforce the water withdrawals in the basin. [SOURCE: Page_21]

456   In addition to the impact of human water consumption on streamflow, water supply by snow [SOURCE: Page_21]

457   during winter and summer thaw are very important in the Patagonian watersheds. Although a [SOURCE: Page_21]

458   significant decreasing trend of snow cover in winter has been detected, accumulation and [SOURCE: Page_21]

459   melt of snowpack should be addressed in order to better quantify and isolate the natural and [SOURCE: Page_21]

460   anthropic impact on water availability. The snow cover in Patagonian watersheds is one of [SOURCE: Page_21]

461   the most important variables which contributes to the water reservoirs, although a lack of in- [SOURCE: Page_21]

462   situ measurements in terms of snow properties and spatial distribution limited the analysis in [SOURCE: Page_21]

463   terms of LST and ET. Recent studies have reported a decrease in snow cover (Pérez et al., [SOURCE: Page_21]

464   2018), which is consistent with our results, although further approaches have been concerned [SOURCE: Page_21]

21 [SOURCE: Page_21]

465   in terms of spatial and temporal snow monitoring because cloud cover could affect the [SOURCE: Page_22]

466   retrievals from optical remote sensing. In this context, further efforts related to improve the [SOURCE: Page_22]

467   background concept focusing on snow water equivalent and reservoirs which will be driving [SOURCE: Page_22]

468   the Q and ET under a water scarcity scenario. Moreover, it would be interesting to include [SOURCE: Page_22]

469   the impact of land use and land cover changes on the water dynamics since their important [SOURCE: Page_22]

470   impact on global water cycles is well documented, particularly in ET (Li et al., 2020; Wang [SOURCE: Page_22]

471   et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_22]

473   5    Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_22]

474   In the last two decades, the Simpson watershed located in western Patagonia showed [SOURCE: Page_22]

475   increases in Ta and LST of 1.2 °C and 2.1 °C, respectively, which are concentrated in the [SOURCE: Page_22]

476   autumn (+1.6 °C in Ta and +2.2 °C in LST) and winter (+1.6 °C in Ta and +2.7 °C in LST) [SOURCE: Page_22]

477   seasons. These warming trends are closely linked to significant increases in RS during [SOURCE: Page_22]

478   autumn and winter. Although RS also shows a significant trend in spring, the non-significant [SOURCE: Page_22]

479   increases in Ta and LST in spring can be explained by the dampening effect of evaporative [SOURCE: Page_22]

480   cooling due to greater vegetation activity and the significant increases detected in NDVI and [SOURCE: Page_22]

481   notably in ET, thus reducing the warming effect during spring. However, the snow trends [SOURCE: Page_22]

482   reveal a strong decrease in winter, which is accompanied by the influences of ET and LST. [SOURCE: Page_22]

483   The main input of water, P showed non-significant trends at any temporal scale, however, Q [SOURCE: Page_22]

484   significantly decreased by 9.1 to 13.3 m^3/s/decade (i.e. between 36 % and 56 % of its [SOURCE: Page_22]

485   historical streamflow mean). The decreases in Q are concentrated during the maximum [SOURCE: Page_22]

486   (spring) and minimum (summer) seasonal flows, which are linked to the periods of maximum [SOURCE: Page_22]

487   vegetation activity and ET. The significant trends in NDVI and ET revealed in spring are [SOURCE: Page_22]

488   likely induced by a significant increase in RS given the high correlations between them. [SOURCE: Page_22]

22 [SOURCE: Page_22]

489   Though NDVI exhibits a significant trend in all seasonal scales, Q reductions in summer may [SOURCE: Page_23]

490   be related to the rise in water withdrawals brought on by an accelerated increase in water [SOURCE: Page_23]

491   rights for agricultural and drinking uses, as well as decreases in SC during winter that feeds [SOURCE: Page_23]

492   streamflows during spring and summer. [SOURCE: Page_23]

493   ET exhibits a significant positive trend led by a positive feedback of its drivers (LST, Ta and [SOURCE: Page_23]

494   RS), which is directly linked to the warming impact. Moreover, an associated vegetation [SOURCE: Page_23]

495   greenness in the watershed is detected at monthly, annual and seasonal scales. The detected [SOURCE: Page_23]

496   positive feedback is consistent with the cold and humid climate of the hydrological [SOURCE: Page_23]

497   watershed. Indeed, the greatest greening at watershed scale occurred in 2016 during a severe [SOURCE: Page_23]

498   drought that affected western Patagonia, led by record-breaking P anomalies. The [SOURCE: Page_23]

499   counterintuitive vegetation greenness during the 2016 drought is directly linked to the highest [SOURCE: Page_23]

500   positive anomalies detected in temperatures (Ta and LST) and the lowest negative anomalies [SOURCE: Page_23]

501   in SC, which together allowed for further development of the vegetation that is intensified in [SOURCE: Page_23]

502   the cold seasons (autumn and winter). [SOURCE: Page_23]

503   The results of this study contribute to further understanding local water dynamics in western [SOURCE: Page_23]

504   Patagonia, which in a context of water scarcity and more frequent and extreme droughts, [SOURCE: Page_23]

505   provide support for policy and decision-making when defining sustainable productive [SOURCE: Page_23]

506   practices at watershed scale. However, the impact of land-use change on the water dynamics [SOURCE: Page_23]

507   should be addressed in future studies in order to assure water supply and allocate different [SOURCE: Page_23]

508   uses at watershed scale including agriculture, drinking, tourism, hydroelectricity and [SOURCE: Page_23]

509   aquiculture. [SOURCE: Page_23]
