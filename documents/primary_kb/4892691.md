Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

In view of accelerated climate change and urban demographics, balancing human and ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_2]

needs for water resources is a critical environmental challenge of global significance. Since [SOURCE: Page_2]

water, agriculture, health, and energy are inextricably linked, sustainable development goals [SOURCE: Page_2]

(SDGs) actions in one policy area commonly have impacts on the others, as well as on the [SOURCE: Page_2]

ecosystems that natural resources and human activities ultimately depend upon. Managing urban [SOURCE: Page_2]

water supply systems therefore requires a nexus approach that integrates goals across sectors, [SOURCE: Page_2]

reduces the risk that SDG actions will undermine one another, and ensures sustainable resource [SOURCE: Page_2]

use. We developed a transdisciplinary methodological framework based on a Pareto frontier [SOURCE: Page_2]

analysis to define the sustainable solutions of a multi-objective optimization among four [SOURCE: Page_2]

competing criteria, water provision, water quality, energy cost, and biodiversity conservation. [SOURCE: Page_2]

The study was applied to three mountainous headwater basins in the Ecuadorian Andes, which [SOURCE: Page_2]

provide around 30% of Quito’s total water supply. We found that an optimized management of [SOURCE: Page_2]

water intake structures would meet current consumption needs while reducing the probability of [SOURCE: Page_2]

emergence of water pathogens and limiting the impact on aquatic biodiversity by 30% and 9% [SOURCE: Page_2]

respectively, without any increase in energy costs for pumping water from other sources. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Nonetheless, under future scenarios of climate change and water demand, higher energy [SOURCE: Page_2]

consumption, and therefore an increase in operating costs, would be needed to meet urban [SOURCE: Page_2]

demand and preserve environmental conditions. Overall, the range of Pareto optimal water [SOURCE: Page_2]

supply strategies across the water-health-energy-biodiversity nexus provides valuable [SOURCE: Page_2]

information for decision makers and offers support for achieving sustainable management of [SOURCE: Page_2]

water resources. [SOURCE: Page_2]

### 1. Introduction

Meeting potable water demand is an increasing global challenge due to growing urban [SOURCE: Page_4]

population, socioeconomic development, and climate change (Brown et al., 2015). Higher [SOURCE: Page_4]

pressure on water resources to meet human demands has increased water abstraction, making it [SOURCE: Page_4]

difficult to satisfy urban demands without compromising ecosystem health (Liu et al., 2008). [SOURCE: Page_4]

Facing this concern in large cities requires considering complex interdependencies between a [SOURCE: Page_4]

number of social, economic, and environmental sectors. In particular, water, agriculture, health, [SOURCE: Page_4]

and energy sectors are inextricably linked, with actions in one policy area commonly having [SOURCE: Page_4]

impacts on the others (Weitz et al., 2014). To overcome the limits of traditional sectorial [SOURCE: Page_4]

approaches in water management, the nexus approach analyses synergies and trade-offs among [SOURCE: Page_4]

interlinked sectors, aiming at identifying solutions, fostering water-food-health-energy security [SOURCE: Page_4]

and efficiency, and reducing impacts and risks on water-dependent ecosystems (Abdi-Dehkordi [SOURCE: Page_4]

et al., 2021; Hirwa et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_4]

Different methods have recently been proposed for solving the optimization of water [SOURCE: Page_4]

resources systems (Xiong et al., 2020). For instance, multi-objective non-linear programming has [SOURCE: Page_4]

been applied to a water-energy-ecosystem nexus system to provide the optimal operation of a [SOURCE: Page_4]

regulated river of the Datong River Basin in China (Yin et al., 2022). Similarly a newly [SOURCE: Page_4]

developed population-based optimization method, the Jaya algorithm, has been used for reservoir [SOURCE: Page_4]

operation optimization on the upper Godavari basin in India (Paliwal et al., 2020). More recently, [SOURCE: Page_4]

Flecker et al. (2022) proposed a Pareto-frontier optimization to measure the collective impact of [SOURCE: Page_4]

dams for hydropower extension in the Amazon and identified portfolios of sites that [SOURCE: Page_4]

simultaneously minimize impacts on river flow, river connectivity, sediment transport, fish [SOURCE: Page_4]

diversity, and greenhouse gas emissions while achieving energy production goals. These works [SOURCE: Page_4]

used multi-objective optimization algorithms providing trade-off solutions for multiple [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

competing objectives and reflecting universal decision information for decision-makers in real- [SOURCE: Page_5]

world applications (Maier et al., 2019). While the nexus approach in water management has been [SOURCE: Page_5]

increasingly popular in recent years (in particular the “water-food-energy” nexus), its effective [SOURCE: Page_5]

adoption by stakeholders still requires specific nexus frameworks to be framed within local or [SOURCE: Page_5]

regional contexts, constraints, and water conflicts among users. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Water management issues are particularly severe in large cities in middle and low-income [SOURCE: Page_5]

countries where unplanned urbanization, deteriorating infrastructure, and inadequate policies [SOURCE: Page_5]

have caused major water crises (e.g., Anton, 1993). Interesting examples come from large cities [SOURCE: Page_5]

in the tropical Andes (Bogota, Quito, and La Paz) that have developed at high altitude, where the [SOURCE: Page_5]

current trend of urbanization requires increasing volumes of water to be extracted and [SOURCE: Page_5]

transported for human supply. In addition, the greatest effects of climate change on society and [SOURCE: Page_5]

ecosystems are reflected in the availability and quality of water. Many examples of crises over [SOURCE: Page_5]

the distribution of drinking water have become visible in recent years (e.g. Hagen et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Thus, it is vital to have tools and mechanisms that can reinforce the resilience of these large [SOURCE: Page_5]

Andean cities through better water management, optimizing supply systems, managing green [SOURCE: Page_5]

infrastructure, and improving the capacities of the actors involved to make correct, timely, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

co-responsible decisions. Due to their location in the high tropics, these cities are confronted [SOURCE: Page_5]

with specific, interrelated challenges for water management (Jacobsen & Dangles, 2017): i) those [SOURCE: Page_5]

cities have experienced exponential growth coefficients over recent decades (e.g., 1.82, 2.36 and [SOURCE: Page_5]

2.42 in Bogota, La Paz and Quito, respectively (Buytaert and De Biévre, 2012)); ii) the [SOURCE: Page_5]

elevation-dependent warming process exacerbates the effect of climate change with an air [SOURCE: Page_5]

temperature trend of +0.34°C decade over the past 25 years; iii) the water sources are located in [SOURCE: Page_5]

natural areas that shelter some of the highest levels of biodiversity and endemicity worldwide, [SOURCE: Page_5]

which provide critical ecological services that people depend on; iv) the natural deficiency in [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

oxygen of freshwaters makes them particularly sensitive to abrupt shifts in water quality; and v) [SOURCE: Page_6]

the mountain topography requires redistribution networks transferring water between catchments [SOURCE: Page_6]

with high energy costs. All these challenges can be framed into four sustainable development [SOURCE: Page_6]

goals (SDGs) and six targets proposed by the United Nations in their 2030 Agenda, to illustrate [SOURCE: Page_6]

the water-health-climate-biodiversity-energy nexus that characterizes high altitude Andean cities [SOURCE: Page_6]

(Fig. 1). [SOURCE: Page_6]

Here, we developed a multi-objective optimization framework to evaluate the trade-offs [SOURCE: Page_6]

at watershed scales between potable water provision and three criteria – water pathogens, [SOURCE: Page_6]

biodiversity conservation, and energy costs - emerging from location of water intakes and [SOURCE: Page_6]

reservoirs across the three river networks provisioning the city of Quito, Ecuador. These criteria [SOURCE: Page_6]

were selected through a transdisciplinary collaborative stakeholder‐driven process with the main [SOURCE: Page_6]

stakeholders involved in water management: the Metropolitan Drinking Water Supply Company [SOURCE: Page_6]

of Quito (EPMAPS) that operates the system and the water fund for Quito (FONAG) in charge [SOURCE: Page_6]

of catchment conservation (Rosero-López et al., 2019). Our approach determines the Pareto- [SOURCE: Page_6]

optimal frontier, which represents a set of solutions (i.e., portfolios composed of different [SOURCE: Page_6]

configurations of water intake operation) that minimize negative effects across environmental, [SOURCE: Page_6]

health, and energy objectives for any given level of aggregate water supply. These sets of [SOURCE: Page_6]

solutions were generated for both present and future (2040) climatic and demographic data in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

region. [SOURCE: Page_6]

2.1 Study system [SOURCE: Page_8]

(2019a). The PGS is composed of 11 water intakes and a reservoir (Salve Faccha), which operate [SOURCE: Page_8]

together to satisfy a mean annual historical demand of 1.487 m^3/s (see Appendix S$_1$, Table S$_1$.1) [SOURCE: Page_8]

through a gravity pipeline. The operation rules of the PGS capture water from the rivers and are [SOURCE: Page_8]

limited by the capacity of intake pipes (see Appendix S$_1$, Table S$_2$.1) and flow availability [SOURCE: Page_8]

### 4. Discussion

The lack of an optimal operating strategy for meeting drinking water demand, which involves [SOURCE: Page_23]

valuable health and environmental criteria in its operating process, can produce water supply [SOURCE: Page_23]

portfolios that are far from achieving a sustainable management of water resources. Operating a [SOURCE: Page_23]

water supply system requires strategies that optimize intakes’ water abstraction, releases from [SOURCE: Page_23]

the reservoir for water supply, energy consumption, and preserving ecosystem services (Asadieh [SOURCE: Page_23]

and Afshar, 2018). Trade-off evaluation among multiple criteria has been widely employed in [SOURCE: Page_23]

management (Sousa et al., 2021). For example, previous studies have focused on sustainable [SOURCE: Page_23]

urban water supply to accomplish an optimum compromise among demand, water ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_23]

protection, climate change, and economic performance (e.g. Xiong et al., 2018; Geressu et al., [SOURCE: Page_23]

2020; Maurya et al., 2020). However, these studies usually involved only one or two factors [SOURCE: Page_23]

without considering their fundamental interconnectedness, which is insufficient for [SOURCE: Page_23]

understanding interactions between hydrology and social systems. Our study provides a [SOURCE: Page_23]

23 [SOURCE: Page_23]

methodological framework to address the issues of potable water provision in urban [SOURCE: Page_24]

environments facing both demographic and climate change challenges. [SOURCE: Page_24]

### 4.1. Study limitations

Simulation and optimization models have been highly effective in designing and managing the [SOURCE: Page_24]

sustainability problems of water resource systems (e.g. Zhuang et al., 2018; Song et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_24]

However, their applicability in local water planning exercises can be limited by the scarcity of [SOURCE: Page_24]

environmental data needed to parameterize and test the models, and by the non-linear responses [SOURCE: Page_24]

of socio-ecological systems to external stressors (see Ibrahim et al., 2015; Slaughter et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_24]

This issue is particularly acute in tropical mountains where water demand is increasing rapidly [SOURCE: Page_24]

and climate change is expected to have severe impacts (Jacobsen & Dangles, 2017). In our study [SOURCE: Page_24]

area, despite recent efforts made by the local Water Company (EPMAPS), there is a lack of long- [SOURCE: Page_24]

term data for climate, hydrology, aquatic biodiversity, and water pathogens, both in the reservoir [SOURCE: Page_24]

and in the river basins. These limitations result in difficulties when building our model, [SOURCE: Page_24]

optimizing water abstraction from pumping river intakes, and assessing potential ecological [SOURCE: Page_24]

impacts. To address this issue, we have recently developed a standard monitoring method that [SOURCE: Page_24]

enables relatively rapid measurements of benthic algae over large areas in the watersheds [SOURCE: Page_24]

providing water to Quito (Rosero-López et al., 2021). Also, we have designed an ecosystem- [SOURCE: Page_24]

scale experiment to predict the ecological consequences of water shortage (Rosero-López et al., [SOURCE: Page_24]

2022). Even though these additional studies are key to supporting the validity of our simulation, [SOURCE: Page_24]

further research is necessary to improve our nexus optimization approach. For example, while [SOURCE: Page_24]

health hazards presented by cyanobacteria have been largely documented worldwide (e.g. Codd [SOURCE: Page_24]

et al., 2020), this is only a recent issue in the tropical Andes of Ecuador, where little information [SOURCE: Page_24]

exists. This issue has limited the inclusion of the three basins that contribute to the PPS in our [SOURCE: Page_24]

24 [SOURCE: Page_24]

optimization process, however, in terms of water availability for supply, EPMAPS has not [SOURCE: Page_25]

reported historical water shortage problems in the pumping system so far. Additional data on the [SOURCE: Page_25]

potentially negative impacts of cyanobacteria on water drinkability in relation to climate change [SOURCE: Page_25]

are therefore urgently needed. Also, evaluating the impact of climate change in the long-term [SOURCE: Page_25]

may be limited by the difficulty of regional climate models to simulate climatic patterns at a [SOURCE: Page_25]

local scale. In a previous study in the same watershed, González-Zeas et al. (2019b) found that [SOURCE: Page_25]

temperature and precipitation predictions varied greatly and randomly among adjacent 10 × 10 [SOURCE: Page_25]

km grid cells when using regional climate models. Our optimization procedure therefore relied [SOURCE: Page_25]

on carefully checked downscaling methods (see Gonzalez et al., 2019a for further details), which [SOURCE: Page_25]

are currently being completed by alternative methods (e.g., weather simulators) to conduct [SOURCE: Page_25]

climate change impact assessments for local water systems that do not depend on the output [SOURCE: Page_25]

GCMs or RCMs. [SOURCE: Page_25]

Finally, for multi-objective optimization, improvement of one objective may lead to [SOURCE: Page_25]

deterioration of some of the other objective values (Deb and Gupta, 2006) and different methods [SOURCE: Page_25]

have been proposed to negotiate trade-offs in order to select robust solutions in water resource [SOURCE: Page_25]

management, through the determination of the Pareto frontier (e.g. Bartholomew and Kwakkel, [SOURCE: Page_25]

2020; Flecker et al., 2022). While the PO portfolios provide minimal impact on water pathogens, [SOURCE: Page_25]

biodiversity, and energy costs for a given water yield capacity equal or higher than a required [SOURCE: Page_25]

urban demand (see Fig. 6), this also implies an increasing impact on energy costs and ecological [SOURCE: Page_25]

indicators under future changing conditions in both climate and demand. However, the “optimal [SOURCE: Page_25]

portfolio” might not be achievable given the current intake operating policies. Therefore, a new [SOURCE: Page_25]

paradigm of operation should be concerted by water managers, focusing their attention on not [SOURCE: Page_25]

only minimizing energy costs but also establishing operating rules that consider a percentage of [SOURCE: Page_25]

flow left downstream of the water intakes, as a key feature of environmental water management [SOURCE: Page_25]

25 [SOURCE: Page_25]

(Richter & Thomas, 2007), despite the fact that environmental flows have not yet been [SOURCE: Page_26]

established in Ecuador water legislation. [SOURCE: Page_26]

basin in Central Asia. The mentioned study proposed different scenarios of water management, [SOURCE: Page_26]

comparable to our portfolios of water pressure, in order to show that ecological flows play a key [SOURCE: Page_26]

role in achieving a higher ecosystem service value and higher efficiency of water-land resources, [SOURCE: Page_26]

balancing the contradictions among water shortage, ecological protection, and economic [SOURCE: Page_27]

### 4.3. System operating considerations

Our study paid special attention to the four key factors linked to a water supply system (water [SOURCE: Page_27]

supply, biodiversity conservation, water pathogen, and energy costs) in order to provide [SOURCE: Page_27]

sustainable operating strategies, which allow for a balance between social, economic and [SOURCE: Page_27]

discussion among water managers and system operators, in such a way that they can adapt it to [SOURCE: Page_27]

27 [SOURCE: Page_27]

solutions can represent different optimized levels of operation for water managers. The failure of [SOURCE: Page_28]

addressing these optimal solutions can result in portfolios that are significantly inferior and of [SOURCE: Page_28]

climatic) and visually presents the way in which the benefits of a sustainable operation of the [SOURCE: Page_28]

system can be distributed. [SOURCE: Page_28]

Nonetheless, the potential benefits of sustainable water management strategies imply new [SOURCE: Page_28]

challenges at different levels of operation. First, it is key to promote engagement of the water [SOURCE: Page_28]

company´s stakeholders in the decision-making process, in order to design and implement a plan [SOURCE: Page_28]

### 5. Conclusion

conservation, and energy costs - has been considered through a multi-criteria decision analysis. [SOURCE: Page_28]

Sustainable urban water supply strategies of operation were evaluated under current and future [SOURCE: Page_28]

28 [SOURCE: Page_28]

climate change scenarios for 2040. The JA method for reservoir optimization and multi-objective [SOURCE: Page_29]

cost. Overall, PO solutions provide a range of water strategies that could be adapted to a [SOURCE: Page_29]
