11   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

12             This study addresses the critical need for accurate reference evapotranspiration (ET$_0$) [SOURCE: Page_1]

13   estimation in data-scarce environments such as West Africa, where rapid population growth [SOURCE: Page_1]

14   and climate change intensify water resource challenges. Traditionally, computing ET$_0$ with the [SOURCE: Page_1]

15   FAO-56 Penman-Monteith method requires multiple meteorological inputs—such as solar [SOURCE: Page_1]

16   radiation, humidity, and wind speed—collected from costly, fully instrumented weather [SOURCE: Page_1]

17   stations. However, the availability and maintenance of such equipment can be prohibitive in [SOURCE: Page_1]

18   remote regions. To overcome these constraints, we explore the potential of machine learning [SOURCE: Page_1]

19   (ML), specifically XGBoost (XGB) models, trained on NASA Power climate reanalysis [SOURCE: Page_1]

20   datasets. Our approach relies on a limited subset of easily measured in situ variables—daily [SOURCE: Page_1]

21   minimum and maximum temperatures and rainfall—to estimate ET$_0$. Departing from standard [SOURCE: Page_1]

22   ML practices that depend on short-term, site-specific data, we leverage the extensive historical [SOURCE: Page_1]

1 [SOURCE: Page_1]

23   depth and broad spatial coverage of reanalysis products. We trained and validated twenty [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   locally adapted XGB models using measurements from twenty diverse West African weather [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   stations. Our results show that certain XGB model configurations, notably those incorporating [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   temperature and rainfall data, can approximate ET$_0$ estimates from the FAO-56 Penman- [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   Monteith equation with median RMSE values frequently below 1 mm/day—levels comparable [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   to commonly employed empirical formulas. This finding demonstrates that minimal on-site [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   instrumentation, combined with ML and reanalysis data, can effectively support irrigation [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   scheduling and enhance water-use efficiency under varying agro-ecological conditions. To [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   foster broader implementation, we have released our XGB-ET$_0$ code under the GPLv$_3$ licence [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   (https://github.com/SARRA-cropmodels/RF-ET$_0$), enabling researchers and practitioners to [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   locally train and deploy these models, thereby improving ET$_0$ estimation and sustainable [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   agricultural water management in resource-limited settings. [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

44          Accurately estimating actual evapotranspiration (ETa) is critical for optimizing irrigation [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   practices and promoting sustainable agricultural water management. This need is particularly [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   acute in West Africa, where growing population pressures and increasing variability in water [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

47   availability caused by climate change (Arnell and Lloyd-Hughes, 2014; Ayers et al., 2023; Brouillet [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   and Sultan, 2023; Satgé et al., 2020) have intensified demands on limited water resources. [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   Precision irrigation (PI), which tailors water inputs to compensate for ETa, offers a promising [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   solution to reduce excessive irrigation, even in humid environments (Adeyemi et al., 2017; [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   González Perea et al., 2018). Beyond irrigation scheduling, reliable ETa estimates play a [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   pivotal role in understanding the water cycle, modeling water availability, and predicting [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   changes in hydrological conditions. ETa is also a key input in process-based crop models, [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   helping farmers anticipate the impact of different irrigation strategies on yields and overall [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   water usage (Gu et al., 2020; Lopez-Jimenez et al., 2022; Pereira et al., 2020). Consequently, [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   improving ETa estimation methods under data-scarce conditions is essential for both [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   advancing scientific understanding and informing practical water management decisions in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   region. [SOURCE: Page_3]

59             However, direct experimental methods for estimating ETa often require complex [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   instrumentation, careful handling, and trained personnel, making them difficult to deploy and [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   maintain (Li et al., 2009; Valayamkunnath et al., 2018). As a result, indirect ETa estimation [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   methods have been developed, including several mathematical formulations based on [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   meteorological variables (compiled in (McMahon et al., 2013)). Some of these methods rely [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   on the concept of reference crop evapotranspiration (ET$_0$), which represents the amount of [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   water lost to the atmosphere by a reference healthy, well-watered grass or alfalfa cover; this [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   ET$_0$ is further modulated by a crop and growth stage-specific coefficient (crop coefficient, [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   symbolised as Kc), to provide an estimate of actual evapotranspiration (noted ET$_0$). This ET$_0$, [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   which represents the climate forcing component and is a widely used variable in crop water [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   modelling and management, can be computed based on air temperature, solar radiation, [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   relative humidity, and wind speed using the FAO-56 standardized Penman-Monteith equation [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   (FAO-56-PM) (Richard G. Allen et al., 1998), the most widely-used procedure for estimating [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   the ET$_0$ value. [SOURCE: Page_3]

73             However, obtaining all the meteorological variables required by the FAO-56-PM [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   equation can be challenging. The cost, maintenance, and limited availability of fully [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

75    instrumented weather stations restrict the widespread calculation of ET$_0$, especially in [SOURCE: Page_4]

76    developing countries (Djaman et al., 2016, 2015; Fisher and Pringle Iii, 2013; Valipour, 2015). [SOURCE: Page_4]

77    To circumvent these constraints, a range of empirical ET$_0$ estimation models requiring fewer [SOURCE: Page_4]

78    input variables has emerged (McMahon et al., 2013). While these simplified approaches can [SOURCE: Page_4]

79    reduce the need for complex instrumentation, their accuracy varies markedly depending on [SOURCE: Page_4]

80    local agroclimatic conditions (Djaman et al., 2015; Tabari and Talaee, 2011; Xystrakis and [SOURCE: Page_4]

81    Matzarakis, 2011). Consequently, selecting and calibrating an appropriate empirical model for [SOURCE: Page_4]

82    a given site remains a non-trivial, expert-dependent process that complicates routine ET$_0$ [SOURCE: Page_4]

83    estimation and its integration into decision support services. [SOURCE: Page_4]

84           This study explores whether leveraging climate reanalysis datasets can help overcome [SOURCE: Page_4]

85    these challenges. We hypothesize that machine learning (ML) models trained exclusively on [SOURCE: Page_4]

86    local and extensive historical coverage of reanalysis data, and used to predict ET$_0$ from a [SOURCE: Page_4]

87    limited set of weather variables (from only a few meteorological instruments) can achieve ET$_0$ [SOURCE: Page_4]

88    estimation accuracy comparable to established empirical formulas. If confirmed, this approach [SOURCE: Page_4]

89    could significantly simplify ET$_0$ estimation by reducing instrumentation requirements and [SOURCE: Page_4]

90    expanding applicability in data-scarce contexts. [SOURCE: Page_4]

91           Recent ML advancements have shown promise for deriving agroclimatic indicators, [SOURCE: Page_4]

92    including ET$_0$, from limited meteorological inputs. Such estimations have been performed [SOURCE: Page_4]

93    using methods like support vector machines (SVM), random forests (RF), Gaussian process [SOURCE: Page_4]

94    regression (GPR), and artificial neural networks (ANN) (Chia et al., 2020; Hebbalaguppae [SOURCE: Page_4]

95    Krishnashetty et al., 2021). Yet, most existing ML-based ET$_0$ models rely on short-term, site- [SOURCE: Page_4]

96    specific datasets, limiting their generalizability (see Supplementary Table 1). To our [SOURCE: Page_4]

97    knowledge, no previous work has utilized global climate reanalysis products—datasets [SOURCE: Page_4]

98    generated by assimilating historical weather station data into global circulation models—for [SOURCE: Page_4]

99    training ET$_0$ estimation models. Such reanalysis datasets provide near-global coverage of past [SOURCE: Page_4]

100   weather conditions at various resolutions (often down to 0.5 degrees), sub-daily time steps, [SOURCE: Page_4]

101   and spanning multiple decades. [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

102           In this paper, we explicitly test the hypothesis that ML models, trained from reanalysis [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   data using only a few simple on-site measurements (e.g., daily minimum and maximum [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   temperatures and rainfall), can achieve ET$_0$ estimation accuracies on par with widely used [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   empirical methods. By tapping into the historical depth and spatial consistency of the NASA [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   Power reanalysis dataset, we aim to develop locally adapted models capable of capturing the [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   long-term statistical relationships underlying ET$_0$-PM. Confirming this hypothesis would greatly [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   simplify the ET$_0$ estimation process, providing a practical and precise tool for routine [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   agricultural management and crop modelling in regions with sparse meteorological [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   infrastructure. [SOURCE: Page_5]

111           Unlike previous approaches limited by geographically sparse or short-term training [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   datasets, our method exploits the rich spatiotemporal coverage of reanalysis data to train and [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   evaluate locally adapted ML models systematically. Furthermore, we examine the sensitivity [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   of model performance to different subsets of input variables—a dimension rarely explored in [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   the literature. To evaluate our methodology, we train local extreme gradient boosting (XGB) [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   models (chosen for their robustness in regression tasks, e.g., as shown in hydrological studies [SOURCE: Page_5]

117   such as (Niazkar et al., 2024)) using NASA Power data. We then compare the ET$_0$ estimates [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   generated by these models—under various combinations of meteorological inputs—with ET$_0$ [SOURCE: Page_5]

119   values derived from the FAO-56-PM approach using ground measurements from twenty [SOURCE: Page_5]

120   weather stations spread across West Africa. We assess model performance both statistically [SOURCE: Page_5]

121   and qualitatively and discuss the implications of our findings for local calibration. Finally, we [SOURCE: Page_5]

122   present a Python software module called RF-ET$_0$, allowing researchers and practitioners to [SOURCE: Page_5]

123   easily train and apply these XGB models anywhere in the world. [SOURCE: Page_5]

124   2. Materials and methods [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   2.1. General workflow [SOURCE: Page_6]

126          The workflow for training, evaluating, and testing XGB models to estimate reference [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   evapotranspiration (ET$_0$) involved several key steps. First, ground truth ET$_0$ values (ET$_0$-PM- [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   GT) were computed using the FAO-56 Penman-Monteith equation and daily meteorological [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   data collected from weather stations. These included air temperature (TMIN, TMAX), wind [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   speed, rainfall, relative humidity (HR), and solar radiation (IRRAD). [SOURCE: Page_6]

131          To complement these observations, daily time series data for each site were retrieved [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   from the NASA Power reanalysis dataset, spanning 1979–2020. Using the same FAO-56 [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   Penman-Monteith equation, ET$_0$ values (ET$_0$-PM-RM) were computed from the reanalysis [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   data to serve as the target for model training. The reanalysis dataset was split into a training [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   subset (historical data up to one year before station observations) and a validation subset (the [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   period immediately preceding the observations). [SOURCE: Page_6]

137          XGBoost (XGB) models were trained locally for each site using combinations of [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   meteorological variables derived from reanalysis data as input features (e.g., TMIN, TMAX, [SOURCE: Page_6]

139   WIND, IRRAD), forming distinct model classes. Each model class represented the variable [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   configurations measurable by different types of weather stations. Model training used default [SOURCE: Page_6]

141   hyperparameters with 1000 boosting rounds for robust regression. [SOURCE: Page_6]

142          Model performance was evaluated on the validation dataset by comparing predicted [SOURCE: Page_6]

143   ET$_0$ values against ET$_0$-PM-RM, using root mean square error (RMSE) and the coefficient of [SOURCE: Page_6]

144   determination (R²) as metrics. Finally, the trained models were tested against ground truth [SOURCE: Page_6]

145   ET$_0$ values (ET$_0$-PM-GT) derived from station observations. Performance was assessed [SOURCE: Page_6]

146   across agro-ecological zones to analyze variability and identify the most reliable variable [SOURCE: Page_6]

147   combinations for different climatic conditions. [SOURCE: Page_6]

148   2.2. In situ meteorological datasets [SOURCE: Page_7]

149          For this study, we used the data from the archive of the AfricaRice weather station [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   network (https://dataverse.harvard.edu/dataverse/Weather), providing a representative set of [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   meteorological    observations    for   West   Africa,   as   the   data   provided   from   their [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   agrometeorological stations is openly available, homogeneous, and quality controlled. We [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   selected sites for which original metadata (coordinates and elevation) were provided, and [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   containing all the necessary variables to compute ET$_0$ using the FAO-56 standardized [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   Penman-Monteith equation: air temperature, rainfall, wind speed, vapour pressure, and global [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   radiation. A total of twenty sites were retained for the study, (Figure 1, Table 1). spanning [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   from 17°N, 18°W to 4°N, 18°E covering seven distinct agro-ecological zones as classified by [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   the FAO Global Agro-ecological Zones V$_4$, simplified (FAO and IIASA, 2021). Of these, [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   seventeen sites fall within the "lowland tropics", further categorised into nine "humid", five [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   "sub-humid", and three "semi-arid" zones; the remaining sites are distributed among [SOURCE: Page_7]

161   "desert/arid climate", "land with severe soil/terrain constraints", and "land with ample irrigated [SOURCE: Page_7]

162   soils" (Figure 2), ensuring a representative sample of the agro-ecological variability within [SOURCE: Page_7]

163   West Africa, albeit with some underrepresentation of arid zones. [SOURCE: Page_7]

164          The duration of these records varies depending on the station, and spans from March [SOURCE: Page_7]

165   19, 2012, to December 31, 2020. Table 2 describes dataset durations and periods considered [SOURCE: Page_7]

166   for the analysis on each station. To establish a ground truth for reference evapotranspiration [SOURCE: Page_7]

167   (ET$_0$-PM-GT), we utilised the FAO-56 standardized Penman-Monteith equation, implemented [SOURCE: Page_7]

168   through the reference_ET function in the Python Crop Simulation Environment (PCSE) [SOURCE: Page_7]

169   package v.5.4.0 (de Wit, 2023). [SOURCE: Page_7]

173   regional scale. The data for our measurement sites was retrieved using the [SOURCE: Page_8]

189   2.4. XGB model training and validation [SOURCE: Page_8]

190            In this study, XGBoost (XGB) models were trained and validated using daily weather [SOURCE: Page_8]

191   data from NASA Power for each of the twenty sites. We chose to work only with this family of [SOURCE: Page_8]

193   wanted to focus on the comparison in the performance of models of the same nature, but [SOURCE: Page_8]

196   train twenty local XGB models using data from NASA Power, as its API and companion tools [SOURCE: Page_8]

197   allow for very efficient requests. For each site, a data partitioning was done to split the data [SOURCE: Page_8]

198   into training and validation subsets. Specifically, the training dataset included NASA Power [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

199   records until a year prior to the first on-site observations, while the days leading up to these [SOURCE: Page_9]

200   first observations formed the validation dataset. The in-situ observations themselves served [SOURCE: Page_9]

270   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_11]

271          Our study contributes to the active research on machine learning (ML) methodologies [SOURCE: Page_11]

272   for estimating ET$_0$ from a limited dataset of meteorological variables. In a field inclined towards [SOURCE: Page_11]

273   the application of various ML approaches, we've selected XGB, and specifically extreme [SOURCE: Page_11]

11 [SOURCE: Page_11]

274   gradient boosting (XGBoost) due to its proven robustness and established track record [SOURCE: Page_12]

275   (Nielsen, 2016). Central to our discourse is the challenge of training data acquisition—a facet [SOURCE: Page_12]

276   that, in our view, has not received due attention. The prevalent practice in the literature utilises [SOURCE: Page_12]

277   weather data from a small number of locations over short time series to train models (see [SOURCE: Page_12]

278   Supplementary Table 1 for a curated selection of studies on the topic). These models are [SOURCE: Page_12]

279   then expected to extrapolate ET$_0$ estimations to larger, often regional scales, which risks [SOURCE: Page_12]

280   significant extrapolation errors during application. [SOURCE: Page_12]

281          We propose a methodological pivot, employing a transversal ML approach that utilises [SOURCE: Page_12]

282   local meteorological data from assimilation databases. This strategy has the distinct [SOURCE: Page_12]

283   advantage of being deployable in areas devoid of ground weather data, offering uniform [SOURCE: Page_12]

284   datasets that boast extensive historical records and spatial representativity—crucial for [SOURCE: Page_12]

285   models that need to operate across varied geographic expanses. We harnessed the breadth [SOURCE: Page_12]

286   of NASA Power's data, spanning a grid resolution of 0.5° x 0.5°, effectively training a model [SOURCE: Page_12]

287   for every ~3000km² under West Africa's latitudes. However, we acknowledge that such a [SOURCE: Page_12]

288   resolution may not fully encapsulate the microclimatic diversity within these regions, especially [SOURCE: Page_12]

289   in areas with complex terrain. One solution to this limitation could involve debiasing reanalysis [SOURCE: Page_12]

290   data using historical ground measurements, potentially through methods such as linear [SOURCE: Page_12]

291   regression, prior to training the ML models (Chandler, William S., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_12]

292          Moreover, our approach stands to be further refined by incorporating data from sources [SOURCE: Page_12]

293   with finer resolutions, such as the fifth generation of European ReAnalysis climate products [SOURCE: Page_12]

294   (Copernicus ERA$_5$) (Hersbach et al., 2020; Muñoz-Sabater et al., 2021). This would offer a [SOURCE: Page_12]

295   more detailed 0.1° x 0.1° resolution and an extensive historical reach back to 1950—allowing [SOURCE: Page_12]

296   the inclusion of rarer climatic events in our training set, however at a price of lengthier API [SOURCE: Page_12]

297   calls thus overall computation time. Such granularity is anticipated to enhance the [SOURCE: Page_12]

298   representativeness of training data and, consequently, the performance of ML models, [SOURCE: Page_12]

299   although this hypothesis warrants additional research to quantify the influence of training data [SOURCE: Page_12]

300   volume on model performance. [SOURCE: Page_12]

12 [SOURCE: Page_12]

301           For example, the Hargreaves-Samani model (George H. Hargreaves and Zohrab A. [SOURCE: Page_13]

302   Samani, 1985), the Schendel model (Schendel, U., 1967), and the Priestley-Taylor model [SOURCE: Page_13]

303   (Priestley and Taylor, 1972) are based only on solar radiation and air temperature values ; the [SOURCE: Page_13]

304   Turc radiation-based model (Turc, L, 1961) uses temperature and relative humidity ; the [SOURCE: Page_13]

305   Makkink-based models (Allen, Richard, 2003; Hansen, 1984; Makkink, G.F., 1957) use [SOURCE: Page_13]

306   radiation-related parameters. A thorough review of these methods can be found in (McMahon [SOURCE: Page_13]

307   et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_13]

308           Aiming to serve practitioners with scant meteorological infrastructure, our study sought [SOURCE: Page_13]

309   to validate the accuracy and precision of XGB-based ET$_0$ estimation models against the FAO- [SOURCE: Page_13]

310   56 standardized PM equation. Our experiments scrutinised the impact of a limited set of [SOURCE: Page_13]

311   input variables on ET$_0$ output quality. We also compared the ML-derived ET$_0$ estimates with [SOURCE: Page_13]

312   those calculated from ground truth data across diverse West African climates, ensuring our [SOURCE: Page_13]

313   models' efficacy relative to conventional methods. [SOURCE: Page_13]

314           The practicality of our approach is underscored by its minimal data requirements: a [SOURCE: Page_13]

315   farm manager equipped with only a min/max thermometer and a rain funnel shall be able to [SOURCE: Page_13]

316   use a locally trained model to estimate ET$_0$ with a level of precision and accuracy usually [SOURCE: Page_13]

317   attainable by also having anemometer or hygrometer readings. Existing literature suggests [SOURCE: Page_13]

318   different combinations of variables for efficient ET$_0$ estimation by models : (Granata, 2019) [SOURCE: Page_13]

319   suggests mean temperature, mean relative humidity, and net solar radiation; wind and sunny [SOURCE: Page_13]

320   hours as the strict minimum information for most locations according to (Shabani et al., 2020), [SOURCE: Page_13]

321   and most empirical formulas need at least information about wind speed plus deficit pressure [SOURCE: Page_13]

322   or solar radiation with air temperature. Meanwhile, our approach showed that it can yield [SOURCE: Page_13]

323   models that perform relatively well, starting with only information about minimum, maximum, [SOURCE: Page_13]

324   and average daily air temperature. With a dependency on a high number of devices to [SOURCE: Page_13]

325   measure each variable needed, there is a greater risk in data gaps leading to impossibility to [SOURCE: Page_13]

326   calculate ET$_0$; this issue of missing or incomplete meteorological data has been discussed in [SOURCE: Page_13]

327   (Koudahe et al., 2018). Our finding suggests that practitioners may only need a quality [SOURCE: Page_13]

13 [SOURCE: Page_13]

328   thermometer for reliable ET$_0$ estimation, simplifying an on-farm weather data acquisition [SOURCE: Page_14]

329   process that is already challenging in the region (Dinku, 2019). Stacking up variables will make [SOURCE: Page_14]

330   the estimations more reliable. Compared to other models that overestimate ET$_0$, our models [SOURCE: Page_14]

331   demonstrated better stability and less bias across various climates, although this observation [SOURCE: Page_14]

332   necessitates further validation with a broader dataset. The use of twenty weather stations [SOURCE: Page_14]

333   provides a solid foundation for our performance analysis, but a more expansive dataset would [SOURCE: Page_14]

334   enable a more comprehensive understanding of the models' applicability and robustness. [SOURCE: Page_14]

335          Most of these empiric models have been initially defined in a given climatic [SOURCE: Page_14]

336   environment and are less reliable or at least need recalibration when used in different climates. [SOURCE: Page_14]

337   In the context of climate change, with anticipated shifts in climatic zones (Cui et al., 2021), our [SOURCE: Page_14]

338   universally applicable method, supported by global assimilation data, could significantly aid in [SOURCE: Page_14]

339   ET$_0$ estimation efforts. Our XGB- ET$_0$ models, though in need of further validation, show [SOURCE: Page_14]

340   potential for at least equivalent performance compared to traditional empirical models, with [SOURCE: Page_14]

341   the added benefit of being more robust across different AEZs. This robustness is particularly [SOURCE: Page_14]

342   important in reducing bias risks, a common issue with empirical formulas lacking quality [SOURCE: Page_14]

343   assessment avenues. [SOURCE: Page_14]
