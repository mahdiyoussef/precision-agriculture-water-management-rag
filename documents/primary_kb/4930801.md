Introduction Conservation and utilization of land and water resources are antinomic issues. Indeed, anthropogenic activities associated with farming practices lead to soil erosion, soil and water pollution that is triggered by the increase of demographic pressure, the use of marginal areas such sloping lands and rapid land-use change due to economic demands and exceptional meteorological events (drought and typhoon) linked to the climate change. Commonly, increasing population densities has encouraged agricultural production to shift from the rich-soil lowlands to the degradation-prone marginal uplands where the soil fertility management and the water stock management is more and more complicated. In this context of agricultural intensification, the natural process of soil erosion and the water pollution enhanced and induced by the human activities became a major economic and environmental problem both for the individual concern such as farming system level and the regional area such as the watershed. It affects the livelihoods of smallholders and hinders long-term development in these areas. [SOURCE: Page_2]

The supply of agricultural products and ecosystem services are critical in meeting the needs of society and enhancing the quality of life. If agriculture is to meet the challenge of increasing soil productivity and production in the context of complex mixtures of biophysical, economic and social drivers, then agricultural systems must be encouraged to include the concepts of environmental and social sustainability that are fundamental to resilience. The term sustainability implies both that high yields can be maintained, even in the face of major shocks, and that agricultural practices have acceptable environmental impacts and social constraints. Achieving sustainable, efficient agricultural production systems will require a significant rethinking of agricultural priorities and new approaches to production. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Whilst innovations in agricultural practices have kept abreast with global food supply, they have had inadvertent impacts on the environment and the provision of ecosystem services bringing into question the long-term sustainability of the agricultural sector in meeting [SOURCE: Page_2]

future food demand. Environmental quality has been a casualty of agricultural development, with associated losses in productivity and ecosystem services. Securing future production will require revisiting and broadening these priorities, to include:  Securing and increasing food production under changing climate and market conditions and the looming water crisis;  Protecting and restoring ecosystem functions and services in agricultural landscapes, including opportunities for mitigation of greenhouse gas emissions from agricultural sources;  Improving the capacity of large numbers of small-scale producers to adapt and improve productivity and income in sustainable ways;  Reducing the vulnerability of the poor and groups that are marginal to economic production to climate and other global changes. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Agriculture does not of itself compromise ecosystem services. On the contrary, agriculture is essentially a means of concentrating and enhancing provisioning services – but this can often come at the cost of other functions, particularly regulating services which are intimately related to land cover. For example: deforestation reduces the ability to regulate erosion; loss of wetlands reduces the ability of streams to regulate floods and water quality. It is important that actions to preserve and improve the quality of ecosystem services enhance, rather than compete with, livelihoods of the rural poor either directly, through improvements in local environments and production, or indirectly through financial compensation or payments for ecosystem services. [SOURCE: Page_3]

The focus of Topic 4 is on the tools and methods that effectively promote and enhance the aforementioned discussed services through the window of integrated land and water resources. Over the past 50 years, scientists and land managers have made significant progress in the development of technologies, methods and techniques that promote sustainable agriculture and the delivery of ecosystem services. The principles and techniques embedded in conservation agriculture (CA) and integrated watershed (catchment) development form the basis of these approaches to conserving the natural resource base with the long-term objective of sustainability and stability. It is not possible to undertake a comprehensive review of methods and tools for integrated land and water management that we have out our disposal in such a limited space. Consequently we have limited our review of the topic to CA which forms a pillar, in on-farm management of land and water resources, the concepts associated with integrated watershed management and possible mechanisms that could assist in supporting adoption of these conservation practices. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Reviews In an ongoing long-term study on soil erosion undertaken by a consortium of international organizations and national governments in Southeast Asia, the Management of Soil Erosion [SOURCE: Page_3]

Consortium has studied erosion and land use changes over the past five years in 27 catchments in Indonesia, Lao PDR, Philippines, Thailand and Vietnam (Valentin et al., 2008). The key conclusions of this study are as follows: (i) soil erosion is predominantly influenced by land use rather than environmental characteristics not only at the plot scale but also at the catchment scale; (ii) slash and burn shifting cultivation with sufficiently long rotations (one year of cultivation, eight years of fallow) is too often unjustly blamed for land degradation; (iii) traditional slash and burn systems have been replaced by continuous cropping systems that include maize and cassava, both of which promote high rates of soil erosion at the catchment scale; (iv) conservation technologies are efficient in reducing runoff and total sediment yield at the catchment scale; (v) the adoption of improved soil management technologies by upland farmers is not a function of the degree of intensification of their farming system and/or of their incomes; and (vi) a failure to adopt appropriate land use management strategies will result in further rapid resource degradation with negative impacts to downstream communities. [SOURCE: Page_4]

What is clear from these catchment studies is that traditional practices (slash and burn) are sustainable systems in their own right as long as the fallow period is great than 7 years, and the move towards cash cropping systems significantly enhances the loss of soil particularly when conservation approaches are not implemented. Clearly, moving to different farming systems on these sloping lands necessitates changes in the way land is managed. And hence the concepts of CA – and the implementation of soil conservation practices – become an imperative for sustainable land management. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Conservation agriculture that is based on the adoption of zero- or minimum-tillage, was developed to address soil degradation and has been widely adopted in North and South America. Based on the success of the Brazilian “Zero-tillage Revolution”, land resources managers and agriculturalist have viewed this approach as an attractive potential solution to reversing land degradation and increasing productivity in Sub-Saharan Africa and Asia (Fowler and Rockström, 2001; Hobbs, 2007). Zero-tillage, together with crop residue management (mulches) and crop rotations, forms the pillars of CA, as now actively promoted by a growing number of research and extension programmes, supported by major international initiatives (e.g. FAO – Benites and Ashburner, 2003; FAO, 2008; the Direct-sowing, Mulch-based, Conservation agriculture (DMC) – systems initiative under the Global Forum on Agricultural Research (GFAR), the European Conservation Agriculture Federation (ECAF), etc.). There is clear evidence that CA has had a significant impact on farmer incomes and the health of soils. And it is often supported that CA could become a significant tool in addressing greenhouse gas mitigation. [SOURCE: Page_4]

However from a few recent articles (Erenstein, 2002, 2003; Bolliger et al., 2006; Knowler and Bradshaw, 2007; Affholder et al., 2008, 2010; Lahmar, 2009; Giller et al., 2009), it appears that CA has escaped critical analysis to be applied as a panacea through large policy. The more critical publications (Bolliger et al., 2006; Bolliger, 2007; Gowing and Palmer, 2008) address pertinent issues that include: (a) Which principles of CA, and under which conditions, actually contribute to the effects sought?; (b) What are the trade-offs of implementing CA?; (c) Does CA address a need identified by the farmer or one mainly identified by scientists and policymakers?; and (d) Are there preconditions that need to be in place for adoption by smallholder farmers? Giller et al (2009) argue that the empirical evidence is not clear and consistent on many aspects of CA (increase yields, reduced labour requirements, improve soil fertility and reduce erosion) nor is it always clear which of the principles of CA contribute to the desired effects. Although cases can be found where such claims are supported there are equally convincing scientific reports that contradict these claims. Concerns include decreased yields often observed with CA; increased labour requirements when herbicides are not used; an important gender shift of the labour burden to women; and a lack of mulch due to poor-productivity and the priority given to feeding livestock with crop residues. They conclude that there is an urgent need for critical assessment in terms of which ecological and socio-economic conditions that CA is best suited for smallholder farming. [SOURCE: Page_5]

So, currently debate continues as to the role of CA as an effective conservation practice; one side advocating it as a viable and attractive option, and others who question the approach. Despite this, it seems clear that CA does have an important role to play in addressing the significant challenges facing farmer communities in managing their soil and water resources. It is clearly not a silver bullet but one of the many tools in a tool box that land resource managers can access. CA advocates always stress that CA is not a formula- solution. Every situation requires careful application of the three pillars, configured to local conditions (environmental and social) in an integrated and iterative mode. The aforementioned questions concerning CA as posed by several authors are worthy of addressing if we are going to be more targeted in defining where CA will have the greatest opportunity of being successful. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Clearly there are a range of technologies and options that small scale farmers can adopt that will significantly reduce land and water degradation at the farm level. WOCAT has developed a data base of simple technologies and interventions that promote sustainable land management. However, it is argued that uptake by small scale farmers is low for a range of reasons that are farmer specific. The fact that land degradation is often viewed as a low priority in competing drivers of change that confront small scale farmers, invariably contributes to low adoption rates (Noble et al., 2006). Both the financial investments and labour required to implement these interventions are often viewed as a risk, especially when there are no quick and tangible returns on these investments (Orange et al., 2008). Additionally, in many cases in developing countries the role of off-farm opportunities has a [SOURCE: Page_5]

great priority leading to a decline in focus on the farming enterprise (Clement et al., 2009). Clearly there is a need to rethink the whole discourse on adoption and the way in which land resource planning is undertaken. [SOURCE: Page_6]

In addition to, and often complimentary to CA, there is a wider range of relatively well known sustainable land use practices. These include agro-ecologically sound practices of forest plantation, agro-forestry systems and tree-based land use alternatives (e.g. Baier and Dumanski, 1991; Valentin et al., 1991; Geay and Dao The Tuan, 2000; Castella and Quang, 2002; Cramb, 2005). Although these systems would provide long term benefits to potential adopters, they often do not provide sufficient immediate economic returns to resource- constraint farmers, i.e. quick and tangible returns on investments (Orange et al., 2008; Affholder et al., 2010). Often the benefits associated with the adoption of new and improved approaches to land management are not evident for 3-5 years. Further, the environmental services that these land use options provide, i.e. erosion control, watershed services, biodiversity conservation and carbon storage are usually not rewarded and are commonly only indirectly connected to economic activities. As a result, adoption of improved land and water management systems has to date been low. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Payments for Environmental Services (PES) or other incentive mechanisms present a new approach to enhanced adoption of more sustainable land use options that focus directly on creating a conditional benefit transfer between providers of the environmental services and the beneficiaries of these services. PES schemes are based on the principles that those who benefit from environmental services should pay for them, and that those who contribute to generating these services should be compensated for providing them. Hence, the approach seeks to create mechanisms that internalize what would otherwise be an externality (Pagiola and Platais, 2002; Tomich et al., 2004). Such schemes can take advantage of upland-lowland interactions in generating environmental benefits while improving the livelihoods for upper-catchment agricultural households (Georges et al., 2009). As such, PES is also increasingly seen as an opportunity for poverty reduction and the enhancement of sustainable development within integrated natural resource management approaches (Asquith et al., 2002; Pagiola et al., 2005; Minang et al., 2007) (See Box 1). [SOURCE: Page_6]

Box 1. Yen Binh reforestation program and PES, Vietnam. There are several examples of PES based systems that have been established in the region. These schemes have been of mixed success and have focused largely in Vietnam (Wunder et al., 2005). An example of one of these schemes established in Vietnam is drawn from Yen Bai Province. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Yen Bai is located in the upland region of the Red River Basin, approximately 200 km northwest of Hanoi and is one of the poorest provinces in the northern mountainous area of Vietnam. The province is inhabited by Kinh with several ethnic minorities including the H’mong, Dao, Tay, Thai, Nung, San Chay, Phu La. In the 1960’s approximately, 60% of the province was forested. In order to reverse the decline in forest cover and to resort the forest cover to 60% by 2010, State Forest Enterprises (SFE’s) have embarked on an ambitious reforestation program in which households in the Tan Huong commune (Yen Binh District) signed reforestation and protection contracts with the Yen Binh’s SFE. Farmers were paid approximately USD 155 per hectare for reforestation which included USD 116 for tree planting and USD 39 for tending the forest for the first 3 years after planting. The income generated through these reforestation contracts was quite significant at the household level. Further, households also received financial and technical support for reforestation for production purposes. In contrast, for protection contract the payment that contract households received was USD 1.6 per hectare per year. [SOURCE: Page_7]

In the aforementioned case, the reason for the reforestation and protection was in part driven by the protection of a hydroelectric dam from sedimentation caused by upland agriculture on degraded lands. However, there are a number of myths regarding the generalized hydrological benefits of forests: in particular the assumed hydrological benefits of upstream reforestation for the protection hydroelectric dams from siltation. For example, an in-depth study from a watershed in Central America has shown that well- managed pasture can be as effective as forests in controlling erosion, consumes less water than tree cover, and provides higher incomes to land users (Aylward et al., 1999). Wunder et al. (2005) argue that in terms of reducing flood risk, the massive Vietnamese reforestation efforts are unlikely to have any mitigating impact on large-scale catastrophic flood events, but they may reduce the severity of more localized floods in smaller watersheds. [SOURCE: Page_7]

In order to achieve sustainable development and management of land resources that deliver the desired outcomes of enhance productivity, improved livelihoods and the provision of ecosystem services, an integrated approach to land-use management and planning is required by systemic approach, which leads to consider the basin level as a driven-force coupled with the administrative framework. The development of a management plan that considers the social and economic needs of communities living in both the upper watersheds and the lower river basins is a perquisite. The management plan should comprise all the activities required to organize land and other resource use within a watershed in the course of providing the goods and services defined by clear objectives that are developed in a consultative manner, while at the same time maintaining and supporting the livelihoods of resident populations. The management plan is implemented by all landowners and concerned stakeholders under the guidance of appropriate management bodies and supported by pragmatic policy instruments and innovative financing mechanisms. Incentives need to be offered to encourage desired land uses and land management practices and to align private interests with the public good. Compensation needs to be provided to land users negatively affected by the plans. Such an integrated approach to catchment management does not look at land use management in isolation but integrates the planning of a watershed/district in a holistic manner taking into accounts [SOURCE: Page_7]

the needs of all stakeholders. Recent advance in remote sensing will be invaluable in identifying what could be term ‘hotspots’ within basins that require specific attention as well as assist in the planning of watersheds. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The challenges facing sustainable land and water in the future are not founded on lack of technological tools or interventions that are at the disposal of managers. There are clear impediments to adoption of these practices that will require innovative ways of knowledge dissemination, carefully crafted policies, incentives and commitment from governments and agencies charged with the management of land and water resources. In addition, there will be a need to create desirable innovative productive agro-ecosystems that maintain and enhance a range of ecosystem services and that attract the will of each farmer. In many cases, this can best be achieved by mimicking aspects of natural systems (LeFroy et al 1999), for example:  Paddy fields mimic the water retention of natural wetlands to provide multiple benefits including rice and fish production, flood mitigation, ground water recharge, soil erosion control, water purification;  Plantations can mimic natural forests, with well developed understorey to prevent soil erosion, provide habitat and promote biodiversity. [SOURCE: Page_8]
