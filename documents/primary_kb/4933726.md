11   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   Background and aims: Phenotypic plasticity in associations enables higher resource use efficiency and niche 13   complementarity. This study aims to (1) examine the phenotypic plasticity of soybean functional traits to 14   relay-cropping under drought conditions and (2) evaluate different soybean genotypes and their interactions. [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   Methods: A preliminary investigation comprised field experiments in micro-plots in non-irrigated conditions 16   performed for two years (2022 and 2023) to examine trait responses to barley relay-cropping for four 17   soybean genotypes compared to sole crops. In 2022, we experienced a record-setting drought that highly 18   impacted the soybean yield. Ten above- and below-ground functional traits related to light interception, 19   resource allocation and soil exploration were measured at barley harvest and one month after, at the 20   reproductive stage. [SOURCE: Page_2]

21   Results: Our results showed that relay-cropping can exacerbate drought stress and, consequently, 22   competition for water between crops. This led to phenological delay and growth abortion. However, root 23   functional traits, such as lateral root mean angle, specific root length and root nodules densities, were higher 24   in relay-cropping, demonstrating plant response to water stress and competition. [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   Conclusion: Soybean genotypes showed a plasticity of root functional traits to respond to competition and 26   water stress. Agricultural practices (timely irrigation and/or adapting relay-cropping design) could modulate 27   water stress, and appropriate cultivars would allow relay-cropped soybeans to sustain temporally dry 28   conditions. [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   Keywords: Intercropping, phenotypic plasticity, root traits, soybean, water competition [SOURCE: Page_2]

31       1   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   Crop diversification brings a range of benefits to the agroecosystems (Ren et al. 2014), such as reducing pest, 33   disease and weed outbreaks (Amossé et al. 2013a; Lai et al. 2019), improving soil health by enhancing 34   microbiome diversity and functions (Wang et al. 2022), and increasing carbon sequestration in soil and 35   nutrient availability while reducing nutrient losses (Bergkvist et al. 2011; Amossé et al. 2014; Cong et al. 36   2015). Intercropping (or multiple cropping) is a crop diversification practice involving the cultivation of two 37   or more crops during the same growing season (Willey 1979). Intercropping can vary in spatial and temporal 38   patterns, with rows, strips or mixed intercropping being different spatial arrangements of the associated crops 39   (Vandermeer 1989). It encompasses various forms of multiple cropping, with distinctions based on the 40   functions of associated crops: cash crop, service crop or permanent cover (Amossé et al. 2013b; Duchene et 41   al. 2017; Gardarin et al. 2022). Additionally, differentiation occurs based on the timing of sowing and 42   growing periods – simultaneous or relay-cropping (Lithourgidis et al. 2011; Tanveer et al. 2017). [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   Intercropping shows advantageous productivity when compared to monoculture yields on the same surfaces; 44   an average land equivalent ratio (LER) (sum of yield ratio between associated and pure crops of both crops) 45   of 1.3 for intercropping means that the association produces the same yield as 1.3 ha of monoculture 46   (Bedoussac et al. 2015; Martin-Guay et al. 2018). This productive advantage of intercropping over 47   monoculture is explained by the more efficient use of resources thanks to niche differentiation and 48   complementarity (Vandermeer 1989). It is guided by the interactions between the two crops through 49   competition, facilitation or complementarity (Lithourgidis et al. 2011). Competition and facilitation are both 50   the consequences of one crop affecting the shared environment, which impact the second crop negatively or 51   positively, respectively (Donald 1963; Li et al. 2014). Complementarity is the lack of competition due to 52   ecological niche repartition in the environment (Vandermeer 1989). In plant mixtures, it manifests as one 53   plant’s response to another to alleviate competition through developing different acquisition strategies 54   (Duchene et al. 2017; Tanveer et al. 2017). As it affects resource use, complementarity drives the 55   overyielding of intercropping (Willey 1996; Bedoussac et al. 2015). Most intercropping systems include 56   legume crops to enhance nitrogen acquisition complementarity (Tanveer et al. 2017; Martin-Guay et al. 57   2018; Lamichhane et al. 2023). The interactions between crops are described as indirect as they are produced 58   by modification of the local environment (e.g. shading) (Vandermeer 1989). This alteration of the 59   environment provokes specific responses of the species within the plant community. Since the interactions 60   between plants are via the environment, they are sensitive to environmental conditions and variations. [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   Crop responses to the association could differ under drought, as competition between crops would increase 62   the pressure on water resources. A meta-analysis of legume/cereal intercropping showed that irrigation 63   significantly positively impacted intercropped legume yield: with irrigation, the intercropped legume out 64   yielded monoculture (Ren et al. 2014). Few studies about relay-cropping were conducted under drought 65   conditions. Duncan Schapaugh (1997) and Sandler et al. (2015) reported growth abortion and significant 66   biomass and yield losses of the associated soybean in wheat/soybean relay-cropping when drought occurred 67   during the coexistence phase. 70   Crop responses to intercropping could depend on the genotypes and their phenotypic plasticity, i.e. ‘the 71   ability of individual genotypes to produce different phenotypes when exposed to different environmental 72   conditions' (Pigliucci et al. 2006). 389           4.1     4.1. Impact of the relay-cropping induced water deficit on soybean phenology and 390           productivity 391   Relay-cropping creates a short-term association between a winter and spring crop, barley and soybean, in this 392   study. It allows the second crop to recover its vegetative growth during the recovery phase to ensure a 393   sufficient yield (Tanveer et al. 2017; Mohammed et al. 2022). In non-irrigated conditions, the soybean is 394   sown in a state of water scarcity, leading to a yield loss from 84% to 95% compared to the monocropped 395   soybean. Barley yields in relay-cropping treatments were similar to monocropped barley yields (Figure 2). In 396   2022, the yield differences between pure and relay-cropping can be explained mainly by the experimental 397   conditions: removing the bird nets damaged the barley ears. Although the literature has evidence that relay- 398   cropping increases land productivity even more than simultaneous intercropping (1.7 (Gesch et al. 2023) 399   against 1.3 (Martin-Guay et al. 2018; Li et al. 2023)), we got a mean LER of 0.92 due to poor soybean yield. 400   The LER of relay-cropping for both years is mainly reinforced by the yield of barley (Figure II in 401   supplementary data). The high barley yield explains the major impact of relay-cropping on the available soil 402   water. The water reserve in the soil was already depleted when the soybean was sown, whereas it was high 403   during the soybean monoculture treatments (Figure 1). This statement is corroborated by a side study 404   conducted in the second year, where soil and plant water potential were measured in monocropped and relay- 405   cropped soybeans (unpublished results). Furthermore, due to poor growth, the relay-cropped soybean root 406   system was largely shallower than the monocropped soybean one for all genotypes, preventing it from 407   accessing the water available in deeper soil layers. At barley harvest, the relay-cropped soybeans were 408   delayed in phenological growth compared to monoculture (stage V$_2$ against R$_2$; Table 1); moreover, the 409   plant biomass, height and the leaf areas were significantly lower. After the harvest of the barley, the amount 410   of rainfall did not allow the resumption of vegetative growth during the recovery phase. The relay-cropped 411   soybeans started their reproductive phase very early at only 4 or 6 nods; the monocropped soybean presented 412   substantial shoot biomass and twice the number of nods at this stage (Table 1). The recovery phase is central 413   to the productivity of the soybean in relay-cropping: the longer it is, the higher the biomass produced and, 414   thus, the final yield (Chang et al. 2018; Ahmed et al. 2020). Soybeans are sensitive to water stress (Aminah 415   et al. 2013) In relay-cropping, early growth is critical to ensure an effective recovery. Irrigation at emergence 416   and barley harvest seems mandatory. Similar results with soybeans that experienced flash droughts during 417   the summer in wheat/soybean relay-cropping were observed by Sandler et al. (2015). The strong competition 418   during the early stages of soybean cultivation and the decreased water availability during the recovery phase 419   are major causes of yield reduction in relay-cropping. It can vary from null to more than 70% depending on 420   the environmental conditions (Lamichhane et al. 2023). [SOURCE: Page_16]

463   Relay-cropped soybean showed plasticity in its growth with more resource allocation in roots than 464   monoculture. The drought experienced during the early stages of coexistence and the recovery phase for both 465   years was too intense for the shallower root system of the relay-cropped soybean, which was delayed in its 466   growth. The plasticity of root traits associated with soil exploration can be positively correlated with yield 467   under drought stress (Kashiwagi et al. 2006). More specifically, vertical root expansion through the increase 468   of root angles is a typical response of soybean genotypes to drought. Exploring and encouraging these traits 469   may allow better early growth and development of plants (Dayoub et al. 2021). Phenotype plasticity does not 470   always imply an adaptive response regarding the plant’s resilience to environmental stress (Schneider et 471   Lynch 2020). A distinction must be made between plastic and adaptive response to environmental stress. 472   Phenotype plasticity finds its limits as it comes at a cost for the plant (DeWitt et al. 1998), especially under 473   stress (Valladares et al. 2007). Moreover, contrary to monoculture, associated crops must acclimate to plant 474   interactions in addition to abiotic factors and, despite its adaptive potential, phenotype plasticity. A milder 475   climate could allow the soybean to benefit from its higher root mass per total biomass ratio and grow faster 476   to ensure sufficient biomass and produce attractive yields. 502   This study demonstrated significant water depletion in soil by the first crop in relay-cropping and water 503   competition inducing a growth slowdown and phenological delay. Critical phenotypic plasticity of functional 504   root traits was observed between monocropped and relay-cropped soybeans. This can be 506   explained by the considerable pressure of drought conditions in relay-cropping treatments, which resulted in 507   genotype biomass and yield collapse. Relay-cropping in non-irrigated conditions would increase water 508   competition between the winter and spring crops and induce unidirectional responses of genotype 509   performance traits. Extreme drought events must be forecasted recurrently with climate change, and cropping 510   systems, including genetic plant materials, must be adapted to such climatic events. Relay-cropping, 511   predominantly comprising winter crops, as in this study, would not constitute a new practice for climate 512   adaptation. However, the highlighting of several root traits plasticity factors (i.e. lateral root mean angle, root 513   length and nodule density) brings new perspectives to identify and select appropriate genotypes for relay- 514   cropping under lesser extreme drought conditions that can be modulated by agricultural practices under 515   climate change context (e.g. no or low tillage; relay-cropping design by decreasing sowing density and row 516   numbers and spacing of winter crop; and irrigation by wastewater reuse).
