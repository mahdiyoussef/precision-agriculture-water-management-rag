## C. SYNTHESIS OF RESEARCH WORK

FOREWORD As a hydrochemist, during my first year of research (honours project, 1998) I discovered that when using the right combination of environmental tracers we can elucidate very specific processes occurring within complex hydrosystems. In a fractured rock aquifer system, I was able to use δ18O, δ2H and δ13C isotopic data to reveal that the mineral spring waters we were drinking were derived from rainwater aged ~ 3000 to 3500 years, and had travelled several kilometres below the earth’s surface to collect magmatic CO$_2$. In the years that followed, I developed my specialization in the use of environmental tracers in hydrosystems. During my doctorate (obtained 2003) I used environmental tracer data in a fractured rock aquifer system to identify groundwater mixing, constrain flow pathways in a fracture flow numerical model, and to explore the potential of rare earth elements (REEs) as tracers of groundwater flow. Following on from this I was involved in post-doctoral research that utilised remote sensing and modelling to investigate hydrogeological processes at a regional scale (e.g. Tweed et al., 2007, 2009; Ley-Cooper et al., 2008; Leblanc et al., 2007, 2008, 2009, 2011, 2012; Frappart et al., 2011). I have not included the remote sensing results in this synthesis because the bulk of my research career involved using environmental tracers. However, after this experience of working at regional scales, I was motivated to continue on with multi-disciplinary projects that were able to elucidate basin-scale processes by coupling remote sensing and environmental tracer data. At the basin-scale, I found the environmental tracer data was essential to fill in knowledge gaps on the long term evolution of aquifers in response to environmental change. In the ensuing collaborative research projects I investigated processes controlling the occurrence of saline waters and identified recharge processes in semi-arid and arid basins. More recently with colleagues I have begun to work in wet and dry tropical systems at both local and regional scales. These projects have included modelling the environmental tracer data to quantify hydrogeological processes, and analysing the links between water cycles and solute cycles (including pathways of agricultural contaminants and dissolved inorganic carbon). It has been due to a series of successful collaborative research efforts that I can present the range of works described in this document. In this research synthesis I use the term “I”, however as evident from my list of publications each research project has involved collaborative work with colleagues, and with students at the Honours, Masters and Doctorate level. ## RÉSUMÉ

Deux grands types de variabilités peuvent affecter la dynamique des eaux de surface et souterraines dans l’espace et dans le temps : variabilités naturelles (géologiques et climatiques), et variabilités anthropiques. Comprendre l'influence de ces variabilités sur les hydrosystèmes est essentiel pour la gestion et protection des ressources en eau en terme de quantité et de qualité. L’étude de l’évolution d’un hydrosystème en réponse à tout ou partie de ces variabilités représente un véritable défi scientifique et constitue la trame de cette synthèse : l'analyse de l'influence des variabilités géologique, climatique et anthropique sur les hydrosystèmes au moyen des traceurs environnementaux. [SOURCE: Page_14]

L'étude des dynamiques des hydrosystèmes nécessite une approche hydrochimique capable de capturer la variabilité sur plusieurs échelles spatio-temporelles. Les phénomènes géologiques et géomorphologiques évoluant très lentement par rapport au cycle hydrologique, on se contente souvent de caractériser leur variabilité dans le domaine spatial. Il n’en est pas de même pour les dynamiques climatiques qui, en plus des hétérogénéités spatiales, peuvent présenter une importante diversité de comportements suivant les échelles de temps considérées avec des phénomènes très rapides de crues, des comportements à moyen terme de sécheresses et une variabilité à très long terme liée aux cycles climatiques (incluant la paléohydrologie). Enfin, comme nous le verrons, les pressions anthropiques peuvent dans certains cas bouleverser le fonctionnement des hydrosystèmes et engendrer d’importants risques environnementaux. [SOURCE: Page_14]

À travers huit études de cas en Australie, cette synthèse présente l’apport des traceurs environnementaux pour compléter : 1) les réseaux de surveillance basés sur l’hydrodynamique, 2) la modélisation numérique et 3) l'hydro(géo)logie spatiale. Ces recherches se sont déclinées sur plusieurs types d’hydrosystèmes (aquifères poreux et fracturés, rivières, lacs) dans une variété de contextes climatiques (tempéré, semi-aride, aride et tropical sec et humide) avec des enjeux portant sur la quantité et la qualité des ressources en eau. Mes recherches ont également porté sur plusieurs composantes du cycle de l'eau : recharge et décharge des aquifères, voies de circulation souterraines, et interactions entre les eaux souterraines et les eaux de surface. Pour chaque site, une combinaison adaptée de traceurs environnementaux à été choisie dans la liste suivante : concentrations des ions majeurs, mineurs et traces, isotopes stables du carbone inorganique dissous (δ13C), isotopes stables de l'eau (δ18O et δ2H), radiocarbone (14C), tritium (3H), l'hexafluorure de soufre (SF$_6$) et les chlorofluorocarbones (CFC). Au fil des ans, les procédures d'échantillonnage sur le terrain pour les gaz dissous sont devenues plus simples (CFC), permettant le prélèvement d'échantillons dans les zones reculées. En outre, les développements récents technologiques récents en spectrométrie laser permettent désormais l’enregistrement in situ δ13C, δ18O et mesures de δ2H à des résolutions temporelles très fines. Ces traceurs environnementaux offrent donc la possibilité de capturer les variabilités des hydrosystèmes à très haute fréquence. [SOURCE: Page_14]

Dans les aquifères à géologie fortement hétérogène (roche fracturée et systèmes aquifères alluviaux), les traceurs environnementaux ont été utilisés pour mettre en évidence les voies d'écoulement des eaux souterraines et les processus de mélange. Les ions majeurs [SOURCE: Page_14]

13 | Capturing variability in heterogeneous and changing hydrosystems with environmental tracers [SOURCE: Page_14]

reflètent des réactions de dissolution de minéraux distincts et donc de mélange entre les eaux souterraines de différents aquifères, les valeurs δ18O et δ2H ont mis en évidence des variations temporelles dans les processus de recharge, et les valeurs de 14C et 3H ont indiqué des mélanges entre des eaux souterraines de différente origine. Cependant, d’importantes incertitudes ont été trouvée en ce qui concerne les âges apparents déterminés à partir du CFC, 14C et 3H. Ces incertitudes sont dues à la fois aux processus de transport de soluté et aux fortes hypothèses émises dans les modèles de calcul des âges. [SOURCE: Page_15]

Pour ce qui est de la variabilité climatique, nos études montrent l’importance des échelles d’études. Dans des petits sous-bassins versants (10<km^2) l’évolution sub-synchrone des débits de rivières et de la composition chimique a permis l'utilisation de traceurs environnementaux (δ18O, δ2H, et ions majeurs) pour décomposer les hydrographs à l’échelle des fluctuations journalières (crues) et saisonnières. En comparaison à des échelles spatio-temporelles plus grandes, l’évolution de l’hydrochimie montre des réponses relativement localisées, idiosyncrasiques ou retardés par rapport à l’hydrodynamique. Dans le bassin endoréique aride du Lac Eyre, les traceurs environnementaux n'ont pas permis de mettre en évidence la recharge des nappes souterraines par les eaux de crue épisodiques mais reflètent un processus de recharge dominant par infiltration diffuse de l'eau de pluie. Dans un bassin versant exposé à une forte sécheresse pluriannuelle, une baisse de niveau généralisée a été observée dans les lacs et l’aquifère sous-jascent, mais des évolutions chimiques très variées ont été observées en ce qui concerne la salinité et la concentration de nutriments des lacs. Ainsi la réponse du système chimique à la sécheresse a souligné d'importantes variations inter- lacustres dans les processus de transport et de stockage de contaminants. [SOURCE: Page_15]

De même, les résultats quant à l’analyse de l'influence des activités anthropiques sur les hydrosystèmes à l'aide de traceurs environnementaux dépendent de l'échelle spatio- temporelle des observations et de l'ampleur du changement anthropique. Dans des petits sous-bassins tropicaux humides, le transfert de DIC et de contaminants (pesticides, herbicides, nutriments) ont été étudiés lors d’évènements pluvieux. L'analyse de ces systèmes a également bénéficié de l'utilisation de mesures temporelles à haute résolution (δ18O, δ2H, δ13C, et DIC) qui ont permis de saisir les processus rapides. A ces échelles les systèmes hydrodynamique et hydrochimique évoluent de manière sub-synchrone. À l'échelle d’un grand bassin (MGB ~ 300 000 km^2), l’étude de l'impact de la déforestation met en évidence de fortes différences entre la réponse du système physique, hausse des nappes phréatiques généralisé, est celle du système chimique avec des réponses localisées et retardées. Les traceurs environnementaux ont permis d’établir que la forte salinité des nappes est héritée de l’accumulation des sels cycliques par transpiration sur plusieurs millénaires et ainsi pré-date la déforestation. ## ABSTRACT

Two types of variability can affect the dynamics of surface water and groundwater in space and time: natural occurring (geological and climatic), and anthropogenic induced. Understanding the influence of these variabilities on water systems is essential for the management and protection of water resources in terms of quantity and quality. The study of hydrosystem evolution in response to geological, climatic and anthropogenic variability is a major scientific challenge, and forms the framework of this synthesis: capturing variability in heterogeneous and changing hydrosystems with environmental tracers. [SOURCE: Page_17]

The study of dynamic hydrosystems requires a hydrochemical approach capable of capturing the variability across multiple spatial and temporal scales. Geological and geomorphological changes vary slowly with respect to the water cycle phenomena, therefore we often merely characterize their variability in space. It is not the same for climate dynamics, which in addition to spatial heterogeneities can show a wide diversity of behaviours depending on the time scales considered. These range from the very fast phenomena of floods, to the medium-term of droughts and the very long-term variability due to climate cycles (palaeohydrology). Finally, anthropogenic pressures can disrupt the natural dynamics of the water cycle and cause significant environmental risks. [SOURCE: Page_17]

Through eight case studies in Australia, this synthesis presents the contribution of environmental tracers to complete: 1) physical system monitoring networks, 2) numerical modeling and 3) spatial hydro(geo)logy. This research has involved several types of water systems (porous and fractured aquifers, rivers, and lakes) in a variety of climatic environments (temperate, semi-arid, arid and wet and dry tropical) with issues relating to the quantity and quality of water resources. This research has also focused on several components of the water cycle: recharging and discharging groundwater, groundwater flow paths, and interactions between groundwater and surface water. For each site, an appropriate combination of environmental tracers were selected from the following list: concentrations of major ions, minor and trace elements, stable isotopes of dissolved inorganic carbon (δ13C), stable isotopes of water (δ18O et δ2H), radiocarbon (14C), tritium (3H), sulfur hexafluoride (SF$_6$) and chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs). Over the years, the sampling procedures in the field for dissolved gases have become simpler (e.g. CFCs), permitting the collection of samples in remote areas. In addition, recent technological developments in laser spectrometry now allow in-situ δ13C, δ18O and δ2H at very fine temporal resolutions. These environmental tracers therefore offer the possibility of capturing the variability of hydrosystem processes. [SOURCE: Page_17]

As presented in this synthesis, in hydrosystems comprising heterogeneous geology (fractured rock and alluvial aquifer systems), environmental tracers highlight the groundwater flow paths and mixing process. Major ions reflect dissolution reactions of different minerals and therefore mixing between different groundwater aquifers, δ18O and δ2H values show temporal variations in recharge process, and the values of 14C and 3H can indicate mixing between groundwater of different origins. However, significant uncertainties were found with regard to the apparent ages determined from the CFC, 14C [SOURCE: Page_17]

In terms of climate variability, the studies in this synthesis show the importance of scales. In small sub-catchments (<10 km^2) sub-synchronous development of river flow and chemical composition allowed the use of environmental tracers (δ18O, δ2H and major ions) to deconstruct hydrographs at scales of daily fluctuations (floods) and seasonal changes. In comparison, at larger spatial and temporal scales, the evolution of the chemical system shows responses that are relatively localized, idiosyncratic or delayed relative to the physical system. In the arid inland basin of Lake Eyre, environmental tracers did not highlight the groundwater recharge by episodic floodwaters but reflected a dominant recharge process by diffuse infiltration of rainwater. In a catchment exposed to a severe multi-year drought, a general decrease in lakes and the aquifer water levels was observed, in comparison to the different chemical changes in each lake with respect to salinity and nutrient concentration changes. The chemical system response to drought showed significant inter-lake variations in the processes of storage and transport of contaminants. [SOURCE: Page_18]

Similarly, when analysing the influence of anthropogenic activities on hydrosystem processes using environmental tracers, the results were dependant on the spatio-temporal scale of observations and magnitude of anthropogenic change. In wet tropical sub- catchments, the transfer of DIC and contaminants (pesticides, herbicides and nutrients) were investigated during rainfall events. Analysis of these wet tropical systems benefited from the use of high temporal resolution field measurements (δ18O, δ2H, δ13C, and DIC) to capture the rapid processes. At these scales the physical and chemical systems evolved in a sub-synchronous manner. Whereas, at the scale of a large basin (MGB ~ 300 000 km^2), the study of deforestation impacts highlights major differences between the response of the physical system, generally rising groundwater levels, and the localized and delayed response of the chemical system. Environmental tracers indicated that the high salinity of groundwater in the MDB is inherited from the accumulation of cyclic salts by transpiration over several millennia and thus pre-dates deforestation. [SOURCE: Page_18]

In synthesising these case studies it was evident that whether due to the heterogeneous geology, climate variability or anthropogenic practices, analysis of the influence of variability on water systems requires a multi-scale approach. It is therefore important to consider in each case study the optimum density and sampling frequency that will capture all types variability affecting the system. At large spatial scales (> 10 000 km^2), the differences between the physical and chemical system responses to climatic and anthropogenic forcings are limiting for (1) the use of environmental tracers to constrain the evolution of the physical system and (2) the analysis of changes in water quality. However, this difference in responses can also be advantageous because the chemistry can highlight historical processes that the current physical system may not represent. In changing systems at the basin-scale, environmental tracers have the potential to characterize the natural state and dominant processes that were in place before the impacts from anthropogenic practices and climate variability. [SOURCE: Page_18]

## INTRODUCTION: RESEARCH QUESTION AND APPROACH

The analysis of water cycle dynamics requires a comprehensive understanding of the interactions and evolution of water and solutes between the various water reservoirs. For water resource issues, investigations of these processes must take place at the catchment and basin scales. This is essential information in securing the quality and quantity of water resources required for ecosystem and anthropogenic demands. Large shifts in the water cycle, in response to extreme climate events (droughts and floods) and land use changes, can affect moisture feedback patterns, biodiversity, and food and water supply security (Rockström et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_20]

There remain, however, significant challenges in systematically defining and quantifying hydrosystem processes at the catchment and basin scale, which primarily relates to system variability. A hydrosystem can be variable due to heterogeneous geology, and due to hydro-climatic and anthropogenic forcings. When a natural or human induced variability is introduced to the hydrosystem, the response of hydrological and hydrogeological components also depends on many factors including the scale and magnitude of change, the geographical, geological and land cover factors, and antecedent conditions (e.g. Taylor et al. 2012). These fundamental challenges in hydrosystem investigations have galvanized my research projects, and form the theme of this research synthesis: Can we improve our understanding of the variability in heterogenous and changing hydrosystems with environmental tracers? [SOURCE: Page_20]

Across the globe there are monitoring networks of water resources, mostly focused on physical parameters. However, the spatial and temporal resolution of both surface water and groundwater data often remains insufficient to capture the full variability of these hydrosystems. Using only physical parameters also does not enable the determination of changes in flow pathways, such as the mixing between different systems, or interactions between groundwater and surface water systems. Approaches to compensate for the physical monitoring data (or lack of) have included the incorporation of numerical modelling (e.g. Crosbie et al., 2013), remote sensing (e.g. Leblanc et al., 2009), and environmental tracers (e.g. Baudron et al., 2013). For my part, I have explored the use of environmental tracers to address this question. Naturally occurring ions and isotopes are tracers of the origin, pathways, residence times and chemical evolution of water. In hydrosystem investigations, the use of both environmental isotopes (e.g. Mook, 2000; Gibson et al., 2005; Négrel, Petelet-Giraud, 2011; Schulte et al., 2011) and ions (e.g. Herczeg and Edmunds, 2000; Herczeg and Leaney, 2011) are both well-established approaches. [SOURCE: Page_20]

As described in many studies, capturing the spatial and temporal variability of hydrosystem processes is particularly challenging when delineating groundwater recharge [SOURCE: Page_20]

processes (e.g. Simmers, 1997; Scanlon et al. 2006; Wolski and Savenije, 2006; Leblanc et al., 2009; Favreau et al., 2009). These variations can result from (1) preferential flow pathways, for example due to the presence of high hydraulic conductivity zones in the shallow lithology (Tweed et al., 2007); (2) land clearance, which can alter groundwater recharge pathways from diffuse to discrete processes (e.g. Leblanc et al. 2008); (3) drought can significantly alter the rates of groundwater recharge (e.g. Leblanc et al., 2009), and discharge (e.g. Tweed et al., 2009); and (4) evapotranspiration processes in arid regions can significantly affect the volumes and quality of recharging waters (Seiler and Gat, 2007). Increasingly, environmental tracers have been employed in recharge studies to capture the spatio-temporal variability, such as the use of chloride (e.g. Edmunds and Gaye, 1994; Subyani and Şen, 2006; Gates et al., 2008), and isotopes (e.g. Favreau et al., 2002; Harrington et al., 2002; Zongyu et al., 2005; Cartwright et al., 2006, 2007; Hutchison and Hibbs, 2008; Cartwright and Morgenstern, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_21]

Similarly, environmental tracers have been used to determine groundwater and surface water interactions, since in comparison with other methods (e.g. hydraulics and seepage meters), they can integrate processes over a range of spatial and temporal scales (e.g. Kalbus et al., 2006; Cook, 2012). Hydrochemical tracers able to identify groundwater discharge to rivers include radon-222 (Ellins et al, 1990), chlorofluorocarbons (Cook et al., 2003) and helium-4 (Gardner et al, 2011). The stable isotopes of water (δ18O and δ2H) have been used to distinguish event versus pre-event waters contributing to river flow (e.g. Ladouche et al. 2001; McGlynn and McDonnell, 2003; Schulte et al., 2011), and at the regional scale δ18O and δ2H have also been used to identify inflows to rivers from different provinces (e.g. Winston and Criss, 2003). DIC concentrations and δ13C values have also been used as tracers of storm hydrograph separation for rivers (e.g. Lee and Krothe, 2001), and flow pathways of waters discharging at springs (e.g. Emblanch et al., 2003). [SOURCE: Page_21]
