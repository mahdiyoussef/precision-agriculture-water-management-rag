Abstract Land use is an important driver for irrigation but rarely included in models of renewable resource economics. We set up a dynamic two- period model in which farmers decide on both land-use and water-use, while extracting from a common groundwater resource. We compute the Nash feedback equilibrium and the common property optimal so- lution, for symmetric and heterogeneous players. We first study the case where only water is a decision variable and then the case where water and land-use are decision variables, characterizing the intensive and extensive margins. We show that under Nash, in contrast to the optimal policy, more water is extracted than optimal, but more im- portantly, greater surfaces are irrigated and more water is extracted in the first period than in the second, showing the effect of strategic competition. We show in an example how regulating land-use may be a Pareto-improving policy and that not regulating it increases the difference between optimal and Nash gains, questionning the Gisser- Sanchez effect. 1    Introduction In the context of climate change, it may be necessary to reconsider land- use and water-use. With increasing temperature and lower precipitations, farmers may tend to extend irrigation to land that was not irrigated before or allocate more water to irrigated crops. The first is usually referred to as the extensive margin, the second as the intensive margin (Graveline and Mérel, 2014). Moreover, competition around common pool water resources will increase with decreasing overall water availability. We analyze the case where several farmers share a common groundwater resource and are hence in competition for water. We set up a dynamic model in which farmers decide how much land to irrigate and how much irrigation water to use, withdrawing from the common water resource. In this context, we investigate how the intensive and extensive margin depend on the strategic interactions among players. In much of the theoretical literature on renewable resource economics, only the decision about resource extraction is modeled. Particular attention has been paid to issues of strategic interaction (Gisser and Sánchez, 1980, Provencher and Burt, 1993, Rubio and Casino, 2003) and the use of institutions and in- struments for regulation (Gardner et al., 1997, Jansen et al., 2009, Janssen et al., 2011). Moreover, issues such as catastrophic risks (de Frutos Cachorro et al., 2014, De Frutos Cachorro et al., 2019, Tsur and Zemel, 2014) and water quality affecting ecosystem services (Pereau et al., 2018, 2019, Roseta-Palma, 2002) have been discussed in these frameworks. On the other hand, agricultural land-use decisions combined with water-use decisions have mainly been represented in numerical models, either in posi- tive mathematical programming approaches or with dynamic programming and simulation (Graveline and Mérel, 2014, Reynaud, 2009). This allows the description of heterogeneous and spatially distributed farm types. However, most of these models do not explicitly model the resource dynamics and fail to take strategic interactions into account. The following approaches in par- ticular address similar overall objectives as our model but only with numeric results: Madani and Dinar (2012) analyze water extraction decisions for dif- ferent farm-types in a dynamic resource model under different assumptions on players’ strategic behavior. Graveline and Mérel (2014) analyze land-use and irrigation choices in the Beauce area in France, using a positive mathematical programming approach. Such approach is based on a representative decision maker. Although decisions depend on the resource stock, the model itself is not dynamic. Reynaud (2009) models land-use decisions when agricultural yields depend on dynamic intra-seasonal irrigation decisions, but does not take any water-resource dynamics into account. Moreover, there is a single [SOURCE: Page_4]

decision maker. Robert et al. (2018a,b) model irrigation and land-use deci- sions in a dynamic and stochastic groundwater-resource management model. In their model, irrigation is a long-term investment decision while land-use is annual and depends on the prior investment levels. There is no strategic interaction. De Frutos Cachorro et al. (2017) model irrigation and land- use choices with a dynamic groundwater resource model and present some analytical results but do not take into account strategic interactions. Our general question is to analyze how water and land-use choices unfold in a dynamic system where several, potentially heterogeneous, resource-users are in competition for a common groundwater resource. We aim to obtain some analytical relationships between water and land-use decisions, although these relations quickly become complex. This is why we adopt two main modeling assumptions: first, we set up a two-period decision model. In this framework, we cannot address the question of long-term resource management, but we can describe the dynamic effects that take place, even though they are not strong in a two-period model. Second, we split our analysis into two parts: we first analyze the case where only water-use is a decision variable while land-use is fixed and then the case when both water and land-use are de- cision variables. This allows us to show analytically how land-use decisions and water-use decisions interact, and how these interactions change under strategic behavior. Throughout the paper, we show theoretical results when- ever they are clear enough to be interpreted, we give numerical examples for the more complex cases. The model itself is central to this paper, while the policy applications are illustrations. The scope for policy applications is im- portant though: when irrigated land-use can be adjusted, policy intervention becomes both more necessary and easier to implement. Indeed, the Gisser- Sanchez effect stipulates that the difference in gains and water extraction between the competitive outcome and the optimal outcome is small (Gisser and Sánchez, 1980, Koundouri, 2004). We show that this difference becomes larger when land-use becomes a decision variable. Moreover, the integration of land-use next to water-use raises many more possibilities for policy in- terventions. Indeed, some classical instruments on water resources, such as quotas (De Frutos Cachorro et al., 2017, Gardner et al., 1997, Graveline and Mérel, 2014), or security differentiated water rights (Lefebvre et al., 2012, Salanié and Zaporozhets, 2021) could be integrated in our framework. In ad- dition, such instruments could be made depended on land-use which would make them more easily implementable, as land-use is more readily observable than water-use. Finally, our model could feed into more complex decision models (Robert et al., 2016a,b) of reservoir management (Evers et al., 1998) or input management (Humblot et al., 2017). 5    Conclusion We have set up a model that allows to highlight the importance of land-use on water-use decisions. In particular, we can put forward some theoretical results which allow to understand how water use may increase with land-use, depending on the strategic (or non-strategic) interaction among players. In- deed, we have the following main theoretical results, when land-use is fixed: First, water-use increases with land-use from the same period and decreases with land-use from the other period, both wether surfaces are increased by one player or the other. This holds in Nash and in the common property optimum, both for symmetric and heterogeneous players. Second, when the first period surface is big, compared to a threshold, water-use in the first period is greater than in the second period. This threshold is smaller under Nash than under the common property optimum. Hence, there are cases where extraction in the first period is greater than in the second period only under Nash, not in the common property optimum. When the first period surface is small, the contrary holds, water-use in the first period is smaller than in the second period. We proved this for the completely symmetric case. Third, as expected, total extractions under Nash are greater than under the common property optimum, which we proved for the completely symmetric case. Summarizing these results, the size of the land-use is important to determine how much water is used and in which period the players extract more water. These results could be even more important in a dynamic model [SOURCE: Page_25]

23 [SOURCE: Page_25]

with more time-periods, exacerbating the competition effect. When land-use is optimized we can show numerically the following: First, surface and water choices in the first period are greater under Nash than in the common property optimum, and overall extractions are greater leading to smaller resource stocks. These results can be shown for symmetric and heterogeneous players. Second, with heterogeneous players, the more effi- cient player chooses bigger surfaces and greater water-volumes and obtains more gains than the less efficient player, but the previous principles hold. For the efficient player, individual gains under Nash can be greater than in the common property optimum, while for the less efficient player, they can be smaller. Third, we can design Pareto-improving policies that play only on land-use but obtain more reasonable resource use from Nash players. Finally, we show that fixing land-use exacerbates the Gisser-Sanchez effect. On the contrary, optimizing over land-use attenuates the Gisser-Sanchez effect and hence calls for some form of policy to be implemented. Summarizing, these examples show that, when land-use and water-use are optimized, under Nash, more water is extracted than in the common property optimum, especially in the first period. Moreover, optimizing on land-use on top of water-use leads to smaller gains under Nash than when surfaces were fixed, underlining that strategic interaction exacerbates individual choices on land-use. As a consequence,taking into account the fact that players may optimize over land-use, exacerbates the effects of strategic competition. [SOURCE: Page_26]

Overall, our results highlight the importance of taking into account land-use decisions, next to water-use decisions. We namely show that letting resource users decide on land-use increases the difference between optimal and Nash gains, hence inducing the need for more regulation. We also argue that there is scope for using instruments or policies that play on land-use but do in reality influence water-use decisions and hence may improve water-resource management.
