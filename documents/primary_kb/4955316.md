### 1. Value of the Data

• This dataset provides the ﬁrst global dataset of known coastal springs including nearshore and offshore springs alongside their characteristics and context [1]. • The dataset can be useful for researchers from various disciplines interested in coastal areas dynamics (e.g., hydrogeologists, oceanographers, marine biologists …), water managers, and landscape mappers among others • Speciﬁcally, the dataset can be useful to hydrogeologists in their research on understanding the functioning of coastal springs in broad climatic, hydrologic and hydrogeologic contexts. • By studying the context of these coastal springs, researchers and water managers can identify and target coastal springs potential zones in their areas. • The dataset can serve for calibration and validation of geospatial methods used for springs locations identiﬁcation in diverse settings. • This dataset is the foundation of an open call for the scientiﬁc communities to contribute to this inventory by suggesting updates for accurate information, or new information on coastal springs from their own research and knowledge [SOURCE: Page_3]

### 2. Background

Oceans and seas receive signiﬁcant freshwater inputs from groundwater discharging along continental margins through coastal springs [2]. Coastal springs include nearshore springs dis- charging close to the shoreline and offshore submarine springs discharging directly to the sea ﬂoor known as submarine springs [3]. Increased coastal development and water demand, along [SOURCE: Page_3]

H. Bouimouass, A. Fourno and Y. Hamon et al. / Data in Brief 59 (2025) [PHONE_REDACTED] [SOURCE: Page_4]

with contamination from human activities, pose serious risks to the sustainability of nearshore springs and their ecosystems [4–6]. Despite their importance [7], coastal springs are poorly un- derstood, and the factors controlling their presence and ﬂow are not well constrained [4]. One key step of advancing scientiﬁc knowledge on coastal springs is to know their distribution and the context where they are mostly found [8]. Therefore, a global inventory of coastal springs is a key requirement for improving the understanding of this hydrogeological phenomenon. While efforts are being deployed in inland springs inventory [9], less attention have been made for coastal springs because of many challenges limiting their identiﬁcation [10]. To our knowledge, there is no global dataset or record for known coastal springs. To address this, a global dataset of known nearshore springs has been created, detailing their locations and characteristics, and describing their context including geology, hydrology, and land use [SOURCE: Page_4]

### 3. Data Description

The dataset folder contains three Excel ﬁles accompanied with two ESRI shapeﬁles. The ﬁrst Excel ﬁle named “CSD” Coastal Springs Dataset” contain 1124 lines including headings and 13 columns relative to the dataset of inventoried coastal springs. The columns represent the fol- lowing items: [SOURCE: Page_4]

- Spring_ID - Country - Spring name (for springs known for their names) - Citation (references and links to the studies in the Excel ﬁle “References”) - Longitude (degrees) - Latitude (degrees) - Altitude (position of the spring regarding mean sea-level in m) - Type (OS indicate offshore springs and NS indicate nearshore springs) - Lithology (represents lithology at the spring outlet) - Discharge pattern (perennial, intermittent, permanently dry) - Discharge (indication of discharge rate of the spring reported in the study and corresponding unit) - Salinity (indication of salinity of the spring water reported in the study and corresponding unit) - Watershed_ID (watershed where the spring is located, corresponds to the ﬁrst column of the Excel ﬁle CWD) [SOURCE: Page_4]

The second Excel ﬁle named “CWD” (Coastal Watersheds Dataset) contain 432 lines and 20 columns, including headings, representing the dataset of multiple environmental parameters de- scribing the context of the coastal watersheds around the inventoried springs. The columns rep- resent the following items: [SOURCE: Page_4]

- Watershed_ID - X centroid (Longitude of the centroid of the watershed polygon in degrees) - Y centroid (Latitude of the centroid of the watershed polygon in degrees) - Watershed area (km^2 ) - Lithology_Class (the dominant class out of 1: unconsolidated sediments, 2: basic volcanic rocks, 3: siliciclastic sedimentary rocks, 4: basic plutonic rocks, 5: mixed sedimentary rocks, 6: carbonate sedimentary rocks, 7: acid volcanic rocks, 8: metamorphic rocks, 9: acid plutonic rocks, 10: intermediate volcanic rocks, 11: water Bodies, 12: pyroclastics, and 13: intermedi- ate plutonic rocks, 14: evaporites, 15: no data, and 16: ice and glaciers) - Karst area (km^2 , within the watershed) - Number of faults (per watershed) - Hydrogeological units (MGB refers to major groundwater basin, CHS to complex hydrogeo- logical systems, and LSA to local and shallow aquifers) [SOURCE: Page_4]

A total of 1123 springs were inventoried in the present study (Fig. 1). Offshore springs (n=645) represent 57 % of the inventoried springs in this study, compared to 43 % as nearshore springs (n=478). 657 of springs were found in Europe (58 %) and the least were found in Africa (n=23) and South America (n=3). 66 % of the inventoried springs are found in the Mediter- ranean region representing the most important study hotspots of coastal springs in the globe. 945 springs were found in carbonate rock aquifers (84 %), 142 springs in volcanic rock aquifers (13 %), 35 springs in other consolidated rock aquifers (3 %), and only one spring was reported to be in unconsolidated rock aquifers. The discharge pattern of 974 springs (92 %) is unknown or not reported in the literature. Only 70 coastal springs were reported as permanent, 38 as intermittent and 41 as permanently dry. Discharge estimations or measurement were only reported for 154 springs (91 offshore springs and 63 nearshore springs) corresponding to 14 % of the total inventoried springs. Salinity values were reported for 105 coastal springs (56 offshore spring and 49 nearshore spring), correspond- ing to 9 % of the total inventoried springs. [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 1. Coastal springs inventory

Coastal spring’s locations and characteristics were inventoried using systematic bibliographic research in SCOPUS database using keywords in title, abstract, and keywords of the document following the PRISMA guidelines [12]. SCOPUS is widely used because it has a large database of peer-reviewed articles and covers a wide range of disciplines. The research period was limited to December 2023. Several keywords are selected considering keywords of the most cited pa- per in the ﬁeld on submarine discharge ([2,3,7]). These keywords include “submarine spring”, “coastal spring”, “intertidal spring”, “subtidal spring”, “offshore spring”, and “nearshore spring”. Inclusion criteria were journal articles, book chapters, conference papers, technical reports, and notes. Exclusion criteria are editorials, letters, and erratum. Details of the research methodology and inclusion criteria are presented in Fig. 2. The combination of these keywords has led to 379 studies that are cited by 217 other documents. A manual cleaning step is performed to identify off topic documents. As examples, the keyword spring is validated if it is related to groundwater and not a seasonal context, the keyword submarine is not considered if it is related to subma- rine engines, tidal is retained if it is associated to the keyword spring. In addition, documents without spring location information are excluded. Indeed, 384 documents were excluded after the screening. The retained list of documents yielded further sources through citation tracking. The 212 selected documents were organized by country then by region for further screening to check for documents dealing with the same springs. Following, 93 reports were excluded and 119 were retained (Fig. 2). Details of the retained studies and citations are presented in supple- mentary material. Springs coordinates, when present, were extracted from text and tables. Maps with coordi- nates were extracted as images and georeferenced in QGIS. In case of maps without coordinates, an estimated location of the spring is determined using the map scale and a reference point (e.g., a major city, shoreline shape…). All coordinates were converted to a unique reference sys- tem (WGS84) as they were often initially given in different systems according to the regional reference coordinate system. The locations of springs were visually and individually checked on Google Earth. Many of the geographical coordinates given in the documents were unprecise and needed to be corrected. Some of the nearshore springs were visible on Google Earth, some springs are tagged in their exact location, and other were located based on surface features such as vegetation that devel- ops around springs. Best estimation of location of offshore springs was based on information [SOURCE: Page_6]

### 2. Coastal spring’s contextualisation

In the order to locate the springs in their context, a set of environmental parameters po- tentially inﬂuencing coastal springs and deemed relevant for future users of this dataset were compiled and associated to the dataset of coastal springs. Lithology, karst extent, and number of faults were chosen to describe the geologic context. Hydrogeological units, recharge, and depth to water table describe the hydrogeological context. Precipitation, snow cover, and surface runoff represent the climatic and hydrologic conditions. Elevation and slope represent the topography of the coastal area. Anthropogenic inﬂuences were represented by the extent of equipped irri- gated area, cropland area, and population density. Finally, the inﬂuence of coastal ocean dynam- ics was represented by sea-level extremes due to tides and storm surges. Table 1 summarizes these parameters and their data sources. Because springs outlets are point-scale and depend on environmental settings of a larger scale, we used a spatial delineation based on coastal watersheds to extract the average values for the chosen parameters. To do so, 1st order coastal watersheds from the HydroSHED dataset [20] were used. The coastal watersheds located around the inventoried springs were selected and provided alongside their environmental parameters. Information of few parameters were directly extracted from HydroATLAS [17] including precipitation, snow cover, surface runoff, depth to water-table, elevation, slope, irrigated area, cropland area, and population density. HydroATLAS is a database providing multiple hydro-environmental information aggregated at a high spatial resolution for all watersheds of the HydroSHED delineation. Other watershed parameters were acquired by the postprocessing of different global geospatial datasets in QGIS; [SOURCE: Page_7]

• Karst extent in coastal watersheds was calculated from the Global Karst Map (WOKAM, [14]). • The number of faults per watershed was calculated after merging the AFEAD [13] and GEM [16] datasets. • The type of hydrogeological units and ranges of groundwater recharge were determined from the WHYMAP dataset [17]. • Sea-levels extremes caused by tides and storm surges was determined from GTSR dataset [19]. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Note that the HydroSHED dataset do not cover some paciﬁc islands such as Hawaii and Mau- ritius. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Limitations [SOURCE: Page_8]

The methodology used in the present study may have some limitations. Even though these limitations do not signiﬁcantly affect the quality of the dataset, they have to be mentionned: [SOURCE: Page_8]

• Some studies contain existing coastal springs may be missed during the systematic and ci- tation searching despite the efforts deployed. However, researchers are invited to update the dataset if they have additional data. • The locations of some springs may still be uncertain. However, an uncertainty of few to hun- dreds of meters may be acceptable given the extremely small size of the springs hence the main challenge of their inventory. • The information regarding the provided environmental parameters used to contextualize the springs are averaged values over the coastal watersheds and also depend on the previous studies uncertainties. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The area contributing to the hydrological functioning of the springs, especially karst springs, may be larger than the coastal watershed or a part of it, requiring further investigation at local scale for selected springs. [SOURCE: Page_8]
