28          Hydropower production is altering the Mekong River basin’s riverine ecosystems, which [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   contain the world’s largest inland fishery and provide food security and livelihoods to millions of [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   people. The basin’s hydropower reservoir storage, which may rise from ~2% of its mean annual [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   flow in 2008 to ~20% in 2025, is attenuating seasonal flow variability downstream of many [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   dams with integral powerhouses and large storage reservoirs. In addition, some tributary [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   diversions for off-stream energy production are reducing downstream flows and are augmenting [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   them in recipient tributaries. [SOURCE: Page_2]

35          To help manage tradeoffs between dam benefits (hydropower, irrigation, flood control, [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   domestic water supply, and navigation) and their consequences for livelihoods and ecosystems, [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   we review observed and projected impacts on river flows along both the Mekong mainstream [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   and its tributaries. We include the effects of diversions and inter-basin transfers, which prior [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   reviews of flow alteration in the Mekong basin have largely neglected. We also discuss the [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   extent to which concurrent changes in climate, water demand, and land use, may offset or [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   exacerbate hydropower-induced flow alteration. Our major recommendations for assessing [SOURCE: Page_2]

52   reduce fossil-fuel dependence have spurred the rapid expansion of hydropower (Zarfl et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   developing countries in the next few decades (Zarfl et al., 2015; IEA, 2016), is threatening [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   ecosystems in basins with some of the greatest aquatic biodiversity, including the Amazon, [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   Congo and Mekong (Winemiller et al., 2016). In the Mekong basin, which has the world’s most [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   productive inland fishery (Baran and Myschowoda, 2009; Ziv et al., 2012), seasonally variable [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   fisheries, floodplain recession agriculture, sediment and nutrient transport; safety hazards posed [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   al., 2018). For instance, estimates of hydropower benefits range from $6-32B whereas its [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   potential consequences to capture fisheries range from $2-13B (MRC, 2011; Intralawan et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   2018). Improving these multi-sectoral and transboundary tradeoffs requires accurate [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   characterizations of observed and projected hydrological changes. [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   scrutinized due to its dense population and dependence on fisheries and flood recession [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   agriculture, especially rice (e.g. Intralawan et al., 2018; Dang et al., 2018). Recent studies have [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   synthesized observed and projected hydrological alterations in the basin and their socioeconomic [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   and ecological implications (Pokhrel et al., 2018). Lu et al. (2008) and Li et al. (2017) have [SOURCE: Page_3]

95   2.1    Basin overview [SOURCE: Page_5]

96          The Mekong is one of the world’s most prominent rivers. Its mean annual discharge of [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   14,500 m^3/s (Wang et al., 2017) and length of 4,909 km (Liu et al., 2009) both rank tenth [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   globally, while its drainage area (795,000 km^2) is the 25th largest (MRC, 2005). Its population [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   was approximately 70-75 million in 2005 (Ringler and Cai, 2006; Varis et al., 2012) and could [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   increase to 100-145 million by 2050 (Pech and Sunada, 2008; Varis et al., 2012). The Lower [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   Mekong basin (LMB) (Figure 1) lies in the Southeast Asian countries of Lao PDR (25% of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   basin area), Thailand (23%), Cambodia (20%), Vietnam (8%), and Myanmar (3%). Meanwhile, [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   the upstream portion of the basin (21%) in China is often known as the Upper Mekong Basin [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   (UMB) or Lancang Basin (MRC, 2005). [SOURCE: Page_5]

108          The LMB has a monsoonal climate with a wet season often considered to run from May [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   to October and a dry season lasting from November to April. The flood season analyzed in most [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   studies runs from June to November due to runoff lags in the populous, flood-prone Lower [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   between July and October, a period with extensive flooding that sustains many ecosystems and [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   livelihoods (e.g. Kummu and Sarkkula, 2008, Piman et al., 2013a). Meanwhile, 35% of its dry- [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   MRC (2005), Adamson (2009), and MRC (2018) for more detailed basin descriptions. [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   2.2    Hydropower dam development [SOURCE: Page_7]

118          While hydropower ambitions have existed for over half a century (Jacobs, 2002), most [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   hydropower dams have been constructed in the last decade (Figure 2). In 2008, the Mekong was [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   one of the least regulated large river basins in the world, as its total active reservoir storage [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   capacity (8.6 km^3) amounted to just 2% of its annual discharge (Kummu et al., 2010). However, [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   recent estimates (MRC, 2015); Mekong-WLE, 2017) show that its active reservoir storage [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   (including flood storage) in 2025 will equal approximately 19% of its mean annual flow. This [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   estimate falls within the 17-23% range of earlier projections (Hoanh et al., 2010; Kummu et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   2010, MRC, 2011). According to the Greater Mekong Dam Database (GMDD) (https://wle- [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   mekong.cgiar.org/maps/), 64 of the 187 existing and proposed hydropower dams with an [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   installed capacity of at least 15 MW had been commissioned by June 2017. Out of these 64 [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   dams, 18 dams in China can generate up to 17,770 MW, over 91% of which is installed at the six [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   large Lancang Cascade dams on the UMB mainstream. The other 46 LMB tributary dams can [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   produce up to 8,650 MW. The combined installed capacity of all existing and proposed dams (> [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   15 MW) in the GMDD database exceeds 65,000 MW, while other estimates range from nearly [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   59,000 MW (Dore et al., 2007) to 88,000 MW (Stone, 2011). These estimates include a [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   controversial cascade of 11 proposed LMB mainstream dams with a total installed capacity of [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   13,000 MW (GMDD, 2017). Greater seasonal flow regulation from upstream hydropower [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   reservoirs increases the feasibility of hydropower at downstream locations. Moreover, if [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   increased annual and dry-season flows projected under many climate change scenarios (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   Hoang et al., 2016) exceed climate-driven increases in reservoir evaporation, both total and firm [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   energy production could increase. Such potential basin-wide changes in hydropower generation [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   have not been modeled. [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   2.3    Ecological implications of hydrological alteration [SOURCE: Page_9]

146          Many ecological impacts of dam-induced flow alteration have been debated ( Ringler et [SOURCE: Page_9]

147   al., 2004; Stone, 2010a; Intralawan et al., 2018), as LMB residents depend on fish and other [SOURCE: Page_9]

148   aquatic animals for 47-80% of their protein, more than any major basin in the world (Hortle, [SOURCE: Page_9]

149   2007). Cambodia also has the world’s highest freshwater fish catch rate per capita (McIntyre et [SOURCE: Page_9]

150   al., 2016). Spatiotemporal variations in river and floodplain hydrology influence LMB fisheries. [SOURCE: Page_9]

151   Several components of the flood pulse have been hypothesized to affect fish catches, including [SOURCE: Page_9]

152   flood magnitude, duration, timing, and variability (Sabo et al., 2017; Welcomme, 1979). Overall, [SOURCE: Page_9]

153   Halls et al. (2013) observed that larger annual floods tend to yield greater fish catches. Yet, this [SOURCE: Page_9]

154   correlation is likely due to numerous hydrologically-driven processes, including fish population [SOURCE: Page_9]

155   density and reproduction (Halls and Welcomme, 2004), as well as floodplain primary production [SOURCE: Page_9]

156   and habitat (Chea et al., 2016; Koponen et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_9]

157          Numerous studies have investigated dam impacts to fisheries and other riverine species [SOURCE: Page_9]

158   (e.g. Baran and Myschowoda, 2009; Kang et al., 2009; Dugan et al., 2010; Pukinskis and Geheb, [SOURCE: Page_9]

159   2012; Zhang et al., 2018). As in other parts of the world (Poff et al., 2007), Mekong dams have [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   substantially reduced fish production and diversity stemming from losses in river network [SOURCE: Page_9]

161   connectivity (Ziv et al., 2012; Ou and Winemiller, 2016) and changes in downstream flow [SOURCE: Page_9]

162   (Baran et al., 2011; Li et al., 2013). Many migratory species travel hundreds of kilometers along [SOURCE: Page_9]

163   free-flowing reaches to spawn (e.g. Hogan et al., 2007), some of which are becoming [SOURCE: Page_9]

164   endangered as the percentage of free-flowing reaches in the basin decreases. Between 1990 and [SOURCE: Page_9]

165   2010, river connectivity declined from 93% to 77% and could drop as low as 10% (Grill et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

166   2014). Even run-of-river dams have substantially disrupted fisheries in the Mekong basin, such [SOURCE: Page_9]

167   as the Pak Mun Dam on the Mun River in Thailand (e.g. Molle et al., 2010). Indeed, further [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

168   research is needed to untangle the effects of passage barriers and flow alteration on Mekong [SOURCE: Page_10]

169   fisheries and ecosystems. However, this study focuses on flow alteration. [SOURCE: Page_10]

170          While many ecological studies focus on flow-alteration impacts on specific fisheries (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_10]

171   Hogan et al., 2007; Sabo et al., 2017), some studies have assessed a wider range of riverine [SOURCE: Page_10]

172   ecological communities in the UMB (Kang et al., 2009; Li et al., 2013; Fan et al., 2015; Zhang [SOURCE: Page_10]

173   et al., 2018), and LMB tributaries (Ou and Winemiller, 2016). Yet, while pronounced flow [SOURCE: Page_10]

174   alteration has been associated with severe ecological impacts (e.g. Baran et al., 2011), the [SOURCE: Page_10]

175   optimality of natural flow for some species, i.e. the natural flow paradigm (Poff et al., 1997), has [SOURCE: Page_10]

176   also been questioned. Most notably, Sabo et al. (2017) proposed that, if hydropower dams were [SOURCE: Page_10]

177   collectively operated to generate year-round dry-season flows with punctuated flood pulses, [SOURCE: Page_10]

178   LMB fisheries could maintain or even increase historical yields. Li and Zhao (2016) recommend [SOURCE: Page_10]

179   operating rules with artificial flood releases. Hydropower-induced changes in indicators of [SOURCE: Page_10]

180   ecosystem productivity have also been investigated, as Arias et al. (2014a) suggested that the [SOURCE: Page_10]

181   annual average net primary productivity in open water and floodplain areas of Tonle Sap Lake [SOURCE: Page_10]

182   could decrease by approximately 34%. Overall, a more detailed review of ecological impacts [SOURCE: Page_10]

183   from dam development and other concurrent drivers of changes, such as Castello and Macedo’s [SOURCE: Page_10]

184   (2015) review on the Amazon basin, is warranted. [SOURCE: Page_10]

185   2.4    Toward river basin management [SOURCE: Page_10]

186          The Mekong River Commission (MRC), comprised of Thailand, Lao PDR, Cambodia [SOURCE: Page_10]

187   and Vietnam, has funded and facilitated many research projects that inform transboundary basin [SOURCE: Page_10]

188   management. China and Myanmar also participate in many MRC activities as dialogue partners. [SOURCE: Page_10]

189   Yet, relatively few transboundary streamflow regulations have been implemented. The Mekong [SOURCE: Page_10]

190   Agreement (1995) the MRC countries signed articulates guidelines to limit excessive dry-season [SOURCE: Page_10]

10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

191   withdrawals, but these rules have never been specified (TFDD, 2018). Binational treaties also [SOURCE: Page_11]

192   exist, such as the Vietnam-Cambodia agreement establishing minimum Sesan River flows (Ngo [SOURCE: Page_11]

193   et al., 2017). While a few international agreements constrain dam operation, LMB dam [SOURCE: Page_11]

194   operators, often belonging to different companies, tend to make decisions without concern for [SOURCE: Page_11]

195   downstream dams and overall basin impact (Jeuland et al., 2014; Piman et al., 2016). In contrast, [SOURCE: Page_11]

196   HydroLancang owns all major UMB dams except Dachaoshan (Yu and Geheb, 2017). [SOURCE: Page_11]

197          Widespread concerns about ecosystem and livelihood impacts of seasonal flow regulation [SOURCE: Page_11]

198   have not been considered in major water resources agreements (TFDD, 2018). While extreme [SOURCE: Page_11]

199   floods threaten human safety, infrastructure, and crops, the typical wet-season flood pulse [SOURCE: Page_11]

200   benefits fisheries and floodplain agriculture tremendously. The average annual economic [SOURCE: Page_11]

201   benefits of floods in LMB countries (US $8-10 billion) are more than hundred times greater than [SOURCE: Page_11]

202   their average annual damages (US $60-70 million) (MRC, 2010). In contrast, wet-season [SOURCE: Page_11]

203   droughts, often not considered a hazard in tropical monsoon regions (Adamson and Bird, 2010), [SOURCE: Page_11]

204   can impact agricultural and fisheries production substantially. Hydropower dams may improve [SOURCE: Page_11]

205   dry-season navigation downstream of dams, but they may also impede passage further upstream [SOURCE: Page_11]

206   and irregular releases can be hazardous for smaller boats. These navigation tradeoffs have not [SOURCE: Page_11]

207   been systematically examined. [SOURCE: Page_11]

208          While hydropower development is not centrally coordinated, numerous basin-wide dam [SOURCE: Page_11]

209   development scenarios have been devised (Kummu et al., 2010; MRC, 2011; Lauri et al., 2012; [SOURCE: Page_11]

210   MRC, 2018). Some scenarios, such as the prominent ones in MRC (2011), consider ongoing [SOURCE: Page_11]

211   changes in other sectors, such as water demand (agricultural, industrial and domestic). This [SOURCE: Page_11]

212   allows for direct comparisons between different impacts. Recently, the MRC’s Council Study [SOURCE: Page_11]

213   (MRC, 2018) created a preliminary new scenario set. However, since this study was still under [SOURCE: Page_11]

215   dam development scenarios have not considered possible increases in hydropower potential from [SOURCE: Page_12]

552   4.5    Gaps in studies of projected impacts [SOURCE: Page_29]

553          Hydrological impacts are frequently simulated within different areas of the Mekong [SOURCE: Page_29]

554   basin, but basin-wide projections, including ones considering other ongoing water resources [SOURCE: Page_29]

555   developments, are less frequent, ostensibly due to the large modeling effort required. In contrast, [SOURCE: Page_29]

556   many tributary projections do not employ sets of scenarios that isolate effects of different drivers [SOURCE: Page_29]

557   of change. More model comparison studies are also needed. As our review of 3S basin [SOURCE: Page_29]

558   projections demonstrates, differences in reservoir operating rules, input data, study area [SOURCE: Page_29]

559   definitions, and other assumptions can inhibit these comparisons. [SOURCE: Page_29]

560          Currently, many modeling studies focus on dam impacts without considering alternative [SOURCE: Page_29]

561   operating rules, or design choices, including re-regulation dams. Moreover, many assumptions, [SOURCE: Page_29]

562   such as hydropower maximization, are made when operating rules are unavailable. More efforts [SOURCE: Page_29]

563   to optimize the design and operation of individual dams, cascades (see Bogardi and Duckstein, [SOURCE: Page_29]

564   1992; Schmitt et al., 2018), or dam implementation sequences in basins (Ziv et al., 2012; Grill et [SOURCE: Page_29]

565   al., 2014; Jager et al., 2015) could reveal mitigation opportunities. Flood hazards due to dam [SOURCE: Page_29]

566   failure, emergency spills, multi-reservoir operation, reservoir-induced backwater effects (Rubin [SOURCE: Page_29]

567   et al., 2015), and geomorphic changes merit further research. Finally, while scrutinizing [SOURCE: Page_29]

568   contemporary hydrological modeling practices in Mekong basin studies lies beyond this review [SOURCE: Page_29]

569   (see Kite (2001) and Johnston and Kummu (2012) for reviews of earlier modeling studies), one [SOURCE: Page_29]

570   must recognize that short observational records, along with sparse meteorological station [SOURCE: Page_29]

571   networks (e.g. Lacombe et al., 2014b; Räsänen et al., 2014; Lyon et al., 2017), constrain the [SOURCE: Page_29]

572   calibration and validation of models used in projections. [SOURCE: Page_29]

573 574   5.     Effects of concurrent environmental changes [SOURCE: Page_30]

575          Many ongoing changes in climate, water use, and land cover are occurring as new dams [SOURCE: Page_30]

576   are being constructed. In this section, we build on the recent review by Pokhrel et al. (2018) by [SOURCE: Page_30]

577   evaluating the extent to which these concurrent drivers of change could exacerbate or offset [SOURCE: Page_30]

578   hydrological changes stemming from hydropower production. [SOURCE: Page_30]

579   5.1    Climate change [SOURCE: Page_30]

580          Numerous studies (e.g. Kingston et al., 2011; Cook et al., 2012; Lauri et al., 2012; [SOURCE: Page_30]

581   Thompson et al., 2013; Fan and He, 2015; Hoang et al. 2016; Shrestha et al., 2016; Wang et al., [SOURCE: Page_30]

582   2017) have evaluated possible hydrological effects of future changes in precipitation and [SOURCE: Page_30]

583   temperature. Hydrological models run with recent Climate Model Intercomparison Project 5 [SOURCE: Page_30]

584   (CMIP$_5$) projections have suggested that streamflow will increase during both the wet (8 out of [SOURCE: Page_30]

585   10 scenarios) and dry seasons (all 10 scenarios) at the basin scale, with the uncertainty stemming [SOURCE: Page_30]

586   primarily from precipitation projections (Hoang et al., 2016). In the LMB, these increases in [SOURCE: Page_30]

587   precipitation are consistent with observations from the last half century (Lacombe et al., 2013). [SOURCE: Page_30]

588   However, these projected changes are not ubiquitous, as annual flows may decrease in some [SOURCE: Page_30]

589   tributary basins (e.g. Shrestha et al., 2016; Ngo et al., 2017). Global climate models (GCMs) also [SOURCE: Page_30]

590   consistently predict increases in extreme floods (Hirabayashi et al., 2013; Hoang et al., 2016; [SOURCE: Page_30]

591   Perera et al., 2017) while projected changes in mean annual floods are less consistent (e.g. Wang [SOURCE: Page_30]

592   et al., 2017) and observations at some mainstream stations indicate a decreasing trend (Delgado [SOURCE: Page_30]

593   et al., 2010). GCM projections of future droughts are also uncertain, as Kiem et al. (2008) [SOURCE: Page_30]

594   projected reduced streamflow drought severity due to greater precipitation whereas [SOURCE: Page_30]

595   Thilakaranthe et al. (2017) forecasted that greater interannual variability would worsen droughts. [SOURCE: Page_30]

30 [SOURCE: Page_30]

596          GCMs have been essential for projecting combined impacts of hydropower and climate [SOURCE: Page_31]

597   change. Wang et al. (2017) noted that UMB reservoir storage could alleviate increased flooding [SOURCE: Page_31]

598   along the upper LMB mainstream (down to Nong Khai, Thailand), where they predict flood- [SOURCE: Page_31]

599   control benefits of reservoir storage will outweigh climate-driven increases in flood magnitude [SOURCE: Page_31]

600   through 2070. However, they expect increased precipitation to immediately outweigh the flood- [SOURCE: Page_31]

601   control benefits of reservoir storage further downstream. Hoanh et al. (2010) also stressed that [SOURCE: Page_31]

602   reservoirs cannot mitigate projected climate-induced flood increases, as they would reduce the [SOURCE: Page_31]

603   LMB’s seasonally flooded area by less than 1%. Lauri et al. (2012) reported that climate change [SOURCE: Page_31]

604   could either exacerbate or offset 10% reductions in wet-season flow from hydropower at Kratie [SOURCE: Page_31]

605   from 2032-2042 (-21- 4%). While many GCMs predict increased dry-season flows (e.g. Hoang [SOURCE: Page_31]

606   et al., 2016), even the decreases that some models project are unlikely to fully offset large [SOURCE: Page_31]

607   hydropower-induced increases. [SOURCE: Page_31]

608          Other studies examining joint hydropower-climate change impacts have focused on [SOURCE: Page_31]

609   specific tributary basins. In the 3S basin, Ngo et al. (2017) predicted that climate change would [SOURCE: Page_31]

610   partially offset increases in dry-season flows from Jan-May, offset decreases in Jun-Jul, while [SOURCE: Page_31]

611   changes from Aug-Dec vary by sub-basin. Using ten GCM runs from Lauri et al. (2012), Arias et [SOURCE: Page_31]

612   al. (2014a) estimated that hydropower development may worsen climate change-induced habitat [SOURCE: Page_31]

613   losses to Tonle Sap Lake, as expanded areas of open water (32-38%), along with irrigated and [SOURCE: Page_31]

614   rain-fed rice (11-21%), may drastically reduce riparian forests (13-67%) and other seasonally [SOURCE: Page_31]

615   flooded habitat. In other tributary basins, such as the Nam Ou in northern Lao PDR, uncertain [SOURCE: Page_31]

616   directions of change in precipitation (-27-41%) and annual streamflow (-27-160%) (Shrestha et [SOURCE: Page_31]

617   al., 2013) confound efforts to mitigate hydropower impacts. Few studies have examined whether [SOURCE: Page_31]

31 [SOURCE: Page_31]

618   climate-induced increases in streamflow could offset flow depletion downstream of diversions [SOURCE: Page_32]

619   and exacerbate flooding in surcharged basins. [SOURCE: Page_32]

620   5.2    Water demand [SOURCE: Page_32]

621          Historically, the Mekong has been one of the least irrigated basins in monsoonal Asia [SOURCE: Page_32]

622   (Barker and Molle, 2004; Ringler et al., 2004; Haddeland et al., 2006). Yet, higher dry-season [SOURCE: Page_32]

623   flows from hydropower are enabling greater dry-season irrigation withdrawals. In turn, these [SOURCE: Page_32]

624   larger withdrawals are expected to offset elevated dry-season flows increasingly since the largest [SOURCE: Page_32]

625   dams have already been commissioned (MRC, 2011). Indeed, the complementarity of [SOURCE: Page_32]

626   hydropower and irrigation in the Mekong basin (e.g. Lacombe et al., 2014a) differs from some [SOURCE: Page_32]

627   other basins where they directly compete with one another (Zeng and Cai, 2017). [SOURCE: Page_32]

628          LMB irrigation may expand from 6.6 million ha in 2010 to 8.2-9.7 million ha in 2030 [SOURCE: Page_32]

629   (Hoanh et al., 2010; MRC, 2011). This includes a dry-season increase from 1.2 to 1.8 million ha [SOURCE: Page_32]

630   (MRC, 2011), which, considering these estimates of total irrigation expansion, suggest a wet- [SOURCE: Page_32]

631   season increase of 1.0-2.5 million ha. (Note that irrigated areas are difficult to estimate, partly [SOURCE: Page_32]

632   due to the diverse forms of irrigation in the basin (Ringler et al., 2004; Pokhrel et al., 2018)). [SOURCE: Page_32]

633   MRC (2018) is also developing new projections of water withdrawals for irrigation. Some [SOURCE: Page_32]

634   studies project that most irrigation expansion will take place in Lao PDR and Cambodia, where [SOURCE: Page_32]

635   more land is available, and both reservoir storage and export-driven production are growing [SOURCE: Page_32]

636   rapidly (ICEM, 2010; Erban and Gorelick, 2016; Trung et al., 2018). However, elevated dry- [SOURCE: Page_32]

637   season flows in the mainstream could also be diverted to northeastern Thailand, where irrigation [SOURCE: Page_32]

638   infrastructure already exists (Molle and Floch, 2008). Numerous long-term hydropower impact [SOURCE: Page_32]

639   studies (Hoanh et al., 2010; MRC, 2011) that include future changes in irrigation estimate dry- [SOURCE: Page_32]

640   season flow increases smaller than the ones that only consider hydropower development (Lauri [SOURCE: Page_32]

32 [SOURCE: Page_32]

641   et al., 2012). Few studies have examined decreases in irrigation opportunities downstream of [SOURCE: Page_33]

642   diversions while higher dry-season flows in recipient basins have spawned irrigation projects [SOURCE: Page_33]

643   (Baird et al., 2015) [SOURCE: Page_33]

644          Next, the extent to which wet-season irrigation could exacerbate dam-induced reductions [SOURCE: Page_33]

645   in wet-season flow has not received as much attention even though less than 20% of all irrigated [SOURCE: Page_33]

646   land was for dry-season use in 2010 (MRC, 2011). Warmer temperatures could further increase [SOURCE: Page_33]

647   this demand (Hoanh et al., 2010; Mainuddin et al., 2012). While domestic and industrial sectors [SOURCE: Page_33]

648   consume much less water than agriculture, a doubling of the water demand by 2030 could [SOURCE: Page_33]

649   worsen dry-season droughts (e.g. Johnston et al., 2010), making flow regulation from [SOURCE: Page_33]

650   hydropower more valuable for water supply. [SOURCE: Page_33]

651          While hydropower development can provide extensive irrigation co-benefits during the [SOURCE: Page_33]

652   dry season (Lacombe et al., 2014a), rising water levels upstream of new mainstream dams and [SOURCE: Page_33]

653   channel migration below them may require as many as half of all existing and planned pumping [SOURCE: Page_33]

654   stations to be relocated, resized and retrofitted to deal with greater daily flow fluctuations [SOURCE: Page_33]

655   (ICEM, 2010). (In contrast, Pokhrel et al. (2018) suggest that higher dry-season flows may [SOURCE: Page_33]

656   elevate groundwater levels, thereby reducing pumping costs). Many riverbank gardens may [SOURCE: Page_33]

657   become permanently inundated, including 54% on the Mekong mainstream (ICEM, 2010). [SOURCE: Page_33]

658   Higher dry-season flows may also force farmers to grow new varieties of rice (Fox and Wood, [SOURCE: Page_33]

659   2007). Increased withdrawals for producing food to replace declining capture fisheries (see Orr [SOURCE: Page_33]

660   et al., 2012) have not been assessed. [SOURCE: Page_33]

661   5.3    Land cover change [SOURCE: Page_33]

662          While few studies directly compare the hydrological impacts of land cover and [SOURCE: Page_33]

663   hydropower, numerous basin-wide studies (e.g. Kite, 2001; Kiem et al., 2008; Costa-Cabral et [SOURCE: Page_33]

33 [SOURCE: Page_33]

664   al., 2008; MRC 2018) as well as ones in smaller tributary watersheds (e.g. Lacombe et al., 2010; [SOURCE: Page_34]

665   Ty, 2012; Lacombe and Pierret, 2013; Lacombe et al., 2014b, 2016; Lyon et al., 2017; Shrestha [SOURCE: Page_34]

666   et al., 2017) offer some insight regarding the extent to which changes in land cover may offset or [SOURCE: Page_34]

667   exacerbate hydropower-induced flow alteration. These impacts depend on many site-specific [SOURCE: Page_34]

668   factors. For instance, when low-density forest is converted to intensive agriculture, deforestation [SOURCE: Page_34]

669   will exacerbate decreases in high flows from hydropower (Shrestha et al., 2017). However, [SOURCE: Page_34]

670   deforestation has often been associated with increased runoff due to the lower evapotranspiration [SOURCE: Page_34]

671   of newly developed land cover (e.g. Lacombe et al., 2010; Lacombe and Pierret, 2013). Lacombe [SOURCE: Page_34]

672   et al. (2016) observed that afforestation from planting and natural regeneration had opposing [SOURCE: Page_34]

673   effects on annual streamflow since planting, especially for commercial timber, tends to reduce [SOURCE: Page_34]

674   soil infiltration capacity whereas natural regeneration increases it. These caveats also underscore [SOURCE: Page_34]

675   the importance of site-specific studies for evaluating forest preservation policies upstream of [SOURCE: Page_34]

676   hydropower reservoirs (Arias et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_34]

677         Hydrological effects of changes in land cover stemming from economic development [SOURCE: Page_34]

678   catalyzed by new dams, such as road construction and mining, also merit consideration (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_34]

679   Shrestha et al., 2016; Hennig et al., 2016; Hoang and Tran, 2017; Pokhrel et al., 2018). Orr et al. [SOURCE: Page_34]

680   (2012) estimated that livestock production areas would need to expand by 19-63% to compensate [SOURCE: Page_34]

681   for a 40% loss in fish protein (without livestock intensification or expansion of aquaculture or [SOURCE: Page_34]

682   reservoir fisheries). However, they did not spatially model this land conversion, which inhibits it [SOURCE: Page_34]

683   from being assessed with a distributed hydrological model. While increased withdrawals for rice [SOURCE: Page_34]

684   irrigation have been examined (e.g. Hoanh et al., 2010; Piman et al., 2013a), the effects of rice [SOURCE: Page_34]

685   paddies on surface runoff (e.g. Costa-Cabral et al., 2008; Homdee et al., 2011; Lacombe et al., [SOURCE: Page_34]

686   2014b), have not been incorporated into hydropower impact studies. [SOURCE: Page_34]

688   6.     Concluding remarks [SOURCE: Page_35]

689          Understanding hydropower-induced changes to the hydrology of the Mekong basin is [SOURCE: Page_35]

690   essential for anticipating and mitigating their negative impacts. Although many studies have [SOURCE: Page_35]

691   analyzed its hydrological impacts, observed and projected changes to both mainstream and [SOURCE: Page_35]

692   tributaries flows have not been reviewed in sufficient detail. While most dams with integral [SOURCE: Page_35]

693   powerhouses are expected to regulate seasonal flow variability, diversions for off-stream power [SOURCE: Page_35]

694   generation can drastically decrease downstream flows and augment flows in adjacent basins, a [SOURCE: Page_35]

695   change which prior reviews have not captured. Meanwhile, LMB mainstream dams are expected [SOURCE: Page_35]

696   to affect primarily diurnal flow variability, though their effects on flood propagation are [SOURCE: Page_35]

697   underexplored. Flood hazards from dam failures, emergency spills and inter-basin transfers also [SOURCE: Page_35]

698   require further study, as does reservoir evaporation. [SOURCE: Page_35]

699          We also assess whether concurrent changes in climate, water demand, and land cover [SOURCE: Page_35]

700   offset or exacerbate hydrological impacts of hydropower. While some uncertainty in the [SOURCE: Page_35]

701   direction of hydrological responses to climate change remains, modeling studies increasingly [SOURCE: Page_35]

702   suggest that both wet- and dry-season flows will increase in many parts of the basin. Under this [SOURCE: Page_35]

703   circumstance, on-stream hydropower development could play an increasingly important role in [SOURCE: Page_35]

704   offsetting climate-change impacts during the wet season but would exacerbate them during the [SOURCE: Page_35]

705   dry season. Meanwhile, increased dry-season flows could enable irrigation to meet growing food [SOURCE: Page_35]

706   and export demands, as allowed by land availability. However, fertile land flooded by new [SOURCE: Page_35]

707   impoundments or temporarily submerged by higher river water levels (e.g. riverbank gardens) [SOURCE: Page_35]

708   may compromise many agricultural livelihoods. The joint impacts of these concurrent changes [SOURCE: Page_35]

709   and hydropower also depends on the on-site vs. off-site nature of hydropower production. For [SOURCE: Page_35]

35 [SOURCE: Page_35]

710   instance, greater precipitation could offset impacts of diversions in donor basins while [SOURCE: Page_36]

711   aggravating flood hazards in recipient basins. [SOURCE: Page_36]

712          However, comparing hydrological alteration from dams and other concurrent drivers of [SOURCE: Page_36]

713   change is insufficient for addressing transboundary and multi-sectoral tradeoffs of dam [SOURCE: Page_36]

714   development. Rather, integrated models featuring indirect pathways through which hydropower [SOURCE: Page_36]

715   induces hydrological alteration, such as land-cover change near dams, are necessary to fully [SOURCE: Page_36]

716   examine them (e.g. Ringler et al., 2004; Pokhrel et al., 2018). Future investments that increase [SOURCE: Page_36]

717   evapotranspiration, such as food crops or timber, should carefully consider possible decreases in [SOURCE: Page_36]

718   streamflow and, consequently, hydropower production. The effects of deforestation-driven [SOURCE: Page_36]

719   increases in sediment yields on reservoir operations should also be assessed. [SOURCE: Page_36]

720          In addition, while flow alteration often harms riverine ecosystems (Poff et al., 1997), it [SOURCE: Page_36]

721   could also improve some livelihood-relevant ecological indicators (Sabo et al., 2017). This [SOURCE: Page_36]

722   uncertainty further underscores the need for more quantitative relationships between flow [SOURCE: Page_36]

723   alteration and ecological responses. Such assessments must untangle other drivers of ecological [SOURCE: Page_36]

724   change, such as connectivity and sediment concentrations, from flow alteration (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_36]

725   McManamay et al., 2013; Knight et al., 2014; Hogan et al., 2018). Multi-reservoir operation [SOURCE: Page_36]

726   rules that mitigate or even improve ecological and livelihood impacts (e.g. Lacombe et al., [SOURCE: Page_36]

727   2014a; Jeuland et al., 2014; Reis et al., 2015) also merit further research, as do ecological effects [SOURCE: Page_36]

728   of dam construction sequences. [SOURCE: Page_36]

729          Finally, our review offers guidance for research in other basins where extensive [SOURCE: Page_36]

730   hydropower development is possible. We especially encourage the coordination of observational [SOURCE: Page_36]

731   and modeling studies when assessing dam-induced flow alteration. In addition, studies should [SOURCE: Page_36]

732   consider: (i) uncertainty associated with the length of pre- and post-dam streamflow records, (ii) [SOURCE: Page_36]

36 [SOURCE: Page_36]

733   hydrological change metrics relevant for local ecosystems and livelihoods, (iii) different [SOURCE: Page_37]

734   timescales of hydrological alteration, including sub-daily ones, (iv) different modes of [SOURCE: Page_37]

735   hydropower production (on-stream vs. off-stream), (v) impacts of inter-basin transfers, (vi) dam [SOURCE: Page_37]

736   operations during floods, (vii) concurrent environmental changes that might offset or exacerbate [SOURCE: Page_37]

737   hydropower-induced ones, (viii) direct and indirect pathways through which dams can alter [SOURCE: Page_37]

738   streamflow, (ix) scheduling and coordination of hydropower projects to minimize basin-wide [SOURCE: Page_37]

739   impacts, and (x) long-term effects of reservoir sedimentation. Addressing these issues could [SOURCE: Page_37]

740   improve tradeoffs between hydropower production and other river management objectives. [SOURCE: Page_37]
