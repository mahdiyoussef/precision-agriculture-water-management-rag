Abstract The management strategies of large diameter pressure water pipelines (at the piece scale) are generally based on an expert knowledge. Some of these strategies are hardly formalised and partially meet alignment priorities. In fact, the degradation kinetics for this kind of pipelines are badly known and the numerical models to assess future degradation trends are rarely available. Consequently, some pieces in the main water supply are probably replaced at an early stage whereas others subject to a much higher risk are spared. These aspects bring to acquire a better knowledge of the determinants of the mechanical behaviour of the pipelines in order to optimize the asset management (at the whole patrimony scale). In this paper, the current technological locks that will improve and advance knowledge are firstly discussed. A methodology based on reliability assessment is then presented. The goal is to get the necessary information about the real properties (aging, in-service loading more important,…) of some pieces of a main water supply (for different configurations) and geotechnical conditions. This information (‘current state’) can be then compared to the required performance (‘design state’) by using mechanical models coupled with probabilistic approaches. A reference case is presented to illustrate this formalism for improving the resources allocated to the maintenance by an effective risk management. [SOURCE: Page_1]

### 2. Current technological locks for asset management of pipelines

2.1 Concrete pressure pipes Concrete pipes offer important benefits with respect to steel pipes since they resist to corrosion under severe conditions and are equally a cost-saving solution. The most widely used type of concrete pipes to transport drinking water under high pressure are: (i) the lined cylinder pipe, this is a steel cylinder lined with a concrete core and (ii) the embedded cylinder pipe, this is a steel cylinder embedded in a concrete core. These concrete pressure pipes are also known as BONNA pipes. Figure 1 shows this kind of pipe such as those built in North America (USA) or in Europe (France). The outside diameter of this pipe is 250‒4000 mm and its operational length is 2‒6 m. The external concrete revetment is strengthened with steel wires. When steel wires are prestressed, the pipes are also known as Prestressed Concrete Cylinder Pipe, PCCP. Otherwise, they are known as Reinforced Concrete Pressure Pipe, RCPP. [SOURCE: Page_2]

2.2 Current technological locks In order to identify the locks relating to issues on asset management of a main drinking water supply, we focus on the main following aspects: •    the aspects concerning the interaction soil-pipe-water flow, •    the aspects taking into account the different uncertainties and, •    the aspects concerning both the physical and the numerical modelling. These aspect are all coupled and inter-dependent. [SOURCE: Page_3]

2.2.1 Locks concerning interaction soil-pipe-water flow The classical design pipes methods only consider the cross-section and suppose optimal laying conditions, but they are hardly respected in practice (degradation of bedding and/or differential settling after some years of operational service). On the other hand, geotechnical conditions load longitudinally the pipe and consequently, a longitudinal spatial behaviour analysis is required. The modelling of the ring joint is another study element and its evaluation is complex. Buco et al. [4], [5] consider the evaluation of the joint stiffness as essential during the analyse of soil heterogeneities influence on both mechanical parameters and differential settlements. A correct model of ring joint, which should be validated by instrumentation data, is an important point to be considered. [SOURCE: Page_3]

2.2.2 Locks concerning durability The failure of a concrete pipe by corrosion is progressive and the physical phenomena and the trigger corrosion mechanisms are diverse. The corrosion can be localised (pitting or crevice) or global (uniform) in the steel cylinder. Steel wires are also fragile elements and can be easily damaged. They constitute a vector for pipe degradation whether they are broken by corrosion. The degradation of sealing in the joints is also another important corrosion mechanism. In addition, the presence of hydrogen is identified as another trigger corrosion mechanism. Although there is no agreement in the literature on the rupture mechanisms of prestressed steel in concrete and the contribution of hydrogen, researches about this phenomenon (hydrogen embrittlement) are cited in [16]. Considering only one model is not enough to analyse correctly the effect of corrosion. Then, it is necessary to consider several models to characterise the diversity of corrosion mechanisms and to let identify the most influent factors. [SOURCE: Page_3]

2.2.3 Locks concerning numerical modelling In order to understand the interaction soil-pipe-water flow, performing simplified mechanical models with the introduction of non-linear behaviour models can be done. This will allow the variability and the relative uncertainties concerning the characterisation of soil properties to be controlled. The validation and qualification of the mechanical behaviour model of the pipe should be done by comparing results to other issued from literature and by performing experimental campaigns (e.g. the experimental determination of both the length of correlation and the mechanical joint stiffness). [SOURCE: Page_3]

3. Follow-up methodology of concrete pipes We discuss in this paper a proposed follow-up methodology of concrete pipes transporting drinking water under high pressure. This methodology can be based on: •    the acquisition of interest magnitudes on which depends both the aging and the degradation of the concrete pipes; •    the use of some of the interest magnitudes cited above as data input into geomechanical models to study the behaviour of concrete pipes; •    the use of the other interest magnitudes as following-up parameters of the degradations just to validate the models and to readjust them in order to enhance their predictive function; •    the inclusion of the geomechanical models, used in a probabilistic context, into an optimisation approach for inspection and maintenance (proactive approach, identification of alarm values,…). This methodology intends to optimise the resources for maintenance and thus set up an efficient risk management and to associate the diagnostic, the prognostic and the decision- making concerning the desired performances (structural integrity of pipes, the need to ensure continuity of service…). The different steps, illustrated in Figure 2, that are introduced in the implementation of the methodology are described in the next sections. The interaction between these different steps will be presented in §4. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Usually, the stress corrosion cracking causes abrupt brittle ruptures and does not show any signs of visible damage. Concerning prestressed concrete pipes, when a broken wire due to corrosion occurs, a lump will often form at the surrounding area of the mortar layer. The effects of corrosion on the integrity of the pipe are then studied from the inspection of the degraded areas. For example, monitoring techniques as acoustic monitoring ([8], [16]) are used to detect broken prestressed wires. These monitoring techniques are (generally used on visible pipes) allow to owners an early detection of corroded zones (detection of macro-cracks and propagation of cracks) allowing them sufficient time to respond to an eventual rupture of the system. Nonetheless, these techniques can also be limited [8]. Indeed, it is difficult to evaluate broken wires in joint zone. The state of joints is also important. For example, [9] presents prestressed pipe breaks due to both corrosion in joint because of a thin mortar layer and corrosion in steel cylinder area near to joint. The combination of degradation mechanisms leads to an accelerated failure of pipes. A physical model which estimates the failure of a pipe have to include at least two models: one that describes the degradation process and another that describes the geo-mechanical aspects. 3.2 Geomechanical behaviour models The design of longitudinal pipes in the literature is approached by techniques that represent pipes by elastic beams articulated and sitting on an elastic continuum ([11], [12], [21]). These models are grouped in two categories according to [1]: − the local continuous medium models: the soil is represented by a linear or non-linear medium with a behaviour described by stress-strain relations; these models can be solved by 2-D or 3-D numerical techniques restricted to finite dimensions of the system ([2], [13], [15]); − the global simplified models: the local behaviour of soil is replaced by a simplified mechanical model; the most used common model to represent soil-structure interaction is the unidimensional Winkler model ([6], [7], [11], [12]). The rigid or flexible pipes are vulnerable due to stresses generated by soil displacements. Generally, the settlings of pipes are often underestimated or neglected during the design phase. Some studies addressing the influence of settlings on the behaviour of pipes and particularly on the behaviour of joints exist (see for example [17]). The rigidity of joints depends on technology and geometry (flexible joints, welded joints). A realistic numerical value of joint stiffness remains very difficult to identify, although some laboratory experiments have focused on this issue [5]. [SOURCE: Page_5]

3.4 Modelling of soil-pipe-flow water interaction Modelling the behaviour of soil-pipe-flow water interaction remains limited in literature. Indeed, it is a complex three-dimensional problem that requires very sophisticated numerical models expected to make use of very robust soil models (elastic-plastic perfect model, Cam- Clay modified model, non-linear elastic model, elastic-plastic strain hardening model). The soil is a multi-phasic material, is not easy to forecast its behaviour with respect to materials as steel or concrete and its properties can strongly vary in an entirely different area. In addition, a soil is a highly heterogeneous material, and this heterogeneity is proving to be the main cause of both uncertainty and variability. The variability may also involve another feature called “regionalizing data” [3]. This means that the soil properties (permeability coefficient, porosity, internal angle friction, water content, etc.) in two adjacent points of the material (in both vertical and horizontal direction) are statistically closer than the measured ones in two more remote points. The soil properties can then be considered as spatially structured and therefore allow the use of autocorrelation functions making possible to identify the distance (correlation length) from which soil properties become independent [7]. The dispersion of soil properties also needs to be determined (standard deviation or coefficient of variation). In order to ensure a safer design of a new pipe or to evaluate the residual life of an existent buried pipe, a reliable assessment of soil stresses on the pipe due to soil-pipe displacements is necessary. We have to keep in mind that the behaviour of a pipe depends on the stiffness of each section of the pipe, on the position, stiffness and behaviour of the ring joints and on the nature of the soil displacements. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Case study The follow-up methodology of concrete pipes is purposefully focused on the modelling of soil-pipe-flow water interaction system taking into account both the geomechanical behaviour of the pipe and the durability. We explain here the interaction between the different steps of the methodology shown in Figure 3. The steps ‘Geomechanical behaviour models’ and ‘Durability of concrete pipes’ have a double dimension: experimental and theoretical. The experimental one allows access to data not available in current database and that are needed to a better understanding of the physical mechanisms involved and to validation and qualification of developed models. The main purpose of the probabilistic and reliability approaches (time-variant type) involved in the step ‘Uncertainty modelling and Spatial variability’ and applied to developed models in the other steps (‘Geomechanical behaviour models’ and ‘Durability of concrete pipes’) is to assess the risk a pipe presents during operational service with respect to limit states to be defined. 5. Conclusions This work has presented a general vision to optimize the management of pipelines with a follow-up methodology, which provides a best understanding of the mechanical behaviour of concrete pressure pipes. Further more, an emphasis was also placed on the effect of different mechanisms of degradation, particularly the corrosion, and of the surrounding soil on the spatial-temporal behaviour and the integrity of these pipes. Although these two effects can be studied separately, it seems necessary to take into account all the possible interactions and relevant physico-chemical phenomena and the application of a ‘mechano- probabilistic/reliability’ approach allowing a better apprehension of both the risk involved (structural integrity, service disruption,…) and the long-term life of the structures. Experimental campaigns let to access determinant elements as the soil-pipe interaction, the effects of the soil variability and the degradation kinetic of the main structural components of the pipe. Indeed, these key elements allowing to better understand the physical of different coupled systems in BONNA pipes are not well enough known. This experimental approach validates and qualifies the endorsed numerical tools. The different steps of the follow-up methodology of concrete pipes proposed in this article intend to optimise the resources allocated to the asset management by an efficient risk management process.
