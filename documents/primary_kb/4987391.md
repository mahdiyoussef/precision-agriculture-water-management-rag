1    Introduction Human activities, by emitting greenhouse gases (GHG) into atmosphere, are causing a climatic upheaval. The related climate alterations have already begun to impact and will continue to endanger human endeavours. Climate change might have opposite influence on ecosystems. On the one hand, the increasing amount of CO$_2$ in the atmosphere may have a fertilising effect on plants and the rise in temperature extends the growing season (Boisvenue and Running, 2006; Allen et al., 2010). For northern ecosystems, a warmer climate enables Nordic coniferous trees to begin photosynthesis earlier, which significantly increases Net Primary Production (NPP) (Bergh et al., 2003). On the other hand, the rise in temperature and vapour pressure deficit lead to more evapotranspiration that enhances the exposure to water stress of such ecosystems. [SOURCE: Page_6]

According to Allen et al. (2010), no forest type or climate region is invulnerable to climate change. Water availability appears to be a limiting factor for tree growth in changing climate conditions. Water scarcity can cause forest dieback due to repeated or extreme drought events. Dieback is defined by the death of groups of neighbouring trees, often due to natural causes, with the majority of trees either dead or display reduced vigour (Mueller-Dombois, 1987). Allen et al. (2010) demonstrated that drought and heat have an impact on various types of forests. As forests cover 30% of the world’s land surface (FAO, 2020), global warming could significantly alter landscapes worldwide. The temperature has already risen by 1.1°C from 1850-1900 to 2011-2020, and hot extremes have become more frequent and intense since 1950 and are expected to worsen. Drought can create stress and induce mortality, both directly through heat and indirectly through events such as fires, increases in pest insect populations, or pathogen outbreaks Climate change not only threatens the forest production but jeopar- dises also the entire ecosystem. Biodiversity in forests faces moderate to high near-term risk (IPCC, 2023; Millar and Stephenson, 2015). Water stress is a compounding effect leading to the forest dieback. It may act as an ’inciting factor’ that leads to death if trees are already under stress due to ’predisposing factors’ such as age, poor site conditions, or air pollution. Death is exacerbate by ’contributing factors’ such as stem or root damage caused by pests or fungi (McDowell et al., 2008). [SOURCE: Page_6]

Hence, the pivotal role of forests in the implementation of the Paris Agreement, with the potential to contribute approximately a quarter of the planned emission reductions (Grassi et al., 2017), is questioned by climate change effects and the challenge of adapting forests to the effects of climate change is becoming increasingly urgent. This is particularly the case for production forests, which provide the majority of the raw materials used in the timber industry and for decarbonising various industrial sectors. The global forest sector collectively employed more than 18.21 million people and supported over 45.15 million jobs through direct, indirect and induced impacts. The sector’s direct contribution to the world GDP was in excess of $539 billion (Li et al., 2019). However, both climate mitigation and wood production are conditioned by the adaptation of forests under a changing climate. Determining the impact of climate change on forest production remains challenging. However, Bergh et al. (2003) showed that Nordic coniferous trees have experienced increased growth due to climate change, provided that water supply is not limited. Loustau et al. (2005) predicted a small increase in potential forest yield from 2030-20250, followed by a plateau or a decline around 2070 and [SOURCE: Page_6]

1 [SOURCE: Page_6]

2100. The study revealed that while productivity is anticipated to increase in northern French forests, it is likely to decline in southern regions. [SOURCE: Page_7]

It can be posited that silviculture may affect the vulnerability of forest tree stands to the the increasing scarcity of rain and frequency and duration of heatwaves. Current manage- ment practices are highly productive but may not exhibit high adaptive capacities to climate change (Duveneck and Scheller, 2016). In light of the concerning climate projections from IPCC (2023), it is pertinent to consider whether managed ecosystems are able to withstand the predicted conditions. As mentioned by Breda and Badeau (2008), the identification of new insights for classifying factors explaining the vulnerability of forest stands to extreme events should assist forest managers in anticipating catastrophic mortality and a reduction in produc- tivity or biodiversity. In their review on drought-induced tree mortality, Allen et al. (2010) propose to control the density of trees as a solution to reduce competition. This practice, called thinning, is part of many management regimes. This prompts the question of whether these management regimes are conducive to, or detrimental to, the adaptive capacity of the forest. Management regimes providing great adaptive capacities to climate change without neglecting productivity are the most worthy of interest. In production forests, forest managers combine different technical itineraries depending on the types of soil and climate encountered in a forest area. These itineraries range from intensive, short-rotation options including soil preparation, vegetation control and dynamic thinning, to close-to-nature options. In the con- text of sudden climate change, the question arises as to how silviculture options on a given estate can be combined in order to maintain a level of production while minimising the risk of water stress. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Quadratic optimisation, also known as portfolio optimisation or Markowitz mean-variance analysis (Markowitz, 1952), helps to balance two opposing objectives. It is the mathematical translation of the adage ”don’t put all your eggs in one basket”, which initially illustrated the interest of diversification in finance. It is already being used in forest management. Dragice- vic et al. (2016) studied the trade-off productivity-risk in forestry. The aim was to identify the best species mix to maximise forest productivity while minimising variance, taking into account the forest manager’s aversion to risk. The study also aimed to maximise carbon sequestration. Brunette et al. (2014) searched for the optimum combinations of wood produc- tivity and vulnerability based on species combinations and mapped the optimum compositions by French department. The authors primarily focus on the financial aspect, using a proven financial method to answer technical questions in forestry, which strengthens the link between the two disciplines. However, they both note poor data sampling from the National Forest Inventory (IFN) database between 1978 and 2009. The method was performed by Dragicevic (2019), in the Landes de Gascogne considering both agricultural activities, energy production and forestry to maximise the economic returns of the region. At an European scale Gregor et al., (2022), studied different managements and focused on the impact of unmanaged forest or the conversion from needle do broad leaves during the coming century. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Here, we used quadratic optimisation to identify the optimal allocation of management al- ternatives in forests composed of stands of maritime pine or sessile (or pedunculate) oak in France. Our objective is to optimise the distribution of silvicultural alternatives over a given [SOURCE: Page_7]

2 [SOURCE: Page_7]

forest area according to the trade-off between the forest productivity and its temporal sta- bility. Our study was conducted to determine how the optimal allocation would be modified over time in response to climate change from 2006 to 2100. In addition, forest areas with high and low annual precipitation were compared. We applied the quadratic optimisation method to two case studies, the maritime pine forest and the oak forests in France. Our analysis was not based on descriptive historical data, but rather on prospective data derived from climatic projections. Our experiment is based on a solid and consistent database of simulations of French forests conducted using the GO+ model (Moreaux et al., 2020) and available on the Forêt-21 website for professionals and experts. The primary interest and innovation of our approach lie in applying mean-variance optimisation to forest management alternatives, tak- ing into account future climates and silvicultural practices on representative forest areas in France. 2     Material and Methods 2.1    Forests considered The Landes de Gascogne forest is one example of vulnerable forest in France. Situated in south-west France, it encompasses an area of 10,000 km² and has been entirely created and managed by humans for their specific industries during the second half of the 19th century. The maritime pine (Pinus pinaster ) accounts for 87% of the forest area. The forest is pri- vately owned at 90%, with 75% of the forest area belonging to only 20% of the owners. The wood production of the Landes de Gascogne represents 12% of the French wood production (Service Nature Foret, 2015). The region offers a diverse array of markets for pine products. The primary use of the forest is for pulpwood, which accounts for the majority (45%) of the harvested pines in 2019, followed by lumber, which accounts for 43% of the trees. Low-quality logs are employed in the production of wood packing, while logs of higher quality are used in the construction industry. A further 11% of the production is allocated to fuelwood, while the remaining 1% is used for other purposes. Since 2013, the proportion of industrial wood des- ignated for pulp production has exceeded that of lumber wood, which previously constituted 50% of total production (Layton et al., 2021). In the Landes de Gascogne, the pine produc- tion is economically advantageous as Pinus pinaster offers the highest price for logs among the conifers (AGRESTE, 2023). The Landes de Gascogne forest sector was recently affected by several crisis. The hurricanes Martin (1999) and Klaus (2009) caused approximately 70 million cubic metres of windthrow damage. The storm Xynthia in 2010 also damaged forest stands. Furthermore, the adverse effects of natural disasters have been exacerbated by insect pest damage (Dragicevic, 2019; Mora et al., 2014). Climate change represents a novel threat to the Landes de Gascogne forest, which will have to adapt (Kamimura et al., 2016; Mora et al., 2014). A dendrochronological study conducted in 2018 on maritime pine from northern Spain, Caminero et al. (2018), demonstrate the ambivalent effect of climate change. A warm winter, particularly in February, has a positive effect on growth. Nevertheless, low precipitation in May has a negative effect on growth. The study indicates that the impact of elevated temper- atures and reduced rainfall the previous fall has a negative effect on the growth of maritime pine in the following year. Additionally, the authors also posit that maritime pine may have both local morphological adaptations, such as deep roots, and physiological adaptations, such [SOURCE: Page_8]

as tight stomatal regulation. [SOURCE: Page_9]

The sessile oak (Quercus petraea) and the pedunculate oak (Quercus robur ) are emblem- atic species of the French forest. They account together for 23% of the volume of wood and cover 4 million hectares in metropolitan France (IGN, 2023a). Unlike the maritime pine, which is mainly found in the Landes de Gascogne forest, sessile and pedunculate oaks can be found all over France (Ducousso and Bordacs, 2003). The silvicultural management of sessile and pedunculate oaks is a long-term process that takes one or two centuries to produce quality oaks. According to Polge (1984), high-quality trees are characterised by their narrow annual rings and are harvested from long rotation forests of 180 years. In France, Quercus petraea and Quercus robur are used for the construction of oak barrels for the ageing of wine and spirits. (Mosedale et al., 1999; Prida and Puech, 2006). Oak trees are also used for timber, with their price being more than twice that of other broad-leaf trees such as beech and poplar (AGRESTE, 2023). The wood products derived from oak are numerous and diverse, includ- ing flooring and carpentry, exterior cladding, interior joinery, furniture, and packaging wood Fortin and Ningre (2012). The significance of oak in the French forests is attributable to both its abundance and its high economic value. The growth tendency of sessile and pedunculate oaks was found to be largely unaffected by the reduction of precipitation or the augmentation of temperature between 2006 and 2020, according to the data from the National Forest Inven- tory (IGN, 2023b). However, at the local level, the water condition may influence the growth of the trees. In 1994, a 13,000-hectare forest in Alsace experienced a decline due to drought Breda and Badeau (2008) found a significant negative relationship between seasonal soil water deficit and growth index. Such a site is known to be water-limiting (Sardin, 2008). In order to highlight the effect of local climate, precipitation regime and available water capacity on the forest productivity, we chose to oppose Alsace to Brittany, where water is highly available (Groupement d’intérêt scientifique sur les sols, 2011). 2.2    Simulations The study was conducted using the results of the model GO+ (Moreaux et al., 2020), a process based model including a library of forest operations and management practices. This model has been previously used for simulating the effects of climate scenarios on forest growth and production (Loustau et al., 2005), nutrient balance (Achat et al., 2018), and French forests (Forets-21). As a mechanistic model, it simulates biophysical and geochemical processes to represent the growth of a managed forest. The modelling process considers radiative transfer, convective heat exchange, evapotranspiration, photosynthesis, respiration, plant phenology, growth and mortality, biomass nutrient content, and soil carbon dynamics. The forest is represented as three stacked layers, soil, tree understorey and overstorey. Meteorological variables force the model at a one-hour time step. The model employs discretised climatic projections at a resolution of 8×8 km or 32×32 km depending on the tree species. The model is thus run for each of these grid cells in conjunction with the local climatic conditions. Some parameters can be chosen by the user to represent different types of managed forests such as the tree species of the mono-specific stand, the climate scenario, its management practices, its available water capacity (AWC) and the age class of the trees (Moreaux et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_9]

Both productivity mean (Figure 2a) and standard deviation (Figure 2b) are higher under RCP 8.5 compared to RCP 4.5. This figure shows that each management regime responds differently to climate in terms of variability when consid- ering productivity of differently managed forests. Scenario 4.5 is more favourable to the forest than RCP 8.5. A closer examination shows that the productivity moving average reaches a peak around 2040 under RCP 4.5. This result is consistent across all management regimes, except for the short one. The standard deviation remains stable over time under RCP 4.5, except for the long management regime under the two highest AWC. The standard deviation appears to be more stable under RCP 4.5 than RCP 8.5, and this result is consistent across all AWCs except for long management under the two higher AWCs. Under the highest GHG emission scenario (RCP 8.5), productivity has been decreasing since 2010, a result observed for all management practices, except for the short one with the maximum AWC. At the same time, the standard deviation plateau at a relatively high value. The moving standard devia- tion is never less than the half of the moving average and can be up to 85% of the average (from 2050, the long management regime has a mean inferior to 14 m^3 .ha−1 .yr−1 and its stan- dard deviation is around 12 m^3 .ha−1 .yr−1 ). Scenario 8.5 also clearly discriminates between management regimes based on their variance. Both mean and variance tend to increase with increasing AWC. Under the lowest AWC, all management regimes appear to have a similar trend. Their trajectories differ with increasing AWC. [SOURCE: Page_15]

The classic and long regimes have a relatively high mean, with the long management regime always having the highest variance. 4     Discussion 4.1    Impacts of climate on managed forests Projections indicate a decrease in rainfall throughout the century, which will exacerbate drought conditions, a significant factor contributing to tree mortality (Allen et al. (2010); Caminero et al. (2018); Bergh et al. (2003)). In a study conducted by Loustau et al. (2005) a combination of ORCHIDEE, CASTANEA, and GRAECO models are utilised and they found similar results. As Gross Primary Production (GPP) is expected to continue increasing until 2100, and autotrophic respiration will also expand, Net Primary Production (NPP) is projected to peak around 2030. In France, the production is expected to increase more signif- icantly in the west than in the east, while precipitation is projected to decline. IGN (2023b) showed in a report that coniferous trees are experiencing an increase in growth in regions where the warming and the precipitation reduction are less pronounced. This is evident in the maritime pine forests of the Landes de Gascognes. [SOURCE: Page_23]

Further information is provided by the climatic model developed by Meteo France, ARPEGE/- climate, which utilises data from 1960 to 2100. It is projected that air temperature and vapour pressure deficit will increase, while annual precipitation may either decrease or remain stable (Loustau et al., 2005). The increase in CO$_2$ concentration in the atmosphere could explain the observed increase in average productivity across time under the RCP 4.5 scenario. Nev- ertheless, under scenario 8.5, despite the ample availability of CO$_2$ for photosynthesis, water availability may become limiting. [SOURCE: Page_23]

4.2    GO+ model operation and sensitivity to climate A sensitivity analysis of the GO+ model was performed by Moreaux et al. (2020). The au- thors show that variables related to tree growth such as plant respiration and Net Primary Production (NPP) or Gross Primary Production (GPP) are very sensitive to CO$_2$ and precip- itation. NPP and GPP are directly linked to productivity as it defined as the annual biomass accretion and timber harvest. So, CO$_2$ and precipitation have an impact on the forest pro- ductivity. Variation of air temperature and water vapour saturation deficit (the dryness of the air) present a global negative impact on the variables of the models, with the exception of respiration. A positive impact on respiration would lead to a greater difference between GPP [SOURCE: Page_23]

18 [SOURCE: Page_23]

and NPP, resulting in a reduction of plant growth and therefore forest productivity. Some analysis was performed at a long- term scale which revealed that the temperature step increase depleted biomass growth. While temperature has a short-term positive effect, it also has a long-term neutral or negative effect on productivity through biomass accretion. The timescale modifies the sensitivity profile of GO+ as the effects of the fluxes of CO$_2$ , energy and water would accumulate. This study demonstrates the value of GO+ model in understanding the long-term impact of climate change on forest ecosystems (Moreaux et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_24]

Our findings indicate that the forest could de more productive under RCP 8.5 than under RCP 4.5. Gregor et al. (2022) had similar results with an optimisation study: Low emission RCP has a production that is between 20% and 29% less significant than that of today in the future. RCP 8.5, however, meets the standards of today. We could explain the higher productivity mean under RCP 8.5 by CO$_2$ fertilisation, as did Gregor et al.. The experiment of Hickler et al. (2008) showed a more effective net primary production (NPP) under high concentrations of CO$_2$ . The variance appears to be more stable under RCP 4.5 than under RCP 8.5, this might be due to an increase in the intensity and frequency of extreme droughts with higher GHG emissions (Lindner et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_24]

The model elucidates the influence of climate on productivity. In GO+, trees die ”naturally” when their carbon balance is negative. This occurs when the annual respiration is greater than the annual GPP. This is due to a combination of factors, including a strong water deficit, a high air vapour deficit and high temperatures. The latter is because maintenance respiration is temperature-dependent (Moreaux et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_24]

The reduction in stem growth under water stress is partly due to the reduction in NPP and partly due to the concentration of photosynthetic carbon in the roots rather than in the stem. The water stress index directly influences the carbon from NPP to belowground or above- ground organs. This phenomenon is known as ”root-shoot allocation”. Stressed trees would preferentially use the carbon generated to grow their roots instead of their stem or branches. In the GO+ model, the proportion of carbon stored in underground parts is positively cor- related with the stress index (Moreaux et al., 2020). Breda and Badeau (2008) proposed in 2008 that the root-shoot allocation plays a central role in oaks resilience after a drought. The carbon allocation strategy, which involves partitioning carbon among growth, maintenance respiration and storage, may be a crucial process for oak performance to recover its carbon balance following an extreme event. Carbon allocation may act as a significant contributor to fitness in an extreme event. [SOURCE: Page_24]

The annual water stress index is calculated as Istress = 1 − Edry,T /Epot,T where Edry,T is the annual transpiration realised at year T and Epot,T is the potential transpiration, calculated with a stomatal model that takes into account only the radiations and the CO$_2$ without being limited by the lack of water. The calculation of Edry reveals an increase with an increasing vapour pressure deficit, which is positively correlated with temperature. To verify the conser- vation principles, some tests were performed on the GO+ model. The annual precipitation between 1984 and 2010 demonstrated coherence with Edry (Moreaux et al., 2020). There is no direct interaction between the stress index, which governs root-shoot allocation, [SOURCE: Page_24]

19 [SOURCE: Page_24]

and the management regime. The fraction of biomass stocked in aboveground parts is inversely proportional to the value of the stress index. However, the NPP depends on the management regime (Moreaux et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_25]

Mature trees from long rotation produce more biomass than other management regimes. GPP is proportional to Leaf Area Index (LAI). Older trees, which are bigger and have more foliage, photosynthesise more and produce more carbon. Even in the case of water stress, where a greater proportion of carbon would be allocated to underground organs, they would still grow consequently. Conversely, short rotation with younger trees has a smaller primary production. As adaptive regime cuts more trees to prevent water stress, its GPP is reduced (Moreaux et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_25]

4.3    The consequences of the management regimes on forest pro- ductivity The optimisation portfolios obtained were not unexpected. The following examples relate to pine. Similar conclusions could be drawn for oak tree with a comparison of the optimal allo- cations for oak with the mean and standard deviation values. When the aim is to reduce the variance of pine productivity, the adaptive regime is always selected, which is attempted as it presents the lowest standard deviation of all the regimes (Figure 2b). [SOURCE: Page_25]

As the adaptive management regime knows more thinning actions than the classic regime, it is less productive. In the model, preventive thinning remove 30% of the trees if the 3-year accumulated stress index [0;1] exceeds 2.5 Table 2. The number of trees is thus reduced under the adaptive regime compared to the classic regime. But preventive thinning would stabilise the production over years, as the effects of rainfall scarcity on productivity would be attenu- ated when the trees density is lowered. (Moreaux et al., 2020). [SOURCE: Page_25]

Given its high variance (Figure 2a) and productivity (Figure 2b), the long management regime is often selected when the objective is to maximise productivity. The long management regime allows trees to grow for longer, with the harvested quantities of wood, during thinning or fi- nal cut being of greater importance (Moreaux et al., 2020). The study by Dragicevic (2019) found some similar results about lumber, which is conducted under long management, while considering economic returns. In their optimisation lumber offers high returns but comes with high levels of risk. Long management is the most productive pine regime under both cli- mate scenarios, although its productivity declines in the coming century. This decrease occurs sooner in higher AWC. As precipitation decreases from 2040, it affects the fulfilment of AWC, with higher AWC suffering the most. IGN (2023b) that stands with good AWC are the most disadvantaged when there is a lack of summer rainfall. The current ”optimal” stands would become less productive due to the drought while regions with lower AWC would maintain their growth. This result is observed for the long regime but in general, the highest AWC are more productive (Figure 2a). However, as the classic management regime becomes more productive than the long one with time, it is mainly chosen at the end of the century Figure 2a. The hypothesis put forth to explain this phenomenon is that older trees are less able to survive to more extreme climatic [SOURCE: Page_25]

20 [SOURCE: Page_25]

conditions (Kramer et al., 2020; Lindenmayer and Laurance, 2017). As climate change is projected to intensify over time (IPCC, 2023), long-term management will present greater challenges than classic management that has younger trees. [SOURCE: Page_26]

Our findings indicate that pine short management regime presents a relatively low mean (Figure 2a) but also a relatively high variance (Figure 2b). The unsatisfactory outcomes of the short rotation cycle for pine trees are elucidated by Loustau et al. (2005). Given the interconnection between forest management and climate scenarios, it has been demonstrated that short rotations are more susceptible to climate variations. This sensitivity can be at- tributed to juveniles being more responsive to climatic conditions compared to mature trees. Consequently, long rotations, characterised by their older trees, would be less vulnerable to climatic fluctuations when compared to the continuous succession of young trees in short rota- tion cycles. For pine trees, the final age difference between trees from short and long regimes is greater than 55 years. In the GO+ model, the productivity of short rotation is relatively low due to the harvesting of trees with an average circumference at breast height of 50cm (in contrast to 120 cm for classic or adaptive management and 150 cm for long management). Furthermore, there is an annual empirical mortality rate that is slightly higher for young trees in the model. Trees under short management regimes experience a higher annual empirical mortality rate than others. One contributing factor to the high annual variability of short rotation is the regular cutting that is responsible for productivity peaks.This study considers only biological productivity, and thus does not take into account some economic considerations. The results would have been different as shown by Dragicevic (2019), who observed that in the Landes de Gascognes, industry-oriented timber (from short rotation) is associated with lower econometrical returns and risk. [SOURCE: Page_26]

It should be noted that short rotations typically correspond to highly intensive forest man- agement regimes (Loustau et al., 2005). As pulpwood constitutes the primary sector for pine production in the Landes de Gascogne, involving young trees, the economic sustainability of wood production in the region is threatened by climate change. Layton et al. (2021) suggests a transition in raw material flows to ensure the long-term economic viability of the area. How- ever, this transition may prove challenging, given that the entire pulp sector is concentrated in the Landes de Gascogne. Pulpwood production is cost-effective due to regular harvesting of trees at a young age. Nonetheless, the entire sector will need to adapt in the coming decades. The opposite phenomenon is observed in oak forest coppice management. The short rota- tion cycle of coppice management (an oak management regime) minimises the risk of climatic damage to the production process. However, it is primarily used for energy wood, a market that is less lucrative than that for greater oaks used for barrels and timber. It should be noted that this study does not account for the marketable value of the product. [SOURCE: Page_26]

As with all management regimes, there are advantages and disadvantages (Figure 6). There- fore, it is necessary to make a strategic decision regarding the choice of the regime. However, one may wonder what would happen if the forests were left unsupervised. According to the optimisation of (Bagdon et al., 2016), no management leads to a non-optimal scenario. A goal-programming model was employed to optimise the management of a ponderosa pine [SOURCE: Page_26]

It was particularly susceptible to fires and did not achieve the expected wood quantities. These finding were corroborated by the robust multi-criteria optimisation proposed by Gregor et al., (2022). It was found that unmanaged forest are more beneficent to biodi- versity, although this does result in a decrease in harvests. Some studies have indicated that unmanaged forests are more susceptible to drought and dieback induced by climate change (Rodrı́guez-Calcerrada et al., 2017; Petritan et al., 2021). Although forest managers are used to think in terms of longer time scales, preliminary stud- ies of ours show that this operational view is not suitable for studying the effects of climate change on the forest. With such an average productivity over time, extreme climate events were diluted over the years and had less impact on forest growth. The mean productivity was higher, while its variance was lower, but as less variability was captured, it was less relevant for the optimisation model. [SOURCE: Page_27]

4.4    Optimisation and simulations considerations The shape of Pareto front (Figure 4) is convex, as would be expected: the higher the mean, the higher the standard deviation (Hull, 2018). The pine production is risky, in their Markovitz optimisation of Landes de Gascogne region land uses, Dragicevic (2019) found that huge weigh of maritime pine in the portfolio was obtained under high variance: with a high risk is asso- ciated strong returns. [SOURCE: Page_27]

The management allocation across the domain considered is changing over time for both species suggesting it would be implausible that a constant level of production could be main- tained over the course of a century without a change in management practices. For example, in the beginning of the century, a risk-averse manager of a pine forest could easily achieve 11 m³.ha−1 .yr−1 by implementing the adaptive management regime. However, by the end of the century, the only management regime that would be able to reach this target, albeit with an increase in the standard deviation, would be classic sylvicultural practice. [SOURCE: Page_27]

The difference of absolute values of productivity between oak and pine forest can be ex- plained to some extent by the version of GO+ model. The pine simulations were obtained with a version that takes into account the nitrogen cycle. However, this version was not well calibrated for oak trees and sanctioned too heavily the forest growth in case of nitrogen stress. Consequently, a previous version, which did not include the nitrogen cycle, was used for the oak forest simulation. [SOURCE: Page_27]

It is not possible to compare the allocation of management regimes in a pine and oak for- est, as the managements are completely different. However, the adaptive management is composed of preventive thinning in both cases. This practice is primarily employed in the case of high risk aversion for pine trees, but surprisingly, it exhibits a high productivity for oak trees and is employed in the case of low risk aversion. [SOURCE: Page_27]

Another limitation of the GO+ model is that it does not allow for the differentiation be- tween sessile and pedunculate oak trees. Even if sessile and pedunculate oaks have genetic similarities, they exhibit different physiological responses and have diverse ecological prefer- ences. The pedunculate oak is a species that exhibits a nomadic post-pioneer behaviour. It precedes the establishment of the sessile oak and has been able, naturally or with the help of human, to colonise or recolonise large areas. In isolated or sparsely populated areas, the species is noted for its resilience and adaptability, with the capacity to thrive in a wide range of environmental conditions. Conversely, it becomes much more demanding in forest stands, where it has difficulty competing and requires rich soils with a constant supply of water. Con- versely, the sessile oak is more social, more resistant to summer drought, and at its best in fairly acidic environments. Sardin (2008) and Vivin et al. (1993) showed that in situation of water deficit, sessile and pedunculate oaks avoid stress following the same mechanisms. However, the mortality rate was more important for pedunculate oak than for sessile oak. Their study showed that sessile oak was better able to cope with both internal water stress and severe soil drought than pedunculate oak. In a study conducted on historical data, Breda and Badeau (2008) showed that sessile and pedunculate oaks had similar growth and climatic response between 1950 and 1970 but pedunculate oak growth start declining since a drought year, 1972. Historically, pedunculate oak knew declines where sessile oak was remaining un- affected (Macaire, 1984; Becker and Lévy, 1982). Sardin recommend the preferential use of sessile oak in highly water-constrained environments, such as those found in Alsace. [SOURCE: Page_28]

4.5    Limitations The present study employs a mathematical optimisation approach to assess the potential for forest productivity values simulated by the process-based model GO+ in response to cli- mate and management scenarios. The study is conducted with a number of hypotheses, and therefore its results are limited to this context. Firstly, the simulations are derived from a process-based model, which represents a simplified version of the natural processes by which forests grow. It should be noted that certain processes are not included in the GO+ model, despite their well-established role in forest growth. These include, e.g., nutrient limitations, mycorrhizal development, pests, windstorms or fire. Secondly, the climate scenarios utilised are not predictions, but only two of hundreds of possible future climate trajectories. In fact, the forcing data used were subjected to an evaluation against the actual climate from 2006 to 2020. This revealed that they exhibited a lack of efficacy in their representation of ex- treme temperatures and rainfall shortage. Furthermore, the management practices employed were collaboratively constructed with forest managers, yet they represent a simplified repre- sentation of real forestry practices. The results obtained and the subsequent discussion are only applicable within the aforementioned framework. The main interest and novelty of our approach lies more in the implementation of mean-variance optimisation of forest manage- ment alternatives considering future climates and silviculture over representative forest areas in France. [SOURCE: Page_28]

The GO+ model used does not consider the impact of extreme weather events, such as storms and fires, which have a significant negative effect on forest productivity. These events are known to become more frequent with climate change (Stevens-Rumann et al., 2018; Jones [SOURCE: Page_28]

23 [SOURCE: Page_28]

et al., 2022; Parks and Abatzoglou, 2020), yet they were not included in this version of GO+. However, Labarre et al. (in prep.) conducted a study on the impact of fire events on optimal allocation using a augmented version of the GO+ model. As the model does not differentiate between pedunculate and sessile oak, an improvement to the GO+ model could be achieved by dissociating the two oak species, enabling the consideration of their differing responses to water stress. This could then inform the conduct of further optimisation studies, which could be conducted over the distribution of the two oak species, depending on the precipitation and soil water of the territory considered. [SOURCE: Page_29]

The simple mean-variance method does not permit the consideration of multiple Ecosystems Services (ES) as it could be done, e.g., with robust multicriteria optimisation, or with specific constraints (Gregor et al., 2022; Bagdon et al., 2016). It is a common tool for comparing antagonists objectives across different scenarios and to identify a robust solution that would be the most satisfying one under all scenarios. It assists in navigating a framework that is characterised by profound uncertainty, including climate change, fires and storms. It is already widely used in forest science (Radke et al., 2017; Yousefpour et al., 2017; Shavazipour and Stewart, 2019). Our study has not considered the economic impact of management alterna- tives in terms of revenues from the sale of the wood or the cost of subsequent operations in the stands. Neither the human resources necessary to conduct a regime nor the costs associated with it were accounted for. This study does not aim to provide direct recommendations but rather to investigate the impact of climate change on the biological productivity. Wood is considered as a resource to be managed, despite its value on the market. [SOURCE: Page_29]

Another limitation is obviously the use of a simulated forest instead of a real one. Mod- elling is always a simplified representation of the world, but it has its benefits. In the context of climate change, the need for new solutions is clear. Modelling can be an effective tool for identifying and testing potential solutions, as it allows for the assessment of risk and ambition. It also provides a means of evaluating different ideas to identify the most suitable approach. [SOURCE: Page_29]

5    Conclusions and perspectives The objective of this study was to identify the optimal management allocation to maximise stable productivity for pine or oak forest owners from 2006 to 2100. The study examined how the allocation portfolio is modified over time in response to climate change, between areas with different precipitation patterns, according to the soil water capacity and to the risk aversion of the manager. The productivity of the forest is enhanced by heavy precipita- tion, high greenhouse gas emissions and high available water capacity. The optimal allocation varies according to climate scenarios. In the context of the RCP 4.5 scenario, forest produc- tivity is projected to increase until 2040. In contrast, under the RCP 8.5 scenario, forest productivity is projected to decrease throughout the century. With regard to pine forests, it can be seen that adaptive management represents the only management regime capable of stabilising production, whereas classic management ensures a high but unstable productivity. With regard to oak forests, even though the optimisation led to a diversification of manage- ments, it can be concluded that coppice and adaptive managements are the most selected ones. [SOURCE: Page_29]

24 [SOURCE: Page_29]

An interesting perspective would be to implement statistical meta model to study the re- lationship between the mean and the variance of the productivity overtime, according to the climate scenario, the precipitation levels, the management regime and the AWC. This meta- model would facilitate the prediction of data that were not initially modelled with GO+. As we also optimise the management regimes of oak trees with the same methodology used for pine trees, a spatial optimisation of both oak and pine on a given territory could be interesting not only for the selection of optimal management but also for the identification of the most suitable species. In this case, however, productivity will not be a relevant indicator as the quality of the wood for oak timber is more important than its quantity. Multicriteria optimi- sation could be used to take economic values or ES into account. [SOURCE: Page_30]

One of the key findings of our research was to highlight the potential interest of adaptative, dynamic sivicultural itineraries. Given that this approach has not yet been implemented, it is important to gain a deeper understanding of it. Modelling is frequently employed in the context of adaptive management, as evidenced in the review of (Reyer et al., 2015). In many cases, a lower productivity is also observed for adaptive management (Bagdon et al., 2016). This as led to the hypothesis that a change from conventional to adaptive manage- ment is inducing new trade-offs between forest ecosystems services. The primary trade-off demonstrated by Gregor et al. (2022) pertains to the reduction of harvests in favour of other ecosystem services such as increased biodiversity, climate change mitigation, and local cool- ing. Such harvest reductions and potentially rising wood demand will exert further pressure on forest ecosystems. We show that a potentially important advantage of this alternative is that it stabilises ecosystem functioning and reduces its temporal variability in a context of climatic disturbance. This conclusion is, of course, contingent on the hypothetical framework of our research, especially the climate scenarios and models used. It therefore deserves to be explored further. [SOURCE: Page_30]

As production decreases across the 21th century in our simulations, the effects of climate change are intensifying the pressure on pine and oak forests, with limited time available for them to adapt. We saw that silviculture through, e.g., the thinning and harvest practices, plays a pivotal role in the adaptation of forests to climate change and in supporting wood industries by maintaining wood production. A promising avenue for further research and de- velopment would be to explore other adaptive silvicultural practices and integrate them into anticipatory and dynamic silvicultural strategies. [SOURCE: Page_30]

Forests play a crucial role in providing environmental and economic services but face threats from cli- mate change. Adapting forest management is essential to sustain wood production in less favourable climates. The objective of this study was to identify the management strategy that will ensure a high average productivity and stable production under future climatic conditions. The mean-variance optimisation technique was employed to identify the optimal allocation of management regimes for maximum sustainable production (maximising the mean while minimising the temporal variance) over a 30-year sliding window spanning the 21st century. To compare forest management practices, data was obtained from a process-based model that projects forest growth and implements manage- ment rules under different climate scenarios. The optimisation was implemented for a theoretical spatial domain including three classes of available water capacity, and was duplicated for two case studies, the maritime pine forest and oak forest. In the high greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions sce- nario, the productivity of both species is projected to decrease over the century, while the inter-annual stability would also decrease. Conversely, in the intermediate GHG emission scenario, there is a peak of productivity in 2040, while maintaining a high level of stability over time. The Pareto fronts ob- tained demonstrate that the optimal management portfolio changes significantly over the century, depending on the climate scenario and local rainfall patterns. The results of this study indicate also that adaptive and anticipatory forest management strategies have the potential to minimise temporal variance and enhance resilience in productive forests. key words: forest management, mean-variance optimisation, climate change adaptation [SOURCE: Page_39]

L’objectif de cette étude est d’identifier la stratégie de gestion forestière qui garantira une productivité moyenne élevée et une production stable dans les conditions climatiques futures.
