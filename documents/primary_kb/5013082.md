## 7    ABSTRACT

8     Until recently, reuse models for food packaging plastics were generally considered burdensome. [SOURCE: Page_2]

9    Today, reuse is placed second in the waste management hierarchy of the plastics circular [SOURCE: Page_2]

10    economy. This work addresses, for the first time, the reuse of biobased and biodegradable [SOURCE: Page_2]

11    materials such as PHBV and PHBV-quercetin. The safety and structural integrity of the materials [SOURCE: Page_2]

12    were evaluated in two scenarios: (i) within a full reuse cycle including: food contact/detergent- [SOURCE: Page_2]

13    washing/food contact; and (ii) successive washing operations in water, detergent and NaOH [SOURCE: Page_2]

14    aqueous solutions. PHBV material that underwent the full reuse cycle using aqueous (10 % [SOURCE: Page_2]

15    ethanol) and acidic (3 % acetic acid) food simulants showed an overall migration close to zero [SOURCE: Page_2]

16    with a maintenance of its physical-chemical properties. It was also able to withstand up to [SOURCE: Page_2]

17    20 cycles of washing in water and detergent with slight changes in its intrinsic properties. Its [SOURCE: Page_2]

1 © 2023 published by Elsevier. This manuscript is made available under the CC BY NC user license https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/ [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   washing with a 1 wt% aqueous NaOH solution, for one cycle, led to the removal of its external [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   surface without altering its physical-chemical properties. The use of quercetin as additive had no [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   consequence in the first scenario (the full reuse cycle). Even the migration of a part of it (2 wt% [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   from the initial amount in the polymer) in the first contact with the food was far below the [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   admissible daily intake. However, the release of quercetin during detergent washing steps and [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   the production of acidic oxidation products during NaOH washing contributed to accelerate [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   material degradation, questioning the use of such additives for PHAs. [SOURCE: Page_3]

## 25   I. INTRODUCTION

26   Plastics have revolutionized our daily life and are in 2022 one of the most commonly used [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   materials for food packaging. Essential for food safety and security, food packaging, represents [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   the largest market use of plastics and accounts for nearly half (up to 40 %) of the plastic waste [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   generated worldwide 1-2. After being used once, most food packaging is landfilled or incinerated, [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   with all the negative effects that has on the environment^3-4. It has also been shown to slowly but [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   inexorably turn into persistent and widespread micro- and nanoparticles that have already been [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   found even in food chains within living organisms 5-6. [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   A plastics waste hierarchy that places reuse and/or recycling as the first option will maximize the [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   economic value of the polymer, extend its life cycle, and reduce the spreading of these fine [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   particles. This is also an integral part of the European and French strategies, in particular through [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   the 3R (Reduction, Reuse, recycle) decree to move towards a more sustainable plastic economy, [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   by making at least 50 % of plastic packaging reusable by 2030 7. Going further by replacing non- [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   biodegradable petroleum-based food packaging with bio-based and biodegradable materials, [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   while ensuring a closed-loop regeneration system, with the biodegradation as the ultimate carbon [SOURCE: Page_4]

40   cycle, is today the only way to overcome the disastrous impact of plastic waste pollution 8. [SOURCE: Page_4]

41   Of the currently available food contact bio-based plastics, few are biodegradable under natural [SOURCE: Page_4]

42   conditions, such as poly(hydroxyalkanoates) (PHAs) 9. PHAs are thermoplastic polyesters [SOURCE: Page_4]

43   synthesized by microorganisms under specific growth conditions 10. Depending on the carbon [SOURCE: Page_4]

44   source and the microorganism, 3-hydroxyalkanoic monomers of different lengths can be [SOURCE: Page_4]

45   combined to give rise to PHA polymers with great structural diversity^11. In recent years, they [SOURCE: Page_4]

46   have attracted the attention of scientists thanks to their properties close to those of some [SOURCE: Page_4]

47   conventional plastics 12, especially for PHBV poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate) [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   which has better physical properties (toughness, flexibility and processability) compared to other [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   PHA homo-polyesters 13. PHBV materials are authorized for food contact applications by the [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   European Plastics Regulation (EU) No 10/2011, and their inertness in liquid food simulants, has [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   already been assessed, through overall migration testing 14-15. [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   On the other hand, there is a great interest in the use of polyphenols as natural additives for PHA [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   polymers whether as thermal stabilizers, oxygen/radical scavengers or antimicrobial agents. [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   Indeed, several studies have shown that the use of polyphenols such as catechin, tannic acid and [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   lignin at a certain amount contributes to improve the thermal resistance of PHB and PHBV [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   polymers, without a clear evidence on their negative effect on the polymers biodegradability 16-18 [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   19. Gallic acid incorporated in PHBV matrix at 5 wt% acted as oxygen scavenger and antiradical [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   agent, resulting in an active food packaging 20. The inhibitory effect of PHB-vanillin films has [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   been demonstrated against bacterial and fungal food contaminants such as Escherichia coli and [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   Aspergillus parasiticus 21. Thus, the use of natural polyphenols will provide stability and new [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   functional properties to PHAs. Moreover, they will make it possible to avoid the use of certain [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   controversial synthetic additives (BHA, BHT), while guaranteeing the absence of propagation of [SOURCE: Page_5]

63   toxic compounds in the environment during the biodegradation of materials. [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   Despite a growing demand for biobased and biodegradable materials in the food packaging [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   sector, the management of post-consumer materials is far from satisfactory. Indeed, when they [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   exist, these wastes are not even recovered in industrial composting installations, because they [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   cannot be assimilated to biowaste, and are rather directed towards incineration or landfill. The [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   landfilling of PHAs does not have negative consequences for the environment, as they are [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   biodegradable under natural conditions. However, these polymers are still expensive (compared [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   to other polymers), and their reuse and recycling can certainly prevent the use of the resource, [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   thus promoting their increasing exploitation. [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   For the first time, the circularity of biodegradable packaging waste is addressed. The objective of [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   this paper is to evaluate the ability of PHBV-based materials to be reused as food packaging, in [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   terms of consumer safety, material stability and environmental impacts. A first study conducted [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   in our laboratory showed that up to 50 dishwashing cycles, the thermomechanical and structural [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   properties of PHBV are mainly maintained, with an overall migration below the 10 mg. dm-2 [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   limit (established by the EU committee)22. To further investigate, this paper focuses on the [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   evolution of structural and physicochemical properties of PHBV and PHBV-quercetin (PHBV- [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   Q) materials, as well as their safety under different reuse conditions. The scientific findings of [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   this paper might be used by industry players or associations involved in the collection, cleaning [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   and reuse of plastic food packaging. [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   Quercetin is a secondary plant metabolite mainly present as quercetin glycoside (conjugated to a [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   sugar residue) 23. Although showing a mutagenic effect on bacteria in vitro, it has been shown to [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   be non-mutagenic, carcinogenic or reproductive in vivo. This is mainly because absorbed [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   quercetin is rapidly and efficiently metabolized into non-mutagenic derivatives 24. Used as an [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   additive for LLDPE and PHBV, it helped protect the polymers from oxidative and thermal [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   degradation during processing 19, 25. [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   The reuse of PHBV and PHBV-Q materials will first be simulated through a full reuse cycle, [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   including food contact/washing/food contact cycle, in the presence of different food simulants. [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   Then, successive washing cycles (up to 20) with water and detergent will be applied. The plastic [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   pre-treatment before mechanical recycling process will be carried out by means of a deep [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   cleaning with a NaOH solution that is often used for PET in order to peel the polymer surface in [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   which the contaminants are accumulated 26. [SOURCE: Page_6]

## 94   II. MATERIAL AND METHODS

95   II.1 Materials [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   PHBV powder was purchased from Natureplast (Ifs, France) under the reference PHI003. As [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   reported by the supplier, it contains 1-3 mol% of valerate and has a true density of 1.24 g.cm-3. [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   Chemicals, Boron Nitride (98 %), quercetin (95 %), sodium hydroxide (98 %), crotonic acid [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   (98 %) and methanol (99.8 %) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich France. [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   Acetic acid and ethanol were provided by Sigma Aldrich with a purity > 99 % and were used to [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   prepare the food simulants: aqueous 3 % (w/v) acetic acid, and 10 %(v/v) and 95 % (v/v) [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   ethanol. [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   Dishwasher detergent (5-15 % oxygen bleach, < 5 % polycarboxylates, non-ionic surface agent, [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   phosphonates) from SUN® was used to prepare a 0.3 wt% washing solution. [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   II.2 Preparation of the PHBV/quercetin films [SOURCE: Page_7]

106   Before thermal processing, PHBV and quercetin powders were dried at 60 °C for 24 h, and [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   boron nitride (0.5 wt%) was added to PHBV as a nucleating agent. Then, the resulting powder [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   was pre-mixed with quercetin at 1 wt%, in a powder blender (Turbula T10B, Laboservices, [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   Montpellier, France). The mixture was extruded in a lab scale Thermo Scientific Process 11 [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   Twin-Screw Extruder (ThermoScientific, Karlsruhe, Germany), equipped with a L/D ratio of 40, [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   a screw diameter of 11 mm, and a 3 mm rod die. The temperature profile set at 180 °C was [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   uniform from the feeding to the die and the screw profile was the one described in Vandi [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   et al. 27. The screw speed was set at 200 rpm, and the resident time was approximately 2 min. [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   After pelitization, PHBV/quercetin blend was transformed into thin films of 12x^12 cm and [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   300 µm thickness, using heated hydraulic press (20T, Pinette Emidecau Indutries, Chalon-sur- [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   Saône, France). Pellets were heated at 180 °C during 5 min and then pressed between Teflon [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   coated plates by regularly increasing the pressure from 5 bar to 150 bar, over 7 minutes. Films [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   were cooled down at room temperature during 10 minutes. Then, they were left for stabilization [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   during 14 days at 50 % of humidity. PHBV films with 1 wt% of quercetin were named PHBV-Q. [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   Virgin PHBV films without additives were prepared in the same way. Before each [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   experimentation, polymer films were dried at 60 °C for 24 h. [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   II.3 Migration into food simulants [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   (3 % (w/v) acetic acid) corresponding to food with hydrophilic character with a pH below 4.5, [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   and food simulant A (10 % (v/v) ethanol) corresponding to food with hydrophilic character [SOURCE: Page_8]

128   (European Standard EN 10/2011) 28. Migration studies were conducted in triplicate at 40 °C, [SOURCE: Page_8]

129   over 10 days (EN 10/2011 regulation for long time storage). Five 2.8 cm diameter polymer discs, [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   mounted on a glass rod, were double-side immersed in 100 mL of food simulant (total area to [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   volume around 6 dm^2.L-1) (scheme S$_1$, supplementary data). Material degradation and migration [SOURCE: Page_8]

## 215   III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

216   III.1 Stability of PHBV and PHBV-Q films after one cycle of reuse [SOURCE: Page_12]

217   One cycle of reuse corresponds to: contact with food simulants, washing with detergent, [SOURCE: Page_12]

218   followed by further food contact (After each step, the material was dried for 24 h). Three food [SOURCE: Page_12]

219   simulants were selected to represent hydrophilic, amphiphilic and lipophilic food: simulant A [SOURCE: Page_12]

220   (10 % ethanol) can simulate some cheese preserved in aqueous medium like feta or mozzarella; [SOURCE: Page_12]
