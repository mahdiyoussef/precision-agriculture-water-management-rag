25   For sustainable food production in the Mekong Delta, local information on irrigation status is essential for [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   allocating water resources efficiently at the community level. ALOS-2 PALSAR-2 L-band SAR can be used to [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   detect submerged and nonsubmerged soil covered by rice plants at a spatial resolution compatible with field [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   observation but a low temporal resolution. In this study, a new multiscale data assimilation technique is developed [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   to estimate the spatiotemporal dynamics of field water levels at a temporal resolution appropriate to inform [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   decision-making on when to initiate irrigation. The method employs an irrigation model based on parameters [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   representing farmers’ irrigation practices. ALOS-2 PALSAR-2 data are used to derive the hydrological [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   parameters of the model, including an irrigation parameter representing how deep the field water level dropped [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   until the next round of irrigation was initiated. We developed observation operators for the soil submersion status [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   under vegetation and spatial submersion percentages instead of assimilating soil moisture products. The study [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   uses ALOS-2 PALSAR-2 data (25-100 m spatial resolution, approximately every 42 days) over the Mekong Delta [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   and experimental data collected in situ for model initialization and validation. The estimated irrigation parameter [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   controlling the maximum depth of field water level showed spatio-temporal consistency with the [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   ground-observed value (RMSE=4.24 cm). The values of the parameters also showed spatial consistency            with [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   respect to   DEM data; he paddies with low irrigation model parameter values were prone to be located in [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   low-elevation zones (<2 m), whereas high model parameters values tended to be located in paddies in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   high-elevation zones (>3 m).The results show promising applications using L-band SAR observations for [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

3 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   The objective of this study is to develop a SAR-based monitoring system that provides daily paddy field water [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   level data for irrigation management, required in the simulation of GHG emissions and rice productivity. In Asia, [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   rice is the most important staple food, providing an average 32% of total calorie uptake (Maclean et al., 2002). [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   About 90% of the global paddy area and annual output of rice production are concentrated in monsoonal Asia [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   (FAO, 2020). Approximately 75% of global rice production is produced in irrigated lowlands encompassing delta [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   basins in Asia, such as the Mekong, Irrawaddy, Chao Phraya, and Bengal Rivers (Barker et al., 1985; Maclean et [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   al., 2002). To meet the increasing food demand derived from global population growth, rice cropping in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   region is becoming more intensive with the use of double/triple rice cropping techniques (FAO, 2020). However, [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   rice production requires large amounts of water (3,000-5,000 L kg-1 rice, IRRI 2001) and has become a major [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   source of the potent greenhouse gas methane, approximately 11% of anthropogenic methane emissions come from [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   rice paddy submerged soils (IPCC 2013). Therefore, water-saving irrigation practices that have the potential to [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   mitigate methane emissions by oxidizing the soil environment, such as alternate wetting and drying (AWD), are [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   desirable for dissemination in this region to ensure sustainable water demand while lowering greenhouse gas [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   emissions. [SOURCE: Page_3]

60        AWD is an intermittent irrigation technique to lower the amount of water used for irrigation without a [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   significant loss of rice productivity (Bouman et al., 2007, Lampayan et al., 2015). This approach consists of [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   temporarily suspending irrigation until the paddy soil becomes dry/oxidized. Initially, irrigation water is applied [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   to obtain flooded conditions (5-10 cm of water above the soil surface). Then, after a certain number of days have [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   passed, the field water level drops below the soil surface. The next irrigation is scheduled before the soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   becomes dry. However, the perception of soil dryness and consequently the number of days between 2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   irrigations varies widely among farmers and sites (from 1 day to more than 10 days) depending on the soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

4 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   texture (clay/silt/sand content), percolation rate, precipitation and evaporation (Bouman et al., 2007, Lampayan [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   et al., 2015). Various soil drought indexes have been used for scheduling irrigation (Bouman et al., 2007); for [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   example, the soil matric potential is used to define the value of the soil drought index as the irrigation criterion. [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   Scientists have also advocated for the use of the farmer-determined water level has also been suggested (hereafter [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   referred to as Dbefore_irrigation, i.e., how deep the field water level drops before the next round of irrigation is [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   initiated). This specific water depth has been widely used by policy makers regarding irrigation activity, as rice [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   farmers can easily monitor this value (Bouman et al., 2007; Rejesus et al., 2011). To define Dbefore_irrigation, several [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   field experiments have been conducted in Asia (e.g., Arai et al., 2021; Belder et al., 2004; Bouman & Tuong., [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   2001; Lampayan et al., 2015). These studies report that increasing the interval between rounds of irrigations to [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   allow the field water level to drop to deeper soil layers is a promising technique to save water, reduce methane [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   emissions and increase rice yield in the Mekong Delta (Arai, 2022). ‘Irrigation when the field water level has [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   leached 15-20 cm below the soil” is the criterion suggested by the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI). [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   In Vietnam, in particular the Mekong delta, highly intensive cropping system was developed based on the [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   construction and upgrading of a multilevel dike system, allowing double and triple rice crop per year. The AWD [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   practices have also been adopted in certain regions as a water saving regulation. [SOURCE: Page_4]

82         However, despite the wide adoption of AWD, the values of Dbefore_irrigation differ by location (Ishido et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   2016; Taminato&Matsubara 2016). Most importantly, the adoption of AWD by farmers is constrained by [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   several social/infrastructural factors. These factors include distance from the irrigation canal/drainage, density of [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   subcanals inside the dikes, and location of pump ownership (Yamaguchi et al., 2017). At such community scale [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   (i.e., 10-100m spatial resolution with daily temporal resolution), heterogeneity is caused by nonsynchronized [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   intermittent drainage in adjacent rice paddies, in land surface elevations and in cropping calendars (Dang et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

5 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   2016; Evers & Benedikter 2009, Miyashita et al., 2016; Sakamoto et al., 2006, 2007). Other natural factors arise, [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   such as irregular daily field water level dynamics of the irrigation canal due to ebbs and flows of tides [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   (Wassmann et al., 2004). [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   These heterogeneous water management practices cause difficulties in monitoring/verifying mitigation measures [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   operations (e.g., AWD). Such monitoring is required for irrigation management, such as allocation of [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   irrigation/drainage canals and pumping apparatuses for irrigation/drainage schedule synchronization, [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   opening/closing schedules of water gates attached to each dike surrounding rice paddies, dike heightening, [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   selection of paddies that require field water level observations, etc. [SOURCE: Page_5]

96        In this context, there is a need to monitor the irrigation status [i.e., field water level dynamics and farmers’ [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   irrigation practices (e.g., Dbefore_irrigation)] to evaluate the actual dissemination status of water-saving irrigation [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   activities by each stakeholder and verify the longevity of already implemented mitigation measures. To address [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   this issue, various studies have been conducted using remote sensing techniques to evaluate the soil submersion [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   status. Using optical data, the rice paddy covered with water can be distinguished at the beginning of the season [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   based on the reflectance of the water surface. The method often uses the normalized difference water index [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   (NDWI) and the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) from remote sensing images to depict the [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   differences between rice paddies and nonrice paddy areas (Zhang et al., 2020). However, as soon as the rice [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   paddy is covered by vegetation, it is no longer possible to detect flooded and nonflooded paddy fields. Using [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   SAR data with penetration capability, soil submersion/nonsubmersion is expected to be detectable in rice [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   paddies for a longer period during the rice growth stage, depending on the SAR wavelength. Using C-band SAR [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   data, Lam-Dao et al. (2009) reported that soil submersion could be detected up to 20 days after sowing using the [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   HH backscattering intensity of Advanced Synthetic Aperture Radar (ASAR). Using longer-wavelength SAR [SOURCE: Page_5]

6 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   data with deeper penetration in the vegetation layer, Arai et al. (2018) demonstrated the performance of [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   quadruple/dual-polarimetric L-band SAR (ALOS-2 PALSAR-2: Advanced Land Observing Satellite-2/Phased [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   Array type L-band Synthetic Aperture Radar-2) data to distinguish submerged paddies and nonsubmerged [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   paddies across all rice growth stages. However, the high resolution polarimetric images cover limited area (40 km [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   swath) and irregular temporal frequency. For larger region, ScanSAR data are more adapted. However, the 42-day [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   repeat cycle by which PALSAR-2 ScanSAR data are acquired is not sufficient to monitor the temporal dynamics [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   of paddy field water levels. Future L-band SAR missions, such as ALOS-4, ROSE-L and NISAR, will have 14- [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   and 12-day repeat cycles, respectively, will be still not sufficient for temporal monitoring. Many hydrological [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   simulation studies (e.g., Al-Yaari et al., 2014; Ines et al., 2013; Montzka et al., 2011) applying data assimilation [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   (hereafter, assimilation) have used high temporal resolution passive microwave radiometer data [e.g., Soil [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   Moisture and Ocean Salinity (SMOS), in which the L-band (1.4 GHz) data are collected every 3 days]. However, [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   their spatial resolution of 20-50 km is not adapted to the heterogeneity of the rice paddies in Asia. In this regard, it [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   is very important to develop a technique using high spatial resolution SAR data at low temporal resolution to [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   simulate the temporal dynamics of the irrigation status of rice paddies. [SOURCE: Page_6]

123        The objective of this study is to develop a system that provides daily field water level data for irrigation [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   management at the community scale. The starting point is the use of ALOS-2 PALSAR-2 data which provide [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   pixel-based information on submersion status of paddy fields every 42 days. We prepared a model simulating [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   field water level dynamics, based on knowledge of the hysteresis of the soil water retention curve and our [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   long-term ground observation data.      and we use the SAR data to optimize model parameters. To estimate the [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   values of Dbefore_irrigation at the community scale, we assimilate the backscattering coefficient of the ALOS-2 [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   PALSAR-2 data by preparing a pixel-based observation operator to detect the submersion status of paddy fields. [SOURCE: Page_6]

7 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   To solve the filter divergence issues that become critical due to the low temporal resolution of satellite [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   observations, we designed a focal statistics-based observation operator to evaluate the temporal submersion [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   percentage and the spatial submersion percentage in a focal analysis window. [SOURCE: Page_7]

134    2. Methodology [SOURCE: Page_7]

135        In field water level simulation at local scale, soil properties and farmers’ irrigation practices need to be [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   considered. Unfortunately, most models simulating hydraulic states of agricultural soils require the field water [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   level data as “forcing” input data (e.g., “depth of water retention layer” as input data of DNDC-rice model: [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   Fumoto et al., 2008; Soil, Water, Atmosphere and Plant model: Govindarajan et al., 2008). The difficulty is [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   mainly due to large uncertainties linked to anthropogenic activities (e.g., irrigation/drainage schedule, [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   management of tidal irrigation dikes, cropping calendars). In addition, perturbation of the ground water table [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   caused by irregular dynamics of tidal movement of the canals also contribute to uncertainty (Arai et al., 2022a; [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   Hong Van et al., 2014). However, because most paddy soils over the delta are heavy clay soils characterized by [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   a low percolation rate (Dang et al., 2016; Evers & Benedikter 2009, Ishido et al., 2016), the soil-matric potential [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   is expected to be the major factor controlling the states of soil water energy. Therefore, it is expected that [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   realistically simulating irrigation status is still possible by parameterizing these local soil properties with ground [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   observation data relating on field waterlevel dynamics. The model is then run by assimilating SAR data [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   simultaneously to adaptatively optimize the parameters representing irrigation practices through the simulation [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   on every pixel. [SOURCE: Page_7]

149        As the common water management practice in the region, soil water saturation was maintained for one [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   week after sowing, and the paddies were then flooded for one week to prevent weed germination. Irrigation [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   practices consist in supplying water to a level of approximately 5 cm above the soil. In continuous flooding (CF) [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   paddies, irrigation is repeated to maintain the 5 cm water level. In the AWD paddies, irrigation was halted 2 [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   weeks after sowing until the next fertilization time or until the water levels in the paddies dropped to a 15-cm [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   depth.. During the flowering period (10 days from the heading date, the first day when 5% of the total number of [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   spikes have headed), the field was maintained under continuous flooding to prevent spikelet sterility. Finally, [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   most paddies were drained 2-3 weeks before the rice is harvested. [SOURCE: Page_7]

8 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_8]

157        To realize stable L-band SAR data assimilation for field water level simulations and parameter [SOURCE: Page_8]

158   optimization, several difficulties derived from the unique characteristics of ALOS-2 PALSAR-2 ScanSAR [SOURCE: Page_8]

159   observations at 42-day observation repeat must be resolved: (1) the need to consider local irrigation activities (2) [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   the low temporal resolution of satellite data (3) the need to estimate field water level for each pixel, 4) the [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   development of a robust uncertainty quantification scheme. [SOURCE: Page_8]

162        To satisfy these conditions, the data assimilation system development was conducted following 5 key [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   steps: (1) preparing a high spatiotemporal resolution model and its SAR data assimilation module,; (2) [SOURCE: Page_8]

164   developing observation operators for soil submersion maps derived from L-band SAR data to update not only [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   relatively short-term “fast” state variables (i.e., field water level in this study) but also long-term “slow” model [SOURCE: Page_8]

166   parameters representing farmers’ irrigation practices; (3) applying a local particle filter with an adaptive model [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   parameter optimization scheme (4) comparison of the estimated model parameters and updated state variables [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   with ground-observed data at each region of interest (ROI) . A flowchart of this study is illustrated in Figure 1. [SOURCE: Page_8]

227   2.2. Preparation of a model simulating field water level [SOURCE: Page_11]

12 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_12]

228        To convert SAR based soil submersion data into information related to field water level and irrigation [SOURCE: Page_12]

229   activities through the data assimilation, we designed an irrigation model whose parameters are adaptatively [SOURCE: Page_12]

230   optimized by the SAR data. [SOURCE: Page_12]

231        For the local-scale simulation with large ensemble numbers without sacrificing the high spatial resolution [SOURCE: Page_12]

232   of SAR data, we designed a model with low degrees of freedom for the low-cost and flexible data assimilation [SOURCE: Page_12]

233   system. The advantages of this irrigation model are as follows: (1) all model parameters are updatable through [SOURCE: Page_12]

234   data assimilation with up-to-date satellite data or optimizable locally with ground observation data using [SOURCE: Page_12]

235   hierarchical Bayesian schemes with Monte Carlo methods; (2) the major factors controlling the field water level [SOURCE: Page_12]

236   simulation are considered, including anthropogenic activities (i.e., irrigation timing/duration and drainage) as [SOURCE: Page_12]

237   the model parameters; and (3) the adaptive simulation of hydrological status with a carbon-nitrogen [SOURCE: Page_12]

238   biogeochemical models. [SOURCE: Page_12]

239        The irrigation model simulates the field water level lowering dynamics, designed as a logit function-like [SOURCE: Page_12]

280   2.3 Data assimilation scheme with a local particle filter [SOURCE: Page_14]

281        The local particle filter is an advanced particle filtering technique based on the philosophy of the [SOURCE: Page_14]

282   localization scheme of the local ensemble transform Kalman filter (Hunt et al., 2007). “Particle” has the same [SOURCE: Page_14]

283   meaning with “ensemble member” of ensemble Kalman filter (i.e., multiple simulation with perturbations on [SOURCE: Page_14]

284   variables or model parameters). The technique is implemented to decrease the number of required ensembles for [SOURCE: Page_14]

285   high-dimensional systems (Potthast et al., 2019). To stabilize the assimilation system by avoiding the occurrence [SOURCE: Page_14]

286   of filter divergence, we developed a dual-scale data assimilation scheme by implementing a focal analysis [SOURCE: Page_14]

287   scheme as the spatial localization into the particle filtering system. The scheme allows us to assimilate not only [SOURCE: Page_14]

288   the instantaneous state variable but also temporally cumulative state variables. A four-dimensional filtering [SOURCE: Page_14]

289   technique with temporal localization is implemented as described in Hunt et al. (2004). The window length is set [SOURCE: Page_14]

290   with 3 sequential observation datasets in the same observation window. Each observation is assimilated twice [SOURCE: Page_14]

291   during the 2 sequential observation filter cycles (hereafter, repetitive cycling) to compensate for the low temporal [SOURCE: Page_14]

292   localization of SAR datasets. Initial states in each ensemble were prepared by 2 years of spin-up running with [SOURCE: Page_14]

293   multiplied random values for Dbefore_irrigation (mean: -15 cm; standard deviation: 7.5 cm) and Hafter_irrigation (mean: 5 [SOURCE: Page_14]

294   cm, standard deviation: 2.5 cm) [SOURCE: Page_14]

295        Field-based simulations of the water level in paddies are difficult to conduct due to heterogeneity among [SOURCE: Page_14]

296   adjacent paddies, as stated in the introduction. However, knowing the spatial percentage of the submerged area, [SOURCE: Page_14]

297   it is possible to derive useful a priori information related to the soil submersion probability from satellite [SOURCE: Page_14]

298   observation data, and the temporal percentage represents the historical information at the pixel level (Aires, [SOURCE: Page_14]

299   2020). Field water level differences among adjacent rice paddies influence the reduction rate of the field water [SOURCE: Page_14]

300   level (Janssen & Lennartz B, 2008,2009; Tuong et al., 1994). In particular, the water balance of rice paddies in [SOURCE: Page_14]

301   lowland areas is affected by the net lateral water flow (Tsubo et al., 2007) and social/infrastructural (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_14]

302   thickness/height of dykes or paddy bunds) factors. Arai et al. (2018) also reported that a large part of the paddies [SOURCE: Page_14]

303   in the Vietnamese Mekong Delta are continuously submerged during the rainy season. Once the large paddies in [SOURCE: Page_14]

304   the dike system are flooded, farmers need to pump out the water for several days to prepare the land for rice [SOURCE: Page_14]

305   cropping. Conversely, once the soil emerges for long periods of time during the dry season in this region, the [SOURCE: Page_14]

306   soil structure tends to shrink, and the water holding capacity decreases with an increased percolation rate [SOURCE: Page_14]

307   compared with swelling soil in continuously submerged paddies. Therefore, in the dry season, large areas of [SOURCE: Page_14]

308   paddies were detected as non submerged. Based on these findings, we designed the focal statistics-based [SOURCE: Page_14]

309   observation operator by assuming that the spatial submersion percentage in the focal analysis window (Figure 1) [SOURCE: Page_14]

15 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_15]

310   and temporal submersion percentage of a pixel located at the center of the focal analysis window are positively [SOURCE: Page_15]

311   correlated (Cf Figure 1, middle right illustration). [SOURCE: Page_15]

514   Development of the observation operator for SAR data assimilation by developing inundation classification [SOURCE: Page_33]

515   methodology [SOURCE: Page_33]

516        Inundation was classified with following 3 states as inundated paddies, semi-inundated paddies, and [SOURCE: Page_33]

517   non-inundated paddies regarding the difference of field water level. In the studied clay soil, field water level at 0 [SOURCE: Page_33]

518   cm of the soil surface and -5 cm below the soil surface were differentiated by the L-band microwave signals. [SOURCE: Page_33]

519   This indicated that L-band microwave can penetrate shallow soil surface to approximately -5cm below soil [SOURCE: Page_33]

520   surface. Since semi-inundated paddies was characterized by higher volume scattering and dipole scattering from [SOURCE: Page_33]

521   rice plants (Figure 4e), these semi-double bounce derived from the interaction between ground water table and [SOURCE: Page_33]

522   root/stem scattering pattern was able to be detected. Though this penetration depth in the soil would be highly [SOURCE: Page_33]

523   depending on the soil types, dielectric properties controlling states such as the soil moisture and electrical [SOURCE: Page_33]

524   conductivity in the soil surface layer, it still has the potential to provide the information of irrigation activities [SOURCE: Page_33]

525   such as AWD implementation status. Further development differentiating semi-inundated paddies and [SOURCE: Page_33]

526   non-inundated paddies is expected, so that the SAR based application can provide information supporting the [SOURCE: Page_33]

527   local scale decision making on irrigation activities more specifically in future. Non inundated paddies were [SOURCE: Page_33]

528   distinguished by the 7 components polarimetric decomposition from semi-inundated/inundated paddies with [SOURCE: Page_33]

529   only 1.3% cross-validation error. Although Arai et al. (2018) also reported similar results based on the [SOURCE: Page_33]

530   Freeman–Durden method, this 7-component experiment exceeded their classification performance, with only [SOURCE: Page_33]

531   2.2% error using only 2 scattering components (single scattering and double bounce). More importantly, we [SOURCE: Page_33]

532   found that semi-inundation and noninundation were distinguished by only 1.7% error based on the 2 indexes [SOURCE: Page_33]

533   [i.e., volume/(volume+OD+CD+MD) and odd/(odd+double)]. However, distinguishing inundated paddies from [SOURCE: Page_33]

534   semi-inundated/noninundated paddies was carried out with a relatively high cross-validation error (12.2%). This [SOURCE: Page_33]

535   finding indicated that classification between inundation and semi-inundation remains a difficult problem. [SOURCE: Page_33]

536   Although classification between semi-inundation and inundation was accomplished with relatively low error [SOURCE: Page_33]

537   (7.3%) during 0-20 days after the sowing and fallow period, the cross-validation error was prone to become [SOURCE: Page_33]

538   greater in later rice growing stages (Appendix F, Table S$_4$). It was interesting to note that non-3components [SOURCE: Page_33]

539   scattering intensities. (i.e., volume+OD+CD+MD) showed a potential to detect semi-inundated paddies ( -5 [SOURCE: Page_33]

540   <WL < 0 cm below the soil surface, Figure 4e). Since methane emission in this region showed most closed [SOURCE: Page_33]

541   relationship with the inundation period with -5 cm below soil surface (Arai, 2022), this information has a [SOURCE: Page_33]

542   potential to evaluate regional methane emission sophisticatedly in further development. [SOURCE: Page_33]

34 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_34]

543        Consistently, HH&HV σ0 values, such as in ScanSAR mode, also showed relatively worse performance in [SOURCE: Page_34]

544   classifying inundation and semi-inundation (8.7%-29.5%) due to the difficulty in distinguishing the difference in [SOURCE: Page_34]

545   ground-volume interaction between inundated paddies and semi-inundated paddies. The cross-validation error of [SOURCE: Page_34]

546   distinguishing inundated paddies from semi-inundated/non inundated paddies based on HH/HV σ0 values became [SOURCE: Page_34]

547   large, particularly during 21-60 days after the sowing period (18.2%; Appendix F, Table S$_4$). To detecting [SOURCE: Page_34]

548   inundation in this middle rice growing stages properly, polarimetric decomposition with the quadruple [SOURCE: Page_34]

549   polarimetric data had the good performance. According to the HH/HV 2D plot (Figure 6d-f), HH σ0 values were [SOURCE: Page_34]

550   not significantly differentiated between inundated paddies and noninundated paddies. However, HV σ0 values [SOURCE: Page_34]

551   tended to be greater in non inundated paddies than in inundated paddies during this stage due to the limitation of [SOURCE: Page_34]

552   HV signal’s dynamic range (Figure 6d). This finding indicates that differences in volume diffusion, helix [SOURCE: Page_34]

553   scattering or dipole scattering between inundated paddies and noninundated paddies, particularly during 21-60 [SOURCE: Page_34]

554   days after the sowing period, are important classification criteria that limit the performance of inundation [SOURCE: Page_34]

555   classification performance. Since the incidence angle at the center of each scene of the quadruple observation data [SOURCE: Page_34]

556   varied among the observation orbit-path from 27 to 37 degree in this study, further analysis with more ground data [SOURCE: Page_34]

557   considering local incidence angle is desirable particularly for inundation detection of rice paddies whose rice age [SOURCE: Page_34]

558   is 20-60 days after sowing. Further advancement of dual/quadruple polarimetric analytical methods to provide [SOURCE: Page_34]

559   more precise signatures from HH-HV signals is also expected to overcome this limiting factor. For future [SOURCE: Page_34]

560   application use, observation error modelling based on the distance between σ0 values and the inundation [SOURCE: Page_34]

561   classifying hyper-plane, which considers local incidence angle and the days after sowing will be required to [SOURCE: Page_34]

562   improve the data assimilation performance with multi-type SAR datasets (e.g., ALOS-4, NISAR, ROSE-L, [SOURCE: Page_34]

563   Sentinel-1). [SOURCE: Page_34]

565   4.2. Seasonal dynamics of Dbefore_irrigation in semidike systems and spatial dynamics of Dbefore_irrigation in full-dike [SOURCE: Page_34]

566   systems [SOURCE: Page_34]

567        At the site-A, the irregular variation in the diurnal canal water level dynamics, which would influence the [SOURCE: Page_34]

568   ground water level in the paddies, indicated the difficulty of creating 2-dimensional simulations of the paddy [SOURCE: Page_34]

569   field water level due to the difficulty of reproducing the influence of the ebb and flow of tides on the simulation. [SOURCE: Page_34]

570   During the dry season from January to June, when it hardly rained and the water level of the irrigation canal [SOURCE: Page_34]

571   significantly decreased, few soil submersion events were detected in the paddies (Figure 4a, d), and relatively [SOURCE: Page_34]

35 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_35]

572   low values of field water level (Figure 4e, h) and Dbefore_irrigation (Figure 4i, l) were updated. As the precipitation [SOURCE: Page_35]

573   rate and water level of the irrigation canal tended to increase, more submerged paddy pixels were detected, and [SOURCE: Page_35]

574   high values of field water level and Dbefore_irrigation were updated (Figure 4g, k). Although there was a relatively [SOURCE: Page_35]

575   large discrepancy between the estimated values of Dbefore_irrigation and the ground-observed values of the [SOURCE: Page_35]

576   temporally local minimum field water level at the beginning stage of data assimilation, the discrepancy tended [SOURCE: Page_35]

577   to diminish as the prior spread adjusted to the true states through the data assimilation cycles (Figure 6c). The [SOURCE: Page_35]

578   Dbefore_irrigation value became relatively low in the annually driest cropping season [spring-summer cropping [SOURCE: Page_35]

579   season (SS) from March to May] and high in the annually wettest season [summer-autumn cropping season [SOURCE: Page_35]

580   (SA) from June to September] (Figure 4, 6c). These results indicated that the Dbefore_irrigation estimated by the local [SOURCE: Page_35]

581   particle filter has the potential to regionally evaluate the dissemination status of a safe-AWD (Lampayan et al., [SOURCE: Page_35]

582   2015; Rejesus et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_35]

583        In contrast to the site-A with a semidike system, seasonal dynamics were negligible at the site-B with a [SOURCE: Page_35]

584   full-dike system (Figures 9, and 10). The range of the estimated Dbefore_irrigation was shorter in the full-dike sites [SOURCE: Page_35]

585   than that at the site-A with a semidike system, whose estimated values were widely scattered from [SOURCE: Page_35]

586   approximately -27.6 cm to +1.0 cm, because the values are year-round stable in a full-dike system. However, [SOURCE: Page_35]

587   dynamic spatial differences in the Dbefore_irrigation values were found at the sites with full-dike systems [SOURCE: Page_35]

588   (Appendix-H. Figure S$_4$), in contrast to those at the site-A (Figure 7). As indicated by the ground-observed field [SOURCE: Page_35]

589   water level at the site-B, the field water level also tended to be greater in continuously submerged paddies than [SOURCE: Page_35]

590   in intermittently drained paddies, as was the case for the estimated values of Dbefore_irrigation (Appendix-I. Figure [SOURCE: Page_35]

591   S$_5$). The field water level was mostly greater than 10 cm above the soil surface in continuously [SOURCE: Page_35]

592   submersion-prone paddies, although the irrigation height is recommended as 5 cm to implement safe-AWD [SOURCE: Page_35]

593   (Lampayan et al., 2015; Rejesus et al., 2011) in this region. Related to this result, a significant influence of the [SOURCE: Page_35]

594   spatial distribution of infrastructural properties (altitude of the soil surface and distance from the irrigation [SOURCE: Page_35]

595   canal) on the AWD adoption rate has been reported at the targeted sites (e.g., Miyashita et al., 2016; Yamaguchi [SOURCE: Page_35]

596   et al., 2019). These results indicate that the areas with a high Dbefore_irrigation as estimated by the local particle filter [SOURCE: Page_35]

597   also exhibit submersion-prone factors, which is consistent among all cropping seasons. Compared with the [SOURCE: Page_35]

598   site-A, a relatively high annual methane emission reduction rate by the implementation of AWD to lower the [SOURCE: Page_35]

599   field water level and dry the soil has been reported at the site-B [methane emission reduction rate: site-B: [SOURCE: Page_35]

600   49-74% (Taminato & Matsubara, 2016); site-At: 31.5-66.0% (Arai, 2022)]. The spatial contrast illustrated by [SOURCE: Page_35]

601   the pixels with both high and low values of the estimated Dbefore_irrigation among all cropping seasons might [SOURCE: Page_35]

36 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_36]

602   indicate that the impact on the spatial distribution of land hydraulic behavior as affected by (or affects) the field [SOURCE: Page_36]

603   water management status. The spatial distribution of field water levels and Dbefore_irrigation updated by the local [SOURCE: Page_36]

604   particle filter resulted in linearly shaped borders between soil-submerged clusters (Figure 7e-l, Figure S4e-l), [SOURCE: Page_36]

605   with distributions consistent with the location of subcanals for drainage and irrigation. Notably, the updated [SOURCE: Page_36]

606   field water levels and Dbefore_irrigation tended to be lower along the subcanals. This result indicated that the [SOURCE: Page_36]

607   dual-scale assimilation technique can detect the impacts of social factors at the community scale while also [SOURCE: Page_36]

608   considering lateral water seepage/percolation, even when the allocation/distribution of social factors and lateral [SOURCE: Page_36]

609   flow was not included in the simulation. At the site-B, a relatively large discrepancy between the estimated [SOURCE: Page_36]

610   Dbefore_irrigation and the local minimum field water level of the ROI was detected when intermittent drainage was [SOURCE: Page_36]

611   conducted during the assimilation time. The reason for this difference might be due to the relatively intense side [SOURCE: Page_36]

612   flow, which rapidly decreases the field water level, and the results indicated that the estimation accuracy [SOURCE: Page_36]

613   deteriorates if intense side flow with drainage occurs on the satellite observation date. To reduce the bias, [SOURCE: Page_36]

614   frequent state updates with more frequent observation data are desirable (e.g., ALOS-4, NISAR, ROSE-L). [SOURCE: Page_36]

615   Furthermore, the presentation of a local particle filter system assimilating new L-band SAR observation [SOURCE: Page_36]

616   techniques with higher spatiotemporal resolution is expected to delineate finer spatial distributions of social [SOURCE: Page_36]

617   factors and improve the system from community-scale evaluations to individual farming-scale evaluations. [SOURCE: Page_36]

619   4.3. Performance of the parameter estimation in the Mekong Delta [SOURCE: Page_36]

620        The validation of the estimated Dbefore_irrigation model parameter showed a 1-to-1 relationship (Figure 9) with [SOURCE: Page_36]

621   the ground-observed value of the temporally local minimum water level irrespective of the differences in [SOURCE: Page_36]

622   observation sites, although some biased plots were found at the sites-B&C. At the ROI at the site-B, the local [SOURCE: Page_36]

623   minimum field water level was mostly higher than the soil surface in the paddies that were prone to continuous [SOURCE: Page_36]

624   submersion (Figure S$_5$). In such paddies, it was difficult for the assimilation system to adequately differentiate [SOURCE: Page_36]

625   the values of Dbefore_irrigation when the values were greater than 0 cm (Figures 10 and 11). This is because of the [SOURCE: Page_36]

626   limitation of observation operators’ sensitivities, which evaluate only the difference between the soil submersion [SOURCE: Page_36]

627   and nonsubmersion statuses (i.e., they are still unable to directly observe the field water level under the soil [SOURCE: Page_36]

628   surface by PALSAR-2 data with the currently available algorithms). Therefore, the bias became relatively great [SOURCE: Page_36]

629   at the continuously inundated paddies (number of ROIs whose temporally local minimum values exceeded the [SOURCE: Page_36]

630   soil surface: 25 pixels; mean ± standard deviation of the Dbefore_irrigation: 4.99±2.13 cm above the soil surface; [SOURCE: Page_36]

37 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_37]

631   Figure 9), where paddies were prone to continuous submersion with high field water levels (mostly greater than [SOURCE: Page_37]

632   10 cm above the soil surface). Regarding the main purpose of this application, the approach presented here can [SOURCE: Page_37]

633   provide adequate values of field water level and Dbefore_irrigation as long as the values are lower than the soil [SOURCE: Page_37]

634   surface. Although it is still difficult for this application to differentiate continuously inundated paddies and [SOURCE: Page_37]

635   intermittent irrigation paddies from the momentary value of Dbefore_irrigation, it might detect continuously [SOURCE: Page_37]

636   inundated paddy pixels based on time series data since the detected Dbefore_irrigation values from continuously [SOURCE: Page_37]

637   inundation-prone paddies’ ROIs were constant nearly year-round, unlike those of intermittent irrigation paddies [SOURCE: Page_37]

638   (Figure S$_5$). Relatively large biases were also found at the site-C, which is located in a sandbank adjacent to the [SOURCE: Page_37]

639   main Mekong River (Hau River, Figure 9), due to the intense side flow of the dike system caused by large-scale [SOURCE: Page_37]

640   irregular temporal dynamics of the river water level as affected by the ebb and flow of the tides. As Lamapayan [SOURCE: Page_37]

641   et al. (2015) summarized from various field results obtained over Asian rice paddies, the optimum Dbefore_irrigation [SOURCE: Page_37]

642   value is 15-20 cm below the soil surface, irrespective of differences in soil type. Arai et al. (2021) also reported [SOURCE: Page_37]

643   that rice yield tended to be higher in rice paddies whose Dbefore_irrigation values were 15-20 cm (i.e., safe-AWD [SOURCE: Page_37]

644   rice paddies) than in continuously submerged paddies. By spatiotemporally identifying rice paddy pixels whose [SOURCE: Page_37]

645   Dbefore_irrigation values are 15 cm below the soil surface, this technique has the potential to map rice paddies where [SOURCE: Page_37]

646   the Dbefore_irrigation values should be lowered by improving infrastructural/social factors as well as reducing [SOURCE: Page_37]

647   irrigation water usage. In addition, by locally identifying rice paddies whose Dbefore_irrigation values are less than [SOURCE: Page_37]

648   -20 cm, this technique can consistently detect the water demand or severe drought stress with high [SOURCE: Page_37]

649   spatiotemporal resolution. Spatiotemporal identification of drainage/irrigation-required zones inside dikes with [SOURCE: Page_37]

650   factors restricting the adoption of AWD (Miyashita et al., 2016; Yamaguchi et al., 2019) has the potential to [SOURCE: Page_37]

651   provide useful information for stakeholders at the local community scale. Interestingly, the low-Dbefore_irrigation [SOURCE: Page_37]

652   region was located in the low-elevation zone (<2 m), and the high-Dbefore_irrigation region was located in the [SOURCE: Page_37]

653   high-elevation zone (>3 m), even though we did not use the DEM for our data assimilation process. Comparison [SOURCE: Page_37]

654   with the DEM also showed that the estimated Dbefore_irrigation values significantly decreased where triple rice [SOURCE: Page_37]

655   cropping was practiced in the low-elevation zone, particularly in the dry season, and became high where triple [SOURCE: Page_37]

656   rice cropping was practiced to a certain degree in the low-elevation zone continuously throughout the year. [SOURCE: Page_37]

657   Consistent with other ground-based social science surveys, continuously submerged paddies are more common [SOURCE: Page_37]

658   in low-elevation areas that are relatively far from dike/river systems, and elevation and the distance from a canal [SOURCE: Page_37]

659   exhibit a statistically significant relationship with the rate of AWD adoption (e.g., Miyashita et al., 2016; [SOURCE: Page_37]

660   Yamaguchi et al., 2019). These results indicated the necessity of spatiotemporally identifying AWD irrigation [SOURCE: Page_37]

38 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_38]

661   implementation, and our system has the potential to provide adequate information at both local and regional [SOURCE: Page_38]

662   scales. With safe-AWD, it is recommended to irrigate water into paddies when the water level drops to -15 cm [SOURCE: Page_38]

663   (i.e., Dbefore_irrigation=-15 cm) to reduce irrigation water and methane emissions from soil without sacrificing rice [SOURCE: Page_38]

664   yield. By assimilating L-band SAR data, we estimated these field-level values before irrigation. We found that [SOURCE: Page_38]

665   almost 50% of paddies were irrigated before the field water level dropped to -15 cm (53.0% for June 2017, 56.9% [SOURCE: Page_38]

666   for December 2017). In particular, approximately 15% of the total rice paddies in the delta were irrigated before [SOURCE: Page_38]

667   the water level dropped to -5 cm and nearly continuously flooded (13.8% for June 2017, 19.8% for December [SOURCE: Page_38]

668   2017), and approximately 33% of the total rice paddies in the delta were irrigated before the water level dropped to [SOURCE: Page_38]

669   -10 cm (30.6% for June 2017, 35.4% for December 2017). These results indicated that the Mekong Delta still has [SOURCE: Page_38]

670   great potential to reduce methane emissions without sacrificing rice yield. With respect to the adaptation measures [SOURCE: Page_38]

671   for soil drought/soil salinity, we found that the measures are applied to approximately 25% of the total rice paddies [SOURCE: Page_38]

672   in the Mekong Delta (26.2% for June 2017, 24.1% for December 2017) . It is important to identify these [SOURCE: Page_38]

673   freshwater-scarce zones and recommend a smart freshwater grid system to distribute freshwater to these zones. [SOURCE: Page_38]

674   However, these significantly high/low values were detected only where triple rice cropping was practiced. The [SOURCE: Page_38]

675   results indicate that higher temporal resolution SAR data, such as ALOS-4/NISAR/ROSE-L, may be essential to [SOURCE: Page_38]

676   quantify the irrigation status, particularly for single/double cropping paddies. [SOURCE: Page_38]

677         The issue of methane emissions from the Mekong Delta caused by continuous fresh organic carbon [SOURCE: Page_38]

678   supply through double/triple rice cropping into frequently irrigated paddy soil (Yan et al., 2009, Arai et al., [SOURCE: Page_38]

679   2022a) has become a matter of concern for sustainable agroecosystem management. To operationally evaluate [SOURCE: Page_38]

680   the methane emission status, rice yield as affected by drought stress and spatiotemporal implementation status of [SOURCE: Page_38]

681   AWD irrigation to provide decision support and inform carbon pricing schemes, coupling the field water level [SOURCE: Page_38]

682   simulation with methane emission simulation models is a viable approach (Fumoto et al., 2008, Arai et al., [SOURCE: Page_38]

683   2018). However, most hydrological or crop/soil biogeochemistry models require field water levels as input data [SOURCE: Page_38]

684   and cannot simulate the field water level itself. By updating realistic field water level data with spatiotemporally [SOURCE: Page_38]

685   optimized Dbefore_irrigation based on SAR data, these models could simulate rice productivities and greenhouse gas [SOURCE: Page_38]

686   emissions such as methane by adaptatively optimizing the input of field water level on every SAR data pixel [SOURCE: Page_38]

687   without requiring a large amount of ground-observed field water level data (Arai et al., 2022a). [SOURCE: Page_38]

689   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_38]

39 of 44 [SOURCE: Page_39]

690   The concept and required techniques of SAR data assimilation with relatively high spatial resolution and low [SOURCE: Page_39]

691   temporal resolution are demonstrated in this study. This study evaluates a soil drought index (i.e., Dbefore_irrigation) [SOURCE: Page_39]

692   that has high potential to inform decision making regarding the sustainable water and carbon resource [SOURCE: Page_39]

693   management of rice paddies. Moreover, this approach can support adaptive simulation with biogeochemistry [SOURCE: Page_39]

694   models considering farmer irrigation practices. By prioritizing the estimation of model parameters with long [SOURCE: Page_39]

695   temporal scales rather than updating state variables with short temporal scales, adequate performance with [SOURCE: Page_39]

696   improved accuracy was confirmed based on validation with ground observation datasets. Since most field-scale [SOURCE: Page_39]

697   hydraulic simulation models for paddy soils treat field water level data as only forcing inputs, we implemented a [SOURCE: Page_39]

698   field water level simulation scheme optimized for a high spatial resolution data assimilation system. Validation [SOURCE: Page_39]

699   with ground observation data was conducted by taking advantage of high spatial resolution assimilation, and [SOURCE: Page_39]

700   adequate performance of the dual-scale observation operators and model parameter estimations by local particle [SOURCE: Page_39]

701   filter were demonstrated. The spatiotemporal dynamics of Dbefore_irrigation model parameters were detected [SOURCE: Page_39]

702   depending on the differences in infrastructure (i.e., seasonal dynamics in a semidike system and spatial contrast [SOURCE: Page_39]

703   between intermittently drained paddies and paddies prone to continuous submersion in a full-dike system). [SOURCE: Page_39]

704   Although some factors causing bias remain (side flow in rice paddies that are located adjacent to a river with [SOURCE: Page_39]

705   relatively drastic daily/seasonal dynamics due to the ebb and flow of tides, intermittent drainage or continuous [SOURCE: Page_39]

706   submersion management), the validation results of the dual-observation operators indicated the potential for [SOURCE: Page_39]

707   improvement by downscaling the spatial localization and shortening the assimilation cycle by improving the [SOURCE: Page_39]

708   linearity of the observation operators with new L-band SAR data observation techniques with increased [SOURCE: Page_39]

709   spatiotemporal resolution (e.g., ALOS-4, NISAR, ROSE-L). [SOURCE: Page_39]
