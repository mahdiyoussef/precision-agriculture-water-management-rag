Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

1   Understanding the complex dynamics of fruit growth is crucial for optimizing agricultural 2   practices and improving food production. Observational difficulties pose a challenge to the 3   development of models capable of providing a description of the cellular processes involved, 4   while reproducing the macroscopic observables of interest. Here we present a mechanistic 5   framework for modeling the growth of fleshy fruits. The framework integrates single cell 6   expansion and division processes with organ-wide resource sharing processes. Using empirical 7   data on tomato fruit growth under different irrigation and fruit charge regimes, our model 8   accurately reproduces the temporal patterns of total cell number and fruit mass, and predicts 9   cell mass distributions at harvest. 19   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   Harvested fruits represent the culmination of complex processes involving cellular, multicel- 21   lular and human components. Cell expansion is considered to be the most significant factor 22   influencing the final size of the fruit [1, 2], which is a crucial determinant for growers. Cell 23   division is necessary to produce differentiated tissue, it determines the final total number 24   of cells and therefore affects many properties such as texture, firmness and nutrient content 25   [3, 4]. Both expansion and division processes are regulated by plant and environmental cues 26   [5]. These processes require carbon and nitrogen to function, which are provided through 27   water transport and shared among cells. 28        In light of the mounting global demand for food and the imperative to minimize envi- 29   ronmental impact [6], significant research efforts are being directed to understanding the 30   interactions between agricultural, genetic and environmental factors and their influence on 31   fruit quality, with the aim of enhancing yield indicators and reducing water consumption. 32   Tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum) are a model species for fleshy fruit biology, due to their 33   short life cycle, their great genotypic [7] and phenotypic [8] diversity, and their economic and 34   nutrient importance. Consequently, they represent a privileged system for the study of the 35   effects of molecular mechanisms and agricultural practices on fruit growth [9, 10]. 36        Integrating experimental and theoretical knowledge is hindered by inherent challenges in 37   studying many fruit systems. Indeed, observing the behavior of fleshy fruit cells in vitro is 38   inherently problematic as it involves disrupting the 3D structure, thereby interrupting the 39   processes of interest. Furthermore, fruit growth spans several weeks and goes through diverse 40   phases, making comprehensive in vivo observation with cellular resolution throughout the 41   entire process very challenging to obtain [11]. Instead, macroscopic observations such as 42   total cell number and mass are more accessible experimentally. Typically, when measured 43   throughout development, such data display sigmoid patterns, as documented for fruits [12, 44   13, 14] and leaves [15, 16, 17]. This behavior, markedly different from that of a clonal 45   population exponentially colonizing a Petri dish, cannot be explained by the simple sum of 46   single-cell behaviors [18], and requires the invocation of collective or plant-cell regulation 47   arguments [19]. 48        In coping with the aforementioned experimental complications and information lacks, 49   researchers have developed statistical and dynamical models. Statistical models have suc- 50   cessfully captured the characteristic saturating patterns observed in plant tissues through 51   the use of logistic-like, parametric curves [20, 21, 12, 22]. While helpful for highlighting the 52   general properties of growth patterns, this approach cannot elucidate the role of the under- 53   lying biological mechanisms and their impact on growth dynamics. In contrast, dynamical 54   models aim at incorporating biological processes by means of a mechanistic description of 55   the cell behaviors [23, 24, 25, 26]. 56        Dynamical models of fruit growth often posit a temporal separation between periods of 57   cell division and expansion. 58   either total cell number [27] or total mass [28, 29, 30]. Similarly, models of resource sharing, 59   which are employed to explain patterns of mass growth at various levels of plant organization 60   [31, 32, 33], are generally developed under the simplification of no cell division [34, 35]. 61   However, such view is very simplistic, as expansion and division processes overlap during the 62   initial stages of fruit development [36, 37, 38]. Furthermore, the above approaches hinder 63   our comprehension of how expansion, division and resource sharing processes collectively 64   influence cell mass distributions. 65        In light of the complex relations between cellular, tissue and organ scales and the impact 66   of the cellular scale on the regulation of many processes that determine final size and major 67   quality traits, there is a pressing need to develop models integrating expansion and division 68   timescales. Few attempts have been made in this direction. In [39], division occurs according 69   to internal clocks mimicking cell cycles, independently from expansion and resource shar- 70   ing. While capturing observed patters of tomato fruit cell number and mass, most of the 71   focus however is oriented towards elucidating the role played by genetic material content. 72   Moreover, no attempt is made in fitting the observed mass distributions. In [25], cells ei- 73   ther expand or divide, depending on predefined functions of cell age. While incorporating 74   resource-sharing concepts, the model does not provide information on cell size distributions 75   nor how they relate to the processes. 76        In this study, we propose a unifying framework aimed at bridging the gap between the 77   aforementioned approaches. We develop a general, mechanistic model describing the division- 78   expansion dynamics within a population of cells sharing a common resource. Conceptually, 79   our framework integrates two well-established theories developed independently. Firstly, we 80   draw upon the size-dependent description of the expansion and division processes [40], a 81   framework commonly applied to bacteria [41], plankton [42] and microalgae [43]. Secondly, 82   we incorporate the source-sink formalism to characterize resource flow and sharing between 83   cells, as extensively employed in plant physiology [31, 44, 45]. [SOURCE: Page_4]

475   In addressing the challenges presented by our model — specifically, initial conditions con- 476   centrated near 0 with rapid decay, the need for precision in capturing phenomena close to 477   0 to accurately describe early cell divisions, ensuring total population conservation in the 478   absence of a reaction term, minimizing numerical diffusion to preserve the distinct effects of 479   growth and division, and the complexity introduced by nonlocal terms that complicate the 480   use of implicit or Crank-Nicolson schemes — we embarked on a comprehensive evaluation 481   of numerical methods.
