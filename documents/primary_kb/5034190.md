Abstract Vegetable farming relies on recurring soil preparation, organic fertilizers and minimal soil coverage, which can result in decreased soil fertility, reduced self-sufficiency, and harmed water quality. The ban of herbicides necessitates alternative weed control methods. SEFerSol is a comparative analysis of two innovative cultivation systems, the first emphasizing the consistent use of green manure without organic fertilizers, while the second involves minimal soil disturbance and continuous ground cover, against a reference system. Our findings indicate that the first system improves self-sufficiency and water quality while the second system displays enhanced fertility and weed management but suffers from lower water quality and decreased self-sufficiency. Keywords : Green manure, soil conservation, weeds, self-sufficiency, water quality [SOURCE: Page_2]

1. Introduction In the vegetable sector, the average treatment frequency index (TFI) excluding biocontrol varies widely depending on the crop studied. For example, according to 2018 Agreste figures, the average TFI for carrots in France is 7.1, while it is only 3.4 for lettuce. This TFI is already much lower in organic farming: 0.6 IFT on average excluding biocontrol on open field systems over 2017,2018 and 2019 (Synthèse DEPHY FERME, 2023). In particular, weed management is a major issue in the vegetable sector. Although herbicides have lower TFIs than fungicides and insecticides, they remain a constant problem in open fields, as the most difficult inputs to reduce according to the DEPHY FERME 2023 summary (18% over 10 years, compared with 44% for fungicides and 57% for insecticides). Additionally, herbicides are banned in organic certification, where as alternatives exist for insecticides and fungicides. For several years, Guillaume Delaunay, head of the vegetable farming department at “Lycée du Pflixbourg” in Wintzenheim, has been carrying out trials on mulching and green manures. In 2015, he decided to step up his efforts by integrating the results of his trials with recent advances in non-tillage, fertility and weed management, as well as the sound advice of local vegetable farm experts. Indeed, vegetable farming and more specifically diversified vegetable farming involves an intensive use of soil, which can lead to a significant loss of fertility (A.O.Aweto & G.M.Ogurie, 1992). The SEFerSol project focused on 5 main objectives: -   Improving soil fertility -   Improving weed management -   Protecting water resources -   Increasing input independence (understood here as all elements not produced on the farm, including plant protection products, fertilisers, fuel and seeds). -   Being economically viable. The experiment has taken place exclusively in organic vegetable farm on the EPLEFPA's farm, 'Les Jardins du Pflixbourg', within in a dedicated area. A particular feature of this project is the involvement of students and teachers on the school's plots. This provides valuable manpower and facilitates the dissemination of knowledge, but also presents certain limitations, particularly with regard to the rigour of certain data sets or the time required to collect them again at a later date.    MAX Green manure system The first system, called “Green Manure Max” (In French, “Engrais Verts Max” summarised in the rest of this article as EVMAX) focuses on maximising the use of green manures. The idea here is to avoid organic fertilisation altogether, or at least nitrogen fertilisation, and to use green manures as a mean of limiting weeds through competition, adding organic matter to the soil, capturing nitrates and limiting tillage. However, we have forecasted a slow nitrogen cycle with this system that may impede plant development, in particular those with a short cycle such as lettuce ("Les engrais verts en maraîchage, le guide", 2007). We have established two fundamental rules have been established for the EVMAX system:    No organic fertilisation, only green manure, with compost added if necessary.    Soil preparation limited to dragged implements, without PTO-driven implements, for more superficial tillage. [SOURCE: Page_4]

In addition, the system is established in permanent beds for practical reasons. Technically, green manures are sown whenever possible between crops, either as winter green manures or as summer green manures between lettuce and cabbage (see "Rotation and specific practices"). Green manure mixtures vary according to availability and the results observed in previous years. While in the early years, the diversity of green manures was a priority, the decision was made over time to rather focus on sourcing with geographical proximity, ideally with green manures originating directly from Alsace. Whenever possible, green manures are also sown as intercrops between rows of lettuces, cabbages, potatoes and leeks. These intercropped green manures are mainly Fabaceae (clover, faba beans or vetch). Their purpose is in some extent to limit weediness, mainly to provide extra nitrogen without competition with the crops.  Soil conservation system The second system is called Soil Conservation (hereinafter CONSV). This is based on no-tillage and permanent soil covering with a covercrop, a mulch or plastic sheets, depending on the time and the need. Organic fertilisation is possible, based on a nitrogen balance sheet that takes into account residues, mineralisation and inputs. The aim of this system is to promote soil fertility, in particular by allowing the growth of soil's biology, including both macros and micro-organisms, to limit the development of weeds and to promote plant resistance to pests and diseases through functional diversity. In the SEFerSol experiment, mulch is defined as a mixture of mixed "organic waste", e.g. grass cuttings, leaves, shredded material, etc. . Mulch combines several functions: weed control, thermal protection of the soil against summer heat and control of evaporation.  Reference These two systems, EVMAX and CONSV, were compared with a reference vegetable farming system (hereinafter called REF). This vegetable farming system was established on the basis of the most common practices in Alsace. At the beginning of the project, a survey was carried out by Bio in Grand Est, our project partner, among the region's organic vegetable farmers. The REF system is characterized by tillage and use of rotatory tools whenever necessary, particularly for potato cultivation, fertilisation practices adapted to the plant needs but not taking into account the soil stocks, and the use of green manures during the winter period where possible.  Rotation and specific practices On these three systems, REF, EVMAX and CONSV, the same five-year long crop rotation is followed, Potato, Lettuce/Cabbage, Squash, Leek, Carrot. The aim of this long rotation is to reduce the impact of [SOURCE: Page_4]

For example, while the potatoes were grown conventionally on the EVMAX system, they were not ridged on the CONSV system. They were simply placed on the surface of the soil and covered with mulch. Three varieties of squash were grown on each system: “Butternut”, “Potimarron” and “Sucrine du Berry”. It aims at limiting the weed growth on the beds on one hand and to also reduce the quantity of the plastic wastes left on the soil on the other. However, it is a time- consuming process. In addition, in the soil conservation system, woven cloth is better suited to the presence of mulch, as it is stronger than plastic sheets, which can easily split under the pressure of residues (RCW or other woody material). On the other hand, in CONSV system, mulch was added to achieve a thickness of around twenty centimetres so that the leek could be planted directly inside. On the EVMAX system, clover was planted between the rows of leeks, alike lettuce and squash, in order to cover the soil and to provide nitrogen. Finally, as far as carrots are concerned, the EVMAX system was run in the traditional way, whereas in the CONSV system, the carrots were sown in the mulch using a strip-till, a tool developed by the Atelier Paysan, a partner in the project, to open up the sowing line under the mulch, aiming at no-tillage production. 2.1 Experimental design With regard to the practical implementation of these systems, the experimental area was laid out as follows (Figure 1) Two plots, SEFerSol^1 and SEFerSol^2, subdivided into beds each measuring 50 m in length and 1.8 m in width, including one walkway. SEFerSol^1 plot is about 12, and SEFerSol^2 is about approximately 9 ares, so we have 12 and 9 beds respectively, divided equally between the three systems. Measurement areas (A, B and C) were established on the central bed, three measurement zones 3m long per system, to ensure the statistical validity of the measured data. These measurement zones are only located on the central beds in order to avoid edge effects and the "contamination" of one system by another. The advantage of subdividing the trial into two plots is to limit annual effects, linked in particular to extreme weather conditions, which could alter the results if the crops were only present once on one plot. For logistical reasons, it is impossible to have all the crops present at the same time, so this is the best compromise that has been found at this stage. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Conclusion As a preamble to the conclusion, it should be remembered that this trial is part of the Ecophyto plan and that we have not dealt much with plant protection products in the results. No other product than biocontrol were used in the trial, and in the last rotation, only slugkiller was used as a preventive measure in all 3 systems. It is therefore difficult to reach a conclusion as to the impact of our systems on disease pressure, as none of our three systems experienced significant biotic pressure requiring treatment during the experiment. It is now time to talk about the other objectives, and the prospects they open up for the future. In terms of fertility, which was the main focus of the measurements carried out in the trial, the CONSV system clearly outperformed the other two, which were at a fairly similar level. Nevertheless, this fertility is obtained at the cost of autonomy: large quantities of mulch are used, and these quantities are produced off the farm. Even if the cost is not prohibitive, we are entitled to wonder about the sustainability of the system: if all vegetable farmers were to use it, where would they find enough material to supply everyone? A farmer once said, during a visit to the experimental site: "This system is a thief's system, your soil is only enriched by what you take from elsewhere". A harsh remark, but a fair one? The impact on water is not negligible. As we have seen, the CONSV system regularly exceeds drinking water limits in terms of nitrates levels.
