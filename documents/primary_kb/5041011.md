Abstract Advancing our understanding of environmental interactions in rice crops con- tributes to food production in water-limited regions. This paper proposes an integrated crop modeling architecture, demonstrating how machine-learning (ML) models enhance classic Mechanistic Crop Modeling (MCM) estimations by learning directly from environmental data. Here, we quantify the impact of noise-induced uncertainty on the CERES-Rice crop growth model, particularly relevant for drought-tolerant varieties that exhibit complex adaptation mecha- nisms, such as Nerica 4. Environment characterization is achieved through a novel 3D Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), offering enhanced precision and scalabil- ity when coupled with remote-sensing satellite-derived environmental data. By coupling both MCM and ML models, we achieved higher estimations for grain yield (R$_2$ = 0.99) and biomass (R$_2$ = 0.8) in the northwest Tambacounda region of Senegal in Africa, providing reliable estimates of 30% grain conversion efficiency and 2.18kg/hamm water use efficiency from an environment char- acterized by sandy soils with high saturated hydraulic conductivity (1.1cm/h) and the lowest regional precipitation (513mm, 49%). [SOURCE: Page_3]

1 Introduction Food security is a global concern, with challenges ranging from climate change and water deficits to soil degradation and changes in land use. Addressing these challenges at the regional scale with a growing population is crucial to ensuring self-sufficient food systems and mitigating the effects on food production [1–5]. Agriculture plays a crucial role in food production and, at the same time, con- tributes significantly to global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, with estimates ranging from 10% to 30% of total human-induced emissions [6, 7]. Rice cultiva- tion in flooded fields, contributes significantly to global greenhouse gas emissions, primarily through the production of methane (CH$_4$) [8]. Flooded paddies create anaer- obic conditions in the soil, which promote the decomposition of organic matter by microorganisms, releasing methane [9]. Historically, rice has been cultivated in flooded conditions [10, 11]. However, hybrid drought-resistant rice has developed intricate adaptation mechanisms, demonstrating efficient water use, short growing cycles, and deep roots, performing well in environments with low water content [12–15]. In this context, aerobic rice is an alternative crop grown in well-drained, unflooded soils, par- ticularly in regions with limited water availability or where irrigation is not sustainable, relying primarily on rainfall [16]. A major challenge in quantifying biomass and crop efficiency in non-industrialized agriculture is the significant lack of resources, technology, and infrastructure, such as meteorological stations with high spatial and temporal resolution [17, 18]. Earth Obser- vation (EO) technologies have democratized access to technology, developing satellite- driven databases that enable the study of regions with limited infrastructure.[19–22]. Building on this foundation, recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have enabled a set of tools for addressing global food security challenges. AI applies compu- tational techniques, such as machine learning (ML), multispectral image processing, and pattern recognition, to model, optimize, and automate processes such as crop monitoring, yield prediction, and resource management [23–30]. The mathematical representation of crop growth under diverse environmental con- ditions provides a powerful approach for assessing food production, overcoming the challenges of large-scale field experiments [31–35]. However, the use of crop models at regional scale in uncontrolled conditions face the challenge of accurately capturing how plants respond to environmental factors like soil type, climate, farming practices, and biotic interactions. Plant growth and development operate across multiple scales, from cellular processes to ecosystem-level interactions, a challenge intensified by the complex adaptation mechanisms of diverse drought-tolerant cultivars [12, 36]. Mechanistic crop models (MCMs) have been widely used for decades in the field of agronomic research, with over 70 models developed in the last 40 years to simulate crop growth and yield under diverse conditions [31, 37]. The growth and development of rice depend on the accumulation of heat over time, quantified through Growing Degree Days (GDD), which the MCM uses to simulate crop growth [38, 39]. While these mod- els have proven valuable, still rely on empirical elements and parameters with limited biological relevance, and numerous mechanisms remain poorly understood. Significant efforts are required to quantitatively link genotype to phenotype and address model limitations to improve the accuracy of modeling studies [34, 38]. Moreover, crop model [SOURCE: Page_4]

estimations inherently involve uncertainties arising from model structure, parametriza- tion, and input data, such as climate variables [40]. A systematic review of 277 studies conducted between 1991 and 2019 across 82 countries and 460 locations assessed uncer- tainty in crop model outputs, specifically grain yield. The review identified that 52% of studies focused on input uncertainty related to climate change projections, 28% addressed model parameter uncertainties, and 20% examined model structure uncer- tainties, often through multi-model ensembles. However, uncertainty associated with meteorological variables remains under-explored, despite its relevance. Random fluctu- ations, or noise, inherent in physical systems and instruments, arise from factors such as thermal effects, radiation, degradation, and instrumental failure [41, 42]. Address- ing these uncertainties is essential to enhance the reliability of crop models [40]. For instance, a review of 140 studies using the CERES model under various conditions, including well-managed fields with appropriate irrigation and fertilization, reported simulated root mean square errors (RMSE) for rice grain yield ranging from 200 to 1,672 kg/ha. Notably, the normalized RMSE varied between 11.8% and 25.6% across more than 500 rice stations in China [43, 44]. In contrast, drought-tolerant rice varieties have been less widely studied with the CERES-Rice model. In a controlled experiment with sixteen upland rice genotypes, RMSEn values ranged from 0.76 to 1.06 [45]. A rainfed field experiment in Senegal, however, reported a mean model calibration effi- ciency of 0.66 for 21 rice varieties [46]. If such uncertainties inherent in the model are not critically addressed, they could lead to not fully representative recommendations for policymakers and stakeholders, particularly when developing adaptation strategies [40]. These results highlight the need for further refinement in crop modeling, a task increasingly supported by advances in plant physiology, mathematics, computational tools, and AI. This progress is enabling the development of more mechanistic models through detailed response functions [32, 39, 47]. A new paradigm called ’simulation intelligence’ has emerged, integrating elements from both mechanistic crop models (MCMs) and data-driven methods based on machine learning (ML) [48]. The surro- gate models based on ML have gained prominence in plant and crop sciences due to their potential to achieve both high-accuracy estimation and the capability to explain plant responses [48] [49] [50]. Combining MCM and ML, given their intrinsic comple- mentarity, allows for three main modeling approaches: parallel, serial, and modular structures, that can be adopted to represent complex agricultural interactions [51–55]. In this context, MCM and ML strategies should complement each other to improve the understanding of plant responses to environmental variability, ensuring high- accuracy estimations and reliable recommendations for policymakers and stakeholders. This is particularly critical for large agricultural regions where field conditions are heterogeneous and cannot be strictly controlled [31, 48, 49, 56]. [SOURCE: Page_5]

2.2 Environments Definition To study crop development in response to the environment (GxE), it is crucial to define the environmental types within the study area. This definition is independent of the cultivar’s response, as each cultivar’s behavior varies based on its genetic composition [69] [70]. In this study, environments are characterized regionally by key factors, such as soil type and climate. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Fig. 2 Environment characterization methodology based on Climate and Soil data. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Figure 2 illustrates the methodology for environmental characterization, aimed at defining soil and climate types at a regional scale. This approach employs a clustering strategy, where soil and climate data are clustered independently. Principal component analysis (PCA) is first applied to reduce the dimensionality of the data, accounting for multiple variables. The optimal number of clusters is determined using silhouette scoring, a technique that evaluates the quality of clustering by measuring the cohe- sion and separation of the data points. For the clustering stage, the Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) algorithm is employed in three-dimensional space, using the first three PCA dimensions. The resulting clusters are represented by deformed ellipsoids, as shown in Figure 2-c. This GMM approach is then compared to the traditional cluster- ing algorithm [71–75]. The output of this process is a detailed map categorizing each region based on its soil and climate types. Following this, spatial correlation analysis is performed to generate all possible combinations of these types, resulting in distinct environmental classifications that allow for the analysis of plant responses to specific environmental conditions. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Field-based crop measurements, including phenological parameters such as anthesis and maturity stages in days, as well as productivity attributes such as biomass and grain yield in kilograms per hectare, are crucial for assessing crop performance. Parameters in the context of crop modeling are measurable factors or variables that influence plant growth and yield, such as the timing of phenological stages, the number [SOURCE: Page_8]

These parameters are critical to establishing the environmental and agronomic conditions under which a crop develops, and they are essential to ensure accurate calibration and prediction of the model. 2.4 AI arquitecture The linear auto-regressive with exogenous inputs (ARX) model is widely used in time-series forecasting due to its effectiveness in capturing the dynamics of temporal [SOURCE: Page_9]

7 [SOURCE: Page_9]

data. In this study, we extend the ARX model to the Nonlinear auto-regressive with exogenous inputs (NARX) framework by utilizing a feedforward neural network. The primary objective is to approximate the function f (x), where f (x) is derived from the mechanistic crop model (MCM) CERES-Rice that has been adjusted based on filed observations. Specifically, the function f (x) is driven by key variables such as biomass, grain yield, the number of grains, and the number of tillers, all of which are critical components of the crop growth and development process. The auto-regressive component of the NARX model relies on Time Lagged Data (TLD), which involves using past values of the input variables from environment to predict future outputs. This approach effectively integrates the temporal dependencies within the data, where past observations of environment influence the current state of the crop dynamic system, this allows for the prediction of organic mater cumulation dynamics of biomass and grain yield. By incorporating time-lagged data, the model reduces noise and disturbances, lead- ing to a more precise characterization of the underlying process. Such improvements enhance accuracy, enabling reliable predictions of crop performance and yield across diverse environmental conditions. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Dataset to train and validate the ML Crop observation data originate from a rainfed experiment conducted across nine sites for the N$_4$ cultivar [46]. The field-based rainfed study collected data on grain yield, biomass, grain number, and tiller number at maturity. These experimental data serve as ground truth for evaluating model performance. From this field experiment, the mechanistic model generates temporal dynamics for each variable from sowing to maturity, forming the basis for training the ML module. A total of 954 data points were obtained from the nine sites, averaging 106 samples per site. The dataset for ML model calibration and validation consists of 95,400 data points, generated by taking the full time series for each site and augmenting it 100 [SOURCE: Page_10]

8 [SOURCE: Page_10]

times using additive Gaussian white noise. The dataset for ML calibration includes data from five of the nine field experiment sites, incorporating the variability of noise from each available meteorological station. Of this dataset, 70% of the noisy data is used for ML model calibration, while 30% is used for validation. Finally, once the ML model is calibrated and validated with noisy data, it is further validated using the four remaining sites from the original field experiment dataset. [SOURCE: Page_11]

2.5 Large scale insilico Simulation The crop model simulates the accumulation dynamics of organic matter in response to environmental conditions. Simulations incorporate climate and soil data spanning 20 years (2000–2019), with a fixed sowing date in the second week of July, consistent with regional averages [46]. The in silico data set comprises 10,100 simulations, each executed daily in seven phenological stages: (i) germination, (ii) tillering, (iii) panicle initiation, (iv) panicle emergence, (v) anthesis, (vi) grain filling, and (vii) maturation. Each simulation quantifies grain yield, biomass, grain number, and tiller count. The Water Stress Photosynthesis Deficit (WSPD) factor quantifies the impact of soil water availability on crop photosynthesis by assessing the relations between soil moisture and evapotranspirative demand using the Penman-Monteith equation. WSPD is calculated daily, ranging from 0 (no stress) to 1 (severe stress), directly influencing photosynthetic efficiency, biomass accumulation, and yield formation. Higher values indicate increased water stress, leading to reduced carbon assimilation and compromised growth [76]. Finally, the crop model outputs, combined with environmental data, are processed through the Machine Learning module to generate the final crop yield estimate. [SOURCE: Page_11]

4 Discussion By investigating the interaction between environmental factors such as temperature, rainfall, soil texture, and hydraulic properties, this research explores how these fac- tors influence crop yield and biomass accumulation under varying conditions on the regional scale. The clustering approach using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) in the 3D space of the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) demonstrates an enhanced capability to identify distinct soil types and climate settings, surpassing traditional clustering algorithms used in environmental characterization, such as hierarchical clustering and K-means [71–75]. This method efficiently differentiates environmental conditions through the region and, when integrated with satellite-derived databases, facilitates scalable analysis at the regional level, enabling the assessment of cultivar efficiency and productivity. To the best of the author’s knowledge, this work presents the first quantifica- tion of noise-induced uncertainty in mechanistic crop model CERES-Rice, with a particular focus on temperature. In addressing model limitations and their scope of applicability [31], we highlight the challenges posed by the inherent uncertainty within these models, which remain difficult to calibrate due to the complexities of biological systems [35, 77], intensified with new varieties exhibiting intricate adaptation mecha- nisms. This complexity suggests that many underlying mechanisms are not yet fully understood, emphasizing the need for advanced tools to explore these uncertainties [32, 48, 49], particularly in non-industrial agriculture under uncontrolled conditions [SOURCE: Page_19]

To address these gaps, we propose integrating time series mod- eling with a machine learning technique that does not have assumptions and learns directly from the environment and the observed data. By harnessing daily environ- mental inputs and ecophysiological model-based dynamics, this integration enhances the accuracy of traditional crop model estimates. This integration achieves R² values of 0.99 for grain yield and 0.80 for both biomass and grain number in the rainfed experiment. This research also investigates the plant response of the Nerica 4 cultivar, a hybrid rice variety resulting from the cross between African rice (Oryza glaberrima) and Asian rice (Oryza sativa). Known for its high grain yield performance under sub- optimal water resource conditions, Nerica 4 demonstrates adaptability to poor soils and limited water environments, attributed to its deep root system and short growth cycle, with key traits including enhanced productivity and resistance to disease and pests [15, 78].This pattern aligns with machine learning (ML) estimates, revealing a key finding: the ML-based approach identifies Environment 6, a dry region with low water retention capacity, as exhibiting: (i) A yield of 1113 kg/ha with the highest grain conversion efficiency in the region at 30%. This elevated WUE is consistent with the findings of 41 cases of aerobic rice, where the WUE values range from 2 to 15 kg/H·mm [14], reflecting the adaptability of Nerica 4 cultivar to drought conditions. In contrast, traditional mechanistic crop model (MCM) estimates indicate a maximum WUE of 0.2421 kg/H·mm, consistent with findings from 56 studies on flood- irrigated rice, which report significantly lower WUE values ranging from 0.028 to 0.125 kg/H·mm [79]. These results highlight the superior water use efficiency of aerobic rice systems compared to conventional flooded cultivation methods, and the limita- tion of traditional mechanistic crop model to capture the efficiency and productivity of drought resistance rice crops. This aligns with the challenges of accurately repre- senting the Nerica 4 cultivar in rainfed rice studies using the state of the art model parametrization [46]. Mechanistic crop models assume that drought effects begin when soil water content decreases below saturation, as represented by the WSPD parame- ter in the CERES-Rice model. WSPD serves as a limiting factor for organic matter accumulation and directly influences biomass-to-grain conversion efficiency [57–59]. As shown in Figure 13, environments 6, which exhibit the highest WSPD values, estimate a lower water use efficiency (WUE) and a reduced harvest index (HI). These results are consistent with flooded rice cultivars but not with drought-resistant, adapted varieties as Nerica 4 [12, 13, 80]. [SOURCE: Page_20]

5 Conclusion The integration of the unsupervised Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) in 3D with Principal Component Analysis (PCA) represents a significant advancement in environ- mental characterization, surpassing conventional clustering methods such as K-means and hierarchical clustering. This approach enables a more precise identification of distinct environmental compositions, including soil types and climate settings, refin- ing our understanding of regional variability. When coupled with satellite-derived databases, it enhances large-scale assessments, providing a scalable and data-driven [SOURCE: Page_20]

18 [SOURCE: Page_20]

framework for improved environmental characterization and optimize decision-making in precision agriculture. A key contribution of this study is quantifying noise-induced uncertainty in the CERES-Rice crop model, with a particular focus on temperature variability. This approach addresses the complexities of modeling crop responses under fluctuating environmental conditions. By integrating machine learning with a multivariate time series approach, the study enhances the accuracy of mechanistic models, particularly in capturing crop yield and biomass dynamics, learning directly from environmental data, and reducing reliance on assumptions commonly embedded in traditional models. The research highlights the need for a more comprehensive crop modeling approach that combines both mechanistic and data-driven techniques, emphasizing the importance of refining modeling mechanisms to better capture the complexities of drought-resistant varieties and their adaptation to diverse environmental stressors. The complexities of drought-resistant crop varieties, such as their intricate water use mechanisms and adaptive traits, are not always captured adequately by traditional models, leading to significant gaps in predictions. Furthermore, the integration of machine learning techniques, although promising, faces challenges. Our results, which achieve R² values of 0.99 for grain yield and 0.80 for biomass and grain number, demonstrate the power of this integrated approach to deliver high-accuracy recommendations to policymakers and stakeholders. The exceptional adaptability of the Nerica 4 cultivar to drought conditions is consistently confirmed by machine learning-based estimates. The findings align with the superior water use efficiency (WUE) of aerobic rice systems like Nerica 4, compared to traditional flooded rice varieties. This research challenges traditional crop models, which do not fully capture the efficiencies of drought-resistant varieties like Nerica 4, as reported in the state of the art, particularly in rainfed environments at regional fields. This study also highlights the limitations of mechanistic crop models, particularly in capturing the complexities of drought resistance and water use efficiency in aerobic- adapted cultivars. Our results call for improved model mechanisms that better reflects the behavior of drought-tolerant crops under varying environmental stressors. The proposed machine learning coupling approach represents a significant advance in accu- rately estimating key variables such as grain conversion efficiency and water use efficiency (WUE) in aerobic rice. Future work should focus on advancing multiscale modeling to improve the rep- resentation of crop–environment interactions. Technological advancements will be essential for enhancing model precision and capturing complex stress responses. Integrating machine learning and multiscale measurement systems into mechanistic modeling offers promising pathways for optimizing resource use in agricultural systems. [SOURCE: Page_21]
