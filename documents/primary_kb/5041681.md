Exploring the optimality of threshold-based crop irrigation feedback strategies* Ruben Chenevat^1 , Bruno Cheviron^2 , Alain Rapaport^1 and Sébastien Roux^1 *†‡ April 21, 2025 [SOURCE: Page_2]

Abstract The development of effective crop irrigation strategies is a key element of water management in agriculture, seek- ing a necessary parsimonious use of resources. Years of practice and accumulation of knowledge have led to numerous irrigation policies, including Threshold-Based Irrigation Strategies (TBIS). This work involves the Controlled Crop Irrigation (CCI) model, a simple dynamical system describing the order-1 biophysical processes at play, for which optimal irrigation policies may be proven and written as TBIS. We aim at studying both the optimal TBIS and their sub-optimal declensions, to identify the most influential parameters (which dictate the structure of optimal irrigation scenarios) but also the effects of errors made on their determination. Much attention is therefore drawn to the crucial ”decision time” at which the irrigator decides to increase soil water content from an initial ”stress tolerance level” to a ”maximal biomass production” setpoint. In this study, we couple known results from optimal control theory and numerical methods for sensitivity analysis and global model exploration. The most striking results are that the optimal decision time is mostly driven by plant growth dynamics and the correct evaluation of stress effects on biomass pro- duction, as slightly too low soil moisture levels during the productive stage induce significant losses in final biomass. The genericity of the CCI model and the modularity of the methodology offer promising perspectives for various operational contexts. [SOURCE: Page_2]

1     Introduction Threshold-Based Irrigation Strategies (TBIS) produce irrigation scenarios, i.e. chronicles of irrigation events, from the application of decision rules in which irrigation is triggered when selected values of one or several variables of the soil-plant-atmosphere system are reached, or assumed to be reached, via observations, measurements or modelling. This leaves the possibility of considering different irrigation triggering criteria, associated with different irrigation amounts, across the different stages of the cropping season. However, the settings, setpoints and objectives of irrigation are most often predefined thus neither reconsidered nor adjusted during the course of the season. Such ambitions are rather left for ex-post analyses or ex-ante planning purposes, facing the issue of relevant if not optimal resource management. The classical problem is to reduce water consumption under a certain limit (quota) while still ensuring enough biomass production (objective) and economic viability, for various operational contexts and environmental constraints. TBIS are common practice in irrigation management, either (i) implicitly, in an informal way, throughout expert irrigation decisions [7, 20, 22] aiming to counterbalance the observed detrimental effects of unobserved resource de- pletion, or (ii) explicitly via indications on soil water status drawn from soil water content or matric potential measure- ments [12, 37, 38], whereas (iii) modelling tools and their decision support declensions [8, 13, 19] remain surprisingly rarely used in practice. And yet, apart from costly and risky trial-and-error attempts, this is the only approach that allows generating, comparing and improving scenarios of water consumption, biomass production and economic yield, as controlled by the tested decision rules, thresholds and associated parameter sets. * *This work was supported by Labex Numev (ANR-10-LABX-0020) and by the French National Research Agency under the Investments for the Future Program, referred as ANR-16-CONV-0004. †1 Ruben Chenevat, Alain Rapaport and Sébastien Roux are with MISTEA, Univ. TBIS require choosing threshold values endowed with physical sense from the points of view of soil physics, crop physiology and root water uptake properties and performance, while also playing on the explored ranges of values for the sake of optimization purposes. Chosen threshold values are expected to be related to characteristic values of soil water content. These values are θsat the saturation value, when all soil pores are filled with water, θ f c the field capacity value, above which drainage occurs as water contents between θsat and θ f c are non-equilibrium conditions, θx a quite lower value, whose definition and evaluation are uncertain, under which water uptake by plant roots becomes difficult and incomplete, hampering crop transpiration process as the result of incipient water stress, θwp a far lower value, known as the permanent wilting point, at which water uptake by the roots becomes impossible, and θr the residual water content, not even accessible to evaporation [1]. The objective assigned to optimization is thus to maximize biomass (in this paper) or benefit (in general) for known constraints on system performances and total water consumption afforded, while staying within realistic admissible limits in terms of soil water content. Ancestral habits, field-based knowledge and common sense lead to an intuitive but fuzzy compromise between favoring crop production by avoiding too low water contents, staying above θx (if possible), and sparing water by staying under θ f c but only in situations in which water costs are significant, water availability is really limited and irrigation amounts may be properly adjusted. More often than not, the irrigators’ behaviour is quite different between the initial part of the cropping season and the quick growth, most productive part of it. A current practice, in the frame of deficit irrigation, is indeed to delay the first irrigation and provide limited water amounts in the first series of irrigations, both assuming limited early crop demands and aiming to favor root deepening, thus tolerating lower water contents and lower irrigation triggering thresholds than in the productive phase [21, 27]. In the latter, the rate of biomass production is deemed strongly correlated to the absence of any significant water stress, while the cumulative biomass produced will benefit from low cumulative water stresses [5, 14, 23]. On the other hand, even for models having proven their ability to reproduce crop development and production in various mild stress conditions, literature shows a lack of frameworks, methodologies or formal approaches for exploring the merits and drawbacks of different TBIS [36]. As regards mathematical formalisms and methods to be employed, irrigation planning can be seen as a ”control problem”, which consists in ”finding a control” (an irrigation chronicle following decision rules) for a dynamical system (coupled model equations describing soil filling and biomass production in dynamic interaction) over a period of time (the full cropping season) to handle the evolution of the system according to a certain objective. Taking advantage of advanced control theory in agriculture presents a high potential to improve operating efficiency [11, 28]. Some studies investigated the irrigation problem through the model predictive control point of view [10, 29, 32], targeting adaptive and robust strategies regarding unknown entries like weather or model uncertainties. In a purely deterministic approach, other studies addressed the problem of optimizing irrigation planning, using optimal control tools where a certain objective function is optimized (final biomass, entirely consuming the water quota). To this end, several analytical formalisms have been used to investigate the optimal irrigation problem with various points of view [18, 24, 31, 33]. The choice made in this paper is to address the generic, methodological objective by resorting to a parsimonious Controlled Crop-Irrigation (CCI) model [2, 4], prone to analytical optimization techniques but thought to possess enough physical sense to describe the order-1 biophysical processes at play. The CCI model is suited to fully controlled conditions, assuming all obtained effects are due to irrigation (greenhouse conditions, associated crops, well- gauged contexts). In addition, the mentioned context (greenhouses) certainly allows feedback loop (process where the output observation can drive the input action), allowing to stick to the predefined plan, within closed-loop modelling. So TBIS typically are control feedback strategies that naturally follow the CCI model approach. The originality of our work is to bring, thanks to the CCI model framework, a coupled analysis of optimal TBIS strategies gathering results from the optimal control theory and statistical methods of sensitivity analyses. Indeed, the CCI model has the key property to present optimal solution in terms of TBIS, and is suited to a large numerical exploration. A first objective is to find what characterizes the structure of optimal TBIS, for a wide variety of contexts (soil capacity and initial filling, climatic demand, crop type) and constraints (water quota, irrigation rate). A closely- related objective, of applied interest, is to assess the effects (on water consumption, biomass production and overall irrigation efficiency) of mistakes committed on the various thresholds. In coherence, the present work now explores the optimality or sub-optimality of various sets of irrigation-triggering thresholds. [SOURCE: Page_3]

2       Material and methods 2.1     Threshold-Based Irrigation Strategies A TBIS is composed of time periods when the farmer does not irrigate, time periods when the farmer irrigates at maximum rate, and time periods when the flow of irrigation is adapted to have the soil moisture remaining constant at or near a chosen target (threshold) value, possibly oscillating around it across repeated cycles of water input then consumption, via transpiration and evaporation. To be general, let us renormalize the irrigation rate by its maximal value. That is, if the irrigation rate is called Ψ, then let us consider that no irrigation means setting Ψ = 0, an irrigation rate based on different threshold values of the soil moisture across time will be denoted Ψ         e and is supposed to be valued between 0 and 1, and maximum irrigation rate means setting Ψ = 1. Then a TBIS can be characterized by corresponding ”decision rules”, denoted R$_0$ (for no irrigation), RSk (for an irrigation based on a soil moisture threshold value Sk ) and R$_1$ (for maximum irrigation). These rules can depend on time and model state variables (soil moisture S and remaining water budget V in this case). The set of decision rules is supposed to be complete and mutually compatible, i.e. at any time (or situation) there is exactly one decision rule that holds true. 2.2     The Controlled Crop-Irrigation model 2.2.1    Model description We denote here as CCI the Controlled Crop Irrigation model introduced in [2, 4, 18] and based on in the initial formulation [25] related to the FAO crop water simulation model Aquacrop [35]. Basically, the CCI model is a parsi- monious dynamical system of ODEs suitable for applying the optimal control theory [18]. The CCI model has only three state variables : soil moisture (S), crop biomass (B) and remaining water budget (V ). As in [25], soil properties are assumed to be spatially homogeneous with an even plant spacing, not needing to consider and spatial pattern or heterogeneity within the modelled area. With characteristics seen in such a spatially- averaged way, the CCI model consists in a small set of ordinary differential equations representing soil moisture, biomass production and remaining water budget, during a growing season denoted by a time interval [0, T ]. In the present work, contents of soil water S will be normalized between 0 and 1, rescaling θ = θ f c to S = 1 (and keeping θ = 0 to S = 0). Taking S = 1 at θ f c means not considering drainage, following the framework of controlled greenhouse conditions. The CCI model is written as a continuous-time dynamical system, that can be understood as a system evolving with a certain variation per unit of time (for example per day), and parameters representing rates can thus be seen as quantities per unit of time (for example daily). 2.2.2   Optimization criteria and constraints An optimal control problem consists in optimizing an objective function related to the evolution of the associated dynamical system. As in [4], in this work we aim at maximizing the final biomass production B(T ), among all the admissible irrigation controls u(·). A control u is said to be admissible if it takes values in [0, 1] for all t ∈ [0, T ], and if the corresponding state variables fulfill the model constraints. To this end, the system is endowed with biological and operating constraints as follow : • S(t) ≥ Sope for all t ∈ [0, T ] : water stress is tolerated to some extend, but it is assumed by the operator that soil moisture should not go below a value Sope in order to avoid critical stress. In the following, this so-called operat- ing constraint Sope ∈ (S pw , S∗ ) is assumed chosen by the operator and denotes their risk aversion or inclination: the lower Sope is, the more water is saved but the riskier the strategy is for biomass production. • V (T ) ≥ 0 : the total amount of irrigation water used during the season fulfills a quota constraint (defined by the initial condition V (0) = Q). The value of initial quota Q might depend on the availability and cost of irrigation water, or intentional water savings in a context of deficit irrigation, hence either reflects the irrigator’s choice in examining various scenarios, or an strict, external constraint. [SOURCE: Page_5]

2.2.3   Biomass renormalization In the deficit irrigation context there is not enough water to keep soil filling at or above S∗ throughout the whole cropping season, meaning that the maximum biomass production will not be achieved. The present work deals with various limiting quotas for which the optimum irrigation dynamics, thresholds and setpoints are sought. For any given problem parameterization, the lowest cumulative irrigation amount needed to maintain the above condition is identified as the limit Q∗ quota. In accordance, any Q < Q∗ quota will result in periods of stress, hence in losses in maximum possible final biomass, i.e. B(S$_0$ ,Q) < BQ∗ , where B(S$_0$ ,Q) is called the ”contextual optimum” and BQ∗ is called the ”overall optimum”. 2.3     Methodological tools for analysis 2.3.1   Optimal control We will make a link between TBIS and results of optimal control, that has already been successfully applied to the CCI model [4]. As mentioned previously, the optimal control problem, given initial conditions (S$_0$ , 0, Q), is formulated as follows : B(S$_0$ ,Q) = max B(T ) , such that V (T ) ≥ 0                                        (7) u(·) [SOURCE: Page_7]

where u is admissible and (S, B,V ) are the associated solutions to Eqs. (2) to (4). The CCI model is written as a non-autonomous dynamical system under constraints, and is piecewise affine and continuous with respect to the state variables. This framework is then suited to apply theoretical control theory results to characterize optimal solutions. In the first place, a theoretical result about piecewise affine systems [3] has been involved in order to extract properties about the candidates structures of optimal solutions. Secondly, a complete analysis based on the derivation of the non-smooth Pontryagin Maximum Principle [6] has been conducted [4] and led to a formulation of optimal irrigation strategies in term of a simple parameterized time-varying feedback. Another order of these three intervals cannot be part of an optimal solution, and outside of this concatenated interval there is no irrigation. Moreover the values of the optimal bottom and top thresholds are in fact known. Indeed the use of GSA can lead to relating the variability of tdopti to those of model inputs, and thus to point out in particular which input drives early or late irrigation triggering. Moreover, this global exploration is complemented by a local perturbation analysis that will allow to understand the consequence of sub-optimal choices of humidity threshold and decision time. 4    Discussion This paper offers the possibility of handling very different cases (initial, tolerance and setpoint water content levels, soil volume, crop characteristics, maximum irrigation rate, climatic demand) with the same framework, deriving the optimal, associated chronicles of S (water content, soil filling) and u (irrigation chronicle) in the general case (Fig. 2) broken down into different declensions for the different particular cases (Fig. 3). This may have interesting operational applications, as knowledge of the contextual and exploration parameters will decide which of the strategies to follow, either with maximal rate of irrigation scheme (Fig. 2) or with shortcuts and missing stages, in the relevant sketch of Fig. 3. For the irrigation operator, the emerging part of the previous developments, their outcome, certainly is the so called ”decision time” (td ), depending on all previously mentioned elements. Noticeably, those elements include the risk aversion (high Sope setpoint values) or inclination (low Sope values) of the irrigator, which also show throughout the chosen values for the Sope parameter denoting the lower limit of admissible water contents. One step further, the proposed framework altogether accounts for those irrigator’s choices, seeking the optimal solution (S(t), u(t), td ) for the assigned context, and allows questioning those choices, comparing the final biomass production in other parametric configurations. Investigating the effects of mistakes committed on the essential TBIS and decision parameters (S∗ , Sope , td ), whether those mistakes arise from imperfect measurements and (or) conceptualization, is making another step towards future operational applications. The previous sections illustrate the merits and drawbacks of different strategies, either optimal or sub-optimal, in maximizing biomass production from a given cumulative irrigation amount. Hence, a given irrigation strategy may be characterized by those two values: the final biomass and the irrigation amount. An additional, elaborate variable may be introduced to describe irrigation efficiency, relating the biomass produced to the irrigation amount needed for it. This will expectedly not replace the separate criteria in terms of quota (using less than a certain quota) and yield (producing more than a contractual yield) but rather provide an additional criteria, allowing to sort the eligible strategies according to their efficiency. The proposed framework may be enriched with the adjunction of economic variables of three kinds: fixed costs accounting for system operation, variables costs linked to water and energy consumption and the expected selling price (or valorization) of the produced biomass. From the economic point of view, the eligible strategies would still respect the quota and ensure sufficient, if not maximal income. 5    Conclusion In this work, we dealt with the problem of improving crop irrigation strategies, in various contexts suffering limited resources. For this purpose, we considered a class of crop irrigation strategies whose structure is based on assumed known or operator-defined setpoint levels of soil moisture, in succession throughout the cropping season. It has been noticed that these Threshold-Based Irrigation Strategies (TBIS) emerge from solving an optimal control problem for- mulated with the CCI model, the Controlled Crop Irrigation model used in this paper. More precisely, optimal policies are described as TBIS with two thresholds : a bottom one tolerating lower water contents in the early stage of the season, and a top one preventing significant water stress in the productive stage. Theoretical results show that such optimal strategies are easily determined by model inputs and the one-dimensional optimization of a time of decision to trigger maximal irrigation rate. This optimization is thus suited to efficient numerical simulations, and we imple- mented a global model exploration in order to provide properties about the nature of the crucial time of decision, and the consequences of near-optimal decisions in terms of biomass losses and water savings. Our first numerical exploration aimed at assessing Sobol Indices giving valuable insights on the global sensitivity of the decision time with respect to a wide range of soil, plant, operational characteristics. We highlighted that in both alternatives of risk aversion or inclination, the optimal time of triggering maximal irrigation rate is strongly related to plant characteristics, regardless of the initial irrigation water quota. In particular, the values of optimal decision time globally follow an increasingly monotonous behaviour with respect to a characteristic growth time, and a decreasingly monotonous behaviour with respect to the soil water content of incipient stress. A second numerical experiment investigates the sub-optimality of TBIS based on small errors around the optimal values of decision time and top soil moisture threshold. We observed that in both alternatives as above and in any case of initial irrigation water quota, mistakes over the top soil moisture threshold (especially when underestimating it, by less than 10% of the optimal value) may undermine the biomass production by up to 15% compared to the maximal potential biomass. Conversely, mistakes over the decision time tend to have moderate effects over the biomass production (a few percents of additional losses from those induced by the threshold mistake, when triggering maximal irrigation rate too early or too late by 10% of the season duration). However, early and late decisions lead to noteworthy different outcomes in terms of chances to violate the operating constraint or saving a fraction of irrigation water. Early decisions naturally imply that the water quota will be consumed before the end of the cropping season, and we showed that it is likely to obtain a soil moisture trajectory not fulfilling the operating constraint over stress limitation, with even higher chances in the case of higher risk aversion and with lower irrigation water quotas. Late decisions may induce irrigation policies that do not entirely consume the initial water quota, and we observed that greater initial water quotas lead to higher chances of saving water at the end of the season, with a slightly stronger increase in the case of higher risk inclination. Our results demonstrate the capabilities of the coupled optimal control and global exploration methodology, to point out some principles of enhancing the management of irrigation scheduling. With our in-depth analysis of TBIS, we opened a perspective to assess their potential in terms of water use efficiency. This could lead to suggest a multi- criteria approach for a decision making based on a compromise between producing more biomass, saving more water, and preventing prolonged periods of water stress. The present study case was limited to greenhouse systems without considering the impact of nutrients, future work might extend the methodology to more general systems. Further perspectives might additionally consider identifying sets of parameter corresponding to particular contexts (nature of soil, plant. . . ), in order to put the TBIS to the test on experimental studies.
