### 2.1. Study area

The Berrechid plain is located in northwestern Morocco, between the cities of Settat and Casablanca, encompassing an area of approximately 1500 km². This region is situated within the Atlantic coastal basin (Fig. 1) and is characterized by a semi-arid climate. Annual rainfall ranges between 280 mm and 320 mm, with more than 90 % of the precipitation concentrated between October and April. Temperatures fluctuate from 6.5◦ C in January to as high as 38◦ C in August (El Bilali et al., 2021). Hydrogeologically, the Berrechid plain features a significant aquifer system composed mainly of Pliocene sands, sandstones, and sandy limestones, with thicknesses ranging from 5 to 40 m (Moukhliss and Taleb, 2021). The aquifer demonstrates considerable variability in transmissivity, from 1 0–4–10–1 m^2 /s, due to the diverse geological materials (El Meknassi Yousoufi et al., 2024). Recharge occurs primarily through rainfall infiltration and groundwater inflow from adjacent areas, such as the Settat Plateau (Moukhliss and Taleb, 2021; El Bilali et al., 2021). The selection of this study area is primarily due to the availability of a well-documented database on groundwater level var­ iations, which is crucial for model training and validation. Additionally, the Berrechid aquifer is representative of many other aquifers in Morocco, which are highly vulnerable to drought and overexploitation due to intensive irrigation (Hssaisoune et al., 2020). The Berrechid plain supports extensive farming activities, with 90,000 ha dedicated to rainfed agriculture, 20,000 ha of irrigated land, and around 40,000 ha of uncultivated land. The rapid development of intensive irrigated agriculture, driven by easy access to the aquifer, fertile soils, and proximity to agricultural markets, has led to significant overexploitation of groundwater. By 2018, the Berrechid plain had over 7000 private wells and boreholes, contributing to a notable drop in the water table. This overexploitation exacerbates water stress and threatens the sustainability of water resources in the region (El Meknassi Yousoufi et al., 2024). [SOURCE: Page_4]

### 2.2. Groundwater observed data

The observed groundwater level data from the six monitoring bores studied were extracted from a large database provided by the Bouregreg Chaouia Hydraulic Basin Agency (ABHBC). This database contains measurements of depth to the water table which have been converted into monthly groundwater levels. The six monitoring bores were selected for their long time series and their spatial distribution, which best represents the hydrodynamic spatial variability of the water table (Fig. 1). These monitoring bores were also chosen for their minimal measurement gaps, with no gaps exceeding two consecutive months. Remaining gaps were corrected using the linear interpolation method, which estimates the missing monthly groundwater levels by leveraging the values of neighbouring months, providing accurate results for brief periods of missing data (Huang, 2021; Bikše et al., 2023). The time series for each monitoring well was divided into training and validation periods, with the final 12 months serving as the validation period. The field visits conducted on November 6, 2024, highlighted the significant impact of groundwater overexploitation on the Ber­ rechid aquifer. Agricultural practices around the monitoring bores vary from intensive irrigation to rain-fed agriculture (Fig. 2). Notably, the presence of water storage basins near most monitoring bores, particularly around P$_2$ and P$_4$, indicates a strong reliance on groundwater pumping to sustain local agriculture (Fig. 3). This is especially relevant in Berrechid, a region known for its export- oriented production of crops such as corn, carrots, and potatoes (Ouassissou et al., 2019). ### 2.3. Remote sensing data

Groundwater levels are affected by various geo-environmental and climatic factors, including precipitation, evapotranspiration, soil moisture, aquifer hydrodynamic parameters, pumping and irrigation activities, and aquifer recharge (Boo et al., 2024). Among these, precipitation, temperature, and evapotranspiration are the most commonly used climate variables for groundwater level pre­ diction (Rajaee et al., 2019; Boo et al., 2024). This study uses four remotely sensed climate variables as input, GPM precipitation, MODIS land surface temperature, MODIS Actual Evapotranspiration, and the MODIS normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI). These climate variables are among the most commonly used in water resource monitoring due to their high spatial and temporal resolution, as well as their open source accessibility (Pimentel et al., 2023). The Global Precipitation Mission (GPM) programme has a spatial resolution of 10 km. The land surface temperature (LST) variation at each well was extracted from the MODIS MYD11A$_2$ product Version 6, which provides an average 8-day per-pixel land surface temperature and emissivity (LST&E) at a spatial resolution of 1 km. Monthly actual evapotranspiration (AET) data was obtained from the MODIS MOD16A$_2$ evapotranspiration product version 6 with a spatial resolution of 500 m and a temporal resolution of 8 days. The monthly variation of NDVI at the studied monitoring bores was extracted from the NDVI product (MOD13Q$_1$) version 6 of the Terra Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS), with a spatial resolution of 250 m and a temporal frequency of 16 days (https://modis.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/dataprod/). A major advantage of the LSTM model is its capability to remember information from the start of the time series while continuously integrating new data. ### 2.5. LSTM model with Input variables importance method

Predicting groundwater levels using remote sensing data from various climate variables for multiple monitoring bores with distinct dynamics requires a comprehensive understanding of the input data (Sahu et al., 2020). Analyzing variable importance can elucidate the relationship between descriptive variables and the target variable, identifying which features are irrelevant to the model for each monitoring bore (Wei et al., 2015). Therefore, it is advisable to utilize input feature selection techniques that assign a score to each input feature, reflecting the importance of each variable (Sahu et al., 2020). We applied the XGBoost method to each monitoring bore, a feature importance selection technique that constructs boosted trees to derive importance scores for the hydro-climatic variables in the prediction process. The scores generated by XGBoost demonstrate the value or significance of each hydro-climatic variable in constructing the boosted decision trees within the model. The more frequently a variable is used to make critical decisions in simulating groundwater levels, the higher its relative importance (Friedman, 2002; Zheng et al., 2017). ### 2.6. LSTM model with a dimension reduction method

The objective of this approach is to develop a generalized macro-variable across different monitoring bores, which represents the [SOURCE: Page_7]

overall hydro-climatic process influencing groundwater variations in the aquifer to which the monitoring bores belong. In this work, the LSTM-PC$_1$ model integrates the use of the first principal component (PC$_1$) obtained from Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of the four climatic variables. PC$_1$ is a linear combination of the four hydro-climatic variables and thus behave as macro-variable. PC$_1$ is orthogonal to other principal components and therefore it represents independent sources of variance, i.e., independent associated processes (Bouramtane et al., 2022). Taking into account only PC$_1$ makes it possible to concentrate the information of the four hydro-climatic variable in a reduced one factorial axe while losing a minimum of the information contained in the dataset, which constitutes a dimensional reduction of the data hyper-space (Reddy et al., 2020). Furthermore, analysing variable loading scores in this principal component deepens the understanding of this process and identifies its underlying mechanisms, thereby enriching the study of interactions between climatic variables and groundwater levels (Mohsine et al., 2023). ### 2.7. LSTM Models hyperparametrs optimisation

The study employed the Grid Search method, a conventional approach for optimizing hyperparameters. This method exhaustively searches a defined subset of a learning algorithm’s hyperparameter space to find the optimal combination (Zhang et al., 2022; Elzain et al., 2022). Essentially, it iterates through all specified hyperparameters to identify the best configuration. Additionally, Grid Search performance is typically assessed using cross-validation on the training set (Ghawi and Pfeffer, 2019). In this research, the hyper­ parameters of the LSTM model tuned included the number of neurons, batch size, learning rate, dropout rate, and number of epochs. ### 2.8. Monte Carlo simulation for uncertainty quantification

To assess the uncertainty associated with the predictive performance of the models during the 12-month validation period, a Monte Carlo simulation approach was applied (Wu et al., 2021; Ashofteh et al., 2025). The objective is to evaluate the robustness of the model’s predictions under slight perturbations of the input data, thereby producing an ensemble of plausible forecast scenarios. A total of 200 simulations were performed for each well and model. For each iteration, the input features were first standardized and then perturbed by adding Gaussian noise (Gal and Ghahramani., 2016). This noise had a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 0.1, which was empirically chosen and adjusted for each well in order to reflect the distribution, variability, and fluctuation range of the observed data. This approach ensures that the synthetic perturbations realistically mimic local uncertainties and variability in measurement conditions. The perturbed inputs were used to produce a set of forecasts, from which the mean prediction and 95 % confidence interval were calculated. This approach provides not only an average prediction but also a probabilistic range, allowing for a better under­ standing of model reliability under uncertainty (Sun et al., 2012). ### 3.2. Dimension reduction of hydro-climatic input variables

3.2.1. Variance analysis of principal components Table 3 presents the eigenvalues of the principal components at each monitoring bore, highlighting that PC$_1$ consistently exhibits values exceeding 2 across all six bores, with an average eigenvalue of 2.75. In contrast, the remaining three principal components display eigenvalues below 1, indicating that PC$_1$ accounts for more variance than two individual variables combined, whereas the other components each explain less variance than a single variable (Kaiser, 1960; Braeken and Van Assen, 2017). Table 4 further details the percentage of variance in the input variables explained by the first principal components (PCs) across the six monitoring bores. PC$_1$ distinctly emerges as the dominant component, capturing the largest share of data variance. On average, PC$_1$ explains 68.3 % of the total variance in the input variables, with individual contributions ranging from 64.5 % to 73.7 % (Table 4). Fig. 6 illustrates the spatial distribution of hydro-climatic data through a two-dimensional representation of PC$_1$ versus PC$_2$. The pronounced dispersion of observations along the PC$_1$ axis underscores its role as the primary driver of hydro-climatic variance, encapsulating the most significant patterns in the dataset (Machiwal and Singh, 2015; Mohsine et al., 2023). These findings indicate that PC$_1$ effectively captures the dominant hydro-climatic process governing the variability of observed climatic and hydrological factors, which, in turn, interact with groundwater levels at each monitoring bore. Given that PCA is very robust for identifying and highlighting environmental variations that influence groundwater in the location of each monitoring bore (Mohsine et al., 2023), significant variations between the monitoring bores are observed, with some moni­ toring bores showing a stronger dependence on PC$_1$ than others (Table 4). These variations on the amount of variance explained by PC$_1$ are potentially due to inherent differences in monitoring bores hydrological, land use, topographical and geological characteristics in each well location (Rukundo and Doğan, 2019; Yue et al., 2023). Land use patterns and local hydrological conditions vary considerably from one monitoring bore to another, as clearly observed during our field visits (Fig. 2). Irrigation practices differed based on crop type and proximity to residential areas. These variations contribute to differences in the monitoring bores’ hydrodynamic behavior and their response to the hydro-climatic variations considered by PC$_1$ (Machiwal and Singh., 2015). The percentages of variance explained by the other principal components (PC$_2$ to PC$_4$) are significantly lower than those of PC$_1$ (Table 3). With an average for explained variance of 19.7 %, 9.6 % and 2.1 % respectively for PC$_2$, PC$_3$ and PC$_4$.Those principal components seem to capture additional but less significant variations than PC$_1$. These later captures the maximum variance in the data, while the subsequent ones explain increasingly less important or residual variations for each well (Yue et al., 2023). For this reason, in the rest of the analysis we will focus solely on PC$_1$. While PC$_1$ captured the majority of the variance across monitoring bores, it is important to note that PCA assumes linear re­ lationships among input variables. As such, potential nonlinear interactions that may influence groundwater level variability could be underrepresented. Future work may consider complementary nonlinear dimensionality reduction techniques, such as autoencoders, to further explore and validate the robustness of input simplification strategies. ### 3.3. Model performance evaluation using evaluation criteria

Both LSTM-XGB and LSTM-PC$_1$ models demonstrate remarkable performance in replicating the groundwater levels during the training period of the six monitoring bores (Table 7). Both models exhibit high R$_2$ values, ranging between 0.982 and 0.999, indicating a strong fit of the models to the training data. Additionally, RMSE and MAE values are generally low for both models, with RMSE ranging from 0.013 m to 0.485 m and MAE ranging from 0.010 m to 0.322 m, indicating precise simulation of observed groundwater levels. However, during the training phase, the model learns patterns and relationships within the data by iteratively adjusting its parameters to minimize the error between predicted and observed groundwater level values (Zhang et al., 2018a,b,c; Sahu et al., 2020). While this step is crucial for model development, its evaluation alone may not adequately assess the model’s ability to generalize to new, unseen data. Therefore, the subsequent focus on the validation period is essential as it provides an independent evaluation of the models’ performance on unseen data, offering a more reliable indication of its real-world predictive capabilities (Cui et al., 2022). During the validation period, the two models LSTM-XGB and LSTM-PC$_1$ showed very good prediction performance (Table 8). R$_2$ values ranging between 0.885 and 0.999 indicating that both models effectively fit the observed fluctuations in the groundwater level validation data. The RMSE and MAE values, spanning from 0.036 m to 0.990 m, and 0.026 m to 0.835 m respectively. These low error rates between the simulated and observed values demonstrate an overall good to very good reconstruction for both models. However, the results demonstrate that the LSTM-XGB model had a lower error rate than the LSTM-PC$_1$ model in all monitoring bores except for well P$_1$ and P$_3$. The results of the LSTM-XGB model show an error rate of less than 0.1 m for monitoring bores P$_1$, P$_4$, P$_6$ and P$_7$, and between 0.1 m and 0.3 m for monitoring bores P$_2$ and P$_3$. The largest error deviation for both models is observed in monitoring bore P$_5$, but this does not exceed 1.0 m, reflecting a good model performance (Sun et al., 2022; Bouramtane et al., 2023). ### 3.5. Incertitude analysis

Table 10 summarizes the performance metrics of the mean prediction derived from Monte Carlo simulations applied to the LSTM- XGB and LSTM-PC$_1$ models across the six monitored wells. The Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) values remain below 1 meter for all wells, with the only exception being well P$_5$ under the LSTM-XGB model, where the MAE slightly exceeds the threshold at 1.004 m. Nevertheless, this deviation remains within acceptable bounds for groundwater management ap­ plications. In addition, all R² values exceed 0.90 for both models and across all wells, indicating a strong capacity to reproduce the temporal dynamics of groundwater levels. Fig. 10 and Fig. 11 further supports these findings by illustrating the 95 % confidence intervals, Min–Max prediction ranges, mean predictions, and observed groundwater levels for all six wells. For the LSTM-XGB model, the observed data fall within the confidence intervals for most wells, with minor deviations below the lower bound occurring during the final months for wells P$_2$ and P$_5$. For the LSTM-PC$_1$ model, the observed values for all wells are mostly contained within the confidence intervals, except for well P$_2$, where observations during the last months consistently fall below the lower limit. Despite these slight discrepancies, the mean predictions from both models successfully capture the observed temporal variability and general trends in groundwater fluctuations. These results emphasize not only the predictive robustness of both LSTM-XGB and LSTM-PC$_1$ models but also the added value of the Monte Carlo simulation framework, which provides a probabilistic representation of forecast uncertainty. This is particularly important for decision-making in groundwater management under uncertainty, especially in irrigation-intensive regions where pumping dynamics are irregular and difficult to anticipate. While this study primarily focuses on input dimensionality reduction and predictive uncertainty using data-driven models, future work could explore the integration of physics-informed neural networks or hybrid approaches (Cai et al., 2022; Zhan et al., 2024). Such frameworks may improve the physical interpretability of forecasts and enhance model transferability across basins with limited data or heterogeneous hydro­ geological conditions. ### 3.6. The optimal input variable for a basin scale model

Several studies have highlighted the advantages of identifying the best input variables from all available variables for simulating groundwater levels using RNN (Sahoo et al., 2017; Sahu et al., 2020; Sharafati et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2023). However here, although LSTM-XGB model uses the variable that best explain the specific variability of groundwater levels for each well and therefore the best prediction variables, it does not consistently lead to a significant better performance for all monitoring bores compared to LSTM-PC$_1$. As shown in Table 9, the difference in performance remains, very small and insignificant in a hydrogeological perspective (Gaganis and Smith, 2001). Additionally, based on the plot of groundwater level variation (Fig. 8) and Taylor diagram results (Fig. 9), it is challenging to conclude that globally one model is definitively better than the other. This varying performance prediction indicates that the effectiveness of each model may depend on the input variables and specific characteristics and dynamics of the groundwater system in each site (Enemark et al., 2019; Sahu et al., 2020; Bouramtane et al., 2023). While identifying the best input variable avoids over-fitting the model to the training data, it also ensures an easier interpretability and an improvement of the accuracy (Sahu et al., 2020; Yi et al., 2024). The use of the first principal component (PC$_1$) as a model input also demonstrated its effectiveness in achieving high prediction performance for monitoring bores in different locations (Kim et al., 2021; Mirboluki et al., 2024). ### 4. Conclusion

This study systematically evaluates the effectiveness of using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction as a generalized input method for predicting groundwater levels in the Berrechid plain, Morocco. It compares the efficacy of using the optimal input variable identified by the XGBoost model with the first principal component (PC$_1$) for groundwater level forecasting in the Berrechid aquifer system. Two distinct models were employed: the LSTM-XGB model, which integrates a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network with the best hydro-climatic variable selected through XGBoost, and the LSTM-PC$_1$ model, which uses PC$_1$ as the predictive input for the LSTM. The importance scores derived from the XGBoost model indicate that actual evapotranspiration (AET) and the normalized dif­ ference vegetation index (NDVI) are the key input features influencing groundwater levels. Specifically, NDVI emerged as the primary variable for monitoring bores P$_2$, P$_4$, and P$_5$, while AET was crucial for P$_1$ and P$_6$. Monitoring bore P$_3$ identified Land Surface Temperature (LST) as the optimal predictor. This analysis highlights the relevance of these variables in capturing the intricate dy­ namics of the aquifer system. [SOURCE: Page_17]

PC$_1$ consistently exhibits eigenvalues exceeding 2 across all six bores, with an average eigenvalue of 2.75, meaning that it explains the variance of more than two hydro-climatic variables. The variance analysis demonstrated that PC$_1$ accounts for approximately 64.6–73.7 % of the total variance among the hydro-climatic variables across the monitoring bores, with an average of 68.3 %. The significant correlations identified between PC$_1$ and the hydro-climatic variables, specifically, LST (loadings ranging from − 0.926–0.943), AET (from − 0.839–0.827), and NDVI (from − 0.934–0.936), underscore its critical roles in shaping groundwater dynamics. Performance evaluations conducted on both training and validation datasets reveal that both the LSTM-XGB and LSTM-PC$_1$ models effectively replicate groundwater levels, achieving high R² values, generally above 0.85, and low RMSE values, typically around 0.1 ms across the monitoring bores. While the LSTM-XGB model relies on site-specific input variables selected for each well, the LSTM-PC$_1$ model achieves comparable performance, with R² values nearing 0.80, demonstrating its effectiveness as a simpler and more gener­ alized approach. Overall, this study underscores the advantages of employing PCA in groundwater level forecasting. The utilization of PC$_1$ simplifies input selection while enhancing the robustness of predictions across multiple sites, making it an invaluable tool for effective water resource management. However, it is important to acknowledge that PCA assumes linear relationships among input variables, which may limit its ability to fully capture complex, nonlinear interactions driving groundwater dynamics. Future work could explore alternative dimensionality reduction methods such as autoencoders or kernel PCA to assess whether they offer additional benefits in modeling such complexities. Given the increasing reliance on groundwater for irrigation in the semi-arid Berrechid plain, where prolonged droughts and high temperatures significantly affect water availability, the insights gained from this research provide a valuable framework for developing predictive models. Such models can inform sustainable practices in water resource planning and management, crucial for mitigating the impacts of climate change and ensuring the resilience of agricultural systems in this region.
