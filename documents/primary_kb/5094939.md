Box 1 ICTs innovation for irrigation systems, an outlook from the HubIS project. [SOURCE: Page_6]

Low cost sensors for on-farm irrigation scheduling have been tested in an experimental farm in the Semi-arid Crau Plain, Southeast France. The issue was to develop technical solutions to find a balance between water application volumes and agricultural labor (Vandôme et al., 2024). The development and evaluation of water level sensors was achieved involving end users, with the aim to enable them to maintain and develop the sensors. The latter proved useful to improve water accounting (information on the duration of irrigation events) and reduce labor thanks to the pairing of the sensors with a telecom system (text message when water reaches a given length of the plot). Researchers proposed manufacturing workshops to start dissemination outside the experimental farm. Discussions with farmers also helped them adapt the sensors to their needs. Such co-design of low-tech solutions, as in Spain (Gavilan et al., 2024) or Tunisia (Vandôme et al., 2023), opens up new perspectives for decision support tools accessible to users with low financial capacities, generally unattainable with the usual commercial solutions. Second, the potentiality of using satellite imagery to support irrigation practices has been tested over the French pilot site. Temporal series of Sentinel− 2 images over 2021, 2022 and 2023 hay growing seasons have been analyzed by a service provider, TerraNIS, in order to monitor vegetation development over intensely irrigated grassland plots. Potential local needs and useful indicators – derived from satellite imagery or from in-situ measurements and observations – have been first defined with experts. Based on these inputs, a dashboard has been developed and showcased during an online workshop with potential end-users (Oct− 2023). Discussions focused on indicators choice and on the usefulness of such dashboard. First, the NDVI indicator has been explicated in a very coarse way to facilitate discussions (“although it cannot be directly linked to yield without calibration and modelling, it is sufficient to identify trends in hay plots”, a researcher). Second, for WUA managers looking at a detailed knowledge of actually irrigated plots to adapt water spatial distribution to restrictions, the dashboard appeared inherently limited because water flows cannot be captured by satellite images. Still, hay farmers representatives presented other needs that could be met by such dashboard, for example threshold alerts of spatial heterogeneity in hay flora or notifications of abnormal evolution compared to previous year.
