39     Abstract [SOURCE: Page_4]

40     1. Protected areas are a widely adopted resource management strategy for mitigating the [SOURCE: Page_4]

41         consequences of global change and preserve functioning ecosystems. Long-term species [SOURCE: Page_4]

42         monitoring programmes, aided by bio-logging technology, provide insights into the extent [SOURCE: Page_4]

43         and spatial variation of areas occupied by wild animals and inform conservation and [SOURCE: Page_4]

44         management. High-resolution GPS-acceleration data offer a more accurate understanding [SOURCE: Page_4]

45         of animal behavior and area use, compared to location-based inference, emphasizing the [SOURCE: Page_4]

46         significance of specific sites amid long-term climate change. [SOURCE: Page_4]

47     2. We based our case-study on the largest colony of little penguins (Eudyptula minor) located [SOURCE: Page_4]

48         at Phillip Island. Based on a ten-year bio-logging dataset (247 individual tracks), we [SOURCE: Page_4]

49         combine high-resolution bio-logging data from GPS-accelerometer loggers with proxies for [SOURCE: Page_4]

50         resource availability (e.g. Sea Surface Tenperature, thermocline, water turbidity). Using [SOURCE: Page_4]

51         machine learning techniques and Generalized Additive Mixed Models, we quantify the [SOURCE: Page_4]

52         environmental factors determining spatio-temporal variability in foraging effort (defined as [SOURCE: Page_4]

53         hunting time) across different breeding seasons and stages. [SOURCE: Page_4]

54     3. Little penguins increased their hunting time by reducing spatial displacement (shorter step [SOURCE: Page_4]

55         length) and diving deeper, with a slower increase in hunting effort below 10 m depth. In [SOURCE: Page_4]

56         relation to environmental conditions, penguins increased hunting effort in coastal areas with [SOURCE: Page_4]

57         high turbid and productive waters and decreased effort with increasing Sea Surface [SOURCE: Page_4]

58         Temperature. This gives insights into how these animals allocate effort differently according [SOURCE: Page_4]

59         to shifting environmental conditions. [SOURCE: Page_4]

60     4. Our analysis offers crucial long-term insights into little penguin area usage in the Bass Strait [SOURCE: Page_4]

61         at sufficient spatial and temporal resolution for management and conservation planning. The [SOURCE: Page_4]

62         Bass Strait is facing intense climatic and anthripogenic pressures, and the findings here on [SOURCE: Page_4]

63         intensity of area usage and strategy shifting according to environmental conditions, are of [SOURCE: Page_5]

64         great relevance for the marine spatial planning currently under development along the coast. [SOURCE: Page_5]

65     5. Policy implications: High-resolution behavioral information obtained from bio-logging data [SOURCE: Page_5]

66         using GPS-accelerometer tags provides understanding of how species shift strategies in [SOURCE: Page_5]

67         response to environmental variability. This is vital to implement climate-adaptive [SOURCE: Page_5]

68         conservation and management strategies. Given the growing availability of long-term [SOURCE: Page_5]

69         accelerometer datasets within the ecological community, we recommend integrating such [SOURCE: Page_5]

70         high-resolution information into conservation programs. [SOURCE: Page_5]

74          Introduction [SOURCE: Page_6]

75         Planet Earth faces the dramatic and accelerating consequences of climate change with the [SOURCE: Page_6]

76     expansion and intensification of anthropogenic stressors (Halpern et al. 2019; Abrahms et al. [SOURCE: Page_6]

77     2023). Ongoing global conservation and resource management efforts see the designation of [SOURCE: Page_6]

78     protected areas as a widely used strategy to maintain species richness and population [SOURCE: Page_6]

79     abundance and preserve ecosystems' functioning (Shin et al. 2022). The design and [SOURCE: Page_6]

80     implementation of protected areas have evolved to support resilient social-ecological systems [SOURCE: Page_6]

81     (Padleton et al. 2017; Claudet et al. 2020). However, challenges affecting the effectiveness of [SOURCE: Page_6]

82     protected areas include the continuous availability of data on current states and trends of nature [SOURCE: Page_6]

83     and threats. Hence, long-term species monitoring programmes are essential to inform, [SOURCE: Page_6]

84     implement and verify the effectiveness of such mitigation strategies (Soriano-Redondo et al. [SOURCE: Page_6]

85     2023). [SOURCE: Page_6]

86         Bio-logging technology, electronic devices attached to animals, provides vital insights into [SOURCE: Page_6]

87     their movements and behavior, informing wildlife conservation and management efforts (Joly [SOURCE: Page_6]

88     et al. 2019; Hindell et al. 2020; Davies et al. 2021). Population-level spatial distributions [SOURCE: Page_6]

89     derived from bio-logging data has aided in assessing the extent and impact of human-wildlife [SOURCE: Page_6]

90     interactions, such as interaction with fisheries and landscape fragmentation (Clay et al. 2019; [SOURCE: Page_6]

91     Boudreau et al. 2022). However, animals use different areas for specific purposes like [SOURCE: Page_6]

92     migration, foraging, or reproduction, emphasising each location's unique functions in their life [SOURCE: Page_6]

93     cycles. Hence it is important to identify how and why animals use specific areas, the relevance [SOURCE: Page_6]

94     of these sites in face of long-term climatic shifts, and exposure to existing and emerging human [SOURCE: Page_6]

95     pressures. [SOURCE: Page_6]

96         One of animals' most important activities is finding and consuming food. Movement is [SOURCE: Page_6]

97     necessary to forage for food and gain the energy needed to balance growth, body maintenance, [SOURCE: Page_6]

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.10.14.562344; this version posted October 17, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission. [SOURCE: Page_7]

98      reproduction, and physical activity (Pontzer & McGrosky 2022). Behavioural inference solely [SOURCE: Page_7]

99      based on location data may misrepresent the underlying importance of the area used. Measures [SOURCE: Page_7]

100     of daily physical activity, foraging intensity and efficiency can be used as indices to track [SOURCE: Page_7]

101     spatio-temporal variations in energy spent and gained across the environment and/or resource [SOURCE: Page_7]

102     exploitation. Accelerometer tags, record high-resolution data reflecting changes in speed due [SOURCE: Page_7]

103     to the movement of the animal and provide measurements of effort and relative energy [SOURCE: Page_7]

104     expenditure related to different behaviours and activities performed by individual animals, [SOURCE: Page_7]

105     including foraging (Wilson et al. 2020; Chimienti et al. 2022). By recording at high frequency [SOURCE: Page_7]

106     (> 1Hz), acceleration data capture behavioural activities not visible at coarser scale (e.g. prey [SOURCE: Page_7]

107     hunting events) and, thus, are extremely useful for explaining area-use variation. When coupled [SOURCE: Page_7]

108     with remote sensing data on environmental variability, accelerometer derived metrics can [SOURCE: Page_7]

109     explain the animal decisions behind area-use variation and inform management and [SOURCE: Page_7]

110     conservation. [SOURCE: Page_7]

111         The Bass Coast and Bass Strait waters are characterised by rich biodiversity, holding [SOURCE: Page_7]

112     significant ecological, societal, and economic importance for both the region and the state of [SOURCE: Page_7]

113     Victoria, Australia (Haddow et al. 2019). While a significant tourist and recreational [SOURCE: Page_7]

114     destination, the region provides critical breeding habitat for charismatic marine top predators. [SOURCE: Page_7]

115     Mainly, Phillip Island serves as a breeding area for approximately one-quarter of all seals in [SOURCE: Page_7]

116     Victoria (McIntosh et al. 2018), approximately 1,000,000 short-tailed shearwaters (Latine [SOURCE: Page_7]

117     name) visiting Australia, and the largest colony of Little Penguins (Eudyptula minor) consisting [SOURCE: Page_7]

118     of ~32,000 breeding adults (Sutherland & Dann 2012). Yet, the area is under climatic and [SOURCE: Page_7]

119     anthropogenic pressure (Hobday & Pecl 2014; Halpern et al. 2019) threatening this globally [SOURCE: Page_7]

120     Important Bird Area (IBA), and Marine Spatial Planning for the Bass Coast is currently under [SOURCE: Page_7]

121     development. Overall, the Bass Strait's ecological, economic, and historical significance [SOURCE: Page_7]

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.10.14.562344; this version posted October 17, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission. [SOURCE: Page_8]

122     underscores the need for its conservation and sustainable management to preserve its diverse [SOURCE: Page_8]

123     marine life, support regional economies, and maintain its cultural heritage. [SOURCE: Page_8]

124         The long-term population monitoring programme at the penguins' breeding colony of Phillip [SOURCE: Page_8]

125     Island has regularly monitored individual penguins’ foraging behaviour during their breeding [SOURCE: Page_8]

126     season with bio-logging technology, recording both horizontal and vertical movements. Little [SOURCE: Page_8]

127     penguins are high trophic level generalist predators feeding in the shallower part of the water [SOURCE: Page_8]

128     column (20-57 m), (Ropert-Coudert, Chiaradia & Kato 2006; Ropert-Coudert et al. 2006; [SOURCE: Page_8]

129     Chiaradia et al. 2007) in dynamic coastal environments (Carroll et al. 2018; Meyer et al. 2020; [SOURCE: Page_8]

130     Cavallo et al. 2020). Foraging performance (measured as trip duration, mass gain, and use of [SOURCE: Page_8]

131     prey encounters) can vary within and among years, between adjacent colonies, as well as with [SOURCE: Page_8]

132     environmental conditions, determining annual breeding success (Chiaradia & Nisbet 2006; [SOURCE: Page_8]

133     Ropert-Coudert, Kato & Chiaradia 2009; Saraux et al. 2016; Sánchez et al. 2018; Joly et al. [SOURCE: Page_8]

134     2022). [SOURCE: Page_8]

135         Identifying and managing the areas where little penguins concentrate their foraging efforts [SOURCE: Page_8]

136     is crucial to their survival and breeding performance. Although short-term studies have been [SOURCE: Page_8]

137     conducted (Carroll et al. 2016; Sánchez et al. 2018), there is a lack of understanding of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

138     long-term spatio-temporal variability of foraging intensity at sea, showing where, how and why [SOURCE: Page_8]

139     foraging hotspots develop. Based on a ten-year bio-logging dataset on little penguins, we [SOURCE: Page_8]

140     combine high-resolution bio-logging data from GPS-accelerometer loggers with metrics of [SOURCE: Page_8]

141     environmental variability. Specifically, we test how the variation in Sea Surface Temperature [SOURCE: Page_8]

142     (SST), thermocline, currents and water turbidity affect the foraging intensity of little penguins. [SOURCE: Page_8]

143     These variables provide information on changes happening at lower trophic levels, affecting [SOURCE: Page_8]

144     the abundance and distribution of prey consumed by little penguins. We predict different [SOURCE: Page_8]

145     effects of these environmental parameters. Higher SST and stratified waters result in higher [SOURCE: Page_8]

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.10.14.562344; this version posted October 17, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission. [SOURCE: Page_9]

146     predictability of prey patches (Cox et al. 2018) leading to reduced foraging effort and increased [SOURCE: Page_9]

147     efficiency (measured as average diving time and number of ondulations within dives over a [SOURCE: Page_9]

148     foraging trip) (Meyer et al. 2020). However, increased SST can negatively affect penguins [SOURCE: Page_9]

149     (Afán et al. 2015). Temperature above the 19-21 °C range can reduce foraging success (Carroll [SOURCE: Page_9]

150     et al. 2016). Water turbidity might make prey more difficult to detect if these marine predators [SOURCE: Page_9]

151     rely on visual cues for detecting and capturing their prey (Kowalczyk et al. 2015), as in shallow [SOURCE: Page_9]

152     diving seabirds such as shearwaters (Darby et al. 2022). Overall, how these environmental [SOURCE: Page_9]

153     conditions drive the spatio-temporal variability in foraging intensity across breeding seasons [SOURCE: Page_9]

154     and stages is not known. Finally, given the current discussion to implement Marine Spatial [SOURCE: Page_9]

155     Planning for the State of Victoria, Australia, we highlight the need for high resolution data to [SOURCE: Page_9]

156     responsible stewardship and conservation off Phillip Island to protect this valuable marine [SOURCE: Page_9]

157     ecosystem into the future. [SOURCE: Page_9]

158     Material and Methods [SOURCE: Page_9]

159         Penguin tracking and data preparation [SOURCE: Page_9]

160         This study covers 11 breeding seasons, from 2010 to 2020 (inclusive). During breeding [SOURCE: Page_9]

161     (Austral spring/summer, from August to February), the little penguin is a central-place forager [SOURCE: Page_9]

162     with foraging trips between 1 and 16 days (Saraux et al. 2011). Typically, their breeding season [SOURCE: Page_9]

163     can be divided into three stages: Incubation (lasting ~35 days), Guard (~2 weeks) during which [SOURCE: Page_9]

164     both parents alternate foraging trips at sea and Post Guard (~5-8 weeks), when older chicks [SOURCE: Page_9]

165     are left alone during the day and parents come back at night for feeding (Chiaradia & Nisbet [SOURCE: Page_9]

166     2006). Penguins were captured from their nest boxes and equipped with GPS-Accelerometer [SOURCE: Page_9]

167     loggers attached to the lower dorsal region of the bird using Tesa® tape. [SOURCE: Page_9]

168         Given the length of the study and the rapid developments in bio-logging technology within [SOURCE: Page_9]

169     these ten years, data were collected using different loggers (Little Leonardo, Japan, [SOURCE: Page_9]

170     TechnoSmart, Italy and WACU IPHC Strasbourg for accelerometer loggers; and Cat Track, [SOURCE: Page_9]

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.10.14.562344; this version posted October 17, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission. [SOURCE: Page_10]

171     and Technosmart for GPS loggers, and at varying sampling resolutions depending on logger [SOURCE: Page_10]

172     manufacture, battery and storage capacity, animals' breeding stage and length of the foraging [SOURCE: Page_10]

173     trips (Zimmer et al. 2011; Sánchez et al. 2018; Dupuis et al. 2023). Loggers were set to record: [SOURCE: Page_10]

174     i) GPS locations at an interval from 10 sec to 5 min, depending on logger type, season and [SOURCE: Page_10]

175     breeding stage (Dupuis et al. 2023), ii) underwater pressure (in millibars) or depth (in meter) [SOURCE: Page_10]

176     at 1 Hz and iii) tri-axial acceleration at 25 or 50 Hz. As penguins do not forage at night, only [SOURCE: Page_10]

177     GPS data between nautical dawn and dusk were analysed, generating diurnal "foraging [SOURCE: Page_10]

178     segments". GPS locations for each foraging segment were checked, filtered, and interpolated [SOURCE: Page_10]

179     at 15 min intervals (Dupuis et al. 2023). Step length was then calculated from interpolated [SOURCE: Page_10]

180     foraging segments using the R package MomentuHMM (McClintock & Michelot 2018). [SOURCE: Page_10]

181     Underwater pressure was converted in meters of depth and tri-axial acceleration data were [SOURCE: Page_10]

182     subsampled at 25 Hz. Both depth and acceleration data were treated and analysed as in [SOURCE: Page_10]

183     Chimienti et al. 2022. Specifically, the approach using the supervised machine learning [SOURCE: Page_10]

184     algorithm, Random Forest, developed in Chimienti et al. 2022 was used to infer the behaviours [SOURCE: Page_10]

185     little penguins performed while foraging at sea, such as hunting, swimming, descending and [SOURCE: Page_10]

186     ascending within the water column, porpoising and preening near the water surface (Chimienti [SOURCE: Page_10]

187     et al. 2022). GPS locations, diving depth, and resulting information from behavioural [SOURCE: Page_10]

188     classification were then matched by date and time. Since many dives could be performed within [SOURCE: Page_10]

189     the 15 minutes between the two consecutive GPS locations, for each time interval we calculated [SOURCE: Page_10]

190     the average maximum diving depth and time spent hunting indicating hunting effort. [SOURCE: Page_10]

201         Data analysis [SOURCE: Page_11]

202         The analysis aims at quantifying the drivers of the spatio-temporal variability in the foraging [SOURCE: Page_11]

203     intensity of little penguins across breeding seasons and stages. Hence only GPS locations where [SOURCE: Page_11]

204     penguins were diving were considered for further analyses. Given the spatio-temporal [SOURCE: Page_11]

205     discrepancy between the small movement scale of the little penguins (Figure S$_1$, Supporting [SOURCE: Page_11]

206     Information) and the scale of the available environmental data, the combined GPS dataset was [SOURCE: Page_11]

207     rasterised at the same resolution as the environmental data. Daily rasters were calculated at [SOURCE: Page_11]

208     10x^10 km considering the reproductive stage and recording for each cell the median values for [SOURCE: Page_11]

209     time spent hunting, maximum dive depth and step length, number of data points and number [SOURCE: Page_11]

210     of unique individuals. These datasets were then matched with the environmental variables. SST [SOURCE: Page_11]

211     values were averaged over weekly periods, given that mismatches between SST and the [SOURCE: Page_11]

212     distribution of predators suggest time lags effects (Grémillet et al. 2008; Ramírez et al. 2016). [SOURCE: Page_11]

213         We used Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs, mgcv package, (Wood 2011) to [SOURCE: Page_11]

214     investigate the relationships between foraging intensity, animals’ movement characteristics and [SOURCE: Page_11]

215     environmental conditions. Time spent hunting was used as a response variable, while [SOURCE: Page_11]

216     maximum diving depth, step length, bathymetry, thermocline depth and gradient, currents’ [SOURCE: Page_11]

217     speed, SST and ZSD were included as explanatory variables. Spatial coordinates were added [SOURCE: Page_11]

218     to the model to account for spatial autocorrelation, breeding stage and season were added as [SOURCE: Page_11]

219     fixed effects since we were interested in the between years comparison. Before modelling, all [SOURCE: Page_11]

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.10.14.562344; this version posted October 17, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission. [SOURCE: Page_12]

220     explanatory variables were checked for multi-collinearity. Thermocline depth and gradient [SOURCE: Page_12]

221     were modelled within the same spline to quantify the effect of the thermocline conditions on [SOURCE: Page_12]

222     foraging intensity. We performed model selection on the full model for the type of spline to [SOURCE: Page_12]

223     use; isotropic (s) versus the anisotropic (te, ti) splines. Data exploration showed variation in [SOURCE: Page_12]

224     SST values across breeding stages (see Results). Hence, we hypothesised that the relationships [SOURCE: Page_12]

225     with SST values were likely to change between breeding stages, and we used the “by” term [SOURCE: Page_12]

226     within the spline for SST adding the interaction with the breeding stage. Day was used as [SOURCE: Page_12]

227     grouping for random effects. Starting from the full model structure, we run models with all [SOURCE: Page_12]

228     possible variable combinations using the “MuMin” package [REF]. The model with the lowest [SOURCE: Page_12]

229     corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) was selected as best, and we reported and [SOURCE: Page_12]

230     considered the top 5 models as competing models. Models were run using the Tweedie [SOURCE: Page_12]

231     distribution family and checked for violations of model assumptions in terms of residual [SOURCE: Page_12]

232     autocorrelation, heterogeneity, and normality. All data manipulation and analysis were [SOURCE: Page_12]

233     performed in R statistical computing software version 4.2.2, (R Core Team 2022). [SOURCE: Page_12]

279         Discussion [SOURCE: Page_15]

280         We used a unique long-term (ten years) high-resolution bio-logging dataset to quantify how, [SOURCE: Page_15]

281     where, and why little penguins breeding on Phillip Island invest in their hunting activity in [SOURCE: Page_15]

282     relation to environmental conditions. We used satellite derived environmental paramters [SOURCE: Page_15]

283     indicating horizontal variability of surface waters (e.g. SST) as well as the vertical stratification [SOURCE: Page_15]

284     of the water (e.g. thermocline depth, water turbidity). Hence, we assessed the species' capacity [SOURCE: Page_15]

285     to adjust their behaviour in both horizontal and vertical space. Penguins increased their time [SOURCE: Page_15]

286     spent hunting in coastal, colder and more turbid waters while reducing spatial displacement [SOURCE: Page_15]

287     (shorter step lengths) and diving deeper; with a slower increase in time spent hunting below 10 [SOURCE: Page_15]

288     m of depth (max diving depth 74 m, a new record for the species – see Ropert-Coudert et al. [SOURCE: Page_15]

289     2006). Horizontal and vertical movements reveal distinct facets of behavior; horizontal [SOURCE: Page_15]

290     movements may respond to large-scale general environmental cues such as SST, while vertical [SOURCE: Page_15]

291     movements may respond to more localised prey presences and prey pursuit tactics (Wilson, [SOURCE: Page_15]

292     Ropert-Coudert & Kato 2002; Ropert-Coudert et al. 2006; Ramasco et al. 2015). While sample [SOURCE: Page_15]

293     sizes were unbalanced among years and relatively small in some years, due to the combination [SOURCE: Page_15]

294     of GPS and accelerometer data, the great inter-annual variation in little penguin’s spatial [SOURCE: Page_15]

295     distribution was further confirmed by the analysis of core and home ranges (50% and 95% UD) [SOURCE: Page_15]

296     over the same period based on the more robust and consistent GPS-only dataset (Dupuis et al. [SOURCE: Page_15]

297     2023). [SOURCE: Page_15]

298         Productive aquatic ecosystems can be characterised by turbid waters (Abrahams & [SOURCE: Page_15]

299     Kattenfeld 1997). High turbidity in coastal waters can also result from human-induced [SOURCE: Page_15]

300     activities, freshwater inflow, or wind-driven resuspension of sediments (Arendt et al. 2011; [SOURCE: Page_15]

301     Ortega et al. 2020), which is known to enhance productivity (Kowalczyk et al. 2015). Marine [SOURCE: Page_15]

302     predators, as little penguins, can use various sensory cues to detect and hunt their prey in turbid [SOURCE: Page_15]

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.10.14.562344; this version posted October 17, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission. [SOURCE: Page_16]

303     or dark waters (Davis et al. 1999; Darby et al. 2022). The effect of water visibility/turbidity on [SOURCE: Page_16]

304     prey detection and foraging in aquatic systems supports contrasting theories depending on [SOURCE: Page_16]

305     foraging strategies and type of prey hunted. High levels of turbidity can influence predator- [SOURCE: Page_16]

306     prey interactions by lowering prey capture and offering refuge from predation but also elevate [SOURCE: Page_16]

307     predation by enhancing contrast and providing abundant resources (Abrahams & Kattenfeld [SOURCE: Page_16]

308     1997; Ortega et al. 2020; Goździejewska & Kruk 2022). Hence, it is possible that, in turbid [SOURCE: Page_16]

309     waters, penguins either exploit the increased availability of nutrients acquiring more prey or [SOURCE: Page_16]

310     require longer time to locate and catch their prey. The effects of climate and human driven [SOURCE: Page_16]

311     habitat changes on water turbidity and how they affect trophic interactions remain understudied [SOURCE: Page_16]

312     (Goździejewska & Kruk 2022). Further work should be directed towards how this species [SOURCE: Page_16]

313     benefits from highly turbid areas and the related foraging success. [SOURCE: Page_16]

314         An increase in SST, marine heatwaves and changes in the predominance of the main currents [SOURCE: Page_16]

315     influencing the dynamics of the Bass Strait (the nutrient-poor South Australian Current, the [SOURCE: Page_16]

316     nutrient-rich sub-Antarctic Surface Water and the nutrient-poor East Australian Current) are [SOURCE: Page_16]

317     likely also to shift the trophic interactions within the area (Poloczanska et al. 2007; Afán et al. [SOURCE: Page_16]

318     2015; Oliver et al. 2017). SST distributions within the little penguins' foraging area varied [SOURCE: Page_16]

319     between years. Additionally, little penguins are exposed to warmer waters as the breeding [SOURCE: Page_16]

320     season progresses from spring to summer. Our analysis showed that hunting time decreased [SOURCE: Page_16]

321     with warmer waters, especially during the Guard and Post Guard stages (min-max SST 12- [SOURCE: Page_16]

322     18.4 ºC). During the breeding season, little penguins can switch foraging strategy, requiring [SOURCE: Page_16]

323     different hunting time, and targeting different types of prey and prey aggregations with varying [SOURCE: Page_16]

324     energetic content (Cavallo et al. 2020; Sutton & Arnould 2022). More dispersed and/or less [SOURCE: Page_16]

325     abundant prey in colder and less-stratified waters, or increased prey availability in warmer and [SOURCE: Page_16]

326     stratified waters (Meyer et al. 2020) can lead to changes in effort allocated to hunting activities. [SOURCE: Page_16]

327     An increase in SST beyond the range measured in this study is happening faster and more [SOURCE: Page_16]

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.10.14.562344; this version posted October 17, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission. [SOURCE: Page_17]

328     strongly than anticipated, reducing foraging success (Carroll et al. 2016) and negatively [SOURCE: Page_17]

329     affecting breeding success and mean laying date (Pulvirenti, Reina & Chiaradia 2023). [SOURCE: Page_17]

330         Environmental variables' relatively coarse spatial and temporal resolution may account for [SOURCE: Page_17]

331     some unexplained variation in our models, along with other unknown factors, such as vertical [SOURCE: Page_17]

332     prey distribution and true profitability, ie, prey capture success. Our estimate of time spent [SOURCE: Page_17]

333     hunting does not necessarily indicate a greater prey intake (Florko et al. 2023). During [SOURCE: Page_17]

334     breeding, little penguins must return to the colony to either attend the nest or feed for the chicks. [SOURCE: Page_17]

335     Hence, prey might differ when feeding for chicks vs. for themselves, due to different energy [SOURCE: Page_17]

336     requirements, higher need of proteins for chicks (Chiaradia et al. 2016). While little penguins [SOURCE: Page_17]

337     maximise foraging performance by concentrating efforts on larger quantities of prey (Sutton & [SOURCE: Page_17]

338     Arnould 2022), high nutritious contents (Chiaradia et al. 2012) and lower trophic levels [SOURCE: Page_17]

339     (Chiaradia et al. 2010), further investigation requires validating links between high-resolution [SOURCE: Page_17]

340     prey availability, prey energy content, energy expenditure and hunting intensity. Changing [SOURCE: Page_17]

341     environments, shifts across trophic levels as well as in energy balances (energy gain versus [SOURCE: Page_17]

342     energy expenditure) can ultimately affect breeding performance (Joly et al. 2022) and life [SOURCE: Page_17]

343     history strategies (Pontzer & McGrosky 2022). [SOURCE: Page_17]
