Groundwater storage (size measured by its depth) is the precipitation remain- ing after canopy interception, minus the sum of evapotranspiration and runoff, which are affected by change and variability in climate, vegetation and soil 60 properties (Kim & Jackson, 2012). Temperature influences the hydrologic cycle by increasing evaporation of usable surface water and plant transpiration. In turn, these changes can impact precipitation volumes, timing and intensity rates, as well as surface and groundwater flows and storage, which are essential for the survival of streams, lakes, wetlands and other ecosystems that depend on them, from 65 regional to local scales. Variations in these factors control the amount of water that reaches the surface, evaporates or seeps into the atmosphere, accumulates as snow or ice, infiltrates into the groundwater system, flows out of the ground and finally becomes the baseflow of streams and rivers (Kumar, 2012). In this way, aquifer recharge in Italy is particularly 70 sensitive to climate change, putting them in a critical situation in case of prolonged drought (Di Matteo et al., 2021) and with increasing rainfall extremes (Treppiedi et al., 2021). In Italy groundwater reserves are mostly unprotected from the consequences of climate change brought about by climate variability combined with increased water demand.                                                                                       75 In this context, historical reconstruction of groundwater resource data remains a challenge for environmental hydrogeology (McCallum et al., 2020). Ultimately, because predictions remain model- and scenario-dependent (Wu et al., 2020), global hydrology models are ineffective for planning of water management strategies, which can only be achieved and implemented locally at the aquifer scale. Also past hydroclimatic changes 80 and, in particular, groundwater–climate interactions are poorly understood at this scale (Saccò et al., 2021), not least because of the difficulty of reconstructing groundwater data Q2 over long periods (Zanchettin et al., 2019). To better invest in the future, we need to improve our understanding of how ground- water depth has responded to historical climatic changes, beyond human impacts (Li 85 et al., 2019). This study builds on the concept and methods used in a simple groundwater depth model to bridge the gap between the large amounts of knowledge exchanged in climate research and the paucity of data for historical modelling. For this purpose, the Groundwater REsource Model (GREM) was developed to estimate past values of annual groundwater depth for the Eastern Po Plain, where groundwater depth is supported by 90 moderate amounts of precipitation. The proposed model is sober because it relies on simplified inputs including large-scale teleconnection indices, a regional water balance proxy and a subregional storm intensity index. These indicators were chosen to over- come the limitations imposed by more complex, physics-based models, which would not have allowed reconstructing the groundwater depth time series in historical times due to 95 the lack of past hydrogeological input data. In this way, GREM helps to better understand how groundwater interacts with the regional water cycle and responds to the complex vicissitudes of how past climate sustained the Eastern Po Plain aquifers. The North Atlantic Oscillation is the most important pattern influencing weather and climate variability and precipitation extremes in Europe, from synoptic to millennial timescales (Casanueva et al., 2014). It consists of a mean sea-level pressure dipole pattern between the subtropical and subpolar areas of the North Atlantic and characterizes the intensity and location of the westerly flow. On multidecadal timescales, the Atlantic          180 Multidecadal Variability, which is a warming/cooling pattern of sea-surface temperatures in the North Atlantic with periodicities of ~60 years, is also known to influence European surface temperatures and regimes over Europe, including northern Italy (Sutton & Dong, 2012). The Annual Storm Severity           185 Index Sum exemplifies storm anomalies as an annual sum of monthly integer scores from 1 (stormy event) to 4 (extraordinary storm event).
