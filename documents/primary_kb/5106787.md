Ongoing climate change (CC) is already impacting the distribution and prevalence of different Fusarium species. This can lead to shifts in the dominant pathogens causing FER and GER under various cropping systems, potentially altering the mycotoxin profiles observed in maize grain. In this context, the use of agricultural CC mitigation practices could also be questioned in regard to population pathogens evolution. Understanding the modulation of FER and GER pathogens populations to the combined pressures of CC and evolving agricultural practices is essential for predicting the emergence and/or spread of epidemics. The aim of the present study was to describe the representativeness of Fusarium species on maize kernels in various agronomical contexts, including soil type, irrigation management, and geographical location, over a period of 2 years, using a metabarcoding approach targeting the specific EF$_1$ alpha region. This description will allow for the identification of key factors driving the risk of FER and GER and associated mycotoxin contamination. @ExChange [SOURCE: Page_1]

Conclusion and perspectives This study provided information on the representativeness of Fusarium species on maize in southern France. F. graminearum, F. proliferatum and F. verticillioides were the most predominant species found, as commonly described in European countries as been responsible for ear and kernel rots in maize. Fusarium community assemblies greatly varied according to location, confirming that environmental factors play a major role in shaping their composition. The preliminary results suggest that soil type is likely to affect Fusarium communities present on maize kernels at harvest. This observation could be attributed to variations in the inherent conduciveness of different soil types to support fungal persistence and facilitate re-infection in subsequent cropping cycles. Irrigation practices do not seem to influence representativeness of Fusarium species. [SOURCE: Page_1]
