57      1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

58      Changes in the valley bottoms in the plains and plateaus of Western Europe that occurred [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   during the Holocene are increasingly well known. Geomorphological and paleoenvironmental [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   research revealed the development of (i) mostly authigenic sedimentation during the Middle [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   Holocene and (ii) from the end of the Iron Age on, destabilization of the slopes, which led to [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   the progressive silting of the valley bottoms by overbank silt. These stages correspond to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   progressive shift in the dominance of climatic over anthropogenic forcing in the functioning [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   of fluvial systems (e.g., Brown et al., 2018; Lespez et al., 2015; Notebaert et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   Research conducted in the Paris Basin has informed the construction of these stages with [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   numerous studies (e.g., Antoine et al., 2000; Beauchamp et al., 2017; Chaussé et al., 2008; [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   Granai and Limondin-Lozouet, 2014; Le Jeune et al., 2012; Lespez et al., 2015, 2008; Orth et [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   al., 2004; Pastre, 2018; Pastre et al., 2006, 2002). Regional and local disparities in Europe are [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   due to the high spatial variability of morphosedimentary responses to climatic forcing and to [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   the impact of the different environmental management practices implemented by successive [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   societies during the late Holocene (Broothaerts et al., 2014; Brown et al., 2018; Houben et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   2013; Notebaert et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_5]

73      Beyond this general framework, studies of the impact of hydraulic developments on small [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   hydrosystems are less numerous. Although the consequences of building small dams and [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   weirs are now increasingly better known (e.g., Csiki and Rhoads, 2010; Skalak et al., 2009), [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   studies of the long-term consequences of these structures are still rare in Europe (Downward [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   and Skinner, 2005; Kalicki et al., 2020; Maaß et al., 2019; Poeppl et al., 2015), whereas in [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   northeastern America, such studies are more systematic (e.g., Donovan et al., 2016, 2015; [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   Dow et al., 2020; Merritts et al., 2013, 2011; Schenk and Hupp, 2009; Walter and Merritts, [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   2008). Nevertheless, the focus is more often on the hydro-sedimentary consequences for [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   current systems than their impacts on the fluvial systems during the centuries in which they [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   operated. The forced sedimentation upstream of the hydraulic structures is often highlighted [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   whereas the chronology and nature of sedimentary transformations caused by the creation of [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   reservoirs and the leats and their hydro-sedimentary management remain poorly studied [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   (Beauchamp et al., 2017; Dow et al., 2020) and their ecological impact remains to be [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   documented (Inamdar et al., 2021). From this point of view, the weight of long-term urban [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   pressure on the dynamics of river systems is a major issue. Indeed, historical and geohistorical [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   research has revealed the increasing impact of urban demand on the growing hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   pressure in Europe (Brown, 1997; Mauch & Zeller, 2008), and in France (Guillerme, 1983; [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   Frioux et al., 2010). In the Paris region, the impacts of mills on small rivers are evidence for [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   the influence of urban growth on the increase in hydraulic pressure since the Middle Ages, [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   sometimes far from urban centres (Benoit, 2000; Benoit et al., 2003; Berthier, 2007; [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   Dmitrieva et al., 2018; Jugie et al., 2017; Le Roux, 2010; Rouillard et al., 2011) . Indeed, in [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   the medieval period, Paris was by far the largest city in Europe and its population tripled from [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   60,000 to 80,000 inhabitants in 1200, to 250,000 a century later (Bourlet and Layec, 2013). [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   The city depended on agricultural products for its survival and, as J.-M. Moriceau (1994) [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   stated, “Paris ate its nearby countryside”. The demand for power and food resources has [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   affected the entire river system, especially the 1st to 3rd order in Strahler's classification, as [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   shown by historical research (Benoit et al., 2003). Then, the impact of urbanisation in the 20th [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   and 21st centuries as stream straightening and the imperviousness of catchment areas are now [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   increasingly studied and understood (e.g. Chin, 2006; Jugie et al., 2018; de Milleville et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   2022). However, paleoenvironmental research on the consequences of hydraulic structures [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   and the impact of urbanisation remains in its infancy, especially in the case of small rivers of [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   order 1 to 3, which comprise most of the hydrographic network of many cities, 75% in Paris [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   region. [SOURCE: Page_6]

106      The aim of the present study was to assess the hydro-sedimentary and ecological [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   consequences of the transformation of watersheds by agropastoral practices and hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   developments of small sub-urban rivers over time. The Mérantaise River offers an opportunity [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   to examine the environmental history of a small river that has been an integral part of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

110   metabolism of Paris since medieval times. Our specific objective was to identify the [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   transformations of the hydrosystem over the last three millennia and to discuss the [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   consequences of the different types of pressure to put the role of biophysical and historical [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   legacies into perspective with respect to contemporary dynamics. [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   2.1. Morphological and hydrological characteristics of the Mérantaise river basin [SOURCE: Page_8]

129      The Mérantaise river has a moderate slope (0.76%) (Fig. 1C) and an average width of just [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   over 4 m. Its river basin is largely covered by loess silts. The river is gradually incised into the [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   Tertiary bedrock that is characteristic of the central Paris Basin. Upstream, the plateaus are [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   covered by Lozère sands (Burdigalian), frequently mixed with Pleistocene loess silts due to [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   colluvial processes. These are clayey sands with quartz grains, coarse and poorly classified [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   (0.4-2 mm). Their mineralogy is characterised by low feldspar content (< 5%), high iron [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   hydroxide content (72-77%) and by an association of heavy minerals composed notably of [SOURCE: Page_8]

7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   zircon (20%) and rutile (7%) (Larue and Etienne, 2000). The valley then cuts into the [SOURCE: Page_9]

137   ferruginous millstone clays of Montmorency (Upper Rupelian), and then into white or [SOURCE: Page_9]

138   yellowish nodular marly limestone (Étampe limestone, Upper Rupelian). The valley next [SOURCE: Page_9]

139   incises the Fontainebleau sands (Upper Rupelian) which are the most developed formation of [SOURCE: Page_9]

140   the geological bedrock and reach a thickness of 65-70 m. These are fine sands (median 0.15 [SOURCE: Page_9]

141   mm), yellowish white, well sorted, siliceous (97-99% silica). Their mineralogical signatures [SOURCE: Page_9]

142   can be distinguished from Lozère sands by their high tourmaline content (60%) and low [SOURCE: Page_9]

143   zircon (3.5%) and rutile (2.5%) contents (Larue and Etienne, 2000). Finally, over its last few [SOURCE: Page_9]

144   kilometres, the Mérantaise river flows over the impermeable green clays of the Lower [SOURCE: Page_9]

145   Rupelian (Fig. 1C). The bottom of the river bed is mostly sandy gravels (D50 and D84 are [SOURCE: Page_9]

146   respectively on average 26 and 52 mm, Table 1), although clay slabs are very frequently [SOURCE: Page_9]

147   exposed. [SOURCE: Page_9]

148      The hydrological regime of the Mérantaise river is of the rainfall-evaporation type. It is [SOURCE: Page_9]

149   influenced by the degraded oceanic climate of the central Paris Basin but also by urbanisation [SOURCE: Page_9]

150   of the watershed (Jugie et al., 2018). The average annual precipitation is 706.7 mm. Intense [SOURCE: Page_9]

151   floods following thunderstorms occur regularly in summer, but high water usually occurs in [SOURCE: Page_9]

152   winter, between December and March, while low water usually occurs in late summer, [SOURCE: Page_9]

153   between August and September. The mean annual discharge of the Mérantaise at Châteaufort [SOURCE: Page_9]

154   (58% of the watershed) is 0.1 m^3.s-1, i.e., a specific discharge of 4.9 l.s-1.km². Over a period of [SOURCE: Page_9]

155   nine years (2012-2021), the maximum discharge recorded was 4.0 m^3.s-1 in 2016 while the [SOURCE: Page_9]

156   minimum flow recorded was 0.01 m^3.s-1. The Mérantaise is a low energy river, its specific [SOURCE: Page_9]

157   power calculated at Châteaufort is 16.0 W.m-² (Table 1). [SOURCE: Page_9]

225   3. Materials and Methods [SOURCE: Page_14]

226    3.1. Reconstruction of the sedimentary architecture of the alluvial plain [SOURCE: Page_14]

227     The alluvial fill geometry was first analysed by geophysical prospecting. Several electrical [SOURCE: Page_14]

228   resistivity tomography (ERT) profiles were carried out using an Abem Terrameter LS system [SOURCE: Page_14]

229   composed of 64 electrodes. At the site of the former Mérancis pond, a 94 m long west-east [SOURCE: Page_14]

230   transverse profile was made of the valley (Fig. 3). The east-west profile of Ors mill is 167 m [SOURCE: Page_14]

231   long (Fig. 4). With an inter-electrode spacing of one metre and a Wenner-Schlumberger type [SOURCE: Page_14]

232   electrode configuration, the investigation reached a depth of six metres. The topography of the [SOURCE: Page_14]

233   profiles was surveyed using differential GPS (Trimble Geoexplorer). Data processing was [SOURCE: Page_14]

234   performed with Res^2Dinv software (Loke and Barker, 1996) using the least squares inversion [SOURCE: Page_14]

235   method (Milsom, 2011; Reynolds, 2011). Between three and five iterations were required to [SOURCE: Page_14]

13 [SOURCE: Page_14]

236   obtain a root mean square error of less than 5% between the measured and calculated profile. [SOURCE: Page_15]

237   For better comparison, both ERT profiles were brought to the same value. At the Mérancis site, the [SOURCE: Page_15]

238   range of values varies between 7.43 and 99.7 ohm.m, while at the Ors mill site the values are higher, [SOURCE: Page_15]

239   up to 154 ohm.m. This difference can be explained by the greater presence of coarse sand and the [SOURCE: Page_15]

240   presence of large millstone blocks, which are not found upstream, i.e., at Mérancis. The ERT profiles [SOURCE: Page_15]

241   allow the identification of large sedimentary units with a metric resolution due to the measurement [SOURCE: Page_15]

242   protocol used, but only coring and trench, which allow a fine description of the sediments, allow a [SOURCE: Page_15]

243   resolution of the order of a centimetre. These geophysical profiles were then used to identify the [SOURCE: Page_15]

244   position of the cores needed to reconstruct the architecture of the alluvial plain and the [SOURCE: Page_15]

245   succession of sedimentary facies. A total of nine cores, two auger holes, and four trenches [SOURCE: Page_15]

246   were completed in the valley floor between October 2017 and September 2020 (Table 2). The [SOURCE: Page_15]

247   nine 50-mm diameter cores were drilled to a depth of six metres using a percussion corer [SOURCE: Page_15]

248   (Cobra TT) (Table 2). The nature of the sediments was described and interpreted using [SOURCE: Page_15]

249   standard methods of description in a fluvial context (Brown, 1997). Analyses were performed [SOURCE: Page_15]

250   on two cores, one at each site, hereafter called reference cores C0_MER and C5_MDO (Table [SOURCE: Page_15]

251   2). [SOURCE: Page_15]

283    3.4. Magnetic susceptibility [SOURCE: Page_18]

284      The presence of magnetic minerals in sediments may be due to the lithology of the [SOURCE: Page_18]

285   watershed, to the remobilisation of surface sediments, to their physical characteristics (size, [SOURCE: Page_18]

286   shape, nature) and to deposition processes (Dearing et al., 1996). Magnetic susceptibility [SOURCE: Page_18]

287   (MS) was measured to look for evidence of pedogenesis, redox phases, organic matter [SOURCE: Page_18]

288   accumulation, or magnetic mineral-enriched detrital phases (Dearing et al., 1996; Verosub [SOURCE: Page_18]

289   and Roberts, 1995). Volume MS (expressed as χvol. x 10-5 SI) was measured with a [SOURCE: Page_18]

290   Bartington Susceptibility Meter MS2F probe at 0.5 cm intervals in a double pass (Table 2). [SOURCE: Page_18]

291   Values were averaged and then multiplied by two to correct for the effect of the probe used. [SOURCE: Page_18]

293   3.5.Geochemical analyses [SOURCE: Page_18]

294      The high frequency X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF) method (0.5 cm sampling [SOURCE: Page_18]

295   step) was used to estimate the relative concentration of major elements at the surface of the [SOURCE: Page_18]

296   reference cores (Table 2). Analyses were performed using an ITRAX scanner (Cox analytical [SOURCE: Page_18]

297   systems). The uncalibrated values of the identified elements (Ti, K, Rb, Zr, Si, Fe, Mn, Ca, Sr, [SOURCE: Page_18]

298   Inc/Coh) are expressed in counts per second (cps) (Supplementary data S$_1$). The silicon (Si) [SOURCE: Page_18]

299   signal was always poor in both reference cores. The titanium (Ti) signal was used to identify [SOURCE: Page_18]

300   terrigenous detrital inputs, and hence erosive processes in the watershed, because it is a stable [SOURCE: Page_18]

17 [SOURCE: Page_18]

301   signal that is not affected by weathering or redox processes (Arnaud et al., 2012; Lu et al., [SOURCE: Page_19]

302   2017). The calcium (Ca) signal was used to identify carbonate inputs. As these can be of [SOURCE: Page_19]

303   terrigenous or authigenic origin, the Ca/Ti ratio was used to distinguish them. The iron (Fe) [SOURCE: Page_19]

304   signal was analysed to identify potential variations in redox conditions, and the Fe/Ti ratio [SOURCE: Page_19]

305   was calculated to distinguish detrital sources (Davison, 1993; Kylander et al., 2011). Values [SOURCE: Page_19]

306   were then compared to the mean to identify a relative increase or decrease. [SOURCE: Page_19]

308   3.6.Pollen and Non-Pollen Palynomorphs (NPPs) [SOURCE: Page_19]

309      The palynological study was performed on 42 samples taken from the C0_MER borehole [SOURCE: Page_19]

310   at 10-cm intervals between a depth of 65 cm and 456 cm. With respect to the established age- [SOURCE: Page_19]

311   depth model, the pollen sequence begins at c. 13 CE and ends at c. 1550 CE. The average [SOURCE: Page_19]

312   temporal resolution was estimated to be 37 years per pollen spectrum (minimum 18 years, [SOURCE: Page_19]

313   maximum 87 years). For the analysis of pollen and NPPs (algae, fungal remains, microfossils, [SOURCE: Page_19]

314   other unidentified palynomorphs), the same standard protocol applied to 1.5 to 2 grams of [SOURCE: Page_19]

315   sediment involved sieving to 200 µm, KOH 10% hot, densimetric separation (SPT density [SOURCE: Page_19]

316   1.9). Pollen grains were identified using pollen keys, pollen atlases (Moore et al., 1991; Beug, [SOURCE: Page_19]

317   2004; Reille, 1992, 1995, 1998) and the pollen collection of the Physical Geography [SOURCE: Page_19]

318   Laboratory. Identification of NPPs was based mainly on van Geel (1978, 2001), van Geel and [SOURCE: Page_19]

319   Aptroot (2006), Gelorini (2011), Cugny (2011) and the Non-Pollen Palynomorph Image [SOURCE: Page_19]

320   Database (NPP-ID, Shumilovskikh et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_19]

321      Counts were made until a pollen sum of at least 400 grains (pteridophyte spores, [SOURCE: Page_19]

322   indeterminate pollen grains and NPPs excluded) was obtained, 150 of which were outside the [SOURCE: Page_19]

323   dominant taxon, and at least 20 pollen taxa. The NPPs were counted in parallel with the [SOURCE: Page_19]

324   pollen counts on the same microscope slides. Concentrations were calculated using the [SOURCE: Page_19]

325   volumetric method (Cour, 1974). Pollen percentages were calculated from a sum excluding [SOURCE: Page_19]

1000   subsequently abandoned. The sluice gates were opened and only the dike remained, which [SOURCE: Page_54]

1001   became which no longer hindered the passage of the sediments, hence the slight decrease in [SOURCE: Page_54]

1002   the sedimentation rate compared to the previous period. The former pond was transformed [SOURCE: Page_54]

1003   into a marsh formed by a hydraulic wasteland dominated by alder trees. [SOURCE: Page_54]

1004 [SOURCE: Page_54]

1005   5.3.3. The impact of hydraulic engineering on ecological and hydro-sedimentary dynamics [SOURCE: Page_54]

1006      The 2nd millennium CE in Europe was characterised by the scale of hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_54]

1007   developments (Brown et al., 2018; Lewin, 2010). Research conducted in the Mérantaise [SOURCE: Page_54]

1008   Valley shows that pressure was very strong in the proximity of large cities due to the demand [SOURCE: Page_54]

1009   for food and the need for significant investments. In the Paris region, even if the development [SOURCE: Page_54]

1010   of mills began in antique times and continued during the Carolingian period, the pressure [SOURCE: Page_54]

1011   really only became strong in the 11th and 12th centuries. At the end of this period, the rivers [SOURCE: Page_54]

1012   were fully equipped (Benoit et al., 2003; Rouillard et al., 2011). At the time, the Mérantaise [SOURCE: Page_54]

1013   river had at least the eight mills that still functioned in the 18th century, and geoarchaeological [SOURCE: Page_54]

1014   studies confirm similar pressure in the Paris region, for example on the Crould river [SOURCE: Page_54]

1015   (Bensaadoune et al., 2005; Pastre, 2018). To the pressure caused by the construction of [SOURCE: Page_54]

53 [SOURCE: Page_54]

1016   hydraulic infrastructure, was added that of the potentially irrigable grasslands that they could [SOURCE: Page_55]

1017   contain. Indeed, in the few decades leading up to 1150, in response to growing demographic [SOURCE: Page_55]

1018   pressure, valley bottoms were acquired by aristocrats and monastic orders (Benoit et al., [SOURCE: Page_55]

1019   2003). This led to an increase in productive activities with notable hydrological and [SOURCE: Page_55]

1020   ecological consequences. The emergence of cultivated fields and hay meadows favoured the [SOURCE: Page_55]

1021   development of ruderal plants and weeds, but restricted the riparian zone as early as the High [SOURCE: Page_55]

1022   Middle Ages, as observed in many European regions (Broothearts et al., 2014; Brown et al., [SOURCE: Page_55]

1023   2018). On the Mérantaise river, anthropic pressure did not lead to the disappearance of [SOURCE: Page_55]

1024   wetlands since they were maintained before being artificially favoured by the construction of [SOURCE: Page_55]

1025   reservoir from the 11th century on. Indeed, our study shows that at Mérancis, the ponds were [SOURCE: Page_55]

1026   surrounded by hydrophilic vegetation while at Ors mill, marsh vegetation continued to grow [SOURCE: Page_55]

1027   at the margins of the pond. However, the wetlands and aquatic environments were gradually [SOURCE: Page_55]

1028   degraded. Milling reduced the oxygenation of rivers and increased sedimentation of poorly [SOURCE: Page_55]

1029   decomposing organic matter. At the same time, the development of crops and especially of [SOURCE: Page_55]

1030   livestock in the valley bottoms led to an increase in the nutrient load of small water bodies [SOURCE: Page_55]

1031   (Grönlund et al., 1986). In addition, it is likely that these ponds were also used for fish [SOURCE: Page_55]

1032   farming, as was the case of most the medieval ponds in northern France (Benoit et al., 2003; [SOURCE: Page_55]

1033   Rouillard et al., 2011). These activities stimulated eutrophication and favoured the [SOURCE: Page_55]

1034   development of cyanobacteria recorded in the Mérantaise basin between the 11th and 14th [SOURCE: Page_55]

1035   centuries. In many cases, these cyanobacteria radically modified the wetland and aquatic [SOURCE: Page_55]

1036   ecosystems of the medieval period (e.g., Bradshaw et al., 2005), which explains why in the [SOURCE: Page_55]

1037   Paris region, as early as 1678, retting was forbidden due to significant pollution of the rivers, [SOURCE: Page_55]

1038   and in particular eutrophication, and the presence of nauseating odours (Berthier, 2007). [SOURCE: Page_55]

1039      From a hydro-sedimentary point of view, the establishment of dams and mills was [SOURCE: Page_55]

1040   accompanied by an increase in silty sedimentation in the valley bottoms of northwestern [SOURCE: Page_55]

54 [SOURCE: Page_55]

1041   Europe during the Middle Ages (Brown, 2009; Brown et al., 2018; Notebaert and Verstraeten, [SOURCE: Page_56]

1042   2010). This phenomenon can be explained by the crossing of a threshold in the efficiency of [SOURCE: Page_56]

1043   hydro-sedimentary connectivity rather than by the increase in cultivated land subject to soil [SOURCE: Page_56]

1044   erosion (Houben et al., 2013; Verstraeten et al., 2017). In small streams, the highest [SOURCE: Page_56]

1045   sedimentation rates frequently coincided with maximum density of mills, which was the case [SOURCE: Page_56]

1046   on the Mérantaise and Normandy streams (Beauchamp et al., 2017a; Lespez et al., 2015). [SOURCE: Page_56]

1047   Unfortunately, sedimentary fills of ponds fed by small river systems are still too rarely studied [SOURCE: Page_56]

1048   to ensure reliable assessment. Such investigations, which are mostly performed in the United [SOURCE: Page_56]

1049   States, have been based on topographic approaches and channel geometry. For example, dams [SOURCE: Page_56]

1050   constructed on certain rivers in the mid-Atlantic region in the colonial era are known to have [SOURCE: Page_56]

1051   accelerated sedimentation in valley bottoms, resulting in a rise in alluvial plains of between 2 [SOURCE: Page_56]

1052   and 5 m (Merritts et al., 2011; Walter and Merritts, 2008). However, other authors (Donovan [SOURCE: Page_56]

1053   et al., 2016) pointed out that the role of dams is more difficult to establish, as the aggradation [SOURCE: Page_56]

1054   of alluvial plains is ubiquitous and is not necessarily linked to former reservoirs. Observations [SOURCE: Page_56]

1055   made on the Mérantaise River are in line with the second line of thought: alluvial plain [SOURCE: Page_56]

1056   aggradation continued at a slightly higher rate but with no break between earlier and later [SOURCE: Page_56]

1057   aggradation rates, and with alluvial plains remaining entirely submerged during annual floods [SOURCE: Page_56]

1058   (Jugie et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_56]

1059      Most mills on small streams in northwestern Europe were built on courses set up as [SOURCE: Page_56]

1060   diversions from a weir to a reach (leat) that required most of the flow to turn the mill wheel at [SOURCE: Page_56]

1061   the expense of the existing channel (Lespez et al., 2015; Brown et al. 2018). The few [SOURCE: Page_56]

1062   available studies show that the increase in sedimentation occurred at the same time as these [SOURCE: Page_56]

1063   systems were established (Beauchamp et al., 2017a; Maaß and Schüttrumpf, 2019), as [SOURCE: Page_56]

1064   observed in the later period of Ors mill. This is because the weirs increase the level of the [SOURCE: Page_56]

1065   upstream water tables, thereby facilitating overflow and flooding of the alluvial plain during [SOURCE: Page_56]

55 [SOURCE: Page_56]

1066   floods (Maaß and Schüttrumpf, 2019). This increase in overflow frequency helps explain the [SOURCE: Page_57]

1067   aggradation of medieval alluvial plains under the influx of overflow silt, especially since this [SOURCE: Page_57]

1068   operation was considered by medieval and modern societies to enrich valley bottom meadows [SOURCE: Page_57]

1069   with nutrients (Benoit et al., 2003). However, after a certain period of time, the raising of the [SOURCE: Page_57]

1070   banks reduced the frequency of overflow plus, conversely, increased incisions and erosion of [SOURCE: Page_57]

1071   the banks, due to the increase in shear forces accentuated by dredging - as observed on the [SOURCE: Page_57]

1072   Geul in Germany (Maaß and Schüttrumpf, 2019) and on the Seulles in Normandy, France, [SOURCE: Page_57]

1073   (Beauchamp et al., 2017a). The late implementation of the bypass system at Ors mill explains [SOURCE: Page_57]

1074   why these consequences were not fully realized on this reach of the Mérantaise and why [SOURCE: Page_57]

1075   overflow capacities persist despite the strong alteration of channel geometry (Jugie et al., [SOURCE: Page_57]

1076   2018). [SOURCE: Page_57]

1082   6. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_58]

1083   This article contributes to ongoing reflection on the hydro-sedimentary and ecological [SOURCE: Page_58]

1084   consequences of the transformation of a small watershed through agropastoral practices and [SOURCE: Page_58]

1085   the building of hydraulic structures during the recent Holocene. Reconstruction of the hydro- [SOURCE: Page_58]

1086   sedimentary evolution of the Mérantaise valley shows a general trend that matches that [SOURCE: Page_58]

1087   observed in Western Europe with an acceleration in sedimentary aggradation in the last three [SOURCE: Page_58]

1088   thousand years. The first aggradation lasted from the end of the Bronze Age to the beginning [SOURCE: Page_58]

1089   of the Iron Age and is probably explained by the retreat of the forest cover linked to [SOURCE: Page_58]

1090   cultivation of the watershed. During the Gallo-Roman period, aggradation continued but the [SOURCE: Page_58]

57 [SOURCE: Page_58]

1091   development of marshy environments shows that if soil erosion rate increased it did not reach [SOURCE: Page_59]

1092   an irreversible threshold, in contrast to what can be observed in many small valleys in [SOURCE: Page_59]

1093   northwestern Europe. Thus, wetlands continued to occupy a large part of the valley floor [SOURCE: Page_59]

1094   during the Middle Ages. Inherited from the previous periods or artificial (mill ponds), they are [SOURCE: Page_59]

1095   under pressure from human activities linked to both the investment capacity and needs of [SOURCE: Page_59]

1096   Paris. [SOURCE: Page_59]

1097      From the High Middle Ages (7th c. CE), hemp retting was added to agropastoral activities [SOURCE: Page_59]

1098   and cereal cultivation, and for the first time, here we present sedimentary indicators of this [SOURCE: Page_59]

1099   activity in addition to the evidence provided by pollen records. The research also [SOURCE: Page_59]

1100   demonstrates the consequences of the hydrological engineering on the ecological functioning. [SOURCE: Page_59]

1101   Beyond the sedimentary aggradation and the transformation of the vegetation, it underlines [SOURCE: Page_59]

1102   the ecological consequences for the remaining and the artificial wetlands. The impact of [SOURCE: Page_59]

1103   retting and the agro-pastoral development is at the origin of mostly organic pollution [SOURCE: Page_59]

1104   accompanied by eutrophication of the aquatic environments. This attests to the transformation [SOURCE: Page_59]

1105   of wetland vegetation, riverine vegetation, and water quality over the last 1,500 years and is [SOURCE: Page_59]

1106   linked to the intensified use of small rivers in northwestern Europe, which was very clear in [SOURCE: Page_59]

1107   the study area because of the early emergence of urban demand and the increasing need to [SOURCE: Page_59]

1108   supply the city with energy, plant and food resources, long before small rivers were directly [SOURCE: Page_59]

1109   affected by urbanisation. [SOURCE: Page_59]
