47   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

48   Consumers have become increasingly aware of fruit quality on the fruit market (Fearne [SOURCE: Page_4]

49   et al., 2006), paying particular attention to the appearance and internal quality of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   fruit (Berna et al., 2005). Fruit producers are making efforts to satisfy consumer [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   preferences, which may sometimes be in conflict with their own interests since there is [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   often a trade-off between the quantity of fruit harvested and fruit quality (Bevacqua et [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   al., 2019). In addition, different satisfaction criteria are considered by different actors in [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   the food system, but these criteria have rarely been included in a unified framework [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   (Tijskens and van Kooten, 2006). [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   In the case of peach, two of the most important quality attributes are fruit size and sugar [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   concentration. These attributes evolve during fruit growth and are strongly influenced [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   by agricultural practices (Minas et al., 2018). On the one hand, the irrigation regime [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   during the growing season is the major driver of tree and fruit water status (Rahmati et [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   al., 2015). It also impacts the dynamics of fruit sugars, the concentrations of which [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   generally increase with deficit irrigation (Conesa et al., 2021; Razouk et al., 2021;). On [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   the other hand, fruit thinning influences the carbon status and transport inside the plant, [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   decreasing the competition for resources between growing fruit and other plant organs. [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   Thinning intensity is positively correlated with peach fruit size and soluble solids [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   accumulation (i.e., fruit dry mass) in the fruit pulp (Inglese et al., 2002; Wang et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   2022). [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   Consumers rarely have direct access to the product just after harvest. Consequently, [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   producers and wholesalers are forced to store fruit for a certain amount of time. During [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   this post-harvest stage, fruit quality-related attributes change under the influence of [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   storage conditions: transpiration and respiration enhance fruit mass loss, mainly under [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   the effect of storage temperature and relative humidity, whereas sugar concentrations [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   continue to evolve in the fruit pulp (Bovi et al., 2018; Casagrande et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   The problem of fruit waste and loss during storage adds another degree of complexity to [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   this issue. Consumers may consider that excessive fruit shrinkage or softening during [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   the post-harvest stage is unacceptable, making the fruit unmarketable and generating [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   food waste (Nunes and Emond, 2007). Fungal infections such as brown rot caused by [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   Monilinia spp. in stone fruit develop on fruit during the pre-harvest stage, but [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   symptoms generally appear after harvest, with a notable incidence for the post-harvest [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   actors (Martini and Mari, 2014). The infection depends both on fruit characteristics at [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   harvest and storage conditions (Gibert et al., 2009; Bernat et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   In this context, knowledge must be integrated into a framework that bridges the fruit [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   pre- and post-harvest stages (Tijskens and Schouten, 2014). Mathematical modeling [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   can be helpful for this purpose. It could be used to study how fruit quality-related [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   attributes and fruit loss due to fungal disease infections are affected by pre-harvest [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   practices and storage conditions. Data-driven black-box modeling of fruit quality and [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   defects using sensor data during pre or post-harvest is common and has benefitted [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   from technological advances (Li et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2022). However, process-based [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   modeling is an alternative modeling route (Xiao and Li, 2022). In the latter category, [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   agricultural) such as irrigation and thinning on the development of individual fruit [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

92   model developed by Allen et al. (2005) simulates plant functioning and the growth of [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   individual organs, but the model does not account for sugar development. The [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   QualiTree model (Lescourret et al., 2011) allows the simulation of important attributes [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   such as sugar concentrations and fruit surface conductance to water vapor, which [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   post-harvest epidemiological studies help design control strategies to reduce brown rot [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   loss and sugar dynamics, as well as the development of brown rot. Then, the [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   management variables that result in the optimal value of an aggregated objective that [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   2. Modeling framework [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   The proposed modeling framework (Fig. 1) combines process-based and statistical [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   approaches. The entire model runs at a daily scale as of the full bloom date until the [SOURCE: Page_7]

130   end of post-harvest fruit storage. The framework includes a pre-harvest stage, with the [SOURCE: Page_7]

131   simulation of the fruit characteristics under the influence of horticultural practices [SOURCE: Page_7]

132   during the growing season, and a post-harvest storage stage in which fruits are [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   submitted to various storage conditions. The QualiTree model simulates the seasonal [SOURCE: Page_7]

134   dynamics of mass, sugar concentrations and surface conductance to water vapor at the [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   tree scale for the fruit of each fruiting unit (Lescourret et al., 2011; Mirás-Avalos et [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   al., 2011) by explicitly considering the effects of horticultural practices (irrigation [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   regime, fruit thinning and pruning) and meteorological conditions (Section 2.1). At [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   harvest, the simulated values of the fruit properties are used in turn as inputs of two [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   post-harvest models. The first one simulates fruit mass loss (through the processes of [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   transpiration and respiration) and sugar dynamics during storage (Casagrande et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   2021). The second one evaluates the expected time-to-appearance of brown rot [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   symptoms of nectarine during storage under the influence of pre-harvest conditions [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   and storage temperature (Section 2.3). The outputs of these two post-harvest models [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   are the fruit fresh mass, the mass loss during storage, the concentration of each sugar [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   inside the fruit pulp, and the expected time of appearance of brown rot symptoms [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   during storage. [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   2.1. Modeling the development of fruit quality-related characteristics during the [SOURCE: Page_10]

164   growing season: QualiTree [SOURCE: Page_10]

165   The QualiTree model runs on a daily basis, from bloom until the end of the fruit- [SOURCE: Page_10]

166   growing season. The model describes the tree as a set of interacting objects: fruiting [SOURCE: Page_10]

167   units (composed of fruit, leafy shoots and stem wood), old wood (trunk and branches), [SOURCE: Page_10]

168   coarse roots, and fine roots. All these objects are represented in a 3D tree architecture, [SOURCE: Page_10]

169   which is a model input. The model uses a source-sink approach to simulate the growth [SOURCE: Page_10]

170   of the tree parts in terms of dry masses. This includes carbon assimilation [SOURCE: Page_10]

171   (photosynthesis), growth demands, allocation rules and exchanges within the tree. The [SOURCE: Page_10]

172   processes are explicitly controlled by radiation, air temperature and humidity. The [SOURCE: Page_10]

173   effects of water stress on photosynthesis and leafy shoot growth, and the accumulation [SOURCE: Page_10]

174   of water in the fruit are also simulated by the model. QualiTree calculates the water [SOURCE: Page_10]

175   flow and water status in the plant architecture (water potential of stems and leaves), [SOURCE: Page_10]

176   which are important for the above-mentioned processes. For the sake of simplicity, [SOURCE: Page_10]

177   this calculation is not very dependent on the fruit load – only marginally through fruit [SOURCE: Page_10]

178   transpiration. The boundary conditions for plant water status are fixed at the root [SOURCE: Page_10]

179   collar through the hourly water potential Ψr,hourly. The hourly water potential is given [SOURCE: Page_10]

180   as model input, reflecting the combined effect of rainfall and irrigation regimes during [SOURCE: Page_10]

181   the growing season. [SOURCE: Page_10]

182   QualiTree simulates the time course of several fruit quality-related traits at the fruiting [SOURCE: Page_10]

183   unit level and during the growing season, including fruit size, dry mass proportion in [SOURCE: Page_10]

184   fruit flesh, sugar concentrations and fruit surface conductance. Individual sugar [SOURCE: Page_10]

9 [SOURCE: Page_10]

185   concentrations were aggregated into a sweetness index for each individual fruit as a [SOURCE: Page_11]

186   linear combination of sugar concentrations based on the sweetness rating for each [SOURCE: Page_11]

187   sugar relative to that of sucrose. The simulated fruit masses were also used to assess [SOURCE: Page_11]

188   the size code for each individual fruit, based on the OECD international standard. The [SOURCE: Page_11]

189   sizing of fruit using size codes (from D to 4A) makes it possible to select which fruits [SOURCE: Page_11]

361   It was assumed that fruit growers and packers (wholesalers/post-harvest actors, etc.) [SOURCE: Page_20]

543   5. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_32]

544   5.1. The modeling framework satisfactorily integrates the effects of pre-harvest 545   practices and storage conditions on fruit quality development and fruit loss during 546   storage [SOURCE: Page_32]

547   In this study, a modeling framework was designed to simulate, for each individual [SOURCE: Page_32]

548   fruiting unit of a tree, the pre- and post-harvest temporal dynamics of fruit quality [SOURCE: Page_32]

549   traits and the appearance of brown rot disease during storage, under the influence of [SOURCE: Page_32]

550   pre-harvest practices and storage conditions. Rather than focusing on new practices [SOURCE: Page_32]

551   that can have a significant impact, such as pre-harvest bagging to protect fruit from [SOURCE: Page_32]

552   disease (Buthelezi et al., 2021) or UV light irradiation to improve fruit quality (Peng et [SOURCE: Page_32]

553   al., 2022), the emphasis was on a combination of classic pre- and post-harvest [SOURCE: Page_32]

554   practices as the object of optimization. The model was designed and calibrated for the [SOURCE: Page_32]

555   case of nectarine, but it could be extended to other fruits. It could also be [SOURCE: Page_32]

556   supplemented by considering quality traits other than mass and sweetness. Process- [SOURCE: Page_32]

557   based models of fruit acidity in pre- and post-harvest stages have been proposed for [SOURCE: Page_32]

558   banana and tested for different fruit loads and potassium inputs (Etienne et al., 2015, [SOURCE: Page_32]

559   2014). Process-based models of fruit firmness loss have been designed that include the [SOURCE: Page_32]

560   effect of post-harvest conditions (Gwanpua et al., 2022), but they do not include pre- [SOURCE: Page_32]

561   harvest factors, with the exception of the model of Xiao and Li (2022) that includes [SOURCE: Page_32]

562   this possibility without testing it. Much work needs to be done to assess the responses [SOURCE: Page_32]

563   of these models to different pre- and post-harvest practices, their adaptability to [SOURCE: Page_32]

31 [SOURCE: Page_32]

564   different fruit species, and the possibility of incorporating them into our modeling [SOURCE: Page_33]

565   framework. [SOURCE: Page_33]

566   The results of the sensitivity analysis and model exploration showed that fruit quality [SOURCE: Page_33]

567   criteria at the end of the storage period are mainly influenced by pre-harvest practices, [SOURCE: Page_33]

568   namely the irrigation regime during the growing season and the fruit load. A medium [SOURCE: Page_33]

569   or high level of water stress in the last stage of fruit growth improved the average fruit [SOURCE: Page_33]

570   sweetness index, in agreement with Perez-Pastor et al. (2007) on apricot and Atay et [SOURCE: Page_33]

571   al. (2019) on nectarine, but at the expense of the fruit average mass, as pointed out in [SOURCE: Page_33]

572   several studies on peach (Crisosto et al., 1994; Alcobendas et al., 2012). On the [SOURCE: Page_33]

573   contrary, the application of water stress during the fruit growing stages that are not [SOURCE: Page_33]

574   sensitive to water deprivation, such as the pit hardening one, did not impact the yield. [SOURCE: Page_33]

575   This is in line with the findings on regulated deficit irrigation (Fereres and Soriano, [SOURCE: Page_33]

576   2007). The thinning intensity was shown to influence the average fruit mass, probably [SOURCE: Page_33]

577   by modifying the intensity of competition for carbon between fruits, as already [SOURCE: Page_33]

578   pointed out by Inglese et al. (2002) and de la Rosa et al. (2016) on peach, but it had no [SOURCE: Page_33]

579   influence on the average fruit sweetness at the end of storage. These results on pre- [SOURCE: Page_33]

580   harvest practices were confirmed when searching for the pre- and post-harvest [SOURCE: Page_33]

581   scenario that offers the best compromise in terms of fruit quality and quantity. Water- [SOURCE: Page_33]

582   stressed regimes and moderate fruit thinning were selected when the sweetness index [SOURCE: Page_33]

583   was considered as the most important criterion, while the well-irrigated conditions [SOURCE: Page_33]

584   during the last stage of fruit growth were preferred when fruit mass was the most [SOURCE: Page_33]

585   important quality criterion. However, these findings could be refined by revising the [SOURCE: Page_33]

32 [SOURCE: Page_33]

586   model to take account of the possible effect of thinning on water potentials in the [SOURCE: Page_34]

587   event of severe water stress, which is tricky since there is “a common but far from [SOURCE: Page_34]

588   universal decrease in water potential with increasing crop load” (Sadras and [SOURCE: Page_34]

589   Trentacoste, 2011; see Lopez et al. (2019) for a recent study on nectarine where no [SOURCE: Page_34]

590   relationship was found). [SOURCE: Page_34]

591   The sensitivity analysis showed that pre-harvest practices, although they may change [SOURCE: Page_34]

592   the fruit surface conductance to water vapor at harvest, had no influence on the fruit [SOURCE: Page_34]

593   mass loss during storage. Conesa et al. (2021) obtained very close values of mass loss [SOURCE: Page_34]

594   during storage for nectarine (about 6%) for three well-differentiated irrigation [SOURCE: Page_34]

595   treatments. In our study, this loss, which is a matter of concern since fruit may show [SOURCE: Page_34]

596   signs of visual shriveling starting at 3-10% of mass loss (Nunes and Emond, 2007), [SOURCE: Page_34]

597   was principally related to storage conditions. The latter influence both the vapor [SOURCE: Page_34]

598   pressure deficit, which is the main driver of fruit transpiration, and the fruit respiratory [SOURCE: Page_34]

599   process (Xanthopoulos et al., 2017). In contrast, storage conditions had no major [SOURCE: Page_34]

600   influence on the average sweetness of the fruit at the end of storage, in accordance [SOURCE: Page_34]

601   with Aubert et al. (2014), although fruit sweetness may change at high storage [SOURCE: Page_34]

602   temperatures (Brizzolara et al., 2018; Casagrande et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_34]

603   The losses related to the appearance of brown rot during storage were particularly [SOURCE: Page_34]

604   influenced by storage temperature, as well as by pre-harvest practices and the [SOURCE: Page_34]

605   cumulative prevalence of brown rot at harvest. The simulation results supported the [SOURCE: Page_34]

606   important role of the interaction between storage temperature and time in preventing [SOURCE: Page_34]

607   brown rot during post-harvest (Garcia-Benitez et al., 2020). They also revealed that [SOURCE: Page_34]

33 [SOURCE: Page_34]

608   the irrigation regime and fruit thinning could lower the incidence of brown rot during [SOURCE: Page_35]

609   storage since they can influence the magnitude of the fruit cracks, a major driver of [SOURCE: Page_35]

610   fruit sensitivity to brown rot (Oliveira Lino et al., 2016) that was accounted for by a [SOURCE: Page_35]

611   proxy, the fruit mass, in the brown rot model. This is in agreement with Perez-Pastor [SOURCE: Page_35]

612   et al. (2007) and Atay et al. (2019) who found lower fruit loss due to fungal attacks [SOURCE: Page_35]

613   under deficit irrigation in apricot and nectarine. The pre-harvest practices and [SOURCE: Page_35]

614   meteorological conditions could also play a role in the spreading of brown rot [SOURCE: Page_35]

615   infections during the growing season (Bellingeri et al., 2018), and their effects should [SOURCE: Page_35]

616   thus be further included in the model. [SOURCE: Page_35]

617   Predicting instead of setting the harvest date, as was done in this work for the sake of [SOURCE: Page_35]

618   simplicity, would be a progress. Environmental conditions and pre-harvest practices [SOURCE: Page_35]

619   are able to influence the timing and the evolution of the fruit ripening process (Marini [SOURCE: Page_35]

620   et al., 1991), and to thus modify the optimal harvest time. Moreover, fruits can show [SOURCE: Page_35]

621   maturity differences inside the same tree, in which case it would affect the storage [SOURCE: Page_35]

622   potential and the evolution of quality traits during fruit storage (Tijskens et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_35]

623   5.2. Factors governing the choice of the optimal pre- and post-harvest scenarios in [SOURCE: Page_35]

624   terms of fruit quality and quantity [SOURCE: Page_35]

625   The optimal scenarios – in terms of fruit quality and quantity – identified through the [SOURCE: Page_35]

626   modeling framework varied according to the importance assigned to the quality [SOURCE: Page_35]

627   criteria (weights). The optimization process pointed out the existence of a trade-off [SOURCE: Page_35]

628   between the average fruit sweetness and the fruit yield at the tree scale, in line with [SOURCE: Page_35]

629   experimental evidence (Marini, 2003). This trade-off influenced the values of the [SOURCE: Page_35]

34 [SOURCE: Page_35]

630   performance criteria for the selected optimal scenarios. For example, a small average [SOURCE: Page_36]

631   fruit mass was observed when sweetness was the most important criterion, while the [SOURCE: Page_36]

632   fruit sweetness was very low when fruit size was favored. When sweetness was the [SOURCE: Page_36]

633   most important criterion, the selected storage conditions consisted in high temperature [SOURCE: Page_36]

634   and low relative humidity, which favored fruit mass loss and, consequently, the [SOURCE: Page_36]

635   enhancement of sugar concentration in fruit pulp, while low temperature and high [SOURCE: Page_36]

636   relative humidity, which minimize fruit mass loss, were selected when the fruit size [SOURCE: Page_36]

637   was given high priority. [SOURCE: Page_36]

638   The value of the performance score was expressed on an arbitrary scale, but it can be [SOURCE: Page_36]

639   further translated into monetary value since monetary return is one of the major [SOURCE: Page_36]

640   drivers of the behavior of growers and post-harvest actors (Zhao et al., 2017). This [SOURCE: Page_36]

641   monetary value could consider the consumer’s willingness to pay for quality criteria, [SOURCE: Page_36]

642   which has however rarely been studied (e.g., for the US: Parker (1993)). However, [SOURCE: Page_36]

643   despite the consideration of quality criteria that are important for the consumer, the [SOURCE: Page_36]

644   study principally reflected the vision of fruit growers and post-harvest actors whose [SOURCE: Page_36]

645   aim, it is assumed, is to maximize a performance score that was, by construction, [SOURCE: Page_36]

646   highly dependent on fruit yield. The optimal performance scores were the highest [SOURCE: Page_36]

647   when the importance of fruit mass was the highest, in line with the expectations of [SOURCE: Page_36]

648   these actors of the fruit supply chain. [SOURCE: Page_36]

649   Finally, the performance scores obtained under different pre- and post-harvest [SOURCE: Page_36]

650   conditions may also have been influenced by the utility functions associated with [SOURCE: Page_36]

651   singular quality traits and their related parameters (Equation 3 for fruit mass and [SOURCE: Page_36]

35 [SOURCE: Page_36]

652   sweetness). These effects deserve further investigation since the consumer’s [SOURCE: Page_37]

653   perception of fruit quality can depend on other factors such as fruit acidity and [SOURCE: Page_37]

654   firmness (Crisosto and Day, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_37]

655 [SOURCE: Page_37]

656   6. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_37]

657   In this study, a new modeling framework was presented that is able to simulate the [SOURCE: Page_37]

658   interactions between pre- and post-harvest processes under the influence of several [SOURCE: Page_37]

659   conditions during these two stages. The value of this work lies in its original approach, [SOURCE: Page_37]

660   but to be used extensively, the model needs to be consolidated and tested in different [SOURCE: Page_37]

661   situations, including different meteorological years and different ways of storing, since [SOURCE: Page_37]

662   these situations vary greatly between countries. [SOURCE: Page_37]

663   Despite these limitations, the model showed its ability to integrate several [SOURCE: Page_37]

664   ecophysiological and epidemiological processes in the pre- and post-harvest [SOURCE: Page_37]

665   continuum for the case of nectarine. Thus, it could be used to explore different [SOURCE: Page_37]

666   combinations of pre- and post-harvest conditions under different climatic scenarios, [SOURCE: Page_37]

667   simultaneously optimizing fruit quality and yield, and aptitude to storage (to avoid [SOURCE: Page_37]

668   fruit loss). Moreover, this framework can help to design solutions that satisfy the [SOURCE: Page_37]

669   expectations of the different actors in the fruit supply chain and should be used as a [SOURCE: Page_37]

670   tool for dialogue between these actors. [SOURCE: Page_37]
