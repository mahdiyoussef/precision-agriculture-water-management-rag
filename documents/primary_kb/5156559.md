Where to measure water quality? Application to nitrogen pollution in a catchment in France [SOURCE: Page_2]

DESTANDAU François ZAITER Youssef [SOURCE: Page_2]

ABSTRACT Information on the water quality of rivers can be used to judge the effectiveness of past policies or to guide future environmental policies. Consequently, the location of water quality monitoring stations (WQMSs) plays an important role in river pollution control. In the 2000s, a literature developed on the optimization of WQMS location to identify pollution hot spots, average quality, or to minimize the detection time of a potential source of accidental pollution. This article is part of a new literature aimed at localizing WQMSs in order to optimize the economic value of information (EVOI) generated by water quality monitoring networks (WQMNs). The field of study is a catchment in northeastern France where the purpose of quality measurement is to define a policy of reduction of agricultural nitrogen fertilizers in order to reach the standard of 50 mg/l of nitrate at the WQMS. Agro-hydrological and economic models estimate the net benefit of input reduction depending on the location of the WQMS on the basis of different assumptions concerning the ecological damage generated by nitrate. We show that the magnitude of the ecological damage and, consequently, the perception of the contamination generated by nitrate in water, play a decisive role on the optimal location of the WQMS, as well as on the benefit of the economic optimization of locations, compared to traditional optimization. Locating WQMSs in a way that maximizes EVOI will be more attractive for very high or very low levels of damage. However, in this context, linking damage to nitrate concentration or to concentration coupled with riparian population density alone will have little impact. [SOURCE: Page_2]

### 1. Introduction

Water quality measurement was implemented little by little as of the 1950s (Harmancioglu et al., 1999). The aim was to obtain an initial overview of river quality by taking samples at several arbitrarily chosen points. For a long time, the number, location and frequency of measurements were based on practical and subjective considerations, without any post-evaluation of the merits of the choice (Strobl and Robillard, 2008). It was not until the 2000s that the literature on the optimal location of water quality monitoring stations (WQMSs) and, thus, the formation of optimal monitoring networks (WQMNs) emerged. Destandau and Point (2000) optimized a WQMN in order to identify pollution hot spots, Alvarez-Vazquez et al. (2006) established WQMN in order to determine the average quality of the rivers, whereas Telci et al. (2009) considered that the location of the WQMSs is optimal when the detection time of potential accidental pollution is minimized. The optimal location of a WQMS thus depends on the aim of the network. Park et al. (2006) thus [SOURCE: Page_2]

imagined a WQMN that optimized a multi-criteria function that met different objectives: watershed representativeness, compliance with quality standards, monitoring specific to certain uses or certain pollution sources, etc. [SOURCE: Page_3]

However, none of this literature takes the purpose for which this information is intended into consideration: Who uses this data, to do what and with what impact on social welfare? In short, what is the economic value of this information? The economic value of information (EVOI) is defined as the increase in the expected value of making the best choice with information compared to making the best choice without that information (Keisler et al., 2013). Specifically, in a context where a Decision Maker (DM) must choose between different actions whose generated welfare will depend on a poorly known state of nature, additional information about the state of nature will increase the probability that the DM will choose a more suitable action. The economic value of this information will be measured by the increase in welfare resulting from this more appropriate decision. This is referred to as a Bayesian method because the additional information allows us to correct a priori probabilities on the states of nature into a posteriori probabilities. Different authors have studied the EVOI of a predefined water quality monitoring network (Bouma et al., 2008; Bouma et al., 2011; Destandau and Diop, 2016; Wu and Zheng, 2013). Based on this observation, Destandau and Zaiter (2020a, 2020b) proposed a new way to optimize the establishment of WQMSs by proposing locations that maximize EVOI. This is what they refer to as the economic optimization of WQMNs, as opposed to the "physical" optimization that only considers hydrological objectives, like in the literature cited above. In the case of economic optimization, the impact of the location of a WQMS on the actions implemented by a DM and the resulting welfare are taken into account. The authors develop a theoretical model to show that the benefit of economic optimization over physical optimization depends on the context. In this article, we apply the economic optimization method to the location of WQMSs in a catchment in northeastern France (Souffel catchment). The objective of the WQMN is to monitor the nitrate concentration in order to establish a policy to reduce nitrogen inputs in the five agricultural areas of the catchment. An agro-hydrological model is used to measure the reduction of inputs necessary to reach the standard of 50 mg/l of nitrate at the WQMSs. The location of the WQMSs will be chosen so as to maximize the net benefit of this policy. To do this, the cost of the input reduction for farmers and the ecological damage generated by the presence of nitrate on the whole catchment is estimated according to different calculation assumptions. We can therefore observe the optimal locations of the WQMSs according to the different social welfare assumptions and compare them to the current WQMS locations. This article proposes the first application of the method of economically optimal WQMSs locations described only theoretically until now. Furthermore, this work aims to measure the real benefit (absolute and relative) of this location method over the current WQMS location method on this catchment. Finally, a measure of sensitivity of the result of the perception of the damage generated by the pollution makes it possible to analyze the impact of this "welfare" variable on the benefit of the economic optimization. 2.1 Decision process on the “Souffel” [SOURCE: Page_3]

The catchment is flat (average slope: 2.4 ± 2.2%) with a total of 79 km of rivers. The crop types of this catchment are homogeneously distributed and constitute 84.5% of the total area (Corine Land Cover 2012; v^18.5.1; http://land.copernicus.eu). The Souffel catchment encompasses four rivers: the Souffel and its two tributaries, the Leisbach and the Musau, plus the Kolbsenbach (a tributary of the Leisbach). [SOURCE: Page_4]

Each of the Souffel’s tributaries has one WQMS, whereas the Souffel has three, in particular, to measure the effect of the two wastewater treatment plants (WTP) on water quality. The spatial and temporal logic of the water agency's measurements is representative of the 90th percentile. In other words, over the whole catchment area, approximately 10% of the concentrations are higher than the one measured and 90% are lower. It is a relative logic of hot spots since the extreme measurements are not taken into account. In Fig. 1, we also see that the catchment is divided into five "agricultural sub-catchments" to indicate where the nitrogen matter from the five agricultural areas is found. The industries in the catchment are linked to the WTPs, so industrial pollution is included in the urban pollution. The information provided by the WQMSs according to their location will be used by a Decision Maker (DM) to implement actions that will generate a social benefit. In our application, the DM considers the nitrate concentrations measured in each agricultural sub-catchment to determine the intensity of the reduction of nitrogen inputs that will be imposed in each agricultural area in order to comply with the 50 mg/l standard at each WQMS. This decision will generate costs for the farmers and an ecological benefit as a result of less nitrate in the whole catchment. The method consists of determining the locations of the WQMSs so that social welfare will be maximized when 50 mg/l is reached, represented here by the net benefit generated by the nitrate reduction. To do this, we need to know the impact of nitrogen input reductions on nitrate in the water (Section 2.2), and then the economic impact of this reduction (Section 2.3). [SOURCE: Page_4]

2.2 Agro-hydrological model [SOURCE: Page_5]

Knowing the impact of the location of the WQMS on the reduction of inputs requires a model of the input-concentration relationship. Models focus either, from a static point of view, on the impact of the reduction in fertilizer application, or, from a dynamic point of view, on the application schedule and the dynamic of nitrate transfers toward the river network (EU Sciences HUB, 2014). The input-concentration relationship is highly variable over time and closely linked to meteorological conditions and soil type: inputs are applied at specific periods during the year; the nitrogen surplus is leached in the fall under the plots and then flows into the rivers by horizontal flow from the catchments towards the river when it rains; and the conversion of the mass into nitrate concentration directly depends on the flow that also varies over time. Nevertheless, even if some models like SWAT (Rasolomanana, 2012) attempt to take these temporal variations into account, the approach developed in this work to compare costs and benefits is based on average annual values, whether it be a question of river flows (Fig. A.1) or of the export of nitrate from the catchments to the river. This degree of simplification remains sufficiently realistic for the study catchment because the monthly and spatial variations in nitrate concentrations are low for the six Rhine-Meuse Water Agency (AERM) stations, leading to a homogeneity of the nitrate transfer within the catchment itself. The model that linearly links the quantity of nitrogen applied in the five agricultural areas to the nitrate mass that arrives in the catchment, broken down into 500-m homogeneous reaches, is defined below. [SOURCE: Page_5]

2.2.2. Nitrate from urban and natural sources The average mass of nitrogen discharged every day by the four WTPs is provided by the Rhine- Meuse Water Agency on the basis of self-monitored data on its website (eau-rhin-meuse.fr). For the year 2017, these nitrogen quantities were: 0.9 kg/day (in K21); 4.92 kg/day (in L11); 25.99 kg/day (in S22); and 6.26 kg/day (in S14). These quantities are converted into equivalent nitrate to obtain nitrate flows in mg/s (Sumelius et al., 2005). The nitrate flow discharged by these WTPs is then transferred upstream from reach to reach, undergoing a reduction via the self-purifying capacity of the environment. Based on the expertise of the Rhine-Meuse Water Agency for river networks in Eastern France, a reduction of 5% (value used by the Rhine-Meuse Water Agency within the framework of the Pegase model (Everbeck et al., 2019)) of the nitrate mass every 500 m was adopted. According to Verchère (2010), the nitrate present in the surface waters also has natural sources linked to the nitrogen cycle, estimated at between 3 mg/l to 5mg/l. Based on this study, a contribution of 4 mg/l is associated with this geochemical background. [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 2.3. Economic model

The economic model aims at estimating the total ecological damage generated by nitrate on the Souffel catchment as a function of the nitrate level in the different river reaches or the ecological benefits of a reduction in nitrate concentration. It also estimates the cost of a reduction in farm N inputs for each agricultural area via a reduction in yield. [SOURCE: Page_7]

2.3.1. Costs linked to the reduction of nitrogen inputs Farm N inputs make it possible to increase crop yield. Consequently, the cost of a reduction in these inputs can be calculated by the decrease in crop yield. This estimation method has been implemented for over 30 years, especially in the literature from the Scandinavian countries. Within the framework of the Helsinki Convention, a reduction target of 50% of the nutrients was fixed to reduce eutrophication in the Baltic Sea. This target led to numerous studies, like Gren (1991) on Laholm Bay in Sweden, and Helin (2006) in Southwestern Finland. Our aim was not to estimate the decrease in yield for a specific N input reduction target but, instead, to obtain a continuous function that would allow us to estimate the impact on yield at every level of input reduction. Crop yield as a function of input quantity obviously depends on the crop, the type of fertilizer (mineral, organic), the proportions in which the input is applied and the weather conditions. In this paper, we adopted the scenario of a homogeneous crop like corn that would cover 80% of the agricultural area of the Souffel catchment. In Canada, the University of Guelph (Niemeyer, 2020) estimated the evolution of corn yield according to the quantity of nitrogen inputs over several years. This study, as well as a study on actual fertilization practices and corn yields for the Souffel catchment (Martin and Baudet, 2017), made it possible to estimate the average yield of a hectare of corn as a function of the quantity of nitrogen fertilizer applied^1 (see Table C.1), and to then predict the loss of benefit or cost, depending on the reduction of nitrogen inputs, as follows (see Fig. C.1): [SOURCE: Page_7]

In fact, if we can directly quantify additional costs on the production of drinking water, for example (Price and Heberling, 2018), then the recreational uses of water, or even a certain existence value that can be attributed to good quality water, can appear to be more subjective. Damage is linked to the impairment of use values, like the additional cost of producing drinking water, or non- use values like the recreational uses of water. In France, the estimations of the OECD (2012) or Aquamoney (2010) are good examples. At a smaller scale, other studies on water bodies focused on the impact of nitrates under conditions of high vulnerability, like the pollution of groundwater coupled with other pollutants, e.g., pesticides, or an impact on health due to the use of polluted water to produce drinking water (Rinaudo et al., 2005; Khader et al., 2013). The damage proposed in these studies was normalized to obtain a range of damage per inhabitant, and converted into “2020 euros” in order to be used in this study on the Souffel. The damage was then multiplied by the population of the Souffel catchment (61,248 inhabitants). The range of damage D obtained, taken from the highly different contexts of the studies cited above, was analyzed to prioritize the results in order to determine those that overestimate and those that underestimate the damage on the Souffel (see Table D.1). ### 3.1. Economically optimal location of the WMQSs

The DM has five action levers, namely, nitrogen input reductions 𝑁     ̃𝑖 in each agricultural area 𝑖. Each of the five decisions will be guided by the observation of the nitrate concentration in each of the five agricultural sub-catchments, specifically, the part of the rivers that lies in each agricultural area. As a result, only five WQMSs will be relevant for this policy, one per agricultural sub-catchment. As shown in Equation (6), the location 𝐿𝑖 of the five WQMSs will advise the DM as to the nitrate concentration 𝐶𝑖 of each sub-catchment. The DM will respond by determining the reduction intensity of nitrogen inputs, 𝑁̃𝑖 , needed to reduce concentrations to the maximum threshold of 50 mg/l. These reductions will impact the nitrate concentration over all of the catchment reaches and, consequently, the net benefit calculated as the difference between the ecological benefit and the costs to farmers. [SOURCE: Page_9]

3.3 Positioning WQMSs so as to maximize the net benefit [SOURCE: Page_11]

In our case study, the DM will consider only the nitrogen inputs of the five agricultural areas in order to reach the 50 mg/l standard at the WQMS. Thus, only one WQMS is necessary on the upstream Souffel. The locations of the WQMSs that maximize the net benefit under four damage assumptions - total 2M or 4M damage with unifactor or bifactor damage (see Section 2.3.2), and the concentrations obtained after input reduction - are shown in Appendix E. The damage assumptions have no impact on the location of the WQMS of the downstream Souffel (Fig. E.1). As is the case with the current WQMN (Fig. 1), the WQMS is located on the reach where the concentration is maximal. On the Musau, with the current WQMN, a zone where the concentration is greater than 50 mg/l persists upstream. However, taking the damage into account, regardless of the hypothesis, the WQMS is located where the concentration is maximal in order to maintain levels below 50 mg/l throughout. In fact, the weak flow of this river makes each nitrogen input that much more harmful. On the upstream Souffel, the current WQMN consists of two WQMSs that "frame" the largest WTP in the catchment. When the WQMN is optimized to reduce nitrogen inputs alone, only the WQMS downstream of the WTP remains. This is the only sub-catchment that maintains concentrations above 50 mg/l with a high range of total damage. This is due to the presence of the WTP that alone contributes a significant portion of the nitrate to the water (Fig. 1 and Fig A.2). It should be noted that the removal of this WTP and the construction of a new WTP on the downstream Souffel where the flow is much more greater are planned. For the Kolbsenbach and the Leisbach, the WQMS is located on the reach where the concentration is maximal when the damage is high (Fig. E.1), so that the 50 mg/l standard can be met everywhere. On the other hand, the area where concentrations exceed 50 mg/l is extended when the damage is low, compared to the current WQMN (Fig. 1). For this purpose the WQMSs are moved further upstream or downstream. [SOURCE: Page_11]

### 3.4. Benefit of the optimal location compared to the current location of the WQMS

Regardless of the WQMS location, it is possible to estimate the net benefit of a reduction in agricultural nitrogen inputs necessary to reach 50 mg/l at these monitoring stations (Tables 2 and 3). On the basis of these figures, an absolute benefit is calculated as the difference between the net benefit of the optimized WQMN and the current WQMN, and a relative benefit is calculated as the percentage of increase of the net benefit with the optimized WQMN (Table 4). [SOURCE: Page_12]

In Table 4, a level of total intermediary damage (3M) is added to better understand how the optimization of the benefit of the location of the WQMS evolves. It appears that the choice between a unifactor and bifactor damage hypothesis has little impact. The magnitude of the benefit remains the same. This is probably due to the fact that downstream, the vulnerability effect linked to high population density (Fig. D.1) is offset by the dilution effect of a stronger flow (Fig. A.1). The result would be different if the pollution abatement issue was greater upstream due to the presence of a swimming, fishing or water supply area. A fine-tuning of the damage hypotheses is therefore not always useful. On the other hand, an increase in the total damage initially decreases the benefit of the optimization, and then increases it later on. [SOURCE: Page_12]

### 4. Conclusion

Water quality is not homogeneous over time and space in rivers. Consequently, information in terms of quality can only partially depend on the places and times of the measurements. From a spatial point of view, in the 2000s, the literature focused on the optimization of the location of quality measurement stations (WQMSs) in order to identify hot spots, average quality, or to minimize the time necessary to detect potential accidental pollution. However, this literature ignores the environmental and economic consequences of decisions that may depend on the location of measuring stations. This article is part of a new literature that aims to localize WQMSs in order to optimize the economic value of information (EVOI) generated by water quality monitoring networks (WQMN). The field of study is a catchment in northeastern France where quality measurement is aimed at defining a policy of reduction of agricultural nitrogen fertilizers in order to reach the standard of 50 mg/l of nitrate at the WQMS. Agro-hydrological and economic models are used to estimate the net benefit of input reduction depending on the location of the WQMS. Several orders of magnitude of the total damage generated by nitrate are compared, as well as two hypotheses on the marginal damage, a [SOURCE: Page_13]

unifactor damage hypothesis with damage that depends only on the nitrate concentration and a bifactor damage hypothesis that also depends on the density of the riverside population. We show that by reducing agricultural nitrogen inputs to 50 mg/l on the current WQMSs, about 10% of the rivers maintain a concentration level above this standard. This logic of locating WQMSs at the 90th percentile aims to identify pollution hot spots without taking extreme values into account. The location of the WQMSs obtained by an optimization of the EVOI is strongly linked to the order of magnitude of the total damage and, thus, to the perception of the contamination generated by the presence of nitrate. For a very high level of damage, the WQMSs will be positioned in such a way as to further reduce pollution; for a low level of total damage, the location of the WQMSs will lead to a lower pollution abatement requirement. The benefit of the economic optimization of the WQMN compared to the traditional method will also strongly depend on the order of magnitude of the total damage. The benefit will be higher for very low or very high levels of damage. However, the choice between unifactor or bifactor damage hypothesis has little impact on the benefit of economic optimization. But this result is contextual. The vulnerability effect linked to high population density, downstream, is offset by the dilution effect of a stronger flow. The result would be different if the pollution abatement issue was greater upstream due to the presence of a swimming, fishing or water supply area. A fine-tuning of the damage hypotheses is therefore not always useful. This article is based on a sensitivity study of the hypotheses concerning the ecological damage generated by nitrate. For future studies, more attention could be paid to different assumptions about the costs of reducing nitrogen fertilizers. For example, rather than simply reducing the amount of nitrogen fertilizer, a change in agricultural practices could be considered. The effectiveness of different policies could also be considered in a less deterministic and more probabilistic way (Barton et al. 2008). Likewise, we may question whether the cost of policies aimed at changing behaviors and practices should also be included in abatement costs. However, the contribution of this method obviously goes far beyond the question of nitrates of agricultural origin. With regard to water pollution, it is a question of knowing how the data produced (and therefore the way in which they are produced) influences environmental policies (choice of a sewerage technology, diversification of agricultural crops, watercourse management, etc.), and so social welfare. The estimation of the social welfare depends on the value that is attributed to the nuisance generated by the pollutants (additional cost for the production of drinking water, sanitary impact, reduction of the fish population, loss of amenities for the residents, etc.). We have seen in this article that the perception of this damage plays a key role in the need or not to "economically" optimize the location of the measurement points. When this damage is perceived as being very significant, implying that the environmental policy is very ambitious, the benefit of the "economic" optimization of the measurement points compared to traditional location methods, will be increased. [SOURCE: Page_14]
