Introduction 1   At a time of climatic and ecological crises that characterise the Anthropocene, the relationship between societies and their environment is changing. Coastal wetlands (CW), whose hybrid nature is often recognized as a heritage asset, are often protected and enhanced through various means (local, national or international protection). These places illustrate the changing relationship with water. In these environments, relationships with water have been radically altered by the widespread land- reclamation period between the 18th and 19th centuries (Derex, 2002; Morera, 2016). During the twentieth century, social perceptions of coastal wetlands changed (Baron- Yellès and Goeldner-Gianella, 2001; Sajaloli, 1996) and gave rise to landscapes heritages now protected. Their specific nature as a land-sea interface and the resulting sensitivity to variations in sea level make them relevant sites for considering the Anthropocene. 2   Today, global changes (rising sea levels, changes in use of CWs, etc.) pose a challenge for current management principles of natural heritages (Chadenas et al., 2024). Insofar as global change and rising sea levels are forcing a transformation of protected heritage areas, how are the management principles and communities associated with the protection of these protected natural areas being renewed (or not)? What is the weight of landscape and structural legacies in conflicts regarding management of these areas and its renewal? The aim of this paper is to take a retrospective look at the landscape evolution of CWs, using a geohistorical approach. Using a case study in [SOURCE: Page_3]

Analysing the renewal of relations between societies and a hybrid heritage: the contribution of geohistory Scientific background [SOURCE: Page_4]

4   CWs cannot be dissociated from their historical trajectory punctuated by natural and social events and the evolution of their representations. They are defined here as socio- ecological systems resulting from the interconnection of natural processes (sea level rise, tides, etc.), social processes (settlement, land reclamation, etc.) and political processes (legal framework for nature conservation, land regulations, etc.). Studying their landscape means considering CWs as an interweaving of political-ecological and socio-economic processes operating at different scales (local, regional, international) (Swyngedouw, 2004, Hurst et al., 2022). Landscape is defined here as “the dialectic between the initiatives of the various actors that perceive, inhabit, and shape the environment according to their own needs, and the surroundings that elicit these initiatives and respond to them.” (Germaine and Gonin, 2024). It reveals the relationships that is “made and remade” between water, discourses and imaginaries, people, institutions and infrastructures across space and time (Hurst et al., 2022) forming hydrosocial cycles (Linton and Budds, 2014). 5   Examining the evolution of these landscapes using a geohistorical approach makes it possible to understand the part of nature and society as well as drivers that have influenced landscape trajectories (Valette, 2019). Applied to natural heritages, this approach depicts the inherited forms passed on through their patrimonialisation (Carcaud and Paly, 2024; Gramond, 2014). By studying management methods observed in CWs, it underlines the succession and confrontation of hydrosocial cycles (Flaminio, 2019). As societies seek to adapt to global change, geohistory provides knowledges about past changes and facilitates territorial planning for an uncertain future (Sajaloli et al., 2023). By taking a step back, use of history acts as an “active revealer of what we are in the process of inventing” (Berdoulay and Soubeyran, 2015). In wetlands, described as “environmental showcases” (Sajaloli, 1996), the geohistory of the landscape highlights the evolutionary trajectories of relationships with water. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Method [SOURCE: Page_5]

6    Developed by Fernand Braudel (Braudel, 1949), geohistory involves using the tools of the geographer on past timeframes in order to draw up a narrative to explain current phenomena. With the advent of environmental issues, geohistory is regaining interest (Jacob-Rousseau, 2024) and is being used to study a wide range of topics, including globalisation (Grataloup, 2015), risks (Dournel, 2014) and the dynamics of hydrosystems (Baud and Reynard, 2015; Hugerot et al., 2021). 16   Masse’s map (Figure 4) shows an agrarian landscape dominated by cultivated land on the higher ground and a few meadows confined to the valley bottoms of the watercourses. The maritime landscape is marked by the presence of large areas of salt marshes on the banks of the Payré, and behind them by marshes developed as salt flats or fish marshes. The estuary is wide and deep enough to support maritime transport for vessels of 40 to 50 barrels as far as the Guittière outer harbour (Source: AN GR 1 VD 60). Fish marshes, with their comb-shaped structure, serve two purposes: one is aquaculture, where “ropes” and “pits” are used to take advantage of services provided by this ecosystem to grow fish, and the other, on “bossis”, is used to provide arable land, mainly for growing wheat. Profits from the cultivation of “bossis” and fishing in fish marshes provide farmers with additional resources (Bouhier, 1957). Coastal dunes were also cultivated (cereals, vegetables and vines) on small plots of land known as “conches”. In this way, the 18th-century landscape reflected an utilitarian relationship with the environment for the purposes of subsistence and navigation. [SOURCE: Page_8]

22   From the 1970s onwards, aquaculture have been popularised on the Payré site with the development of new fish farming methods known as “ecological planning”, which consisted of integrating this activity as closely as possible with the natural functioning of the marshes (Hussenot, 1974; Hussenot and Buchet, 1998). Water: a source of production [SOURCE: Page_14]

30   In the 19th century, relations with wetlands were perceived as positive only if they were improved (Derex, 2017). In an application to rehabilitate some dykes, the Payré estuary is described as being of collective importance as a maritime exchange zone and a salt, fish and agricultural production area. Conclusion 45   The geohistorical approach to the landscape of CWs highlights the hybrid nature of the protected socio-natural heritage. Without wishing to date the start of the Anthropocene that fluctuates geographically (Garnier, 2019), this approach reveals, on the one hand, the age of these now-protected heritages and, on the other, an intensification of transformations in the second half of the 20th century. 46   The landscape trajectory of the estuary and marshes of Payré river shows that the forms of relationship with water are renewed in response to the various factors of change and, above all, to the feelings of loss that they induce: loss of a landscape, loss of practices, etc. By focusing on management trajectories, this geohistorical approach finally reveals the weight of landscape and structural legacies through collective water management in the associated management trajectories. The aim is to preserve cultural heritage in the face of climate change by maintaining a 1950s landscape. 47   In this context, climate change does not seem to fundamentally transform existing frameworks or practices in the management of natural heritage. Rather than signalling indifference toward climate change, this dynamic reflects a public policy framework shaped by short-term management logics and a deep-rooted attachment to the established cultural landscape. ABSTRACTS Atlantic coastal wetlands, once productive areas, are now protected for their natural character. They are now facing new challenges as sea levels rise. By reconstructing the landscape trajectory of the Payré estuary and marshes (Vendée, France), this paper first identifies the drivers of change that have produced landscape heritages that are now protected. Secondly, the analysis of water management evolution characterises the renewal of hydrosocial cycles. In the past, water was productive; today two hydrosocial cycles are coexisting. One is promoting water as a source of biodiversity through ‘ecological’ management, but also as a commodity through the development of green tourism. The other emphasises ancient practices that give the site its identity. These two hydrosocial cycles coexist because of the hybrid nature of this site and the attachment to landscape heritages.
