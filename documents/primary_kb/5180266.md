21   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

22    Vegetation optical depth (VOD) is seasonally sensitive to plant water content and aboveground [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   biomass. This index has a strong penetrability within the vegetation canopy and is less impacted by [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   atmosphere aerosol contamination effects, clouds and sun illumination than optical vegetation indices. [SOURCE: Page_2]

25   VOD is thus increasingly applied in ecological applications, e.g., carbon stock, phenology and [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   vegetation monitoring. However, VOD retrieval over dense forests is subject to uncertainties caused by [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   the thick canopy and complex multiple scattering effects. Thus, a comprehensive evaluation of VOD [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   products over dense forests is needed for effective and accurate applications. This study evaluated the [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   seasonal variations of eight recently developed/reprocessed VOD products at different frequencies (e.g., [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   Ku-, X-, C- and L-band) over Amazon intact forests, supported by the ORCHIDEE-CAN-NHA model- [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   simulated vegetation water content. Furthermore, we also explored the potential causes of VOD retrieval [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   issues, in terms of retrieval algorithm uncertainties. [SOURCE: Page_2]

33     We first confirmed that soil water availability dominated seasonal dynamics of vegetation water [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   content over Amazon intact forests. This was verified by model-simulated vegetation water content and [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   by C-band radar backscatter observations. Generally, evening or midday vegetation water content shows [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   higher correlations with soil moisture than morning or midnight vegetation water content. In terms of [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   ability of morning or midnight VOD products to follow the seasonality of soil moisture, active [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   microwave ASCAT-IB C-VOD (median seasonal correlation with soil moisture (R) ~ 0.50) outperforms [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   the passive microwave VOD products, followed by passive microwave AMSR$_2$ X-VOD (R ~ 0.26) and [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   VODCA X-VOD (R ~ 0.16). However, SMOS-IC L-VOD (R ~ -0.15) and AMSR$_2$ C$_1$-VOD (R ~ -0.20) [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   show obviously negative seasonal correlations with soil moisture across most pixels. This implausible [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   behavior is likely to be caused by the inappropriate setting of time-invariant scattering effects in the [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   passive microwave VOD retrieval algorithms, which could lead to an overestimation of the VOD [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   amplitude during dry seasons. Thus, we recommend that the seasonal scattering effects be considered in [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   the passive microwave VOD retrieval algorithms. These findings can contribute to the improvement of [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   VOD retrieval algorithms and help with the development of their ecological applications over Amazon [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   dense forests. [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   Key words: vegetation optical depth, vegetation water content, seasonality, Amazon forest, [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

52        Amazon forests have been listed as an important tipping element of the Earth system (Lenton et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   2008), representing a major component of the global and regional carbon and hydrological cycles [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   (Restrepo-Coupe et al., 2013; Fassoni-Andrade et al., 2021). Amazon forests cover 5.3 million km^2, [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   representing 40% of the global tropical area (Marengo et al., 2018). The forests hold a large carbon [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   stock but show a long-term decreasing trend of carbon accumulation (Brienen et al., 2015) due to [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   climate change, degradation and deforestation (Brando et al., 2014; Trondoli Matricardi et al., 2020; [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   Barlow et al., 2016). However, some research argues that Amazon forests could be more resilient to [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   disturbance than expected. For example, dry seasonal greening up has been observed in Amazon region, [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   shifting the canopy composition toward younger leaves with higher light use efficiency (Wu et al., 2016; [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   Green et al., 2020; Doughty et al., 2019). An accurate understanding of vegetation dynamics in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   Amazon basin is extremely important for global and regional carbon budgets, as well as for the [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   hydrology and energy exchange (Phillips et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

64        Remote sensing data provide widespread observations for monitoring vegetation dynamics over [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   Amazon dense forests, including both optical (e.g. Sun-Induced Chlorophyll Fluorescence (SIF), Leaf [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   Area Index (LAI), Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and Enhanced Vegetation Index [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   (EVI)) and microwave domain indices (Vegetation Optical Depth (VOD)). Optical vegetation indices [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   usually have high spatial resolution and a long period of observations, and thus can provide long-term [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   monitoring of vegetation dynamics of the top canopy (Brando et al., 2010; Wu et al., 2016; Doughty et [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   al., 2019; Green et al., 2020). In the microwave domain, VOD is a promising ecological indicator with [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   coarse spatial resolution and high temporal resolution, derived from microwave observations at different [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   frequencies with varying extinction effects and penetration ability. The multi-frequency VOD indices [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   broaden the study domain of diverse canopy layers, facilitating the monitoring of the vegetation [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   dynamics (Frappart et al., 2020). Some high frequency VOD products are sensitive to vegetation [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   features of the top of the canopy, including Ku- and X-VOD, which have been used to monitor [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   phenology (Tian et al., 2018; Jones et al., 2011), isohydricity patterns (Konings and Gentine, 2017), and [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   drought resilience (Boulton et al., 2022) over Amazon regions. More recently, low frequency VOD at L- [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   band has been reported as a useful indicator for monitoring Amazon forests biomass dynamics (Fan et [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   al., 2019; Qin et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_4]

80        VOD retrieved from passive or active microwave observations is used to parameterize vegetation [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   microwave extinction effects (including absorption and scattering effects) (Karthikeyan et al., 2019; [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   Frappart et al., 2020). Owing to the higher penetrability of microwave radiations within vegetation, there [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   are less saturation effects over dense forests in VOD retrievals (VODs) than in optical remote sensing [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   vegetation indices (Brandt et al., 2018a; 2018b). In addition, microwaves can penetrate clouds, [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   particularly for long wavelength, which avoids the biases caused by incomplete measurements in only [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   cloud-free periods when using optical-based indices (Konings and Gentine, 2017). Thus, microwave [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   observations can be made regardless of solar illumination and atmosphere aerosol contamination effects, [SOURCE: Page_5]

88   an advantage over optical and infrared spectrum products (Baur et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021). For these [SOURCE: Page_5]

89   reasons, VOD products have been widely used for ecological applications in tropical regions in the field [SOURCE: Page_5]

90   of phenology (Tian et al., 2018; Tong et al., 2019), carbon cycle science (Fan et al., 2019; Wigneron et [SOURCE: Page_5]

91   al., 2020) and vegetation response to drought (Liu et al., 2021a; Boulton et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_5]

92        However, VOD and soil moisture (SM) retrievals are complex in dense forests, due to the intensity [SOURCE: Page_5]

93   of the absorption and multiple scattering microwave effects, the complexity of the effects of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

94   vegetation structure, the litter effects affecting the soil surface reflectivity, dew and precipitation [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   interception (Grant et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2021). In the active domain, microwave VOD is usually [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   retrieved by inverting the semi-empirical Water Cloud Model (WCM) (Ulaby et al., 1978) to simulate [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   the radar backscatter signal from vegetation and bare soil land surfaces (Vreugdenhil et al., 2016, 2017). [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   In WCM, the total backscatter signal is the sum of vegetation contribution, soil contribution attenuated [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   by the vegetation canopy and the double-bounce backscatter between the vegetation canopy and bare [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   soil surface (Liu et al., 2021b). However, WCM does not consider high order scattering mechanisms like [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   double scattering from ground-vegetation interactions (El Hajj et al., 2019). In the passive domain, VOD [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   is typically retrieved by inverting models simulating brightness temperature (TB) measured by [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   radiometers. Modelling TB is more challenging for dense forest canopies than for moderate vegetation [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   cover (Wigneron et al., 2017). Generally, models are based on the 0th-order Tau-Omega (τ-ω) radiative [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   transfer equations which account for several soil and vegetation parameters, e.g., vegetation absorption [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   and scattering, surface temperature and roughness (Baur et al., 2019; Frappart et al., 2020). However, [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   the Tau-Omega model does not simulate volume scattering caused by branches with varying sizes and [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   orientation and multiple reflections between the soil surface and the vegetation elements, inaccurately [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   representing the coherent effects and multiple scattering in dense forests (Feldman et al., 2018a; [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   Wigneron et al., 2017). The scattering effects of vegetation in the Tau-Omega model are characterized [SOURCE: Page_6]

111   by a parameter called the effective scattering albedo (Kurum, 2013), usually assumed to be constant for [SOURCE: Page_6]

112   a certain plant functional type as defined by the International Geosphere-Biosphere Programme (IGBP) [SOURCE: Page_6]

113   scheme (Fernandez-Moran et al., 2017; see Table S$_1$ in Li et al. 2022a). Moreover, previous studies have [SOURCE: Page_6]

114   shown that the accuracy of the SM retrievals may decline with increasing vegetation density due to the [SOURCE: Page_6]

115   decreasing sensitivity of TB to SM (Kurum et al., 2011; Wigneron et al., 2021). The negative impact on [SOURCE: Page_6]

116   SM caused by high vegetation density in turn may also lead to error propagation to VOD retrievals as [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   both SM and VOD are usually retrieved simultaneously (Wigneron et al., 1995; 2000; 2017; Konings et [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   al., 2016; Feldman et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022a). [SOURCE: Page_6]

119        An in-depth evaluation of both active and passive microwave VOD performance is strongly needed [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   over dense forests, especially in tropical wet regions that are a key focus of VOD applications to infer [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   long term biomass changes (Wigneron et al., 2021). VOD has been found to be linearly related to [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   vegetation water content (VWC) (kg/m^2) (Jackson and Schmugge, 1991; Momen et al., 2017; Santi et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   2017). However, evaluations based on field data are rare due to the unavailability of large-scale field [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   observation of VWC data, especially over forests (Li et al., 2021). Currently, most VOD seasonal [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   evaluations have been based on proxies of vegetation greenness (Du et al., 2017; Karthikeyan et al., [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   2019; Tong et al., 2019), e.g., NDVI, EVI and LAI, which implicitly suppose that these indices capture [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   seasonal changes in the ecosystem total water mass. Although this assumption is reasonable for short [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   vegetation, it is unlikely to hold true over dense forests where optical vegetation indices saturate with [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   canopy mass (Jones et al., 2011; Tian et al., 2016) and where seasonal decoupling occurs between wood, [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   root and canopy (foliage) mass changes (Doughty et al., 2015). Particularly, long wavelength VOD is [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   sensitive to the seasonal changes in the whole canopy mass including both the foliar and woody [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   components (Jones et al., 2011), which makes optical vegetation indices unsuitable for the assessment of [SOURCE: Page_7]

133   VOD over dense forests, especially for low frequency VOD as L-VOD (Li et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_7]

134        Process-based terrestrial biosphere models may be a new tool for simulating seasonal changes in [SOURCE: Page_7]

135   the ecosystem water storage that are not observable at large scale, and thus to evaluate VOD (Xu et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

136   2016; 2021). Yet, few biosphere models can simulate VWC as a state variable across large spatial scales, [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   a process which requires a detailed numerical representation of plant hydraulic architecture, including [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   water transport and storage. Until now, most models calibrated at the site level have been used to [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   simulate diurnal and seasonal VWC variations (Powell et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2021). In the present study, [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   we take advantage of the representation of VWC in the ORCHIDEE-CAN-NHA global model, which is [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   a new hydraulic architecture module in the ORCHIDEE-CAN model (Yao et al., 2021) and simulates [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   hourly VWC variations in roots, stems and leaves over the entire Amazon at a spatial resolution of 1°. [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   This model was calibrated at the Caxiuanã rainforest site in Brazil where detailed measurements were [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   collected both for normal and drought conditions (Yao et al., 2021). Additionally, we also take [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   advantage of radar backscatter observations which are sensitive to VWC changes (Konings et al., 2017) [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   for the evaluation of the seasonality of VWC-related variables. [SOURCE: Page_7]

147        Notably, VOD represents the total mass of water in the vegetation layer which is the product of [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   plant mass multiplied by plant relative water content per unit of mass (RWC), the latter reflecting the [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   temporal dynamics of leaf and xylem water potential responding to hydro-meteorological conditions [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   (Konings et al., 2017, 2019; Momen et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2019). The VOD dynamics during SM [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   dry-down periods (Feldman et al., 2018b) and the relationships between VOD changes and subsequent [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   tree mortality (Rao et al., 2019) both indicate that VOD is significantly sensitive to relative water [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   content (RWC) (Konings et al., 2021a). In Amazon intact forests, AGB does not have a strong [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   seasonality and it is close to constant throughout the year, especially when compared with its mean AGB 7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   level (Doughty et al., 2015). Although remote-sensing observations suggest that central Amazon forests [SOURCE: Page_8]

156   with yearly precipitation > 2000 mm increase their photosynthetic capacity during dry seasons (Guan et [SOURCE: Page_8]

157   al., 2015; Saleska et al. 2016), camera observations and Lidar data show that the total LAI during dry [SOURCE: Page_8]

158   seasons is nearly constant (with slightly increase) and the seasonality of total LAI is limited due to [SOURCE: Page_8]

159   coordinated leaf loss and new leaf production (Nunes et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2016). The variation of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

160   photosynthetic seasonality can be explained by the new leaf growth with old leaves shedding, shifting [SOURCE: Page_8]

161   the canopy composition toward younger and more light-use efficient leaves during dry seasons (Wu et [SOURCE: Page_8]

162   al., 2016). Thus, we hypothesize that: (H$_1$) SM and VOD should be seasonally positively correlated [SOURCE: Page_8]

163   because RWC is monotonically related to root-depth SM (Konings et al., 2021a), assuming limited [SOURCE: Page_8]

164   seasonal biomass dynamics over the Amazon intact forests; (H$_2$) the seasonal correlations between SM [SOURCE: Page_8]

165   and VOD should be higher in regions where the dry season is more pronounced. [SOURCE: Page_8]

166      This study aims to comprehensively evaluate the seasonal dynamics of eight active or passive [SOURCE: Page_8]

167   microwave VOD products (Table 1) over Amazon intact forests. Specifically, we: 1) evaluated the [SOURCE: Page_8]

168   seasonal dynamics of ORCHIDEE-CAN-NHA model-simulated leaf and stem RWC and VWC against [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   soil water availability (a proxy of SM here); 2) assessed ASCAT backscatter seasonality relative to SM [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   changes during SM dry-down events; 3) inter-compared the seasonality among VODs, model-simulated [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   VWC, backscatter and SM to evaluate the accuracy of VOD retrievals; and 4) analyzed the potential [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   reasons for VOD retrieval errors from the perspective of retrieval algorithms. Notably, step 1 and step 2 [SOURCE: Page_8]

173   can help to validate the two hypotheses (H$_1$-2), based on which we conduct the evaluation of VOD [SOURCE: Page_8]

174   seasonal variations. We used the SM-VOD seasonal correlation as a main metric to evaluate the [SOURCE: Page_8]

175   accuracy of the VOD seasonality, rather than directly compared the model-simulated VWC with VOD. [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   This is because the model does not separate top canopy and middle canopy which could cause some [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   uncertainties in the evaluation of high frequency VOD products, e.g., Ku-, X-, and C-band VODs. In [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   addition, there is still a need to improve the model and we illustrate the model-related uncertainty in the [SOURCE: Page_9]

179   discussion part. To enhance the robustness of the evaluation results, we also evaluated LAI/SIF-VOD [SOURCE: Page_9]

180   correlations to indicate the impact of canopy biomass on the seasonality of vegetation water content. [SOURCE: Page_9]

## 206   2.1.1     SMOS-IC

207           The SMOS satellite is the first mission equipped with an L-band (1.4 GHz) microwave radiometer [SOURCE: Page_10]

208   for global SM and VOD mapping. The satellite transits the Earth twice each day, at 6:00 am and 6:00 [SOURCE: Page_10]

209   pm local time in ascending and descending orbits, respectively. The SMOS-IC algorithm improved the 10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

210   original algorithm proposed for the SMOS mission (Wigneron et al., 2000), and achieved maximal [SOURCE: Page_11]

211   independence of auxiliary data without requiring modeled SM and optical vegetation indices as inputs [SOURCE: Page_11]

212   for the retrievals (Wigneron et al., 2021). The SMOS-IC L-VOD product has been found to have an [SOURCE: Page_11]

213   almost linear relationship with AGB across large spatial gradients of AGB and presents low saturation [SOURCE: Page_11]

214   signs even at high biomass levels (Brandt et al., 2018a; Fan et al., 2019). The SMOS-IC L-VOD data in [SOURCE: Page_11]

215   version 2 used in this study can be downloaded at https://ib.remote-sensing.inrae.fr/. [SOURCE: Page_11]

216   2.1.2     VOD Climate Archive (VODCA) [SOURCE: Page_11]

217           The TU Wien’s VODCA products (Moesinger et al., 2020) integrated and reprocessed VOD [SOURCE: Page_11]

218   retrievals from Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer 2 (AMSR$_2$), WindSat, Advanced [SOURCE: Page_11]

219   Microwave Scanning Radiometer for EOS (AMSR-E), Special Sensor Microwave Imager (SSM/I) and [SOURCE: Page_11]

220   the Microwave Imager on board the Tropical Rainfall Measuring Mission (TMI) into long-term VOD [SOURCE: Page_11]

221   datasets at Ku-band (1987-2017), X-band (1997-2018) and C-band (2002-2018). The retrieval algorithm [SOURCE: Page_11]

222   is the Land Parameter Retrieval Model (LPRM) Version 6 (Moesinger et al., 2020), where both SM and [SOURCE: Page_11]

223   VOD are obtained simultaneously from the Microwave Polarization Difference Index (Meesters et al., [SOURCE: Page_11]

224   2005).      The   VODCA       LPRM      V$_6$     product     (nighttime    retrievals)     is   available   at [SOURCE: Page_11]

225   https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.2575599. [SOURCE: Page_11]

226   2.1.3     AMSR$_2$ VOD products [SOURCE: Page_11]

227           AMSR$_2$ is an improved successor of AMSR-E onboard the Global Change Observation Mission- [SOURCE: Page_11]

228   Water^1 satellite (GCOM-W$_1$), with local overpass times of 1:30 am and 1:30 pm for descending and [SOURCE: Page_11]

229   ascending orbits, respectively (Imaoka et al., 2012). It has a second C-band channel (C$_2$-band, 7.3 GHz), [SOURCE: Page_11]

230   which can be used to cover areas where radio frequency interference (RFI) exists in the main C$_1$ channel [SOURCE: Page_11]

11 [SOURCE: Page_11]

231   (6.9 GHz). The AMSR$_2$ VOD products used in this study were retrieved from the LPRM version 5 [SOURCE: Page_12]

232   algorithm (Owe et al., 2008). The data can be downloaded from https://hydro^1.gesdisc.eosdis.nasa.gov. [SOURCE: Page_12]

233   2.1.4     ASCAT backscatter and ASCAT-IB VOD [SOURCE: Page_12]

234           The Advanced Scatterometer (ASCAT) is an active microwave sensor which provides long-term C- [SOURCE: Page_12]

235   band (5.25 GHz) backscatter data at Vertical-Vertical (VV) polarization (Figa-Saldana et al., 2002; [SOURCE: Page_12]

332   3.2 Data preprocessing [SOURCE: Page_17]

333        The accuracy of the VOD retrievals is influenced by RFI, indicating microwave radiations emitted [SOURCE: Page_17]

334   by artificial devices. Daily VODs affected by RFI were filtered out by using corresponding flags as [SOURCE: Page_17]

335   recommended by each data producer. We used 10 days smoothing window to produce daily VOD values [SOURCE: Page_17]

336   in order to decrease the noise signal and reduce the potential impact of precipitation interception on [SOURCE: Page_17]

337   VOD retrievals. Because of the diverse spatial resolution of the VOD datasets, all VOD data were [SOURCE: Page_17]

338   resampled into a spatial resolution of 0.25˚ for calculation and comparison using the nearest-neighbor [SOURCE: Page_17]

339   interpolation method, which has been extensively applied in evaluation studies of SM (Al-Yaari et al., [SOURCE: Page_17]

340   2014; Ma et al., 2021). Furthermore, to ensure a fair and reliable evaluation of the VOD products, the [SOURCE: Page_17]

341   assessment was conducted over the same period for all VOD products, namely January 2013 to [SOURCE: Page_17]

342   December 2016. To evaluate whether the VOD seasonality follows the seasonality of the soil water [SOURCE: Page_17]

343   availability and to reduce the impact of the noise (due to the VOD retrieval algorithm) on the assessment [SOURCE: Page_17]

344   of the VOD seasonality, the four-year values were averaged into a mean climatology for both VOD [SOURCE: Page_17]

345   products and SM. Here, we used Pearson correlation coefficients to indicate the seasonal and spatial [SOURCE: Page_17]

346   correlations between VWC-related indices and SM. A level of probability value (p) < 0.05 was used as a [SOURCE: Page_17]

347   measure of significant statistics. [SOURCE: Page_17]

533   5. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_32]

534   5.1 Possible improvement methods for VOD retrievals [SOURCE: Page_32]

535   5.1.1   Uncertainties associated with the retrieval algorithms [SOURCE: Page_32]

32 [SOURCE: Page_32]

536        The present study identified the opposite seasonality between some passive microwave VOD [SOURCE: Page_33]

537   products and SM. However, a higher SM usually leads to a higher RWC (Konings et al., 2021a), which [SOURCE: Page_33]

538   would lead to a higher VOD value under conditions of limited seasonal variations of biomass, especially [SOURCE: Page_33]

539   for L-VOD. Although the seasonal correlation between L-VOD and SIF is positive across some regions [SOURCE: Page_33]

540   (Fig. S5g), the seasonality of total canopy LAI is non-significant (Wu et al., 2016) and limited leaf mass [SOURCE: Page_33]

541   changes should not dominate VWC changes at L-band, as L-band observations are more sensitive to [SOURCE: Page_33]

542   woody components, e.g., branches and trunks (Tian et al., 2018). Additionally, woody growth occurs at [SOURCE: Page_33]

543   a low rate in the dry season (Doughty et al., 2015; Girardin et al., 2016). Thus, the unreasonable [SOURCE: Page_33]

544   seasonality of some passive microwave VODs when compared to the SM seasonality, e.g., SMOS-IC L- [SOURCE: Page_33]

545   VOD, may be caused by algorithm issues in the VOD retrievals. [SOURCE: Page_33]

546        There are two possible reasons accounting for the unexpected opposite seasonality between some [SOURCE: Page_33]

547   passive microwave VODs (especially for L-VOD) and SM from the perspective of retrieval algorithm. [SOURCE: Page_33]

548   The first one is the simplified scattering process considered in the retrieval algorithms, and the other one [SOURCE: Page_33]

549   is the parameter setting of time-invariant scattering effects. The Tau-Omega model on which most [SOURCE: Page_33]

550   passive retrieval algorithms are based does not account for the volume scattering effects across dense [SOURCE: Page_33]

551   forests (Feldman et al., 2018a; Wigneron et al., 2017). To solve this problem, a higher order radiative [SOURCE: Page_33]

552   transfer model, the Two-Stream emission model (Schwank et al., 2018), which holds a stronger physical [SOURCE: Page_33]

553   background with consideration of multiple scattering and reflection, has been applied to SMOS TB to [SOURCE: Page_33]

554   retrieve SM and VOD (Li et al., 2020a). However, the temporal correlations between VODs retrieved [SOURCE: Page_33]

555   from the Tau-Omega model and the Two-Stream model are higher than 0.8, indicating a similar [SOURCE: Page_33]

556   seasonality of VOD retrievals for both models (Li et al., 2020a). Thus, the simplified representation of [SOURCE: Page_33]

557   scattering effects in the Tau-Omega model is probably not the main reason for the unreasonable [SOURCE: Page_33]

558   seasonality of VODs we found in this study. [SOURCE: Page_33]

33 [SOURCE: Page_33]

559       Another possible explanation is that the effective scattering albedo (omega), a calibration parameter, [SOURCE: Page_34]

560   is commonly assumed to be time-invariant in the retrieval algorithms. This assumption could be [SOURCE: Page_34]

561   explained by the fact that omega is usually viewed to be saturated and becomes relatively stable, due to [SOURCE: Page_34]

562   the overwhelming multiple scattering effects, especially in very dense forests (Wigneron et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_34]

563   Similarly, Baur et al., (2021) found that omega was almost constant throughout the year over the [SOURCE: Page_34]

564   Amazon region by retrieving both VOD and omega in two separate Tau-Omega model frameworks [SOURCE: Page_34]

565   using known soil moisture as inputs. However, some evidences show that omega is observed to vary [SOURCE: Page_34]

566   with spatiotemporal land cover dynamics (Konings et al., 2016). Scattering effects depend on the total [SOURCE: Page_34]

567   path of the electromagnetic waves that cross the canopy (Baur et al., 2019), and are a function of plant [SOURCE: Page_34]

568   geometry and of vegetation moisture content, which exhibit considerable variability in time (Owe et al., [SOURCE: Page_34]

569   2008). For example, time dependence of omega is significant over crop fields (Wigneron et al., 2004) [SOURCE: Page_34]

570   and omega may range from 0 to 0.2 with total water content varying from 0 to 120 tons/ha (Ferrazzoli et [SOURCE: Page_34]

571   al., 2002). Additionally, omega is shown to be SM dependent and increases monotonically with [SOURCE: Page_34]

572   increasing soil moisture (Kurum, 2013). In this study, the backscatter observed by ASCAT also [SOURCE: Page_34]

573   demonstrates time-varying scattering effects explaining the backscatter decreases in the dry season, [SOURCE: Page_34]

574   which is in line with this previous study in Prigent et al. (2022). Thus, we deduce that in the Amazon [SOURCE: Page_34]

575   intact forests, omega is not constant over time. On the contrary, it varies in time and should decrease [SOURCE: Page_34]

576   during the dry season. To test the sensitivity of VOD to changes in omega values, we run the L-band [SOURCE: Page_34]

577   VOD retrieval algorithm from SMOS-IC over the study region with an omega value of 0.05 as input for [SOURCE: Page_34]

578   the scattering effects, a value slightly lower than its original value for evergreen forests (0.06). The [SOURCE: Page_34]

579   results show a dramatic decrease in VOD magnitude when varying omega values from 0.06 to 0.05, with [SOURCE: Page_34]

580   the magnitude of decrease even exceeding its original seasonal amplitude (Fig. 9). Thus, neglecting the [SOURCE: Page_34]

581   possible decrease of the omega value during the dry season in the retrieval algorithms may lead to a [SOURCE: Page_34]

34 [SOURCE: Page_34]

582   large overestimation of L-VOD in the dry season. Thus, we concluded that the unreasonable increase of [SOURCE: Page_35]

583   SMOS-IC L-VOD in the dry season may be caused by the time-constant omega parameter used in the [SOURCE: Page_35]

584   retrieval algorithms. We therefore recommend that seasonal scattering effects should be considered in [SOURCE: Page_35]

585   passive VOD retrieval algorithms over Amazon dense forests due to the strong seasonality in the [SOURCE: Page_35]

586   moisture content of the vegetation scattering elements (Kurum et al., 2012), e.g., leaves, branches and [SOURCE: Page_35]

587   trunks. [SOURCE: Page_35]

593   5.1.2 Disturbing factors for retrieval errors related to observations [SOURCE: Page_35]

594        Our results indicate that midnight/morning passive microwave VOD is less influenced by soil [SOURCE: Page_35]

595   temperature with smaller q values than midday/evening VOD, except for AMSR2 X-VOD (Table 2). 35 [SOURCE: Page_35]

596   This conclusion is in line with previous studies indicating that midnight/morning TB is less affected by [SOURCE: Page_36]

597   temperature-related errors because soil and vegetation are closer to temperature equilibrium at night [SOURCE: Page_36]

598   (Frappart et al., 2020); and daytime emitting layer temperature is often more difficult to estimate due to [SOURCE: Page_36]

599   more intense surface heating (Owe, et al., 2008). We also found a low but significant (p<0.05) impact of [SOURCE: Page_36]

600   precipitation on correlations between VODs and SM for both active and passive microwave VOD [SOURCE: Page_36]

601   products, with a mean q value of 0.08, ranging from 0.02 to 0.15 (Table 2). The influence of [SOURCE: Page_36]

602   precipitation may be explained by the fact that precipitation has an impact on leaf surface water. For [SOURCE: Page_36]

603   example, Hornbuckle et al. (2006) found that water on plant surfaces may have a moderate effect on [SOURCE: Page_36]

604   decreasing TB at 1.4 GHz varying by a few Kelvin. Xu et al. (2021) indicated that leaf surface water [SOURCE: Page_36]

605   (including dew and precipitation) drove diurnal X-VOD variations over tropical forests. [SOURCE: Page_36]

606        Soil surface roughness, defined by DEM which is used to identify the complexity of topography, [SOURCE: Page_36]

607   contributes to observations of the soil microwave reflectivity (Ma et al., 2019) through changing the [SOURCE: Page_36]

608   local incidence angle and shadowing effects caused by topography and mixed pixels (Talone et al., 2007; [SOURCE: Page_36]

609   Monerris et al., 2008). Generally, surface roughness effects on retrieval errors are found to be low for [SOURCE: Page_36]

610   passive microwave VODs, with a mean q value of 0.07 (ranging from 0.02 to 0.14) (Table 2), which [SOURCE: Page_36]

611   may be caused by the masking effects of dense vegetation covers (Parrens et al., 2016). Terrain was [SOURCE: Page_36]

612   found to influence more the active microwave backscatter observations (Mladenova and Lakshmi, 2009). [SOURCE: Page_36]

613   This is consistent with our study, as we found that surface roughness can explain 14% (q value~14%) of [SOURCE: Page_36]

614   the spatial distribution of the correlations between SM and ASCAT-IB C-VODs for both morning and [SOURCE: Page_36]

615   evening retrievals (Table 2). The influence of terrain on active microwave observations and VOD [SOURCE: Page_36]

616   retrievals has also been found in previous publications. For example, Mladenova and Lakshmi (2009) [SOURCE: Page_36]

617   found a strong backscatter dependence on the local incidence angle caused by changes in slope over the [SOURCE: Page_36]

618   Northern American Monsoon Experiment region characterized by complex topographic terrain. Liu et al. [SOURCE: Page_36]

36 [SOURCE: Page_36]

619   (2021b) found terrain strongly contributes to the soil parameters C (importance weight of 30.42%) and [SOURCE: Page_37]

620   D (importance weight of 16.46%) in active passive VOD retrievals based on the Water Cloud model. [SOURCE: Page_37]

621   5.2 ASCAT C-band backscatter as an indicator of the forest water content [SOURCE: Page_37]

622        Backscatter observed from microwave radars is sensitive to canopy structure, biomass and water [SOURCE: Page_37]

623   content (Santos et al., 2014; Schroeder et al., 2016; Steele-Dunne et al., 2012; 2017; van Emmerik et al., [SOURCE: Page_37]

624   2015; van Emmerik et al., 2017; Petchiappan et al.,2022). In this study, we found that ASCAT C-VOD [SOURCE: Page_37]

625   and ASCAT C-band backscatter have comparable capabilities for monitoring the seasonality of SM, and [SOURCE: Page_37]

626   therefore the time variations in the ASCAT backscatter may mostly be due to changes in the vegetation [SOURCE: Page_37]

627   water content and not in soil moisture, especially considering the low penetration depth at C-band in [SOURCE: Page_37]

628   dense forests. The ASCAT backscatter was very sensitive to the soil water availability in the Amazon [SOURCE: Page_37]

629   intact forests in space and time especially for water-limited regions (Region 4) (Fig.3, Fig. 4a, Fig. 8). [SOURCE: Page_37]

630   Additionally, evening backscatter is more correlated to SM than morning backscatter (Fig. 3a-b, Fig. 4a), [SOURCE: Page_37]

631   which is consistent with the performance of other VODs and model-simulated VWC in following the [SOURCE: Page_37]

632   SM seasonality (Fig. 2b, Fig. 6, Fig. S$_5$). This is contrary to the seasonal variations of leaf water [SOURCE: Page_37]

633   potential during midday and midnight. The midnight leaf water potential should be more correlated to [SOURCE: Page_37]

634   soil water potential because an equilibrium between leaf water potential and soil water potential is [SOURCE: Page_37]

635   reached at predawn (Richter, 1997; Konings and Gentine, 2017), while Amazon forests tend to keep a [SOURCE: Page_37]

636   relatively constant midday leaf water potential during soil moisture dry-downs because of isohydric [SOURCE: Page_37]

637   regulation (Konings and Gentine, 2017; Zhang et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_37]

638        There are several reasons why vegetation water content is less correlated with SM during [SOURCE: Page_37]

639   midnight/morning retrievals. First, soil moisture in volumetric unit is not perfectly coupled with soil [SOURCE: Page_37]

640   water potential, a threshold of soil moisture exists in the relationship between soil moisture and soil [SOURCE: Page_37]

641   water tension (negatively to soil water potential) (Lee and Kim, 2019). Thus, in the very wet Amazon 37 [SOURCE: Page_37]

642   forests, there may be so much precipitation that vegetation always has an ample supply of water, causing [SOURCE: Page_38]

643   the relatively small variation in the soil water potential and midnight VOD (Konings and Gentine, 2017). [SOURCE: Page_38]

644   Additionally, vegetation water content does not only scale with leaf water potential, and the regulation [SOURCE: Page_38]

645   of vegetation water content change is mainly dominated by sapwood capacitance in tropical forests [SOURCE: Page_38]

646   (Meinzer et al., 2003). However, the main driving force of water movement through the plant organs is [SOURCE: Page_38]

647   transpiration. The water supply for transpiration is composed by vertical water flow (absorbed from soil) [SOURCE: Page_38]

648   and water charge by water storage pool of each organ regulated by capacitance (Yao et al., 2021). The [SOURCE: Page_38]

649   transpiration usually becomes stronger and causes most plants to show mild water stress in the afternoon [SOURCE: Page_38]

650   (Nagasuga, 2004). Thus, the higher stress from vapor pressure deficit and transpiration during [SOURCE: Page_38]

651   midday/evening leads to a higher correlation between VOD and SM. [SOURCE: Page_38]

652   5.3 Limitations and future perspectives [SOURCE: Page_38]

653        While offering a comprehensive evaluation of VODs over Amazon intact forests based on process- [SOURCE: Page_38]

654   based ORCHIDEE-CAN-NHA land surface model and remote sensing observations from active [SOURCE: Page_38]

655   microwave sensor ASCAT radar backscatter, this study still has some limitations. First, the [SOURCE: Page_38]

656   ORCHIDEE-CAN-NHA model is a simplified process-based vegetation hydraulic model, which cannot [SOURCE: Page_38]

657   completely capture the complex vegetation response to soil water availability in regions with high [SOURCE: Page_38]

658   rainfall seasonality. For example, the correlations between backscatter and soil moisture showed an [SOURCE: Page_38]

659   increasing trend with rainfall seasonality increasing from Region 1 to Region 4 (Fig. 4a). However, the [SOURCE: Page_38]

660   ORCHIDEE-CAN-NHA model cannot capture the highest correlation between SM and VWC over the [SOURCE: Page_38]

661   driest region (Region 4) (Fig. 2a, Table S$_2$). Additionally, a time lag effect between plant water storage [SOURCE: Page_38]

662   and terrestrial groundwater storage is expected (Tian et al., 2018), but the ORCHIDEE model shows [SOURCE: Page_38]

663   limited capability to capture the lagged response of vegetation water storage to soil moisture changes. [SOURCE: Page_38]

664   Nevertheless, although there is still a need of improvement of the ORCHIDEE-CAN-NHA model to 38 [SOURCE: Page_38]

665   accurately capture the response of vegetation moisture variations to soil water availability in regions [SOURCE: Page_39]

666   with high rainfall seasonality, the results confirmed that the main driver of the VWC seasonality is the [SOURCE: Page_39]

667   seasonal variations of soil water availability. [SOURCE: Page_39]

668        Temporal or spatial correlations between VODs and SM cannot be viewed as an ‘absolute’ criterion [SOURCE: Page_39]

669   of accuracy for evaluating the VOD retrievals. First, uncertainty exists in the accuracy of the ERA$_5$ SM [SOURCE: Page_39]

670   data in tropical rainforests where SM simulations are largely unvalidated, even though ERA$_5$ SM shows [SOURCE: Page_39]

671   high accuracy at the scale of the international soil moisture network (Li et al., 2020b) and a high [SOURCE: Page_39]

672   temporal correlation (median correlation ~ 0.68) is found between ERA$_5$-SM and ORCHIDEE-CAN- [SOURCE: Page_39]

673   NHA model-simulated SM over Amazon intact forests (Fig. S$_2$). Second, high correlations between SM [SOURCE: Page_39]

674   and VOD does not absolutely mean high accuracy, because the low correlations may be caused by the [SOURCE: Page_39]

675   lagged response of vegetation to water stress and the plant adaptations to the reduced soil water [SOURCE: Page_39]

676   availability (Oliveira et al., 2005). Trees have strong stomatal control strategies and deep roots to adapt [SOURCE: Page_39]

677   to water stress, dampening short-term plant responses to available SM, thus a lagged VWC response [SOURCE: Page_39]

678   exists. Third, a nonlinear relationship between plant and soil water content emerges with threshold [SOURCE: Page_39]

679   behavior depending on SM (Feldman et al., 2018b). Additionally, SM and VPD can be decoupled in the [SOURCE: Page_39]

680   midday/afternoon when VPD and evaporation have larger effects on VOD, which could lead to a lower [SOURCE: Page_39]

681   correlation between SM and VODs. Furthermore, the magnitude of correlations between SM and VODs [SOURCE: Page_39]

682   could also be influenced by seasonal variations in the canopy biomass over different canopy layers, e.g., [SOURCE: Page_39]

683   upper canopy and understory. [SOURCE: Page_39]

684        While the total canopy LAI shows non-significant seasonality (Wu et al., 2016; Tian et al., 2018), [SOURCE: Page_39]

685   there are some limitations to consider when interpreting the time variations in high frequency VOD [SOURCE: Page_39]

686   products because of the complex canopy structure and diverse leaf mass seasonality across different [SOURCE: Page_39]

687   canopy depths. The high frequency VOD products (Ku- and X-VOD) are sensitive to top canopy, where 39 [SOURCE: Page_39]

688   old leaves will shed during dry seasons (Nunes et al., 2022), thus both LAI and RWC should follow the [SOURCE: Page_40]

689   seasonality of SM. A positive correlation between SM and Ku-, X-VOD products can be observed [SOURCE: Page_40]

690   across most pixels (Fig. 6a,b,d), while these VOD products are less sensitive to photosynthetic [SOURCE: Page_40]

691   seasonality (Fig. S6a,b,d) or LAI (Fig. S7a,b,d). Similarly, the active microwave C-band backscatter and [SOURCE: Page_40]

692   VOD retrievals show a strong correlation with SM and a negative correlation with SIF/LAI across most [SOURCE: Page_40]

693   pixels (Fig. 3a-b, Fig. 6g, Fig. S3a-b, Fig. S4a-b, Fig. S6h, Fig. S7h). However, passive microwave C- [SOURCE: Page_40]

694   band VOD products show a large difference of seasonality. The VODCA C-band VOD is more [SOURCE: Page_40]

695   correlated to SM, but AMSR$_2$ C$_1$- and C$_2$-VOD are more correlated to SIF/LAI (Fig. 6c,e,f, Fig. S6c,e,f, [SOURCE: Page_40]

696   Fig. S7c,e,f). Because the sensing depth of passive microwave C-band is largely unknown, there is still a [SOURCE: Page_40]

697   need for a further evaluation of C-band passive microwave VOD products over the Amazon rainforests. [SOURCE: Page_40]

698   For instance, triple collocation method may be used to estimate the correlation metric of independent C- [SOURCE: Page_40]

699   band VOD products. Notably, simultaneously retrieving VOD and SM can cause larger errors than [SOURCE: Page_40]

700   regularized retrieval, and the errors tend to disproportionately propagate into VOD instead of soil [SOURCE: Page_40]

701   moisture in the simultaneous retrieval algorithms (Feldman et al., 2021). Thus, independent C-band and [SOURCE: Page_40]

702   L-band VOD retrievals considering SM as input, rather than simultaneously retrieving SM and VOD, [SOURCE: Page_40]

703   may provide references for further evaluation studies. [SOURCE: Page_40]

704        This study indicated that an unexpected negative correlation between SM and some passive VODs [SOURCE: Page_40]

705   may be caused by a problem in the retrieval algorithms that do not take into account the scattering [SOURCE: Page_40]

706   seasonality thus leading to wrong seasonal cycles of VODs. The impact of scattering effects is more [SOURCE: Page_40]

707   obvious for low frequency VODs because scatterers are large with respect to the wavelength (Kurum, [SOURCE: Page_40]

708   2011) and large scatterers could result in a strong scattering effect, e.g., the single scattering albedo [SOURCE: Page_40]

709   values at L-band are higher than those at C-band across several diverse land use types (Mo et al., 1982). [SOURCE: Page_40]

710   Although SMOS-IC L-VOD retrievals are impacted by the seasonal scattering effects on the seasonal [SOURCE: Page_40]

40 [SOURCE: Page_40]

711   timescale, for the annual VOD retrievals, annual mean SMOS-IC L-VOD holds the best spatial [SOURCE: Page_41]

712   correlation with AGB in comparison to high frequency VODs (Fig. S$_8$, detailed analysis about VOD- [SOURCE: Page_41]

713   AGB relationships can be seen in Appendix. A). Notably, the strong consistency between SMOS-IC L- [SOURCE: Page_41]

714   VOD and AGB/forest-cover in multi-annual dynamics over dense forests has been found in previous [SOURCE: Page_41]

715   publications (Fan et al., 2019; Wigneron et al., 2020; Qin et al., 2021). Thus, we recommend that the [SOURCE: Page_41]

716   SMOS-IC L-VOD can still be used for analyzing multi-year changes in biomass despite the fact a [SOURCE: Page_41]

719   6. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_41]

720        This study evaluated eight recently developed and reprocessed passive and active microwave VOD [SOURCE: Page_41]

721   products (from L- to Ku-band) against the simulated vegetation water content based on process-based [SOURCE: Page_41]

722   ORCHIDEE-CAN-NHA land surface model and direct remote sensing observations of ASCAT [SOURCE: Page_41]

723   backscatter (C-band) over Amazon intact forests. Our results can contribute to better target suitable [SOURCE: Page_41]

724   ecological applications of VOD products and improve passive microwave VOD retrieval algorithms [SOURCE: Page_41]

725   over Amazon dense forests. The primary conclusions are summarized as follows. [SOURCE: Page_41]

726   1) Seasonal variations in the vegetation water content over the Amazon intact forests are mainly [SOURCE: Page_41]

727      dominated by soil water availability. Evening or midday VODs are more sensitive to the vegetation [SOURCE: Page_41]

728      water stress with higher correlations with soil moisture than morning or midnight VODs. [SOURCE: Page_41]

729   2) ASCAT backscatter (C-band) is highly sensitive to the impact of soil moisture stress on vegetation [SOURCE: Page_41]

730      in space and time over the Amazon intact forests. ASCAT C-band backscatter and ASCAT-IB C- [SOURCE: Page_41]

731      VOD can be applied for drought-related applications, e.g., drought center identification and plants [SOURCE: Page_41]

732      response to water stress. 41 [SOURCE: Page_41]

733   3) Implausible seasonality, especially unreasonable increases of several passive microwave VOD [SOURCE: Page_42]

734      retrievals (e.g., SMOS-IC L-VOD) when soil moisture decreases during the dry season may be [SOURCE: Page_42]

735      caused by the time-invariant scattering effects considered in the algorithm. The scattering effects [SOURCE: Page_42]

736      should theoretically decrease during the dry season and lower scattering effects lead to lower VOD [SOURCE: Page_42]

737      values. We recommend that the seasonal scattering effects should be considered accurately in the [SOURCE: Page_42]

738      passive retrieval algorithms to acquire accurate passive microwave VOD seasonal dynamics over [SOURCE: Page_42]

739      Amazon dense forests, and backscatter from active microwave observations may provide a scientific [SOURCE: Page_42]

740      reference for evaluating the seasonal variations in the scattering effects. [SOURCE: Page_42]

741   4) In the passive microwave domain, morning or midnight VOD retrieval is less influenced by soil [SOURCE: Page_42]

742      temperature than evening or midday VOD retrieval. Additionally, SMOS-IC L-VOD is less [SOURCE: Page_42]

743      influenced by disturbing factors than high frequency VODs, including surface roughness, soil [SOURCE: Page_42]

744      temperature and precipitation. [SOURCE: Page_42]
