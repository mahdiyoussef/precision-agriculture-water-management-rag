Co-development of groundwater decision support tools Background [SOURCE: Page_4]

Growing demand for water, exacerbated by climate change, is increasing dependence on groundwater in the southern Mediterranean countries, especially in semi-arid and arid regions (Kuper et al., 2016). Morocco has been facing six consecutive years of drought and water security has become the backbone of any climate resilience strategy for all economic sectors, especially agriculture. Some irrigated cultures like olive, citrus orchards and horticulture have suffered and led to water use conflicts and greater inequality (the wealthier are able to pump deeper). In this context, collaborative governance can be seen as one possible solution to manage complex socio- ecological systems such as groundwater systems. As part of the BRIDGE research collective on co-design of climate and water smart decision support tools and frameworks (Vadez and Bossuet, 2021; Vadez et al, 2023; Dolinska et al, 2023), this ClimBeR research activity relates to building the capacity of local stakeholders (farmers, administration, private companies, associations, etc.) to better co-manage groundwater resources in a sustainable and equitable manner. The establishment of effective collaborative processes remains a major challenge due to the specificities of groundwater ("invisibility”, less easily available data, etc.). Researchers from CIRAD have collaborated since 2022 with Tunisian partners to co-develop user-friendly groundwater co-management decision tools towards the development and implementation of climate resilient groundwater management strategies (Hassenforder, 2023). The project was composed of four distinct activities (fig 1): [SOURCE: Page_4]

Enabling conditions for a multi-stakeholder dialogue [SOURCE: Page_5]

The involvement of various stakeholders in groundwater management ensures that different interests, knowledge, and perspectives are considered, fostering mutual understanding and equitable sharing of available resources. [SOURCE: Page_5]

But collaborative groundwater governance is not a panacea everywhere, and a number of conditions must be met from the start for this collaborative governance to have more chances to be effective and sustained over time. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Through a literature review exercise, the team narrowed down from 157 papers to 73 peer-reviewed publications describing “preconditions” for a dialogue on sustainable natural resources management. 15 preconditions were identified, related to resource characteristics, interactions between actors and the institutional context (table 1). [SOURCE: Page_5]

“ Pre-conditions are the pre-requisite conditions favorable to the start-up of a multi-stakeholders’ dialogue or a participatory process aimed at achieving collaborative governance in the end. The objective of this study was to propose a list of pre-conditions that is manageable (i.e. clear, not too long) so that it can be used by actors interested in collaborative governance to identify to what extent a participatory process or a multi stakeholders dialogue is likely to succeed in their area to sustainably manage groundwater. Not all conditions need to be met before initiating a multi-stakeholders dialogue, but the list of 15 pre-conditions can help identify what are the key leverages and potential obstacles when initiating such a dialogue. [SOURCE: Page_5]

These conditions have been tested in the context of a multi-stakeholder’s dialogue initiated in the Limaoua irrigated area, located in the governorate of Gabes, in south-eastern Tunisia. This dialogue mainly involves the regional agricultural administration and farmers, who are facing problems of degradation and depletion of groundwater resources. [SOURCE: Page_5]

The serious game Ground-WAG-Er [SOURCE: Page_8]

The Ground-Wag-er role-playing game was developed to enable stakeholders to collectively debate and experiment with alternative groundwater management instruments (Fig 3). The game helps each stakeholder better understand the perspective of others, test various combinations of groundwater management instruments and see what trade-offs and possible opposition could appear for different actors, especially for small-scale and large farmers. The game has been tested in pilot sites in Tunisia and demonstrated to Moroccan partners. [SOURCE: Page_8]

In a context of overuse of groundwater resources, and drought impacts on agricultural production, farmers, agricultural administration, research, extension services and other water and agriculture stakeholders in the Limaoua area in south-eastern Tunisia were keen to start a dialogue around the sustainable use of groundwater resources. Some have promoted the idea of creating a local organization, Groupement de Developpement Agricole (GDA), to collaboratively and sustainably manage groundwater resources. After a two-year dialogue, including 8 workshops, local stakeholders agreed on a common goal and collective actions. The GDA was officially created on December 21st 2023 (official journal 2023R02947APSF$_1$). The scope of the GDA is to work on the aquifer “safeguard area”, established in 2017 where only wells under 50 meters deep are allowed. In this safeguard area, there are 637 farms with mostly orchards (olive, citrus, vines). [SOURCE: Page_10]

Limaoua participants adapted the policy framework and management model of the nearby Bsissi oued el Akarit area to their local context. Researchers supported this participatory process through methodological support and monitoring and evaluation. The Limaoua case study was the opportunity to test the P-KCP policy design methodology (Pluchinotta et al. 2019) allowing participants to identify alternative management instruments. This methodology is typically used with decision-makers but it had rarely been used with farmers. Alternative management instruments previously identified were then tested by participants using the Ground-WAG-Er serious game. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Fig 5 : Workshops to initiate reflection on groundwater co-management in Limoua, Gabes region. Tunisia [SOURCE: Page_10]

After Bsissi oued el Akarit, Limaoua is the second area in Tunisia where a water user association was created to manage private boreholes. Water user associations in Tunisia usually manage public boreholes. This case study may serve as an example for other areas trying to set up participatory groundwater management, especially in Morocco where aquifer management contracts struggle to be implemented. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Application for Morocco: Sefrou basin agency [SOURCE: Page_11]

Previous groundwater governance work in Sefrou basin, in the Ain Tinguenay-Ighrezrane-Ouled Mkoudou aquifer area (Faysse et al, 2017) demonstrated the feasibility and potential impact of water co-management, through a co- development and co-assessment of future scenarios. Since 2021, groundwater crisis appeared in the area, due to a strong increase in irrigated areas and a steady decrease in rainfall. The Sefrou basin agency, which is the authority at the subnational level in charge of intersectoral dialogue over water resources, showed an interest to learn from the Tunisian experience during the ClimBeR project. Part of the agency’s mandate is to implement an aquifer contract – which defines the overall governance structure. Once this groundwater governance is set, groundwater management decision support tools like the ones developed under this ClimBeR BRIDGE activity can help discussing in details options for joint groundwater management. A first draft of aquifer contract has been prepared in 2024 and hopefully this contract will be signed in 2025. [SOURCE: Page_11]

Next steps and recommendations Groundwater is a strategic resource in the Maghreb and elsewhere. Yet these resources are increasingly degraded and overexploited. To face these challenges, management instruments need to be put in place both to manage supply and to reduce groundwater abstraction. However, experience in recent years in North Africa has shown that coercive measures alone (plugging wells, removing pumps, fines) are not enough to stop the increase in illegal drilling. Certain contexts, such as small aquifers, are conducive to more participatory approaches, enabling different combinations of alternative management instruments to be debated. Following on from the work presented above, we recommend: [SOURCE: Page_12]

•    To put local stakeholders at the heart of climate change research, because they are the ones facing the consequences of climate change, and they are the ones who must adapt and be resilient. Science must therefore be useful to them, and not the other way round, so that the solutions envisaged can be adopted locally; [SOURCE: Page_12]

•    To identify the areas where a multi-stakeholder dialogue can be envisaged, using the 15 conditions identified above; [SOURCE: Page_12]

•    To continue to fuel the debate on the various possible management instruments, by encouraging exchanges between stakeholders in areas that have implemented different models; [SOURCE: Page_12]

•    To mobilise decision support tools, such as Ground-WAG-er serious game, to enable local stakeholders to test alternative management instruments and different possible scenarios in order to increase their capacity to adapt to climate change and anticipatory governance; [SOURCE: Page_12]

•    To work on different scales, from local to national and international, to provide input for discussions at national level, but also to show in practical terms how more sustainable and equitable groundwater management can be implemented locally. [SOURCE: Page_12]
