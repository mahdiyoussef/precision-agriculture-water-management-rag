Abstract [SOURCE: Page_4]

Maintaining the quality and safety of raw milk during transportation to collection centers poses significant challenges for small-scale producers, especially in regions with limited access to advanced refrigeration systems. This study proposes a cost-effective cooling system utilizing water-based phase change material (PCM) gel packs and UV-reflective covers. The thermal performance was evaluated using a tank filled with water, both with and without PCM, under constant and variable ambient temperature conditions. Results demonstrated that the tanks with PCM achieved a temperature reduction of 10-13 °C within an hour. Comparative analysis revealed similar thermal behaviors for milk and water, enabling the extrapolation of results. A simplified heat transfer model based on energy balance was developed to predict product temperature changes under different operational conditions. Some input parameters were adjusted using experimental data, and the model was subsequently validated against separate experimental datasets, showing reasonable accuracy (RMSE < 3.0 °C). The model could be used for application under comparable conditions. Furthermore, milk quality preservation was assessed through the enumeration of E. coli and total coliforms in milk stored in tanks with and without PCM, as well as through predictive microbiological modeling. Both measured and predicted results suggested a moderate reduction in E. coli growth rates (1.1-1.3 times slower) in the tank with PCM, depending on initial microbial load. Based on these findings, the proposed system offers an effective and economical solution for small milk producers, enhancing cold-chain management and reducing spoilage. [SOURCE: Page_4]

### 1. Introduction

The global production of cow milk has consistently increased each year, driven by the growing demand for dairy products due to population growth, rising incomes, and evolving dietary preferences. In 2022, global cow milk production rose by 0.7%, reaching approximately 758 million tons (IDF, 2023). In Thailand, raw milk production slightly declined from 1.265 million tons in 2021 to 1.204 million tons in 2022. This decline was primarily due to Lumpy Skin Disease and rising dairy farming costs. Despite these setbacks, domestic milk consumption is expected to remain stable, while foreign markets continue to demand Thai dairy products (DPO, 2023). [SOURCE: Page_6]

A typical dairy supply chain consists of several stages (Fig. 1) starting from raw milk production at a farm, transport to a collection center, where biological quality tests are conducted. The milk is then added to bulk tanks, cooled and transported to a processing plant for pasteurization and further processing. The finished products are stored in a cold room, transported to retail outlets via distribution centers, and sold to consumers (Vanany et al., 2024). Unlike high-income countries where milk temperature is well controlled along a cold chain, low- and middle-income countries often face breaks in the cold chain, particularly between milking at farm and delivery to collection centers (Feliciano et al., 2021; Reta & Addis, 2015). In Thailand, most small producers do not cool their raw milk before or during transportation due to the high cost of cooling facility. In practice, raw milk (35-37 °C after milking) is stored in unrefrigerated aluminum or stainless steel tanks and transported by a pickup truck to collection centers during which milk temperatures increases, leading to microbial growth and compromising quality (Paksa, 2019). According to Thai safety standards, the producers must deliver raw milk to collection centers within 2 hours without cooling, if not, they have to cool raw milk below 4 °C after milking and deliver it within 24 hours (MOAC, 2010). While the duration of milk transport from farm to collection centers is averagely 1.3 hours in Thailand (Ruangwittayanusorn et al., 2016), risks of biological hazards and food loss persist. Milk quality is influenced not only by transport duration but also by the initial bacterial contamination levels, which depend on hygienic conditions at the farms. Chaiyapum et al. (2015) found that Escherichia coli (E. coli) levels in raw milk transported to collection centers within an hour were about 3.5 times higher than those transported for longer periods, highlighting the impact of inadequate hygiene management on microbial load despite shorter [SOURCE: Page_6]

transport duration. Therefore, good hygiene practices and milk temperature control through a supply chain is essential to assure the product sanitary quality (Bylund, 1995; Lee et al., 2016). [SOURCE: Page_7]

Figure 1 Stages and the associated operations in a typical milk supply chain (Vanany et al., 2024) [SOURCE: Page_7]

Vapor compression refrigeration is prevalent, but the investment and the energy consumption costs render them unfeasible for small producers with limited financial resources. Numerous studies have proposed affordable, user-friendly, and sustainable systems for decreasing the temperature of raw milk. These studies, for example, proposed spraying cold water on the milk container, immersing it in a cold water bath, inserting a coil of cold water into the container (Paksa, 2019). Wayua et al. (2012) developed and evaluated a low-cost charcoal evaporative cooler for storing camel milk in Kenya. The cooler could maintain an internal temperature about 10.5 °C below the ambient temperature (29-32 °C). Ndyabawe and Kisaalita (2014) developed a 15.5-L evaporative milk cooler powered by biogas, and its cooling and milk preservation performance was demonstrated in Uganda. Phase Change Materials (PCMs) are substances capable of storing or releasing large amounts of energy at specific temperatures (Zhang et al., 2021). In this regard, PCMs are widely implemented in both mechanical (Feng et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2012) and non-mechanical refrigeration systems (Leungtongkum et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024), especially in transport applications. Torres-Toledo et al. (2018) designed two types of raw milk storage tanks. The first one is a new design with an ice compartment in the center of the tank and insulation around the tank. The second is a modified commercially available tank, where the tank lid is welded to an ice compartment, and the tank is insulated. Both types of tanks have a volume of 30 L and can hold up to 8 kg of ice. The authors found that the tanks could lower the temperature to 17 °C within 90 minutes for 30 L raw milk with 6 kg of ice and to 10 °C within 3 hours for 20 L of raw milk with 8 kg of ice. Both tanks allow to maintain the temperatures indicated above for an additional 9 hours. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Prakash et al. (2022) proposed a method to reduce the temperature of raw milk by using a container with its lateral wall filled with PCM made of water mixed with nanoparticles powder of titanium dioxide (TiO$_2$, 10–13 nm) considered as an enhanced thermal conductivity agent. The authors found that adding TiO$_2$ powder to water at a concentration of 0.6% by weight could reduce the temperature of raw milk from 37 °C to below 10 °C within one hour, which is 2.0- 2.3 times faster than using pure ice. In the further study, Prakash and Ravindra (2023) developed PCM encapsulated inside a spherical module considered as an immersion cooling device to chill a pool of milk in a vessel. It was found that the addition of TiO$_2$ at 1.00 wt% in the PCM reduced the time to cool down the milk temperature from 37 °C to less than 10 °C by 42.4%. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The present study proposed a low-cost cooling technique, easy to assemble and adaptable for milk tanks commonly used by small producers. This proposed technique uses gel packs made of water-based PCM (melting point 0 °C) which can be easily solidified using a household freezer. The gel packs are attached to the external sidewalls of a raw milk tank and covered with UV-reflective coated fabric. In this manner, the heat gain from strong solar radiation especially at the top of the tank is limited. Compared to the other proposed techniques such as evaporative coolers or coolers with ice-compartment, this method requires neither electrical power nor mechanical components to modify the current transport practices. In addition, the external application of PCM gel packs avoids direct contact with the milk, eliminating the risk of cross-contamination from ice compartments and removing the need for drainage. Since no modification of the container design is required, the proposed system is adaptable to existing tanks without structural changes, thus cost-effective. The thermal performance of this technique was experimentally investigated through temperature measurements under various ambient conditions. A simplified heat transfer model was also developed to predict product temperature changes under different operational conditions such as initial product temperatures, ambient temperatures, and mass of gel packs. Moreover, the system performance to preserve milk quality was evaluated by performing microbiological enumeration of E. coli (hygiene indicator) and total coliform. A predictive microbiological model was developed to assess E. coli growth in raw cow milk using the time-temperature profiles obtained from the experiments. The findings provide a framework for supporting small producers in maintaining milk safety and quality while minimizing financial burdens, thereby enhancing dairy supply chains. [SOURCE: Page_8]

2. Materials and Methods [SOURCE: Page_8]

2.1 Experimental setup for thermal analysis [SOURCE: Page_9]

A PCM was purchased from a local manufacturer (Igloogel Ezy, Koolion, Thailand). It is made of water mixed with gelling agent (confidential data) and filled in a plastic pouch (width × height: 150 mm × 225 mm, mass: 0.4 kg). It has a melting temperature of 0 °C and a latent heat of fusion of 330 kJ·kg–1 (Schweke et al., 2019). It should be noted that the gelling agent in the PCM provides structural stability to the pouch contents by preventing leakage in case of damage, thereby reducing the risk of cross-contamination with products. While the thermal parameters are similar to those of water, the gelled form improves handling, reuse, and packaging integrity, particularly during repeated freezing and thawing cycles (Cong et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_9]

The PCM packs were placed horizontally in a freezer (Panasonic SF-PC997, Japan) at a temperature setting of −20 °C. For each experiment, fourteen fully frozen PCM packs (total weight: 5.6 kg) were attached to the outer sidewall of an aluminum tank (base diameter: 300 mm, lid diameter: 200 mm, height: 460 mm) using elastic bands, as depicted in Fig. 2b. For thermal analysis, water was used as substitute for milk, and the tank was filled with 20 L of water. Three tanks were prepared for the experiments: one without PCM (denoted Control, Fig. 2a), one with PCM and polyvinyl chloride (PVC) cover (denoted PCM-PVC, Fig. 2c), and one with PCM and high-density polyethylene (HDPE) cover (denoted PCM-HDPE, Fig. 2d). The PVC cover was externally coated with UV protection and internally lined with laminated nonwoven (SC Car Cover, Thailand). The HDPE cover was made of two layers of HDPE fibers with 97% UV protection (Tyvek 1560K, Dupont, USA). It is important to note that the PVC cover is locally manufactured and is 1/4 cheaper than the HDPE cover. The thermal properties of both covers are unavailable from neither literature nor manufacturers. [SOURCE: Page_9]

Indeed, the main purpose of using UV-reflective coated fabric is to minimize heat gain from solar radiation while limiting the aluminum container heating. According to Jantapirak et al. (2024), the temperature of aluminum containers can rapidly rise from 15 °C to 40-50 °C within 2 hours of direct sunlight exposure, which can accelerate PCM melting. To highlight the thermal protection offered by the covers, an additional experiment using only PCM (Fig. 2b) was conducted, and the results of water temperature evolution were compared with the ones of PCM-PVC cover (Fig. 2c). [SOURCE: Page_9]

Figure 2 Photographs of an aluminum tank (a) without PCM, (b) with PCM before covered, (c) with PCM and PVC cover, (d) with PCM and HDPE cover. [SOURCE: Page_10]

The experiment was also conducted using raw milk to compare its cooling behavior with that of water. The milk was purchased from a local supplier and transported within one hour in an insulated box to the Department of Food Engineering laboratory, School of Engineering, King Mongkut’s Institute of Technology Ladkrabang, Bangkok, Thailand. Upon arrival, the milk temperature was approximately 15 °C. To attain the same initial temperature as water, it was warmed to 35-37 °C using a water bath (Memmert, WTB24, Germany) set at 45 °C. In this experiment, three aluminum tanks were prepared: one filled with 20 L of raw milk and without PCM, one with 20 L of raw milk and with PCM, and one with 20 L water and PCM. The PVC cover was applied to the tanks with PCM in this experiment. [SOURCE: Page_10]

2.2 Temperature measurements [SOURCE: Page_10]

Calibrated T-type thermocouples (sensor diameter: 0.3 mm, accuracy: ±0.2 °C within the range of 2 °C to 40 °C) were used for temperature measurements. As shown in Fig. 3, six thermocouples were positioned to measure the water temperature at three different heights (top, middle, and bottom) and at two radial positions (centerline and 2 mm from the sidewall). An additional thermocouple was used to measure ambient temperature. All these thermocouples were connected to a data acquisition unit (Keysight, DAQ970A/DAQM901A, USA). The PCM temperature was measured by inserting a thermocouple into the center of the PCM pack before placing it in a freezer. Two instrumented PCM packs were attached to the aluminum tank walls: [SOURCE: Page_10]

one on the top row and the other one on the bottom row. These thermocouples were connected to a portable data acquisition unit (Lutron, TM-1947SD, Taiwan). [SOURCE: Page_11]

The experiments with water were conducted under three different temperature conditions: two constant temperatures (25 °C and 35 °C) in a temperature-controlled room, and a real ambient temperature condition in the afternoon without shading (to simulate worst-case scenario). Data was recorded every minute for at least 3 hours. Each experiment was conducted with two replications to ensure the reliability and repeatability of the results. The experiment with raw milk was conducted with a single replication under the real ambient temperature condition to minimize the waste of valuable products. [SOURCE: Page_11]

Figure 3 Schematic diagram showing temperature measurement positions. [SOURCE: Page_11]

2.3 Simplified heat transfer model [SOURCE: Page_11]

A simplified heat transfer model based on energy balance was developed to investigate the temperature evolution of milk in a tank equipped with a PCM under various operating conditions, such as ambient temperature, mass and type of PCM. As depicted in Fig. 4, the real tank geometry (a conical frustum at the top and a cylinder at the bottom) was simplified to a vertical circular cylinder with two layers (milk and PCM). The tank layer was neglected because of its small thickness (3 mm) and high thermal conductivity (aluminum alloy, 𝜆 = 177 W·m−1·K−1) resulting in low thermal resistance (~ 2×10–5 m2·K·W–1) in comparison with the convective heat transfer resistances between milk and wall (typically between 10–2 and 10–1 m2·K·W–1) and between PCM and air (typically of the order of 10–1 m2·K·W–1). [SOURCE: Page_11]

Figure 4 Schematic diagram of a simplified heat transfer model. [SOURCE: Page_12]

3.4 Model validation and simulation [SOURCE: Page_21]

By fitting the model results to the experimental values obtained under a constant temperature condition of 25 °C, the RMSE values for the water temperatures at the top and bottom zones in the tank with PCM and PVC cover were 0.4 °C and 0.8 °C, respectively (Fig. 9a). [SOURCE: Page_21]

The model was further validated using experimental data from the other two ambient conditions. Under the constant temperature condition (35.2±0.1 °C), the RMSE values were 0.9 °C and 1.6 °C for the top and bottom zones, respectively (Fig. 9b). Under the varying temperature condition (35.2±1.2 °C), the RMSE was 0.7 °C for the top zone and 3.0 °C for the bottom zone (Fig. 9c). Similar results were observed for the tank with PCM and HDPE cover, where the RMSE values remained below 2.0 °C under constant temperature conditions and below 3.0 °C under variable temperature condition (data not shown). [SOURCE: Page_21]

It was noted that the simulated temperatures began to increase earlier than the experimental values after the PCM was completely melted. This discrepancy may be due to an overestimation of heat exchange between the PCM and ambient air. Nonetheless, the model demonstrated acceptable precision, particularly during melting period. Nevertheless, it uses several simplifying assumptions (e.g., constant external heat transfer coefficients), therefore, the model could be applied only under comparable conditions such as similar tank geometry, ambient air velocity, and absence of solar radiation but it remains capable of accounting for [SOURCE: Page_21]

various initial product temperatures, ambient conditions, and PCM mass. The present model uses constant heat transfer coefficients; incorporating correlations from the literature or additional dedicated experiments could enhance its reliability. This would notably allow extrapolation to a broader range of ambient air velocities. With further experimental validation and refinement, the model could serve as a practical tool for optimizing the use of water-based gel packs to maintain raw milk temperature during transport. [SOURCE: Page_22]

To study the influence of PCM mass on the milk temperature evolution, simulations were undertaken. The results shown in Fig. 10 revealed that the milk temperature cannot be below 10 °C at 120 min with PCM mass varying from 3 to 12 kg. Also, increasing the PCM mass beyond 7 kg does not result in significant temperature reduction. According to Thai regulations, raw milk with ambient temperature must be delivered to the collection center within 2 hours. Thus, the technique proposed in the present study would enable producers to preserve milk with better microbial quality as explained in Section 3.5. [SOURCE: Page_22]

3.5 Microbiological quality [SOURCE: Page_23]

The microbiological quality of raw milk was assessed by enumerating E. coli and total coliform counts in milk stored in a tank equipped with PCM-PVC and comparing these counts with those from a control tank without PCM. The experiment was conducted under variable ambient temperature (𝑇̅𝑒 = 35.4±1.6 °C) to reproduce real practices. As shown in Fig. 11, the counts of E. coli and total coliform in the control tank increased by 1.9 and 4.4 times, respectively, after 2 hours. The tank with PCM-PVC demonstrated moderate slower bacterial growth, with E. coli and total coliform counts increasing by only 1.5 and 3.1 times, respectively. Although the PCM system was unable to lower the milk temperature to the recommended safe storage level (≤ 4 °C), the observed temperature reduction (around 10-13 °C within an hour) slowed the bacteria proliferation and kept milk safely for 1-2 hours after milking. This finding underscores the potential of PCM-based cooling systems to enhance milk safety during short-term storage and transportation. [SOURCE: Page_23]

While the observed reductions in E. coli and total coliform growth are consistent with improved thermal control, it should be noted that differences in the initial microbial load between the control and PCM tanks might have an influence on the results. Therefore, the microbiological findings should be interpreted as indicative of a trend rather than as conclusive evidence. [SOURCE: Page_23]

Error bars indicate the standard deviation of three replicates. [SOURCE: Page_23]

A mathematical model was used to predict the growth of E. coli based on experimental time- temperature profiles of water in the tanks under constant and real variable ambient temperatures. Considering that the milk profiles are the same for water, the predicted results expressed as the ratio of E. coli counts to the initial counts (𝑦⁄𝑦0 ) show no different growth for milk in the PCM-PVC and PCM-HDPE tanks (Fig. 12). At 120 minutes, 𝑦⁄𝑦0 in the tank without PCM was approximately 1.1-1.2 times higher than the one of PCM-PVC and PCM- HDPE. [SOURCE: Page_24]

Under real ambient temperature conditions (Fig. 12), at 120 min, the predicted E. coli counts in the control tank (𝑦⁄𝑦0 = 1.3) and the tanks with PCM-PVC (𝑦⁄𝑦0 ≈ 1.1-1.2) were lower than the measured values (𝑦⁄𝑦0 = 1.9 for the control and 𝑦⁄𝑦0 = 1.5 for PCM-PVC). This discrepancy is due to, on one hand, the difficulties of microbial analysis and on the other hand, the uncertainty in model parameterization (lag phase, growth rate…). The parameters proposed by Ačai et al. (2015) were derived from E. coli pure culture growth in UHT milk, where only the target bacteria was present while there was an antagonistic effect in our study. [SOURCE: Page_24]

Despite these limitations, the predictive model still provides an insight into the effects of temperature control on E. coli proliferation and emphasizes the benefits of PCM-based cooling systems in moderating microbial growth under practical conditions. According to the Thai Agricultural Standard, total coliform counts in raw milk must not exceed 104 CFU·mL–1 considered as the threshold value. Based on the model predictions, if the initial E. coli population in the tanks was 3.5×103 CFU·mL–1, the population in the control tank would exceed the threshold value within 72 minutes while the one with PCM-PVC would allow the population below this value. The result suggests that the proposed cooling system offers a means to preserve milk safety during transportation and short-term storage. [SOURCE: Page_24]

### 4. Conclusions

This study proposed and evaluated a practical and cost-effective cooling system for preserving raw milk quality during transportation from farm to collection centers. The system utilized water-based PCM gel packs attached to the milk tank with a UV-reflective cover. The experiments were conducted under constant and real variable ambient conditions using water as a substitute for milk to demonstrate the system potential to temperature reduction. The comparative study showed that the thermal behavior of milk and water was almost similar. The milk time-temperature profile was considered the same as the one of water. The key findings are as follows: [SOURCE: Page_25]

- The tanks with PCM allowed water temperature reduction rate by approximately 10-13 °C within one hour for both types of cover (PVC and HDPE). According to the lower price of PVC, it is more suitable for small producers. [SOURCE: Page_25]

- A simplified heat transfer model was developed and validated with experimental data, demonstrating an RMSE of less than 3.0 °C. With this level of accuracy, the present model enables reliable predictions of milk temperature variations, especially during PCM melting, under comparable conditions such as same tank geometry and comparable ambient air velocities. [SOURCE: Page_25]

- The above-mentioned temperature reduction allowed for about 1.3-fold slower growth of E. coli and total coliforms than in tanks without PCM. This suggests the system may help reduce the bacterial multiplication during short-term storage and transportation; however, the results should be interpreted cautiously due to variability in initial microbial levels. [SOURCE: Page_25]

- The predicted E. coli growth based on time-temperature profiles differed slightly from the measured results. This can be explained by the growth of several strains of bacteria in raw milk in experimental work, while only one bacterium (without antagonistic effect) was used to create the model growth parameters. Despite this, the model offered useful information on the effectiveness of temperature control strategies. [SOURCE: Page_25]

Overall, the present study addresses several challenges, including the application of PCM combined with a cover on milk tanks to preserve milk quality during short-distance transport, [SOURCE: Page_25]

with a focus on low-cost and practical implementation. These challenges justify the development of both experimental and modeling approaches (based on simplified assumptions) that may be useful for creating practical guidelines for small-scale producers in tropical countries and for stakeholders without expertise in sophisticated modeling techniques. [SOURCE: Page_26]
