ABSTRACT. Deltas are complex social-ecological systems (SESs) that involve many economic activities that interconnect closely with ecological processes and biodiversity. Sustainable management of such intricate SESs requires developing an integrated and understandable representation of their main human and ecological entities, processes, and interactions. We developed a formal representation of the Ile de Camargue (center of the Rhône delta), in which hydro-saline dynamics, water management, economic activities (e.g., agriculture, hunting, fishing, tourism), and natural environments (i.e., habitats for biodiversity) interact. The conceptual model was based on four submodels that provided complementary representations of i) water governance, ii) agriculture, and iii) bird and iv) fish communities. The conceptual model highlights the strong relationships (i.e., causal chains, feedback loops, interactions, side effects, and trade-offs) among the multiple entities of this deltaic SES. It provides information about the region and describes deltas as a SES to support decision-making. One major feedback loop in the delta concerned relationships among regulations, rice fields, and use of marshes as a habitat for birds: strengthening pesticide regulations to improve water quality and bird habitats caused the area of rice cultivation to decrease which degraded habitat conditions for birds. Directly related to pesticide use, the most common trade-off is to decide whether water management should prioritize water quality or quantity in the delta’s Vaccarès lagoon, and opinion differs among stakeholders. The conceptual model could be used as a starting point to develop computer-based integrated assessment and modeling to explore dynamics of and resilience to climate change in the Ile de Camargue. Appendix 1: Detailed information on a) geology, b) climate c) ecological context and d) human activities of the Ile de Camargue a) Geology Traditionally, the Ile de Camargue is divised into two morphological units (Bouteyre and Toni 1972): i) fluvio-lagoonal (from Arles to south of the Vaccarès lagoon), composed of a fluvial cover, with riverbanks deposited over lagoonal salt formations (higher elevations), and ii) lagoonal-marine (south of the Vaccarès lagoon to the Mediterranean sea), consisting of marine and lagoonal deposits covered by a shallow fluvial layer (lower elevations) (Fig. 1). [SOURCE: Page_18]

b) Climate The Rhône delta has a Mediterranean climate, with large amounts of sunshine (> 2,500 h/yr), summer drought, and abundant autumn precipitation. c) Ecological context The Camargue is one of the most biodiverse wetlands in the Mediterranean basin, containing many natural areas (which cover more than 33% of the region) and including a variety of habitat types: ponds, lagoons, marshes, dry grasslands, reedbeds, and salt meadows (with allomorphic vegetation) (Mathevet 2004, Picon 1990). Throughout the year, the Ile de Camargue hosts many waterbird species, which represent the delta's most emblematic taxonomic group, with one of the largest breeding colonies of greater flamingos (Phoenicopterus roseus) in the Mediterranean basin (Johnson and Cézilly 2009). In winter, it is a major staging area for common cranes (Grus grus), greylag geese (Anser anser), ducks (e.g. Mareca strepera, Anas platyrhynchos, Anas crecca), and large amounts of passerines on farmland (Galewski and Devictor 2016). Due to the abundance and diversity of natural habitats, the region supports many fish species (Crivelli 1981). However, ecological continuity can be complex due to the amount of hydraulic infrastructure in the area (Bouchard et al. 2022, Nicolas et al. 2021). Fish populations include marine and freshwater species, with strong conservation concerns for two heritage species: the European eel (Anguilla anguilla) and short-snouted seahorse (Hippocampus hippocampus). The delta was designated a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve in 1977 and a Ramsar site in 1986 due to its international importance for nesting, staging, and wintering waterbirds. France designated the Rhône delta a Regional Natural Park, which is a contract-based park established between national and local governments that must promote conservation of natural and cultural heritage. Strict biodiversity protection is enforced in four nature reserves. Agricultural land is located in the northern, western, and eastern sections of the region (Fig 1), in three zones (Migairou-Leprince 2021): i) alluvial ridges, near the Rhône, with high agricultural potential and cereal systems, vineyards, some cattle breeding, and market gardening; ii) intermediate elevations, consisting of a mosaic of environments with highly variable agricultural potential; and iii) the lowest hydromorphic and halomorphic elevations, generally in marshes or semi-natural environments, which are sometimes grazed by cattle or [SOURCE: Page_18]

managed for environmental conservation. Flooded rice is cultivated in all of these areas, especially the lowest. [SOURCE: Page_19]

d) Human activities The main human activities include agriculture, salt production, hunting, fishing, tourism, (including eco-tourism and birdwatching), and nature conservation. Most of these activities depend directly on water management, either pumping water from the Rhône (e.g. agriculture, hunting marshes) or managing the hydraulic structures/pumping stations that connect the region to the sea (i.e. salt production) (Béchet et al. 2009, Béchet et al. 2012, Béchet and Johnson, 2008, Boutron et al. 2021). Most of the land in the Camargue is privately owned, but ca. 20% of it is owned by national or regional government conservation organizations, including an agency for the conservation of coastal wetlands, as nature reserves and publicly managed marshes. Appendix 2: Detailed information on water-flow processes a) Water flows to and from the Rhône river When the Rhône’s flow is low, seawater can move upstream the river and the pumping stations are stopped to avoid transferring saline water into the irrigation canals (Ibaňez et al. 1997, Levasseur and Doutriaux 1992). The canals are used mainly to fill and drain agricultural fields (No. e5) used to grow crops (No. e6), especially rice, or to rear livestock (No. e7), but are also used to flood natural marshes (No. e8), some of which are used for hunting. As livestock can drink from the canals, they have greatly eroded many of the banks (BRL 2004). Along the canals, fields are filled and drained using hydraulic valves, small bags to stop water flow, or short-term mobile pumping stations (photographs S1, S2, and S3 in the Appendix 6). The canals have low slopes, and even slightly reversed slopes in certain sections, especially after local dredging. Depending on the volume of water pumped and on wind conditions, some canals can flow in either direction. In addition to the ones connected to the Rhône, pumping stations have been installed in several places in the canal network (Nos. e9 and e16) to ensure that upstream canals remain connected to higher-elevation downstream canals. The aquatic ecosystem in the center of the Ile de Camargue includes three interconnected lagoons that form the Vaccarès lagoon system (No. e10 in Fig. 3): Vaccarès, Impériaux and Lion/Dame (Fig. 2b). The Vaccarès lagoon system receives some of the Ile de Camargue's drainage water that is not pumped back into the Rhône. Water is supplied continuously throughout the year via the Fumemorte drainage canal (Fig. 2a), which outlet has no water- control structure. Four other main drainage canals can supply additional drainage water to the Vaccarès lagoon system: i) Roquemaure (Fig. 2a) (depending on the management of the “ Mar” hydraulic structure, 4.5 km from the Vaccarès lagoon system), ii) Rousty (depending on management of the sluice gates at its outlet in the Vaccarès lagoon system; No. e11 in Fig. 3), iii) Sigoulette (depending on management of the sluice gates at its outlet in the Vaccarès lagoon system), and iv) Consécanière (pumping station of the “Three Gorges”). Approximately 10 small drainage canals can supply water to the Vaccarès lagoon system, but their combined volume is much lower (Boutron et al. 2015). As the slopes are shallow, high water levels in the lagoons may cause saline water in them to flow into drainage canals, which prevents pumping water out of fields used for livestock to keep them from being flooded. The wind can also cause or exacerbate this reverse flow. b) Connections to the sea At two locations (Fig. 2a), two pumping stations can be activated to pump additional saline water from the former saltworks into the pre-concentration ponds of the Salins de Giraud saltworks (No. e15), which remains in operation. Exchanges of surface water between the Ile de Camargue and the sea are managed in the coastal lagoons, except for the Station de l'Eolienne pumping station (No.e16), which is used to drain water directly into the sea near the Pertuis de la Fourcade. c) Groundwater flows Groundwater and its associated salt can be exchanged indirectly in the Ile de Camargue due to water management. The Rhône delta contains a deep aquifer and a shallow aquifer composed of fine sediments of fluvio-lagoonal and lagoonal-marine origin. They are separated by a thick layer of clay, silt, and peat, which strongly limits potential exchanges between them (Torres- Rondon 2013). Soil salinity varies greatly within and among the region’s agricultural fields due to geomorphology, including the shallowness of groundwater, and the high evapotranspiration, which causes salt to accumulate at lower elevations (Torres-Rondon 2013). Appendix 4: Detailed information on agriculture a) Livestock farming The Camargue has mixed crop-livestock systems and traditional farms with semi-feral bulls, which allow for crop diversification after a rice monoculture, and livestock farming can replace vineyards on certain fields. Farmers set up grazing areas for livestock on natural meadows, natural marshes, and irrigable fields. Some fields can be managed as marshes to graze bulls or for hunting. The semi-feral bull farms benefit from a local reputation in bullfighting circles. Some farms are owned, while others rent or have grazing contracts for most of their area. Tourism and leasing marshes for hunting are additional sources of income for the farms. Bull breeding and traditional activities are organized to attract tourists, such as the capturing and branding of bulls, bullfighting, an annual bull race, and horse riding. Many of the farms are organic, as their extensive herds are easier to convert to organic requirements. The ability to produce alfalfa and grassland on the farm also makes it easier to convert the fields to organic farming (Migairou- Leprince 2021). [SOURCE: Page_25]

Appendix 5: Detailed information on biodiversity a) Bird Due to the diversity of habitats in the Ile de Camargue, 113 bird species (e.g. flamingos, ducks, herons, cranes) are present for breeding, migration, and wintering periods (Kayser et al. 2008) (Fig. 5). Waterbirds staging and breeding relies directly on flooded areas, salinity and quality of water. For example, salinity and marshland surface area can decrease the abundance of most duck species (Brochet et al. 2009), these criteria depend to some extent on the irrigation management. Studies have highlighted that annual changes in Eurasian teal (Anas crecca) abundance are due to salinity and the open-water area available (Vallecillo et al. 2023). The water level of the Vaccarès lagoon is a good predictor of the abundance of breeding greater flamingos in spring as it correlates with the foraging area available to them (Béchet and Johnson 2008). The Camargue hosts four types of hunting (Mathevet 2004): − Communal, with membership of and subscription to the communal association and leased hunting grounds, which are public or private − Private, in which hunters, who often do not live in the Camargue, lease hunting rights from a landowner or the hunting association. This includes land owned by large landowners, often farmers. The number of ducks taken depends on the price of the hunting right. − Commercial or day, with the release of farm-raised mallards (Anas platyrhynchos) − Business or social, for a landowner's guests, which may occur along with private hunting, with the guests contributing to the costs of marsh management b) Fish Many fish in the Camargue are of commercial interest, such as the European sea bass (Dicentrarchus labrax), sargo (Diplodus sargus), gilt-head bream (Sparus aurata), sole (Solea solea), and turbot (Scophthalmus maximus). As the Gulf of Beauduc and the lagoons have high nutrient concentrations and plankton density, they host the most species. Several species of sharks and rays thrive on the sandy seabed of the Camargue. Eels such as the European eel and silver eel are some of Camargue's most emblematic species, which live there before they leave to reproduce in the Sargasso Sea. The fish community in the irrigation canals comes from the Rhône because its fish pass through pumping stations and then enter the Vaccarès lagoon system (Poizat et al. 1999). The species include the European eel, Rhône shad (Alosa fallax), and sea lamprey (Petromyzon marinus). Fish require high water quality, which is a concern due to the pesticides used in the Camargue and the increased risk of flows of saline water into the canals. Fish are a strong commercial interest in the Camargue, depending on the type of fishing authorized. The Ile de Camargue hosts three main types of fishing: − Professional, mainly in the Vaccarès lagoon system. It requires obtaining authorization to fish. Professional fishing occurs in several types of environments. At sea, nets are used to catch sole, European sea bass, gilt-head bream (Sparus aurata), tuna (Thunnus spp.), and turbot (Scophthalmus maximus). In the ponds, a few dozen fishermen catch mainly eels and silversides (Atherina spp). In the Rhône, fishermen catch mainly species with a high commercial value (e.g. sea bass, eels, flounder, mullet (Mugil spp.). − Licensed recreational, which occurs in the Rhône, the canals, and the former saltworks. It requires obtaining a license from the fishing federation. Unlicensed recreational, which is allowed only in the sea or the Beauduc lagoon. [SOURCE: Page_26]

Appendix 7: Report of the meeting with local stakeholders The main issues to emerge from the meeting could be divided into three groups: the global one, the marginal one and the future one. The global ones are the ones which the majority of the groups spoke about and/or which are an integral part of the questions being asked in the region. The marginal ones are the one cited by only a group or really precise. The future ones are questions that are very likely to arise in the future or prospective scenarios Global issues: - Importance of agriculture to the region, and should be adapted to the environment, taking into account natural areas (Vaccarès) and joint management based on multiple objectives (conservation, fishery, hunting). The dynamics of agricultural land must be taken into account. Historically, there have been a number of increases and decreases in agricultural land. At present, the area is shrinking as a result of salinisation and urbanisation. - Agriculture is particularly affected by changes in subsidies, especially the CAP. Indeed, subsidies have a direct effect on crop rotation, and production systems are highly responsive. Since the reduction in agricultural support and subsidies for industrial tomato growing, rice fields are gradually being replaced by market gardening. The dynamics of organic farming plots show the impact of CAP aid, as there is aid for conversion but not for maintenance, and many plots in the region oscillate between organic and conventional farming. - There seems to be a need for technical and economic balance in farming systems: solutions that are technically possible and economically viable. In particular, through the possibility of using plant protection products and therefore the acceptance of these products and their correct use. This requires collaboration and negotiation rather than just regulation. - Farmers finance most of the hydraulic systems in the area (canal network, pumping station).
