1   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

2   Since the agricultural sector is one of the main drivers responsible for the transgression of planetary [SOURCE: Page_3]

3   boundaries, there is an urgent need to reduce its environmental impacts. To address this issue, [SOURCE: Page_3]

4   international institutions advocate for digital agriculture, but formalised and comprehensive [SOURCE: Page_3]

5   assessment of its environmental impacts is still lacking. This systematic review aims at studying papers [SOURCE: Page_3]

6   dedicated to the environmental assessment of the use of digital technologies in agriculture, i.e. of [SOURCE: Page_3]

7   digital agriculture. In the 69 studies selected by the PRISMA method, most of the DATs dealt with crop [SOURCE: Page_3]

8   production, supporting technical production management (e.g. fertilisation or irrigation). Almost a [SOURCE: Page_3]

9   third of DATs were robots or automation tools. [SOURCE: Page_3]

10   Out of the selected studies, 36 applied the Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) framework. A focus was placed [SOURCE: Page_3]

11   on these 36 case studies, which were examined in the light of the four LCA standardised stages, i.e. [SOURCE: Page_3]

12   goal and scope, life cycle inventory (LCI), life cycle impact assessment (LCIA) and interpretation. Our [SOURCE: Page_3]

13   review results show that most of the studies aimed to assess the environmental impacts of digitalising [SOURCE: Page_3]

14   an agricultural system using either a mass or surface-based functional unit, or sometimes both. The [SOURCE: Page_3]

15   system boundaries seldomly included the DAT life cycle which were sometimes modelled with proxies [SOURCE: Page_3]

16   and assumptions at the LCI stage to address data gaps. Assumptions were also needed to quantify the [SOURCE: Page_3]

17   effects of DAT on the agricultural system, such as yield benefits or food waste reduction. At the LCIA [SOURCE: Page_3]

18   stage, midpoint indicators were often selected. Turning to the interpretation step, it is worth noting [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   that few studies carried out uncertainty and sensitivity analysis. Finally, most of these 36 studies [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   concluded that digitalisation reduced the environmental impacts of the agricultural system due to [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   efficiency gains, or the use of electricity instead of fossil fuel. Conversely, three studies determined [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   mineral resource use, marine eutrophication, land use and ionising radiations, as high technologies [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   system. Research perspectives for DAT environmental assessment were also discussed that take into [SOURCE: Page_3]

1       1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_6]

2   The agricultural sector is one of the main drivers behind the transgression of planetary boundaries, [SOURCE: Page_6]

3   and the surpassing of thresholds essential for a secure human society (Rockström et al., 2020). As the [SOURCE: Page_6]

4   third-largest emitter of greenhouse gases (GHGs) accounting for 22% of the global emissions, [SOURCE: Page_6]

5   agriculture has exceeded the planetary boundary for climate change (IPCC, 2023). It also jeopardises [SOURCE: Page_6]

6   biodiversity integrity through the conversion of natural areas into agricultural lands and the [SOURCE: Page_6]

7   intensification of farming practices (Balvanera et al., 2019). Regarding the freshwater-use planetary [SOURCE: Page_6]

8   boundary, agriculture is responsible for 60% of global freshwater withdrawals (Wu et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_6]

9   Additionally, the agricultural sector exceeds accepted nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) application [SOURCE: Page_6]

10   thresholds, thereby affecting geochemical flows with negative repercussions on aquatic and terrestrial [SOURCE: Page_6]

11   ecosystems (Rockström et al., 2020). Therefore, there is an urgent need to reduce the environmental [SOURCE: Page_6]

12   impacts of agriculture, especially as food requirements are expected to increase as a result of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

13   world’s growing population (WWAP (United Nations World Water Assessment Programme), 2016). [SOURCE: Page_6]

14   To tackle the environmental issues of the agricultural sector, international institutions and European [SOURCE: Page_6]

15   policy programs recommend digital agriculture (European Commission, 2021; FAO and Zhejiang [SOURCE: Page_6]

16   University, 2021). Researchers also assert that digital agriculture can accelerate agroecology (Bellon [SOURCE: Page_6]

17   Maurel and Huyghe, 2017; Duff et al., 2022). Digital agriculture is an agricultural and food system which [SOURCE: Page_6]

18   relies on Digital Agriculture Technologies (DATs), and combines digital sciences and technologies [SOURCE: Page_6]

19   related to the following three domains (Bellon-Maurel et al., 2023; Shepherd et al., 2020): i) Data [SOURCE: Page_6]

20   acquisition. For instance, satellite or aerial images provide information about visible and non-visible [SOURCE: Page_6]

21   radiations of crops, which can be linked to a specific crop’s biophysical proprieties (e.g. health, cover, [SOURCE: Page_6]

22   soil moisture) (Liaghat, 2010); ii) Data transfer and storage, exemplified by Low Power Long Range [SOURCE: Page_6]

23   (LoRa) technology that enhances IoT sensor communication over long ranges, which is particularly [SOURCE: Page_6]

24   important in agriculture to cover big crop field areas (Gutiérrez et al., 2019; Qazi et al., 2022); iii) Data [SOURCE: Page_6]

1 [SOURCE: Page_6]

25   processing, which drive decision-making along the food value chain. It includes deep learning, which is [SOURCE: Page_7]

26   used to detect crop diseases, classify land cover or crops, or estimate the yield from images (Kamilaris [SOURCE: Page_7]

27   and Prenafeta-Boldú, 2018). Robotics can be considered as a fourth domain that combines data [SOURCE: Page_7]

28   acquisition technologies and data processing technologies to automate agricultural operations. [SOURCE: Page_7]

29   DATs were first used in precision agriculture and livestock farming in the 1990s in order to modulate [SOURCE: Page_7]

30   agricultural inputs at field scale, and to monitor livestock production (Bellon-Maurel et al., 2023; Tullo [SOURCE: Page_7]

31   et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2002). The use of these DATs was aimed at rationalizing inputs to mitigate [SOURCE: Page_7]

32   the environmental impacts of agriculture. In contrast to precision agriculture and livestock farming, [SOURCE: Page_7]

33   smart farming provides real-time information. It is a more recent concept driven by the Internet of [SOURCE: Page_7]

34   Things (IoT) and Big Data (Wolfert et al., 2017). Digital agriculture is a broader concept than those that [SOURCE: Page_7]

35   define precision agriculture, precision livestock farming and smart farming, as it can take place at all [SOURCE: Page_7]

36   the agricultural ecosystem levels (i.e. farm, support services, territory, supply chain). Digital agriculture [SOURCE: Page_7]

37   is indeed a very dynamic sector offering new tools and applications. [SOURCE: Page_7]

38   Despite growing interest, the environmental impacts of digital agriculture, i.e. those linked to DAT [SOURCE: Page_7]

39   applications, are poorly known. Reviews by Bunge et al. (2022), Lameski et al. (2018), Lovarelli et al. [SOURCE: Page_7]

40   (2020), Medici et al. (2020), Mor et al. (2021) and Tullo et al. (2019) have examined the environmental [SOURCE: Page_7]

41   impacts of various DATs in use (automated weed control, precision livestock farming, precision [SOURCE: Page_7]

42   agriculture, blockchain or artificial intelligence technologies) based on qualitative assessment. [SOURCE: Page_7]

43   However, a more comprehensive assessment of the wide range of DATs applications is lacking. [SOURCE: Page_7]

44   Furthermore, their environmental impacts still need to be quantified using formalised environmental [SOURCE: Page_7]

45   assessment methods. [SOURCE: Page_7]

46   Life cycle assessment (LCA) is a standardised and recognised method that serves to assess the [SOURCE: Page_7]

47   environmental impacts of products and services (ISO, 2006a, 2006b). It is commonly used to assess [SOURCE: Page_7]

48   food products and agricultural systems (Boschiero et al., 2023; Dorr et al., 2021; Poore and Nemecek, [SOURCE: Page_7]

2 [SOURCE: Page_7]

Postprint version – Huck, Gobrecht, Salou, Bellon-Maurel, Loiseau [SOURCE: Page_8]

49   2018) as well as ICTs and digital technologies (Arushanyan et al., 2014). Therefore, LCA is one of the [SOURCE: Page_8]

50   several methods used to formally assess the environmental impacts of DATs. [SOURCE: Page_8]

51   This paper aims to provide a comprehensive review of the environmental assessment of DATs in the [SOURCE: Page_8]

52   scientific literature, to address the following question: what are the main methodological guidelines [SOURCE: Page_8]

53   and challenges for assessing the environmental impacts of DATs, especially with LCA? [SOURCE: Page_8]

54   Section 2 describes the method used for the systematic review based on the PRISMA method. This [SOURCE: Page_8]

55   section also describes the analysis criteria for the selected papers, with a more in-depth analysis of [SOURCE: Page_8]

56   papers using LCA. Section 3 provides a general overview of the agricultural sectors, the DATs and the [SOURCE: Page_8]

57   environmental assessment methods used in the papers. In section 4, particular emphasis was put on [SOURCE: Page_8]

58   LCA case studies which are analysed according to the four LCA stages of the standardised framework [SOURCE: Page_8]

59   revealing the main methodological gaps to carry out a comprehensive and consistent DATs [SOURCE: Page_8]

60   environmental assessment. Finally, section 5 proposes recommendations to address these gaps as well [SOURCE: Page_8]

61   as discussion about main research perspectives. [SOURCE: Page_8]

62       2. Material & Method [SOURCE: Page_8]

63   This study systematically reviews the environmental assessment of DATs found in scientific literature. [SOURCE: Page_8]

64   The review was performed using Rayyan software (Ouzzani et al., 2016) and following the systematic [SOURCE: Page_8]

65   research articles method PRISMA (Moher et al., 2009; Page et al., 2021). The selected studies based [SOURCE: Page_8]

66   on the LCA framework are then further analysed according to the four stages of the standardised [SOURCE: Page_8]

67   methodology. [SOURCE: Page_8]

91   For the sake of clarity, DATs were grouped into a few LCA-relevant categories. DATs are made up of a [SOURCE: Page_11]

92   succession of technologies used to acquire, transfer, store and process data (Bellon-Maurel et al., [SOURCE: Page_11]

93   2023). Although several DAT classifications already exist, they do not provide a clear distinction [SOURCE: Page_11]

94   between technologies (Leroux and Touraine, 2023; Moreno et al., 2024). Moreover, different DATs can [SOURCE: Page_11]

95   be used to provide the same service. Therefore, we classified DATs into four categories, depending on [SOURCE: Page_11]

96   the services they provide instead of the technology they are based on. The first category includes any [SOURCE: Page_11]

97   DATs supporting production management, i.e. the day-to-day running of agricultural operations to [SOURCE: Page_11]

98   optimise the agricultural production. The second category covers robotics and automation DATs which [SOURCE: Page_11]

99   replace human intervention in agricultural operations. The third category includes DATs used to make [SOURCE: Page_11]

100   strategic decisions, for instance agricultural route planners. The fourth category is related to DATs used [SOURCE: Page_11]

105   mention a method without referring to a standard or guidelines. Based on this analysis, studies were [SOURCE: Page_11]

509   technology. These two last points seem particularly relevant for emerging technologies, such as smart [SOURCE: Page_34]

510   home heating systems. The net energy savings made using this technology depend on user behaviour [SOURCE: Page_34]

555       5.4.    Considering rebound effects [SOURCE: Page_36]

556   Rebound effects can be defined as unintended consumption impacts which occur when improving [SOURCE: Page_36]

557   production efficiency in terms of energy, time, resources or costs savings (Coroamă and Pargman, [SOURCE: Page_36]

558   2020). They are likely to decrease or even exceed the environmental benefits expected from these [SOURCE: Page_36]

559   efficiency gains. It has already been acknowledged that ICT services can trigger rebound effects [SOURCE: Page_36]

560   (Berkhout and Hertin, 2004; Pohl et al., 2019). For instance, the number of meetings increases in the [SOURCE: Page_36]

561   case of teleworking when compared to those set in an office environment. The environmental impacts [SOURCE: Page_36]

562   of dematerialising a service may be overshadowed by re-materialisation, such as the printing of [SOURCE: Page_37]

563   documents available online. By seeking to improve the efficiency of working time or resource use, DATs [SOURCE: Page_37]

564   such as robotics, automation technologies or DATs for the technical management of the production [SOURCE: Page_37]

565   may induce rebound effects. For example, Mehmeti et al. (2016) reported that the water savings made [SOURCE: Page_37]

566   using a DAT for irrigation management were offset by crop cultivation which requires a lot of water or [SOURCE: Page_37]

567   increased irrigation surfaces. Rebound effects were also discussed in Bieser et al. (2023) and Pardo et [SOURCE: Page_37]

568   al. (2022). [SOURCE: Page_37]

569   Integrating rebound effects in LCA raises questions on modelling choices related, for instance, to the [SOURCE: Page_37]

570   definition of functional units, system boundaries or to data collection (Font Vivanco and Van Der Voet, [SOURCE: Page_37]

590       6. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_38]

591   This paper provides an overview on the existing scientific literature on the environmental assessment [SOURCE: Page_38]

592   of digitalisation in agriculture. Most studies were dedicated to DATs used in crop (68%), livestock (22%) [SOURCE: Page_38]

593   or both (3%), or in supply chain (7%). Among the different environmental assessment methods, LCA is [SOURCE: Page_38]

594   the most widely used (36 papers out of 69). Our analysis focused on the latter studies. [SOURCE: Page_38]

595   Some methodological constants and issues were highlighted during this analysis. However, the [SOURCE: Page_38]

596   methodological choices made in the LCA studies were heterogeneous, and not always clearly [SOURCE: Page_38]

597   established, which can limit the understanding, reproducibility and comparability of the studies. At the [SOURCE: Page_38]

598   goal and scope stage, different objectives were identified, the most frequent being the assessment of [SOURCE: Page_38]

599   the environmental impacts of digitalising an agricultural system. This objective led to different [SOURCE: Page_38]

600   situations when defining baseline and digital scenarios in order to consider various soil and climate [SOURCE: Page_38]

601   conditions, farming practices, productions and DATs. The most common FUs used in the studies were [SOURCE: Page_38]

602   mass and surface-based FUs, or sometimes both. DAT operating time was also used in LCA of DATs. [SOURCE: Page_38]

603   Regarding system boundaries, the main issue was that most studies did not include DAT life cycle which [SOURCE: Page_38]

604   makes it impossible to identify any trade-off between these processes, and DAT effects on the [SOURCE: Page_38]

605   agricultural system. Another issue was that agricultural machinery, ancillaries and infrastructures were [SOURCE: Page_38]

606   sometimes excluded from system boundaries, as they were not supposed to be influenced by DAT use [SOURCE: Page_38]

607   or due to a lack of data. [SOURCE: Page_38]

608   At the LCI stage, many proxies and assumptions were made to address data gaps. Several LCIA methods [SOURCE: Page_38]

609   were selected, with ReciPe being the most widely used. Midpoint indicators were selected in 25 [SOURCE: Page_38]

610   studies, sometimes with no justification. Finally, most studies affirmed that DATs mitigated the [SOURCE: Page_38]

611   environmental impacts of the agricultural system, but uncertainty and sensitivity analyses were [SOURCE: Page_39]

612   seldomly carried out. These results must also be considered with caution as many studies did not take [SOURCE: Page_39]

613   into account the DAT life cycle. [SOURCE: Page_39]

614   Based on our review, several recommendations are made on how to assess the environmental impacts [SOURCE: Page_39]

615   of digitalising agricultural systems. At the goal and scope stage, scenarios should be set up according [SOURCE: Page_39]
