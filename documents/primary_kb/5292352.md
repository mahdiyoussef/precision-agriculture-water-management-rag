29   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   In southern Tunisia, in an arid environment subjected to climate change, the olive-production [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   sector is important for the local Economy. With low local biomass production a multi sourced [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   compost composed of olive waste byproducts and seaweed was tested on an Arenic Calcisol. [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   The application at the subsurface of 180 kg (0.5 m^3) of compost per tree in a rainfed olive tree [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   (Olea europaea L.) grove with declining productivity showed a significant increase in total C, [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   N, K, P, and respiration rate in amended horizons. Surprisingly the soil volumic water content [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   (Vw%) at the subsurface (0-20cm) below amended (7.5%± 2.3) was not significantly different [SOURCE: Page_2]

37   below non amended trees (6.9%±3.3). It increased slightly under non-amended trees, from [SOURCE: Page_2]

38   6.6% (±4.3) at a depth of 5 cm to 8.1% (±1.5) at 65 cm depth, while below the amended olive [SOURCE: Page_2]

39   trees the Vw% increased from 6.7% (±3.4) in the composted horizon at 5cm depth to 13.5% [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   (±1.1) at 70 cm depth. The main positive effect of composting on soils was decreasing the [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   water loss due to capillary rise, since the compost developed clear hydrophobic properties. [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   Two years after applying the compost, olive tree branches were 15% longer and olives were [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   40% heavier on amended trees. [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   Growing olive trees (Olea europaea L.) is a major economic issue, especially in arid [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   Mediterranean regions. Rainfed olive groves occupy over 80% of the agricultural area in [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   southern Tunisia, such as the governorate of Medenine. However, in arid regions, production [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   of olives and olive oil increasingly fluctuates and is decreasing, due to climatic and biological [SOURCE: Page_3]

50   disturbances. The volume of olive production depends on several factors: the quality of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   soil on which the trees grow, soil management and the management of soil water content. [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   Water scarcity is a major problem in the Near East and North Africa (NENA) region; thus, [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   increasing water-use efficiency in agriculture is fundamental (Global Soil Partnership, 2014; [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   FAO, 2018). High variability in rainfall and meteorological events is a basic characteristic of [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   the Tunisian climate. Droughts and floods there can reach a significant magnitude (Ouled [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   Belgacem et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   Traditional land-use practices in olive groves, especially conventional tillage, are necessary to [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   eliminate weeds that compete for water and break pore connectivity, thus decreasing water [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   loss due to capillary rise. However, they create bare soil that is exposed to erosive agents such [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   as rain and wind (Abichou et al., 2012; Mellouli et al., 2000; Ravi et al., 2010; Hazzoug et al., [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   2018; Rajhi et al., 2020) and a compacted plow pan that significantly reduces infiltration of [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   rainwater at depth. Therefore, the olive-production sector must improve to address climate [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   change (AllEnvi Programme, 2016; MedECC, 2020) and the degradation of natural resources [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   (e.g. water, soil and biodiversity; IPBES, 2018) that creates patterns of vulnerability, [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   especially for the olive-production sector (Ouled Belgacem et al., 2011) in a complex socio- [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   economic context (Rajhi et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   Climate change significantly impacts agricultural production in the region (FAO, 2019). The [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   worst-case scenario predicts a 10-20% reduction in NENA crop yields by 2050 (IPCC, 2019). [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   Therefore, maintaining and increasing soil organic carbon (SOC) stocks is critical to reduce [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   greenhouse gas emissions and remove carbon dioxide (CO$_2$) from the atmosphere, and is also [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   essential to improve soil health and fertility and increase water holding capacity, especially in [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   bare soils in semi-arid regions (Mellouli et al., 2000). Several soil management strategies [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   have been practiced in the study area, including tillage, coupling different crops with olive [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   trees, applying different doses of olive mill wastewater (OMW) (Mellouli et al., 1998; Rajhi [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   et al., 2020) and applying compost to the soil. [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   Composting is one of the main ways to optimize the use of organic waste (Ayilara et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   2020). However, organic waste is difficult to find in semi-arid and arid areas, where almost no [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   crop residues are available. Thus, we evaluated the influence of a multi sourced organic [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   compost consisting of local waste such as seaweed (Han et al., 2014), considered a nuisance [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   by the tourist hotel sector, trimmed olive tree branches (i.e. “Ramial chipped wood” (RCW)) [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   (Barthes et al., 2010), local fishery waste, byproducts of olive oil production (Riffaldi et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   1993; Gargouri et al., 2013) such as olive stones (i.e. pomace (OP)) and OMW on the soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   water retention of an olive grove. The study focused on the sandy soil of an olive grove in the [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   Chammakh region near Zarzis, in southern Tunisia. In addition, we describe the changes in [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   soil fertility in the study area and discuss socio-economic impacts of composting soil. [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   2. Materials and Methods [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   2.1 Study area [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   Soil properties were determined for a field in an olive grove on OTD (Office des Terres [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   Domaniales) state land in the Chammakh region near Zarzis, Tunisia (33° 34’ 48.53” N, 10° [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   57’ 11.02” E) (Figure 1). The study area is located on the Jeffara coastal plain, southeastern [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   Tunisia, in the governorate of Medenine, near the harbor of Zarzis. The plain is defined by [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   aeolian fine quartz sand deposited on a Late Pleistocene-Pliocene Villafranchian level [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   composed of old indurated calcretes. [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

94   Soils are Arenic Calcisols (IUSS, WRB, 2022) with aeolian fine sand deposits and a calcic Bk [SOURCE: Page_5]

95   horizon 20 cm thick usually deeper than 50 cm. This horizon can be indurated as petrocalcic [SOURCE: Page_5]

96   Bkm and fragmented (Arenic Fractic Calcisols). The indurated parent rock generally appears [SOURCE: Page_5]

97   at a depth of 100 cm. The particle-size distribution is composed of over 80% 80-100 µm fine [SOURCE: Page_5]

98   sands, typical of aeolian deposits (Chepil, 1951). The clay particles responsible for the cation [SOURCE: Page_5]

99   exchange capacity are glued to the quartz grains (Jouquet et al., 2021) and are not part of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   soil structure. Wind erosion transports sand with the adhering clay particles, which can be [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   freed after strong ultrasonic shaking, with a significant difference between dry and wet [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   sieving (Table 1), and the coarse sand is composed of fine quartz sand particles cemented [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   with calcium carbonate (Jouquet et al., 2021). The topsoil’s calcium carbonate content is 14 ± [SOURCE: Page_5]

104   1%, and its pH is ca. 8.0. [SOURCE: Page_5]

105   The SOC of the Arenic Calcisols in the area is depleted in all soil profiles (Figure S$_1$, Table [SOURCE: Page_5]

106   2). Soils generally have < 0.2% SOC content, which varies slightly with depth. C:N ratios [SOURCE: Page_5]

107   generally range from 6-9, which are relatively high. N contents often lie below the detection [SOURCE: Page_5]

108   limit. [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   2.1.1 Climate [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   The study area is located in the upper arid bioclimatic zone, between a BSh (steppe) and BWh [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   (desert) climate, according to the Köppen classification. For the past 100 years, mean rainfall [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   has been ca. 180 mm, which usually falls from October-May, with extremely dry months [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   from June-September. However, this rainfall has high inter-annual and intra-annual [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   variability. The number of dry months (i.e. < 5 mm of rainfall) can sometimes reach more [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   than 8 months per year (Ouessar et al., 2006). In addition to this water deficit, the climate of [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   the study area has relatively large thermal amplitudes. The minimum temperatures recorded in [SOURCE: Page_5]

117   January and February were 4.7 and 5.8°C, respectively (1923-2003), while maximum [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   temperatures reached 35°C in summer (IRA weather station; Rajhi et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

119   2.1.2 Effects of climate change on olive tree growth [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   Physiological effects [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   The predicted increase in temperature due to climate change would disrupt plant physiology [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   and thus influence yields and annual olive tree production. A study of flows of olive-tree [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   pollen in the atmosphere was applied to olive trees in the study area (Ben Dhiab et al., 2017), [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   making it possible to rigorously follow the course of olive tree flowering. It revealed that [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   pollen emissions truly reflect variations in local olive production, which is decreasing, [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   especially after mild winters with a minimum temperature of 10°C. Climate change also has a [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   positive effect on the life cycle of the olive tree fly (Bactocera oleae), which activity is [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   negatively impacted with high temperatures; however, the advanced flowering of the trees [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   increases exposure of olive fruits to flies even before the higher temperatures later in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   season (Ponti et al., 2014; Abichou and Msellem, 2015). These risks may be more likely in [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   regions where successive years of high temperature and their impacts will become more [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   frequent (e.g., Sidi Chammakh Zarzis). [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   The combined effects of high temperatures and water scarcity also influence the long-term [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   dynamics of surface roots, which become suberized and necrotic. The removal of trees more [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   than 125 years old (planted in 1901) to renew the grove revealed a seriously damaged root [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   system, with necrotic roots associated with low olive production (Figure S$_1$). [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   Water limitation [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   Under normal conditions, olive trees are adapted to < 300 mm of rainfall when combined with [SOURCE: Page_6]

139   water-harvesting techniques developed for centuries in the dry Mediterranean climate, such as [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   in Jessour systems in southern Tunisia (Adham et al., 2017). However, in this part of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

141   Jeffara coastal plain, it is not possible to store rainwater to irrigate such large areas. [SOURCE: Page_6]

142   Studying the environment and the biological cycle of the olive tree is necessary to ensure the [SOURCE: Page_6]

143   sustainability of the olive tree ecosystem and to address climate change, which is [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

144   characterized by a significant increase in annual temperature (predicted to exceed 1°C over [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   the next 30 years; Ouled Belgacem et al., 2011; IPCC, 2019) and by irregular and fluctuating [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   mean rainfall amounts (increasingly frequent droughts). In addition to these two phenomena [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   related to climate change, evapotranspiration, which may already be excessive and has [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   increased by at least 35 mm °C-1, makes the atmosphere warmer and able to hold more water [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   vapor in this dry environment (Abichou et al., 2009; IPCC, 2022). Therefore, changes in [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   rainfall patterns will decrease soil water reserves, which will increase demand for [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   groundwater (Masmoudi et al., 2007; Ouled Belgacem et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   2.2 Land use [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   Olive groves occupy over 80% of the agricultural area in the governorate of Medenine, [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   including the entire coastal region (Figure S$_1$). The OTD grove in Sidi Chammakh extends [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   over 2250 ha and has 80,000 trees. With mean rainfall < 200 mm year-1, to decrease the use of [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   fossil groundwater and risks of salinization, rainfed olive trees are the only sustainable form [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   of agricultural production in the area. It is the major source of income for many smallholders. [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   Olive oil has been produced for thousands of years, using trees located on mountain terraces [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   or in valleys where water could be harvested (Adham et al., 2017). In 1901, during the [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   colonial era, a new type of plantation was introduced on the coastal plains, which spread to [SOURCE: Page_7]

161   the entire governorate, in vast plantations on flat areas, with trees var. Zalmati exclusively [SOURCE: Page_7]

162   dedicated to oil production, spaced 24 m part (17 trees ha-1). [SOURCE: Page_7]

163   In these olive groves, intensive interline tillage (from 4 to more than 8 operations per year) is [SOURCE: Page_7]

164   performed to a depth of 15 cm using a spring tooth cultivator with a swallow tail shape [SOURCE: Page_7]

165   (Figure S$_1$). This practice is necessary to (i) eradicate weeds and decrease competition with [SOURCE: Page_7]

166   the trees for water, (ii) increase rainwater infiltration and aerate the soil (Abichou et al., 2009) [SOURCE: Page_7]

167   and, most importantly, (iii) decrease soil evaporation by breaking pore connectivity and [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

168   decreasing capillary rise. Capillary rise in fine sandy soil can reach more than 100 cm (Lu and [SOURCE: Page_8]

169   Likos, 2004; Li et al., 2018). [SOURCE: Page_8]

170   However, the passage of machines creates plow pans and a structural crust with low [SOURCE: Page_8]

171   infiltration after small rains; this effect is generalized on bare sandy soil with a particular [SOURCE: Page_8]

172   structure (Casenave and Valentin, 1992), which significantly reduces infiltration of rainwater [SOURCE: Page_8]

173   at depth and thus hinders recharge of water from the profile. Thus, the water remaining near [SOURCE: Page_8]

174   the surface will be exposed to intense evaporation, and active roots will have access to less [SOURCE: Page_8]

175   water. For olive trees grown under dry conditions, root density peaks in soil zones with more [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   water. When these water reserves are depleted in summer, the roots become suberized and the [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   density of active roots decreases (Rajhi et al., 2020), which requires rejuvenating old roots [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   that have accumulated suberin during drought years (Figure S$_1$). [SOURCE: Page_8]

179   Consequently, this land-use practice considerably reduces soil organic matter (OM) content, [SOURCE: Page_8]

180   which reduces water retention and soil fertility (Hazzoug et al., 2018). Biological activity and [SOURCE: Page_8]

181   related ecosystem services are greatly reduced. This practice, which leaves the soil bare most [SOURCE: Page_8]

182   of the year, exposes the soil surface to wind erosion, where the light SOC particles are most [SOURCE: Page_8]

183   prone to erosion. [SOURCE: Page_8]

184   2.3 Compost preparation [SOURCE: Page_8]

185   One problem with an arid environment is the lack of available OM to prepare compost. [SOURCE: Page_8]

186   Therefore, compost prepared for olive groves in southern Tunisia contain mixed and variable [SOURCE: Page_8]

187   compositions from different sources. The percentages of waste products incorporated in this [SOURCE: Page_8]

188   study were ca. 30% RCW, 20% OP, 30% seaweed, 10% OMW and 10% fish waste. Seaweed [SOURCE: Page_8]

189   is easily collected on many public beaches, such as in front of tourist hotels on the Zarzis [SOURCE: Page_8]

190   seashore (Duarte, 2004; Cocozza et al., 2011). Fish waste is a byproduct of the sardine [SOURCE: Page_8]

191   cannery in the Zarzis harbor. The byproducts of olive oil production OP and OMW (Riffaldi [SOURCE: Page_8]

192   et al., 1993; Gargouri et al., 2013, Rahji et al, 2018), RCW generated by chipping wood from [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

193   pruned and crushed branches from olive groves, fish waste and seaweed were ground and [SOURCE: Page_9]

194   combined. All compost components were mixed, homogenized, returned to the open air, [SOURCE: Page_9]

195   stored in mounds with perforated tubes to let in air, and preserved from anaerobic [SOURCE: Page_9]

196   fermentation. Composting usually lasted seven months, but the compost applied during the [SOURCE: Page_9]

197   experiment was 18 months old. Details of the compost and composting processes are shown [SOURCE: Page_9]

198   in Table 3 and Figure S$_2$. [SOURCE: Page_9]

199   2.4 Compost application and soil sampling [SOURCE: Page_9]

200   The site of compost application was a grove of young olive trees planted in 2010. Four trees [SOURCE: Page_9]

201   (C$_1$-C$_4$, Figure 1) were amended with the multi sourced compost (180 kg each, corresponding [SOURCE: Page_9]

202   to 0.5 m^3 per tree, assuming a mean bulk density of 0.35 g cm-3). The compost was applied in [SOURCE: Page_9]

203   May 2018 around the outermost branches and buried 10 cm deep to protect it from wind [SOURCE: Page_9]

204   erosion (Figure S$_1$). Four trees (T$_1$-T$_4$) were used as a control without application of [SOURCE: Page_9]

205   compost. Soil samples were collected at the base of each tree at 150 cm from the tree trunk, [SOURCE: Page_9]

206   facing north in the shade, at the external limit of the branches. A first set for C and N analysis [SOURCE: Page_9]

207   were sampled at depths of 0-10 and 10-15 cm at the end of 2020. For the second sampling, [SOURCE: Page_9]

208   soil cores (6 cm inner diameter × 20 cm long) were collected at the end of 2021 using an H- [SOURCE: Page_9]

209   4268 hand auger tube to a depth of 20 cm. Each sample was a mixture of four soil cores [SOURCE: Page_9]

210   collected at each site. [SOURCE: Page_9]

212   2.51 Chemical and physical soil analyses [SOURCE: Page_9]

213   The soil pH and electrical conductivity (EC) at each site were determined after soil was [SOURCE: Page_9]

214   extracted with a 1:5 dilution (Rebbah, 2018) and an EC correction factor for saturated paste [SOURCE: Page_9]

215   was applied (= EC$_1$:5 × 7.89) (Aboukila and Norton, 2017). The first set of compost was [SOURCE: Page_9]

216   determined using the Walkley-Black method (Walkley, 1947) after seven months of [SOURCE: Page_9]

217   maturation, and organic matter (OM) was calculated by multiplying total C by 1.724 (APHA, [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

218   2012). Total nitrogen (N) was determined using the standard Kjeldahl method (APHA, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_10]

219   Total organic C (TOC) in the soil profile and the second set of compost were measured 18 [SOURCE: Page_10]

220   months after maturation and soil+compost, organic C and N concentrations were measured [SOURCE: Page_10]

221   via combustion using a SHIMADZUTOC VCSH analyzer (model SSM-5000A). For this [SOURCE: Page_10]

222   measurement calcareous soils were pre-treated with diluted hydrochloric acid 0.1 N HCl with [SOURCE: Page_10]

223   controlled pH 5.0 to eliminate the mineral C composing the carbonate minerals; and the [SOURCE: Page_10]

224   values were adjusted in relation of the carbonate weight loss. Anions (Cl-, NO$_3$-) and cations [SOURCE: Page_10]

225   (Na+, K+, Ca++, Mg++), as well as heavy metals such as Pb, Fe Cu, Cd and Zn, were [SOURCE: Page_10]

226   determined by atomic absorption spectrometry (Avanta, GBC spectrometer, Australia), using [SOURCE: Page_10]

227   air and acetylene as the oxidation method. Total and active limestone calcium carbonate [SOURCE: Page_10]

228   (CaCO$_3$) was determined using Bernard’s calcimeter method. Soils were sieved in water after [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   SOM destruction with H2O$_2$ and after complete dispersion with Na-hexametaphosphate and [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   ultrasonication during 30 min. Five soil particle size classes were considered: clay (<2 μm), [SOURCE: Page_10]

231   silt (2–50 μm), fine sand (50–200 μm) and coarse sand particles (200–2000 μm). The dry [SOURCE: Page_10]

232   particle-size distribution (i.e. total sand, coarse sand, fine sand, silt and clay) was measured. [SOURCE: Page_10]

233   In addition, soil porosity and bulk density were determined (Pansu et al., 2001). [SOURCE: Page_10]

234   In-situ soil water content was determined using time domain reflectometry (TDR). We [SOURCE: Page_10]

235   introduced eight plastic tubes after an auger bore hole to a depth of 100 cm, 150 cm from the [SOURCE: Page_10]

236   tree trunk, facing north in the shade, were the soil samples were collected. Four tubes were [SOURCE: Page_10]

237   introduced near the amended trees and four near the non-amended control trees. Six months [SOURCE: Page_10]

238   after applying the compost, we used the tubes to measure soil water content (by volume) [SOURCE: Page_10]

239   using electromagnetic TDR (IMKO Trime-FM T$_3$ probe) every 10 cm from a depth of 70 cm [SOURCE: Page_10]

240   for amended trees and 65 cm for non-amended trees. This difference was caused by the [SOURCE: Page_10]

241   variable depth of appearance of the petrocalcic layer, which was impossible to drill with an [SOURCE: Page_10]

242   auger. TDR measurements were performed frequently (19 measurements in 30 months) [SOURCE: Page_10]

10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

243   (Zante, 2002). Soil water repellency was measured according to Doerr (1998). The test [SOURCE: Page_11]

244   measured water drop penetration time (WDPT, in s) and consisted of adding a drop of water [SOURCE: Page_11]

245   to a few g of soil. WDPT was measured in 50 replicates each of amended and non-amended [SOURCE: Page_11]

246   soil. [SOURCE: Page_11]

251   solution and titrated with HCl (APHA, 2012). [SOURCE: Page_11]

329   4.       Discussion [SOURCE: Page_14]

330   4.1 Effect of compost on soil Chemical and microbiological properties [SOURCE: Page_14]

331   Organic amendments are known to increase the productivity of arid land (Parr et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_14]

332   Since the high C:N ratio of RCW in sandy soils generally decreases the first year after [SOURCE: Page_14]

333   applying RCW, this negative effect can be limited when N is applied at the same time [SOURCE: Page_14]

334   (Barthes et al., 2010), such as when N-rich seaweed is combined with RCW. This was [SOURCE: Page_14]

335   confirmed by the review on the compost quality of seaweed compared to that of wood chips [SOURCE: Page_14]

336   (Han et al., 2014). Cocozza et al. (2011) observed that when mixed with RCW, green waste [SOURCE: Page_14]

14 [SOURCE: Page_14]

337   and Mediterranean seaweed (Posidonia oceanica) residues yielded high-quality compost with [SOURCE: Page_15]

338   humified OM. [SOURCE: Page_15]

339   The advantage of a composite compost is also to provide diverse sources of nutrients. As sea- [SOURCE: Page_15]

340   weed is enriched in N (Han et al., 2014), but also in K, fish by-products enriched the compost [SOURCE: Page_15]

341   mostly in P (Illera-Vives et al., 2013). The inputs of OMW enriched the compost with N, [SOURCE: Page_15]

342   slightly with P but mostly with high rates of K (Lopez Pineiro, 2008; Nasini et al., 2013: [SOURCE: Page_15]

343   Chaari et al., 2015). Each of these sources provide a high rate of one of the most important [SOURCE: Page_15]

344   fertilisers components with probably higher bio-availability than mineral fertilizers as noticed [SOURCE: Page_15]

345   by López-Mosquer et al., (1997) on the effects of sea weed as amendment. [SOURCE: Page_15]

346   Beached Sea weed compost presented no phytotoxicity and a low heavy metal content in Italy [SOURCE: Page_15]

347   (Castaldi et al., 2004). This was confirmed in our study (Table S$_2$): no significant difference [SOURCE: Page_15]

348   was observed in Pb, Cu, Zn and As contents between the amended and non-amended soils, [SOURCE: Page_15]

349   nor were signs of toxicity. [SOURCE: Page_15]

350   The main problem involved in using seaweed, like for OMW, is the potentially high salt [SOURCE: Page_15]

351   content of the compost (Han et al., 2014; Chaari et al., 2015). The IPBES land-degradation [SOURCE: Page_15]

352   assessment (2018) revealed a reduction in agricultural yield when salinity exceeds 4 mS cm-1 [SOURCE: Page_15]

353   as a protosalic horizonvalue much lower than the requested EC value > 15 mS cm-1 is [SOURCE: Page_15]

354   necessary to qualify a salic horizon for a soil with pH < 8.5 (IUSS, WRB 2022). However our [SOURCE: Page_15]

355   highest E.C. (1:5) value in the composted soil is        ≈ 0.21 mS cm which represent an [SOURCE: Page_15]

356   approximate value of 1.65 mS cm (SAT) far below the limitation of IPBES assessment. [SOURCE: Page_15]

357   The composition of compost influences the distribution of microorganisms. The compost [SOURCE: Page_15]

358   contained a large amount of mineral compounds, which promote bacterial growth (Ayilara et [SOURCE: Page_15]

359   al., 2020). Therefore, microbial activity limits the development of pathogenic organisms and [SOURCE: Page_15]

360   promotes root development (i.e. more active mycorrhizae). In addition, no phytotoxicity was [SOURCE: Page_15]

361   detected in either soil. [SOURCE: Page_15]

362   4.2 Effect of compost on soil physical properties [SOURCE: Page_16]

363   4.21 Soil water content [SOURCE: Page_16]

364   Due to the very dry climate and strong evapotranspiration, there was no clear difference in [SOURCE: Page_16]

365   soil water content in the zone where the compost was applied (i.e. 0-20 cm). The standard [SOURCE: Page_16]

366   deviation decreases from soil surface to depth suggesting that the variability of soil water [SOURCE: Page_16]

367   content in soil surface was exposed to rainfall and drought events (Table S$_1$). Barthes et al. [SOURCE: Page_16]

368   (2010) and Van Donk et al. (2011) observed a decrease in evapotranspiration and an increase [SOURCE: Page_16]

369   in soil water content below RCW applications. Van Donk et al. (2011) observed that the [SOURCE: Page_16]

370   increase in water content depends on the thickness of RCW applied. Rawls et al. (2003) [SOURCE: Page_16]

371   showed that for water retention at -1500 kPa at low SOC contents, the sensitivity of water [SOURCE: Page_16]

372   retention to an increase in OM content was highest in sandy soils. In our study, even with an [SOURCE: Page_16]

373   increase in porosity (i.e. decrease in bulk density), there was no significant difference in water [SOURCE: Page_16]

374   retention at -1500 kPa between amended and non-amended samples (ca. 3% of water content [SOURCE: Page_16]

375   by mass, data not shown). [SOURCE: Page_16]

376   4.22 Soil water repellency [SOURCE: Page_16]

377      All soils and substrates can have hydrophobic properties, but they are more frequent in [SOURCE: Page_16]

378   sandy soils and under dry conditions (Dekker and Ritsema, 1994; Doerr and Thomas, 2000; [SOURCE: Page_16]

379   Jaramillo et al., 2000). Haynes and Naidu (1998) observed the hydrophobic properties of [SOURCE: Page_16]

380   compost. Generally, hydrophobic properties are due to the accumulation of lipid compounds [SOURCE: Page_16]

381   in OM during OM mineralization (Doerr et al., 2000; Horne and Mcintosh, 2000), and more [SOURCE: Page_16]

382   specifically lipid coating of sand particles. In the composite compost, olive tree leaves and [SOURCE: Page_16]

383   olive cake residues (OP) contain a small amount of lipids (4%), as does OMW, which [SOURCE: Page_16]

384   contains 45 g lipids L-1 (Nasini et al., 2013). Some hydrophobic properties have been [SOURCE: Page_16]

385   measured in Tunisia: Bahri and Annabi (2011) studied an application of lipid-rich urban [SOURCE: Page_16]

16 [SOURCE: Page_16]

386   waste on the structural stability of loamy soils in northern Tunisia, while Mellouli et al. [SOURCE: Page_17]

387   (1998) measured the hydrophobicity effect of OMW on water retention. Both were measured [SOURCE: Page_17]

388   in a laboratory trial. [SOURCE: Page_17]

389   Water repellency is considered a physical degradation of soil that decreases water infiltration, [SOURCE: Page_17]

390   being located in “fingerprints” (Dekker and Ritsema, 1994) that cause runoff and erosion [SOURCE: Page_17]

391   (Wallis and Horne, 1993; Doerr et al., 2000). In some cases, it can be considered to improve [SOURCE: Page_17]

392   the structural stability of loamy soils (Bahri and Annabi, 2011), but in sandy soils, stable [SOURCE: Page_17]

393   aggregates do not form due to the lack of clay bonds between the sand particles. Mellouli et [SOURCE: Page_17]

394   al. (1998) observed that OMW inputs and the development of water repellency can increase [SOURCE: Page_17]

395   the water content of sandy soils. Applying OMW, especially in frequently-tilled olive groves, [SOURCE: Page_17]

396   is more effective (Mellouli, et al. 2000) than applying a simple mulch of harvest residues [SOURCE: Page_17]

397   (Scopel et al., 2004; Barthes et al., 2010; Van Donk et al., 2011), especially in areas that [SOURCE: Page_17]

398   produce few plant residues. Inputs of hydrophobic OM only underneath the branches function [SOURCE: Page_17]

399   like a lid that decreases water loss by capillary rise. Moreover, the preferential paths (or [SOURCE: Page_17]

400   fingerprints) in hydrophobic layers can increase the infiltration rate (Dekker and Ritsema; [SOURCE: Page_17]

401   1994) and water storage at depth. Our in-situ measurements indicate a 60% increase in water [SOURCE: Page_17]

402   content at 70 cm under amended olive trees, which we attribute to the local development of [SOURCE: Page_17]

403   water repellency by the compost. In these very fine sandy soil, capillary rise was estimated to [SOURCE: Page_17]

404   be 1.00 m (Lu and Likos, 2004; Li et al. 2018). Applying organic amendment can be [SOURCE: Page_17]

405   considered a lid that decreases evaporation by decreasing pore connectivity, while non- [SOURCE: Page_17]

406   amended trees experience high evaporation. The consistent increase in soil water content with [SOURCE: Page_17]

407   depth could encourage olive tree roots to grow deeper and therefore decrease the risk of [SOURCE: Page_17]

408   suberization and necrosis of surface roots exposed to high temperatures. [SOURCE: Page_17]

409   4.3 Composting: an alternative to improve soil fertility and olive productivity [SOURCE: Page_17]

17 [SOURCE: Page_17]

410   Thus, several soil management strategies are implemented and practiced in the study area. [SOURCE: Page_18]

411   They consist of applying different treatments to the soil, including deep plowing, applying [SOURCE: Page_18]

412   different doses of OMW (Abichou et al., 2004; Nasini et al., 2013; Rajhi et al., 2021) and [SOURCE: Page_18]

413   mulching the soil with OM amendments. Composting multi-sourced OM is a promising [SOURCE: Page_18]

414   alternative in areas with limited biomass production, which is economically profitable (less [SOURCE: Page_18]

415   expensive than chemical fertilizers) and ecologically favorable (reducing environmental risks [SOURCE: Page_18]

416   associated with waste management). In addition, OM amendments influence water-saving [SOURCE: Page_18]

417   processes by decreasing evaporation (Mellouli et al., 1998, 2000; Van Donk et al., 2011; [SOURCE: Page_18]

418   Scopel et al. 2004), and due to their hydrophobic properties. [SOURCE: Page_18]

419   5.       Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_18]

420        We showed how composite compost made from local organic waste and olive tree [SOURCE: Page_18]

421   cultivation byproducts can significantly improve soil fertility, soil water content and olive tree [SOURCE: Page_18]

422   productivity. Composite composting (i) uses byproducts of olive oil production (OP and [SOURCE: Page_18]

423   OMW) generally considered as toxic due to the high polyphenols and salt contents in areas [SOURCE: Page_18]

424   with higher rainfall, (ii) uses seaweed, which the tourist hotel sector considers a nuisance, (iii) [SOURCE: Page_18]

425   using fish-sector waste, (iv) enriches the soil with nutrients such as N, P and K, which [SOURCE: Page_18]

426   increases soil fertility and microbiological composition, and (v) significantly increases the soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

427   water content by limiting soil evaporation by developing hydrophobic properties of the [SOURCE: Page_18]

428   compost during decomposition. The result is a potential increase of the tree growth and the [SOURCE: Page_18]

429   weight of olives is estimated to be 40% higher. [SOURCE: Page_18]

430        The influence of C inputs should be mitigated since sandy soils have no stable soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

431   structure that can physically protect the C in aggregates from mineralization. Compost’s [SOURCE: Page_18]

432   sustainable effects and the evolution with time of hydrophobic properties should be studied as [SOURCE: Page_18]

433   a function of effects of different types of soil tillage. Tilling the soil with a harrow would not [SOURCE: Page_18]

434   form a compacted pan and would thus increase soil water infiltration. The promising results [SOURCE: Page_18]

18 [SOURCE: Page_18]

435   of this study indicate that producing compost at a much larger scale in the area would greatly [SOURCE: Page_19]

436   improve olive production and sustainability, mainly by increasing the soil water reserve in the [SOURCE: Page_19]

437   context of climate change. [SOURCE: Page_19]
