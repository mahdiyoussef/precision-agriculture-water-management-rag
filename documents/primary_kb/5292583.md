In a previous paper, we proposed to 17     couple Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) and Agrarian System Diagnosis (ASD) to build the Life Cycle Inventory 18     (LCI) of a farming region in a context of data scarcity and farming system diversity. This method was applied 19     to the semi-arid irrigated plain of Kairouan (Tunisia). OBJECTIVES: The aim of the present paper is to 20     demonstrate how this new ASD-based LCA method is used to conduct the impact assessment of this farming 21     region, under current conditions and for two prospective scenarios. METHODS: Two functional units (FU) 22     were used: the area and the gross product generated. Life Cycle Impact Assessment (LCIA) was conducted 23     based on a multilevel process, by converting LCI data collected for each cropping/ livestock system into LCIA 24     outputs, which were then consolidated at the farming system (FS) and at the regional scales. Two scenarios, 25     based on observed trends, were assessed: the “agrarian” scenario (driven by a shift from family to corporate 26     farming) and the “hydrological” scenario (driven by a water table drawdown) RESULTS: Environmental 27     hotspots were identified at each scale: the most impactful FS was the “mixed family farming” (intermediate 28     access to water), because of its large share of land at the regional scale (45%). In this FS, the most harmful 29     cropping system was input-intensive olive groves intercropped with a melon-pepper/ cereal/ tomato rotation, 30     in which most impacts (35%) were attributable to the input-intensive “melon” crop, and more particularly to 31     “manufacture and transport of fertilizer”, including farmyard manure. Globally, the most harmful processes 32     were water and fertilizer use (fertilizer manufacturing and transportation). This downscaling is very useful to 33     inform public policies on the most harmful productions. The impacts in human, ecosystem and resource 34     endpoints increased by 11%, 2%, 9% and 23%, 27% and 40%, for the agrarian and hydrological scenarios 35     respectively. SIGNIFICANCE: These results show that observed trends in farming practices evolution (more 36     irrigation) should be stopped. 42   1. Introduction 43 44   Irrigated agriculture is a good way to increase productivity per volume of water consumed: it provides 40% of 45   food and feed, while only accounting for 18% of the cultivated areas worldwide (Jamin et al., 2011). 46   Nevertheless, irrigation is responsible for 70% of global freshwater withdrawals (Siebert et al., 2010) and has 47   many indirect environmental impacts due to input-intensification worldwide (Foley et al., 2011; Madramootoo 48   and Fyles, 2010). Indeed, irrigated agriculture sustainability is a major issue regarding water depletion and 49   pollution due to additional agricultural inputs, as mentioned in the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005). 50   Tools are therefore urgently needed for the exhaustive assessment of the eco-efficiency of irrigated 51   agriculture, i.e. its “ability to fulfil a production while minimizing its total impacts on the environment” 52   (Basset-Mens et al., 2009). Kulak et al., (2015) showed that innovative processes leading to more eco-efficient 53   cropping systems were hampered by a lack of knowledge on their environmental impacts. 54   The environmental impacts of irrigated systems have often been studied in a simple way, i.e. either by 55   focusing on a reduced number of impact categories and /or on a single level or compartment (soil, water, crop, 56   air). Water depletion is often the main impact studied (Llamas, 2005; Lopez-Gunn and Ramón Llamas, 2008; 57   Maneta et al., 2009), but qualitative water degradation originating from irrigation has also been widely 58   investigated, be it at the watershed or plot level (Merchán et al., 2020; Schaible and Aillery, 2012), focusing 59   notably on nitrate field emissions (Pampolino et al., 2007). Later on, based on the energy-water nexus concept 60   (Hussey and Pittock, 2012), the impacts of irrigation on energy consumption (Jackson et al., 2011) and hence 61   on greenhouse gases (Abalos et al., 2014, Maestre-Valero et al., 2018) were investigated. Life Cycle 62   Assessment (LCA) has been increasingly employed to study impacts of irrigation processes (Grant and Beer, 63   2008, Pradeleix et al., 2014, McCarthy 2020, Kalboussi et al., 2022). LCA is a framework for quantifying a wide 64   range of potential environmental impacts over the whole life cycle of a product or service, from “cradle to 65   grave”, per unit of “function” delivered (e.g., one kg of harvested melon). It informs on any environmental 66   burden shifting, either from one stage of the life cycle to another, or from one impact category to another; 67   water depletion being only one of these categories. 68   Until recently, research has focused on the plot or the farm level, ignoring potential trade-offs between 69   impacts occurring in the whole farming region but this last level is now prone to active research (Balkau et al., 70   2021, Bezama et al., 2021). Decision makers need regional LCA to identify environmental hotspots, which 71   should be prioritized to mitigate impacts. [SOURCE: Page_9]

678   4.3.3 Scenario building 679   Fostering farm transition to agroecology requires integrating local dynamics into research and farming system 680   assessment (Prost and Martin et al., 2023). Prospective scenarios were built from ASD outputs, e.g., the trends 681   observed during field visits, and in-depth interviews conducted during ASD fieldwork. The “agrarian scenario” 682   was built on the observed evolution of the farming system share in the region (i.e. family farming FS$_6$ and FS$_7$ 683   tending to be replaced by corporate holdings FS$_1$ to FS$_3$). The “hydrological scenario”, relied on the observed 684   drawdown of the water table. Our choice for the agrarian scenario -i.e. to increase the number of corporate 685   farms while their average cropped area was assumed to remain steady- is open to debate since we observed 686   an increase in the area cropped by corporate FS, for the sake of profitability and economies of scale. However, 687   ASD also revealed that the regular shortage of seasonal workers for harvesting could hamper the expansion of 688   such corporate FS. Part of this hard-to-find workforce could be recruited among the former farmers of family 689   FS, provided they have not already moved to big cities as is commonly observed. 690   Furthermore, the scenarios were kept very simple, and this for two main reasons. First, scenario design is not 691   at the heart of our study, which is focused on impact assessment. Second, we wanted to have “model 692   scenarios”, in order to clearly disassociate the effects due to the change in the FS typology in the region, and 693   the impacts due to the water-table drawdown. It is plausible that the future prospects of this region might lie 694   in a combination of the two scenarios as water table drawdown is expected to continue, which will cause 695   family farming to leave the place. Digging deeper wells is expensive and only corporate agriculture and family 696   business farms can afford boreholes. Because they cannot pay for such large investments, family farmers are 697   forced to sell their land, most often to corporate agriculture and family business farms. With global changes, 698   including decreasing water resources and rising crop evapotranspiration, along with the increase in the 699   demand for more water-demanding commercial crops, water requirements will significantly increase. In this 700   semi-arid farming region, the current agrarian equilibrium built on several flows interconnecting diverse 701   farming systems is therefore threatened by uneven access to water, which is particularly detrimental to family 702   farms. 703   In the future, these methodological limitations (over-simplicity of scenarios) could be overcome, by taking 704   advantage of other information collected during ASD, in order to build more realistic scenarios. Indeed, ASD 705   can be used to determine the maximum thresholds, regarding both available resources (natural resources – 706   e.g., land, groundwater-, human-made materials, local workforce) and acceptable environmental loadings, 707   linked to local impacts. For instance, in our study, FS$_7$ limited its cropping area due to a lack of available water, 708   while some non-family farmers (in FS$_1$ to FS$_4$) limited their cropping area due to lack of seasonal workers. 709   Public decision makers should use such thresholds in their policies to maintain total regional impacts within a 710   "safe operating zone" of regional “planetary boundaries” (Rockström et al., 2009). These thresholds could also 711   inform other approaches for sustainable irrigation and farming system design, e.g., in-silico approaches like 712   linear programming optimization (Acosta-Alba et al., 2012) or participatory approaches, which greatly 713   increase the chances of the scenarios to be implemented (Dogliotti et al., 2014; Le Bellec et al., 2011, 714   Ryschawy et al., 2022).
