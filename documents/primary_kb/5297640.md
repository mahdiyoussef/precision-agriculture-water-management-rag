## ABSTRACT

Water scarcity poses significant challenges in Mediterranean countries, particularly where an [SOURCE: Page_1]

intermittent supply and inefficient networks lead to increased economic and social costs. This [SOURCE: Page_1]

paper analyses the cost structure of Algeria’s drinking water utilities, with a focus on [SOURCE: Page_1]

rationing, inefficiencies and production constraints. Using a translog cost function and a [SOURCE: Page_1]

cluster-robust CRE-IV approach, we identify economies of scale in production and [SOURCE: Page_1]

distribution. The strong complementarities between water losses and distributed volume [SOURCE: Page_1]

suggest a tendency to prioritise water production over maintenance. Furthermore, reduced [SOURCE: Page_1]

service hours combined with production capacity constraints lead to a significant increase in [SOURCE: Page_1]

marginal costs. We estimate the shadow price of water at 18.59 DZD/m³, compared to a [SOURCE: Page_1]

marginal cost of 5.77 DZD/m³, highlighting the inefficiencies of current strategies. Our [SOURCE: Page_2]

findings emphasise the need for better resource allocation, including network rehabilitation, [SOURCE: Page_2]

demand-side management and cost-reflective pricing, to enhance efficiency and sustainability [SOURCE: Page_2]

in water-stressed regions. [SOURCE: Page_2]

### 1. Introduction

Water scarcity and the efficient distribution of drinking water are among the most pressing [SOURCE: Page_3]

challenges in the Mediterranean region, which is highly vulnerable to climate change (Ali et [SOURCE: Page_3]

al., 2022). Algeria, in particular, is under increasing stress due to recurrent droughts, rising [SOURCE: Page_3]

temperatures, and ageing water infrastructure. This has led to significant losses and a growing [SOURCE: Page_3]

reliance on water rationing. Key sources such as reservoirs and dams are often depleted, and [SOURCE: Page_3]

extreme temperatures exacerbate shortages (World Bank, 2021). Consequently, access to a [SOURCE: Page_3]

continuous water supply is often disrupted, a situation also experienced in other [SOURCE: Page_3]

Mediterranean regions such as Tunisia, Morocco, Catalonia and Sicily (MedECC, 2020). [SOURCE: Page_3]

Despite these pressures, Algeria has achieved a high household connection rate (98%) to [SOURCE: Page_3]

the water supply network (Ministry of Water Resources, 2017). However, distribution [SOURCE: Page_3]

inefficiencies remain acute, with leakage causing losses of up to 40% in some areas [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Bouferrouk et al., 2018). In cities such as Algiers, visible leaks highlight these challenges, [SOURCE: Page_3]

increasing operating costs and undermining service continuity. Addressing these issues [SOURCE: Page_3]

requires investment in infrastructure modernisation, leakage reduction and improved water [SOURCE: Page_3]

management strategies. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Although there is an extensive body of economic research on water costs, studies focusing [SOURCE: Page_3]

on Algeria are scarce due to data limitations. Notable contributions include the work of [SOURCE: Page_3]

Zeggagh (2020) and Zeggagh and Mazouz (2024), who have assessed pricing and efficiency [SOURCE: Page_3]

using panel data and cost functions. Their work has highlighted the importance of service [SOURCE: Page_3]

frequency and scale economies. Inefficiencies in water services stem from two intertwined [SOURCE: Page_3]

problems: network losses and supply interruptions. While scarcity leads to rationing, high [SOURCE: Page_3]

leakage exacerbates shortages. Utilities must balance the cost of repairing infrastructure with [SOURCE: Page_3]

the need to sustain distribution, which often involves using more energy to compensate for [SOURCE: Page_3]

lost volumes (Garcia and Thomas, 2001). Understanding these dynamics is essential for [SOURCE: Page_4]

improving the reliability of water services. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Recent studies have used shadow pricing and directional distance functions to estimate the [SOURCE: Page_4]

economic cost of leakage and service inefficiencies. In Chile, for example, leakage accounted [SOURCE: Page_4]

for 32% of the water price (Molinos-Senante et al., 2016), with costs per cubic metre reaching [SOURCE: Page_4]

€0.44 (Molinos-Senante et al., 2019). These results suggest the need for regulatory incentives [SOURCE: Page_4]

### 2. The water sector in Algeria

Algeria has invested significantly in water infrastructure, including dams, desalination [SOURCE: Page_5]

fourth water resource after surface water, groundwater, and desalinated water. It supports [SOURCE: Page_5]

sustainability and agricultural use. [SOURCE: Page_5]

Despite these efforts, water scarcity remains acute in Algeria, where two-thirds of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

country is arid or semi-arid. Continued investment is essential to maintain the supply and [SOURCE: Page_5]

support economic development. While hydraulic infrastructure improves availability, this [SOURCE: Page_5]

alone is not enough. High investment costs and regional disparities restrict supply options, so [SOURCE: Page_5]

demand management is becoming increasingly important. Key strategies include public [SOURCE: Page_5]

awareness, conservation and improved service management. Reforming water tariffs to reflect [SOURCE: Page_5]

usage and scarcity can incentivise efficiency. All users, including households, the agricultural [SOURCE: Page_5]

sector and industry, are subject to tiered pricing structures to encourage responsible use. [SOURCE: Page_5]

According to the UN WWDR (2014), agriculture accounts for 70% of Algeria’s water [SOURCE: Page_6]

Overall, Algeria's water sector has made significant progress in terms of infrastructure [SOURCE: Page_6]

### 3.1.    Technology, service quality, and performance indicators

Water utilities manage the entire water supply chain, from extraction and treatment to [SOURCE: Page_7]

storage, pressurisation and distribution. The technologies and associated costs involved at [SOURCE: Page_7]

each stage vary according to the water source, user density and geography. [SOURCE: Page_7]

In arid and semi-arid regions such as Algeria and other North African countries, chronic [SOURCE: Page_7]

water scarcity, exacerbated by climate change and increasing demand, leads to seasonal [SOURCE: Page_7]

shortages and depleted reservoirs, resulting in service interruptions. These cuts affect [SOURCE: Page_7]

households, agriculture and industry, reducing productivity and highlighting the vulnerability [SOURCE: Page_7]

of infrastructure. In Algeria, distribution schedules often depend on reservoir levels, with [SOURCE: Page_7]

shortages lowering supply hours below essential thresholds. Utilities must balance service [SOURCE: Page_7]

quality and cost when making investment decisions regarding network upgrades, reducing [SOURCE: Page_7]

leakage, and finding alternative sources such as desalination or reuse. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Water scarcity and an intermittent supply increase costs. Emergency measures such as [SOURCE: Page_7]

water trucking and extra pumping, infrastructure wear and tear from frequent shutdowns, and [SOURCE: Page_7]

expensive alternatives such as desalination increase operating expenses. These costs are often [SOURCE: Page_7]

passed on to consumers, which affects affordability, particularly for those on low incomes. A [SOURCE: Page_7]

key performance indicator is weekly supply hours, which reflect service reliability and user [SOURCE: Page_7]

satisfaction. Though perceptions vary, this objective metric enables comparisons. Another [SOURCE: Page_7]

critical issue is network leakage, which leads to water losses before it reaches users. This [SOURCE: Page_7]

inefficiency increases costs and undermines resource conservation, particularly in areas under [SOURCE: Page_7]

water stress. Recent literature (Garcia, 2001; Garcia & Thomas, 2001) emphasises the [SOURCE: Page_7]

importance of accounting for these losses. [SOURCE: Page_7]

Water utilities have an interest in taking these interactions into account. If they produce an [SOURCE: Page_7]

amount of lost water that is sub-optimal, they are not distributing drinking water at the lowest [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

possible cost. Therefore, to reduce this type of inefficiency, they must reduce losses in [SOURCE: Page_8]

proportion to the amount of water demanded and the cost of preventing losses. Consequently, [SOURCE: Page_8]

a key performance indicator for water companies is the water loss rate (the percentage of non- [SOURCE: Page_8]

revenue water), which reflects the effectiveness of network maintenance and cost control [SOURCE: Page_8]

efforts. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Our economic modelling of water supply costs incorporates these variables within a cost [SOURCE: Page_8]

function framework. In this approach, the volume of drinking water distributed is treated as [SOURCE: Page_8]

the primary output, while performance indicators such as water loss rate and distribution [SOURCE: Page_8]

hours are integrated to assess efficiency and service reliability. These are decision variables [SOURCE: Page_8]

that allow adjustments in service management and are therefore endogenous (Torres and [SOURCE: Page_8]

Morrison Paul, 2006; Destandau et Garcia, 2014). They are influenced by constraints such as [SOURCE: Page_8]

technical, environmental, meteorological factors and the availability of water resources, all of [SOURCE: Page_8]

which affect both cost structures and management decisions. In regions facing water scarcity [SOURCE: Page_8]

and climate variability, optimal management of these parameters becomes crucial to maintain [SOURCE: Page_8]

service quality while controlling costs and minimising losses. [SOURCE: Page_8]

### 5.2.      Empirical results

The translog specification used for the variable cost function is a second-order Taylor [SOURCE: Page_18]

series expansion that approximates the variable cost function (in logarithms) around a chosen [SOURCE: Page_18]

reference point. To ensure the validity of this local approximation (White, 1980), we take the [SOURCE: Page_18]

sample mean of the variables (in logarithms) as the reference. Consequently, all right-hand- [SOURCE: Page_18]

side variables are normalised by their respective sample means (mean scaling), allowing the [SOURCE: Page_18]

first-order coefficients of outputs and inputs to be interpreted directly as cost elasticities [SOURCE: Page_18]

evaluated at the sample mean of our Algerian water utilities dataset. [SOURCE: Page_18]

As control functions are used to correct for endogeneity in the cost function, we will first [SOURCE: Page_18]

examine whether this correction is relevant before interpreting the final results of the cost [SOURCE: Page_18]

function. The variables used to estimate the control functions are also mean-scaled. [SOURCE: Page_18]
