### 3. Univ. Bordeaux, CNRS, UMR 5805 EPOC, 33600 Pessac, France

Summary [SOURCE: Page_2]

The global demand for wood biomass is increasing, therefore it is necessary to develop forest [SOURCE: Page_2]

management alternatives that can, simultaneously, produce large amounts of biomass and maintain [SOURCE: Page_2]

1 [SOURCE: Page_2]

ecosystem functions and services in a sustainable way. However, assessing the consequences of [SOURCE: Page_3]

silviculture is challenging, as forest ecosystems function slowly over long periods of time. Therefore, in [SOURCE: Page_3]

this study, an experimental platform was set up to monitor the long-term effects of several forest [SOURCE: Page_3]

management alternatives (FMA) on ecosystem functioning in a pine forest in a temperate region [SOURCE: Page_3]

characterised by oligotrophic conditions. In practice, we monitored three contrasting FMA over a [SOURCE: Page_3]

decade: (i) wood biomass production (WBP), designed using an approach of very intense forestry (high [SOURCE: Page_3]

stand density; seed lot of pines selected to growth fast), (ii) combined objective management (COM; [SOURCE: Page_3]

low stand density), aimed at improving pine growth by alleviating any competition by spontaneous [SOURCE: Page_3]

vegetation, and (iii) nutrient management (NuM; medium stand density), designed to improve tree [SOURCE: Page_3]

nutrition using N-fixers in the stand furrows. Overall, although FMA showed contrasting stand growth [SOURCE: Page_3]

and structures, they had modest effects on forest biogeochemistry over a decade of monitoring: FMA [SOURCE: Page_3]

showed similar trends regarding atmospheric deposition, soil solution chemistry and water table-ditch [SOURCE: Page_3]

chemistry. The main difference observed was a more important role of dissolved organic matter in NuM [SOURCE: Page_3]

biogeochemical functioning. Conversely to their effects on biogeochemistry, the FMA appeared to [SOURCE: Page_3]

influence the biophysical properties of stands. The WBP management (with high stand density) was [SOURCE: Page_3]

shown to be shadier, cooler and wetter than the other FMA. This trend was fairly clear during the [SOURCE: Page_3]

summer periods although differences were observed all year-long. An important result regarding [SOURCE: Page_3]

biophysical effects was that, in addition to being observed in the topsoil layers, they were also evident [SOURCE: Page_3]

in deeper soil layers and in the water table. All in all, our results indicated that contrasting FMA have [SOURCE: Page_3]

tended to influence the ecosystem functioning, in particular its biophysical component, but showed no [SOURCE: Page_3]

early sign of unsustainable biogeochemical functioning. Nonetheless, this latter result should be [SOURCE: Page_3]

confirmed in the long-term through further monitoring. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

The demand for forest biomass has been increasing in Europe because forest biomass can be used for [SOURCE: Page_4]

wood products, paper production, and even for energy production (Achat et al., 2015a; Diaz-Yanez et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al., 2013; Nicholls et al., 2009). An approach to meet this growing demand is to intensify forest [SOURCE: Page_4]

management, for instance planting dense stands of fast-growing species managed in short-rotations [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Achat et al., 2018; Aylott et al., 2008; Heilman and Norby, 1998; Ranger and Nys, 1996). However, [SOURCE: Page_4]

intensifying forestry has shown to be potentially deleterious for ecosystem functioning such as [SOURCE: Page_4]

impoverishing soils (Achat et al., 2015a; Kimmins, 1974) and such an approach needs to be evaluated [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Fox, 2000; Pohjanmies et al., 2021; Toman and Ashton, 1996). Evaluating intensive forestry is all the [SOURCE: Page_4]

more necessary because this kind of forest management exports large quantities of nutrients, whereas [SOURCE: Page_4]

natural nutrient inputs are generally of low magnitude (Achat et al., 2015a; Flueck, 2009; Heilman and [SOURCE: Page_4]

Norby, 1998; Kimmins, 1974; Ranger and Turpault, 1999). In addition, the impacts of management on [SOURCE: Page_4]

forest carbon and water balance and its biophysical functioning is also observed at various spatial scales [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Albaugh et al., 2016; Luyssaert et al., 2018; Magnani et al., 2007). Therefore, the main objective of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

present study was to assess the impact of different levels of forestry intensification on ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_4]

functioning. In practice, we evaluated the effects of three forest management alternatives (FMA), a [SOURCE: Page_4]

theoretical framework developed to compare contrasting silvicultural systems (Mason and Meredieu, [SOURCE: Page_4]

2011). The studied FMA manipulated stand density, competition control, fertilisation, genetics, and soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

preparation, so they fulfil all the criteria of the intensive management definition (Fox, 2000). [SOURCE: Page_4]

One major difficulty when assessing forestry strategies is the time dimension because forest [SOURCE: Page_4]

management has to be evaluated over decades, if not centuries (Homann et al., 2000; Kimmins et al., [SOURCE: Page_4]

2008). To circumvent this obstacle, different approaches are possible such as modelling (Wallman et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al., 2005) or long-term in situ monitoring (Pretzsch et al., 2019). While we value models and regularly [SOURCE: Page_4]

use them (Achat et al., 2018; Jonard et al., 2010; Loustau et al., 2005; Moreaux et al., 2020), the present [SOURCE: Page_4]

study relies on a long-term experimental platform. In addition to being crucial for improving ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_4]

models (Achat et al., 2016a), long-term field experiments enable accurate assessments of ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_4]

responses to silviculture (Prescott, 2014) and are useful for understanding complex ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_4]

functioning (Green et al., 2023). Consequently, we set up a long-term forestry experiment in 2012 in [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

south-western France, a region characterised by debates regarding intensive versus extensive forest [SOURCE: Page_5]

management scenarios (González-García et al., 2014; Mora et al., 2014) and we monitored the [SOURCE: Page_5]

ecosystems under different silvicultural systems. Hereafter, we report the results of one decade of [SOURCE: Page_5]

monitoring that showed how intensively managed forest plantations have influenced biogeochemical [SOURCE: Page_5]

and biophysical aspects of ecosystem functioning (Fox, 2000). Based on the literature, we had three [SOURCE: Page_5]

main expectations at the beginning of the monitoring: one about biophysical effects of FMA and two [SOURCE: Page_5]

about biogeochemical effects. Firstly, we expected that contrasting stand densities would have short- [SOURCE: Page_5]

term effects on the ecosystem biophysical functioning (Pohjanmies et al., 2021) as a time lag in canopy [SOURCE: Page_5]

closure can influence the local microclimate. More precisely, our hypothesis (H$_1$) was that dense [SOURCE: Page_5]

plantations would induce cooler and drier conditions than in sparse stands (Chen et al., 2025; De Frenne [SOURCE: Page_5]

et al., 2021; Zhai et al., 2024) because of high sunlight interception, rainfall interception, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

evapotranspiration (Barbier et al., 2009; De Frenne et al., 2021; Ewers et al., 2005). Secondly, because [SOURCE: Page_5]

ecosystem biogeochemical functioning is mainly influenced by forestry in mature stands (Erisman and [SOURCE: Page_5]

Draaijers, 2003; Johnson and Turner, 2014), or through major disturbances (Paré and Thiffault, 2016; [SOURCE: Page_5]

Smolander and Heiskanen, 2007; Weis et al., 2006), we anticipated that the young forest stands would [SOURCE: Page_5]

not display any strong biogeochemical differences between FMA (H$_2$). Finally, we identified an [SOURCE: Page_5]

exception to this H$_2$ expectation, which was the introduction of N-fixer plants, because this practice [SOURCE: Page_5]

showed short-term modifications of the ecosystem N cycling (Bouillet et al., 2008; Dyck et al., 1983; [SOURCE: Page_5]

Vidal et al., 2021, 2019; Voigtlaender et al., 2012; Watt et al., 2003). We consequently hypothesised an [SOURCE: Page_5]

increase of nitrate production and an improvement of tree N nutrition under such conditions (H$_3$). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Material and Methods [SOURCE: Page_6]

Study region [SOURCE: Page_6]

The XyloSylve forest experimental platform was installed in 2012 in the Landes de Gascogne region [SOURCE: Page_6]

(43.5-45.5°N and 1.5°W and 0.3°E), which is located in south-western France (Figure S$_1$). The [SOURCE: Page_6]

regional climate belongs to the temperate, fully humid, warm summer class (Cfb) of the Köppen-Geiger [SOURCE: Page_6]

classification (Kottek et al., 2006). The local climate is monitored in an open area near (< 1 km) the [SOURCE: Page_6]

forest stands and data from the last two decades indicate mean annual values of 13.1 °C for air [SOURCE: Page_6]

temperature and 908 mm yr-1 for bulk precipitation. Summer precipitation (May-September) is on [SOURCE: Page_6]

average 292 mm yr-1, resulting in moderate water stress (monthly SPEI values = -4.7; Hargreaves [SOURCE: Page_6]

climatic moisture deficit = 354 mm yr-1; Figure S$_2$). Nevertheless, climatic indices varied a lot from year [SOURCE: Page_6]

to year during the period studied (Table S$_1$), implying possible droughts or heatwaves. [SOURCE: Page_6]

The Landes de Gascogne region is a 1.3 Mha flat area, characterised by a low elevation and a sparse [SOURCE: Page_6]

network of rivers (Deirmendjian et al., 2019; Jolivet et al., 2007). Due to these properties, the water [SOURCE: Page_6]

table level fluctuates near the soil surface (0-3 m deep), except near the rivers that function as natural [SOURCE: Page_6]

drainage ditches (Cottinet, 1974). The water table is characterised by oligotrophic conditions but levels [SOURCE: Page_6]

of redox and oxygen concentration vary over time (Deirmendjian et al., 2019; Jolivet et al., 2007; Righi, [SOURCE: Page_6]

1977). The soil parent material consists of Aeolian deposits of quaternary sands ( 23-15 kyr BP), which [SOURCE: Page_6]

are relatively homogeneous in particle size (mainly 100-200 µm) and rich in quartz (Bertran et al., 2011; [SOURCE: Page_6]

Juste, 1965; Sitzia, 2014). Aeolian deposits of this kind are found in several regions of Europe, such as [SOURCE: Page_6]

in England, Netherlands, Denmark, Germany and Poland (Sitzia, 2014). While they are dominated by [SOURCE: Page_6]

the quartz fraction, these sands also contain substantial amounts of orthose, but very low contents of [SOURCE: Page_6]

phyllosilicates or minor minerals such as tourmaline, staurodite, zircon and andalousite (Juste, 1965; [SOURCE: Page_6]

Latouche, 1971; Righi and De Conninck, 1977). Soils that have developed from the Aeolian deposits [SOURCE: Page_6]

are mainly non-structured sandy podzols (albic; entic and/or ortsteinic; (Augusto et al., 2010)). Podzols [SOURCE: Page_6]

with the ortsteinic characteristic have a cemented B soil horizon –locally named alios– that physically [SOURCE: Page_6]

limits the vertical distribution of plant roots and hence plant growth (Delibrias et al., 1966; Gelpe, 1992). [SOURCE: Page_6]

Nevertheless, because of the discontinuous nature of this alios horizon (Richer-de-Forges et al., 2017), [SOURCE: Page_6]

soil exploration by tree roots is only partly impeded by this horizon (Achat et al., 2008) whereas the [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

presence of the water table may be a major obstacle to tree rooting in the wet areas (Bakker et al., 2006). [SOURCE: Page_7]

Soils are acidic and poor in nutrients and oligo-elements (Augusto et al., 2022, 2010), such as potassium [SOURCE: Page_7]

or magnesium (Delmas, 1954; Demounem, 1979). Nevertheless, it is the phosphorus (P) deficiency that [SOURCE: Page_7]

characterises most these soils as the extremely low soil Ptotal content (Achat et al., 2009; Augusto et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

2010) makes them some of the poorest soils worldwide (He et al., 2021; Lambers et al., 2010). In [SOURCE: Page_7]

podzols, the soil physical-chemical reactivity is generally led by organic matter and aluminium-iron [SOURCE: Page_7]

oxides (Achat et al., 2016b; Eusterhues et al., 2005; Grand and Lavkulich, 2013, 2015; Jansen et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

2005), a characteristic that applies to the soils of this region (Achat et al., 2011; Augusto et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_7]

Study forest [SOURCE: Page_7]

Created in the second half of the 19th century (Jolivet et al., 2007), the Landes de Gascogne forest covers [SOURCE: Page_7]

0.9-1.0 Mha, making it the largest artificial forest in Europe (Figure S1A). Mainly composed of even- [SOURCE: Page_7]

aged stands of maritime pines (Pinus pinaster Ait.), this forest is managed for wood production, pulp [SOURCE: Page_7]

and industrial fuelwood (Jolivet et al., 2007; Orazio et al., 2015). Forest management includes plant [SOURCE: Page_7]

breeding, soil preparation at planting, drainage, P-fertilisation, vegetation control, and several thinnings [SOURCE: Page_7]

before a final clear-cut at 35-50 years-old (Chaperon and Cremiere, 1994; Dorlanne, 1991). Such [SOURCE: Page_7]

intensive management, in conjunction with the adaptation of the maritime pine species to harsh [SOURCE: Page_7]

conditions (Chevalier et al., 2024), enable a stand productivity that is moderate in comparison to [SOURCE: Page_7]

European forests (Tóth et al., 2013), but that is high when taking the local soil poverty into account. [SOURCE: Page_7]

It is worth mentioning that the Landes de Gascogne forest is exposed to major disturbances (Senf and [SOURCE: Page_7]

Seidl, 2021), such as huge winter windthrows (in 1999 and 2009), summer droughts (in 2011, 2016 and [SOURCE: Page_7]

2022), and large-scale wildfires (in 2022) due to the high ignitability of maritime pines (Fernandes and [SOURCE: Page_7]

Rigolot, 2007; Tapias et al., 2004). In addition to those hazards, the sustainability of the system is [SOURCE: Page_7]

threatened by current changes in silvicultural practices. Indeed, while the harvesting intensity of this [SOURCE: Page_7]

forest is among the highest in Europe (Levers et al., 2014), recent technical innovations have enabled [SOURCE: Page_7]

the generalisation of rooting system harvests and –to a lesser extent– whole-tree harvests (Banos and [SOURCE: Page_7]

Dehez, 2017), with possible deleterious consequences on ecosystem functioning (Achat et al., 2015a, [SOURCE: Page_7]

2015b; Augusto et al., 2022). [SOURCE: Page_7]

Experimental platform [SOURCE: Page_8]

The XyloSylve experimental platform was initiated with the objective of testing, under real conditions, [SOURCE: Page_8]

innovative silvicultural systems that were expected to supply in biomass in the regional forest-wood [SOURCE: Page_8]

chain. In a first stage, discussions were organised between scientists and stakeholders to define forest [SOURCE: Page_8]

management alternatives (FMA; (Mason and Meredieu, 2011)) that would constitute a gradient of [SOURCE: Page_8]

extensive-intensive approaches, in combination with a conservative-innovative gradient. A list of eight [SOURCE: Page_8]

silvicultural systems was finally defined for field evaluation and three were evaluated in the present [SOURCE: Page_8]

study (see below). These three silvicultural systems were contrasted enough to belong to different [SOURCE: Page_8]

classes of the FMA framework (Mason and Meredieu, 2011). [SOURCE: Page_8]

The XyloSylve platform was established in the INRAE experimental forest (elevation  65 m above sea [SOURCE: Page_8]

level). The area belongs to the “wet moorland” type based on the regional site classification (Jolivet et [SOURCE: Page_8]

al., 2007), meaning that the water table level remains fairly close to the topsoil level (i.e. 0–2 m deep), [SOURCE: Page_8]

and that spontaneous vegetation is dominated by purple moor-grass (Molinia caerulea) and to a lesser [SOURCE: Page_8]

extent by bracken (Pteridium aquilinum) or common heather (Calluna vulgaris). The XyloSylve [SOURCE: Page_8]

platform consists of two distinct sites about 1.5 km apart: (1) the trial site (44.7352–44.7390°N; 0.7918– [SOURCE: Page_8]

0.8005°W) assesses the practical feasibility of the eight FMA, but its results are not presented in the [SOURCE: Page_8]

present study; and (2) the monitoring site (44.7441–44.7505°N; 0.7718–0.7805°W), which provided [SOURCE: Page_8]

data for the present study. The monitoring site is composed of three large plots (8–9 ha each) that test [SOURCE: Page_8]

three contrasting silvicultural systems (see below). These three plots were side-by-side and were located [SOURCE: Page_8]

on a flat horizontal plain, without any geomorphological differences (slope, elevation, microrelief). They [SOURCE: Page_8]

were equipped with sensors and collectors to assess biogeochemical and biophysical changes, from the [SOURCE: Page_8]

atmosphere to the groundwater. The large surface area of each plot was chosen to enable monitoring the [SOURCE: Page_8]

carbon-water fluxes using the eddy-covariance methods (Barcza et al., 2009; Kowalski et al., 2003). The [SOURCE: Page_8]

practical consequence of having large, heavily equipped, plots was that it was not feasible to test all of [SOURCE: Page_8]

the eight silvicultural systems, or to replicate them. The absence of true replication implied that it was [SOURCE: Page_8]

not possible to evaluate our results based on statistical tests because sampling replicates or repeated [SOURCE: Page_8]

measurements over time are pseudoreplicates (Hurlbert, 1984). However, Hurlbert also concluded that [SOURCE: Page_8]

7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

“Replication is often impossible or undesirable when very large-scale systems are studied [...] or when [SOURCE: Page_9]

the cost of replication is very great”, which is the case of our experiment. We followed Hurlbert’s [SOURCE: Page_9]

recommendations for this kind of study, which is not to use inferential statistics, but descriptive analyses [SOURCE: Page_9]

instead. [SOURCE: Page_9]

Forest management alternatives (FMA) [SOURCE: Page_9]

Foresters may influence ecosystem functioning through several practices such as tree species choice (De [SOURCE: Page_9]

Schrijver et al., 2008), vegetation control (Balandier et al., 2006), or thinning regimes (del Campo et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

2022). In the three forest management alternatives (FMA) retained for the monitoring site, we paid [SOURCE: Page_9]

particular attention to stand density and management of the understory. Indeed, stand density influences [SOURCE: Page_9]

productivity while strongly modifying hydrological and biogeochemical processes (Andre et al., 2008; [SOURCE: Page_9]

Cai et al., 2016; Hall and Marchand, 2010). Stand density affects light transmittance (Dumas et al., 2022; [SOURCE: Page_9]

Gaudio et al., 2011), so it interacts with the dynamics of the understory vegetation and the latter is the [SOURCE: Page_9]

second factor taken into account in our selected FMA. It is all the more relevant because spontaneous [SOURCE: Page_9]

vegetation is abundant and functionally important in the study region (Gonzalez et al., 2013; Jarosz et [SOURCE: Page_9]

al., 2008; Rivalland et al., 2005; Vidal et al., 2021). [SOURCE: Page_9]

The three FMA were tested in plots formerly occupied by mature pine stands, which had been clear-cut, [SOURCE: Page_9]

with biomass export during the winter of 2012-2013. The site preparation was also the same for all the [SOURCE: Page_9]

FMA, including soil ploughing-disking and fertilisation with inorganic phosphorus (Table 1), a practice [SOURCE: Page_9]

long-known for improving tree growth in the study region (Trichet et al., 2009). Then, FMA differed [SOURCE: Page_9]

from each other (Table 1; plantations in early-spring 2013): the wood biomass production (WBP) was [SOURCE: Page_9]

designed using a very intense forestry approach (FMA$_5$ in (Mason and Meredieu, 2011)). It included a [SOURCE: Page_9]

dense plantation (Figure S3A) of a seed lot of maritime pine that was selected to be fast- [SOURCE: Page_9]

growing (Table 1). The combined objective management (COM; FMA$_3$ in (Mason and Meredieu, [SOURCE: Page_9]

2011)) aimed at improving pine growth by alleviating any competition. Inter-tree competition was [SOURCE: Page_9]

limited by planting at low density (Figure S3B) while competition due to spontaneous vegetation (Bon [SOURCE: Page_9]

et al., 2023) was reduced with periodic mechanical controls (Table 1). This FMA will include the [SOURCE: Page_9]

plantation of broadleaved species (birch and oak) dedicated to future production diversification and [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

support for biodiversity, which explains the management name, but this is not in the scope of the present [SOURCE: Page_10]

study (see § Perspectives). The nutrient management FMA (NuM) was designed to improve the regional [SOURCE: Page_10]

standard silvicultural system (moderate stand density and vegetation control; Figure S3C) using a [SOURCE: Page_10]

nature-based solution, namely sowing N-fixers in the stand furrows. The species used were common [SOURCE: Page_10]

gorse (Ulex europaeus L.; 100 seeds m-2) and common broom (Cytisus scoparius (L.) Link; 100 seeds m- 2 ), which are local shrubs. Common gorse proved to fix atmospheric nitrogen (N) at a high rate (Augusto [SOURCE: Page_10]

et al., 2005; Vidal et al., 2019) and was expected to be the species that is the best adapted to local [SOURCE: Page_10]

conditions. In general, all of the FMA were designed to produce biomass, but with a level of forestry [SOURCE: Page_10]

intensity that ranks: WBP > COM > NuM. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Study design [SOURCE: Page_10]

The monitoring site is composed of three large plots of 8-9 ha, arranged side-by-side (Figure S4A). In [SOURCE: Page_10]

each plot, monitoring was carried out in three subplots: two peripheral subplots (in NE and SW [SOURCE: Page_10]

directions) dedicated to most measurements and one subplot dedicated to water table sampling and eddy- [SOURCE: Page_10]

covariance monitoring (the central area; Figure S4B). The rationale of this design is to let undisturbed [SOURCE: Page_10]

the biogeochemical monitoring (in the NW-SW peripheral subplots) when pumping the water table for [SOURCE: Page_10]

sampling in the central area. Similarly, the eddy-covariance monitoring was carried out in the central [SOURCE: Page_10]

subplot because this method takes into account processes over large surface areas (Kowalski et al., 2003) [SOURCE: Page_10]

and consequently it was necessary to locate the eddy-covariance tower far away from any forest edge. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Similarly, we located the two peripheral subplots at least 40 m away from the closest forest edge, [SOURCE: Page_10]

because the vicinity of an edge (or a ditch) may affect the local biogeochemical functioning (Ågren et [SOURCE: Page_10]

al., 2024; Wuyts et al., 2008). The monitoring is presented below but also summarised in Table S$_2$. [SOURCE: Page_10]

Soils [SOURCE: Page_10]

The initial properties of soils were studied based on a large sampling campaign. In practice, each plot [SOURCE: Page_10]

states that the canopy exchange (CE) of the reference element is nil (CES = 0) and that the ratio [SOURCE: Page_15]

experiment because the differences between FMA appeared in 2017 (Figure 5A,D), which was [SOURCE: Page_22]

The water table was characterised by acidic conditions, with large fluctuations between pH=4 and pH=5, [SOURCE: Page_23]

Vegetation response to forest management alternatives [SOURCE: Page_25]

Tree nutrition in the study region is characterised by deficiencies in several nutrients, notably potassium [SOURCE: Page_25]

and magnesium (Demounem, 1979) and above all phosphorus (Trichet et al., 2009), which explains the [SOURCE: Page_25]

common use of P fertilisation in local silviculture (Table 1). Fertilisation at plantation probably [SOURCE: Page_25]

improved tree nutrition as shown by foliage P content (Figure 7B), but foliage P content decreased over [SOURCE: Page_25]

time and the same trend was visible for most nutrients (Figure 7A-E), suggesting a phenomenon of [SOURCE: Page_25]

nutrient dilution induced by tree biomass growth (Jarrell and Beverly, 1981; Manghabati et al., 2019). [SOURCE: Page_25]

This decreasing trend was broken for N and K after five years, which coincided with the first major [SOURCE: Page_25]

operations of understory control (Figure S$_9$), as the N-K foliage content increased sharply and remained [SOURCE: Page_25]

at high levels (Figure 7A,C). Spontaneous vegetation may be competitors of trees (South et al., 2006) [SOURCE: Page_25]

and the composition of the local vegetation (Pteridium aquilinum, Molinia caerulea, Rubus fruticosus, [SOURCE: Page_25]

Calluna vulgaris and Ulex europaeus) is well-known at the national scale (Dumas et al., 2022) and at [SOURCE: Page_25]

the regional scale (Bon et al., 2023; Vidal et al., 2021) for being particularly competitive species. Thus, [SOURCE: Page_25]

reducing the biomass of the spontaneous vegetation probably reduced the competition for nutrient [SOURCE: Page_25]

uptake. In addition, vegetation crushing produced large amounts of fresh litter that probably released [SOURCE: Page_25]

nutrients quickly, such as potassium (Bessaad and Korboulewsky, 2020) and nitrogen (Vidal et al., [SOURCE: Page_25]

2019). The foliage nutrient content showed no difference between FMA, except for a lower P content [SOURCE: Page_25]

for NuM and a lower K content for WBP after the first vegetation control. Nitrogen nutrition was not [SOURCE: Page_25]

improved by the introduction of N-fixers in the NuM stand, in contradiction to our a priori [SOURCE: Page_25]

expectation (H$_3$). These trends will be further investigated using the trial site (in completely randomised [SOURCE: Page_25]

blocks) of the XyloSylve platform. [SOURCE: Page_25]

Conclusions and perspectives [SOURCE: Page_26]

Our study took place in a field experimental platform characterised by a temperate climate but also by [SOURCE: Page_26]

particularly unfavourable edaphic conditions. Soils are acidic sandy podzols and inherently poor in [SOURCE: Page_26]

nutrients. In such a context, the nutritional sustainability of forest stands depends on fertiliser inputs [SOURCE: Page_26]

(Heilman and Norby, 1998), symbiotic N fixation (Augusto et al., 2005) and atmospheric deposition [SOURCE: Page_26]

(De Schrijver et al., 2008). Taking into account the demand for nutrients by pine trees (Augusto et al., [SOURCE: Page_26]

2010; Helmisaari, 1995), it is probable that the current rate of atmospheric deposition is high enough to [SOURCE: Page_26]

supply trees with nutrients (except for P, which is supplied through fertilisation), which is well in line [SOURCE: Page_26]

with other field studies carried out in unpolluted regions (Campo and Gallardo, 2012; Johnson and [SOURCE: Page_26]

Turner, 2014). Although it was not possible to calculate input-output budgets reliably, because of the [SOURCE: Page_26]

methodological constraints associated with this approach (Paré and Thiffault, 2016; Ranger and [SOURCE: Page_26]

Turpault, 1999), it is probable that budgets are close to equilibrium (or in surplus for N in the NuM [SOURCE: Page_26]

FMA) at the stage of stand development. The effects of contrasting FMA on the biogeochemical [SOURCE: Page_26]

functioning of ecosystems were modest considering the large structural differences of stands including [SOURCE: Page_26]

25 [SOURCE: Page_26]

nitrogen cycling, despite a large symbiotic fixation flux in the NuM FMA. Nonetheless, it is worth [SOURCE: Page_27]

mentioning that the trees remained small during a long initial period, probably delaying the [SOURCE: Page_27]

biogeochemical consequences of FMA. This is particularly important for the soils studied, because the [SOURCE: Page_27]

functioning of podzols is very dependent on soil organic matter (Augusto et al., 2010; David et al., 1995; [SOURCE: Page_27]

Jansen et al., 2005; Juste, 1965; Lundstrom, 1993), a compartment of the ecosystem that changes slowly [SOURCE: Page_27]

over time (Balesdent et al., 2018; Korner, 2003). Consequently we anticipate that the influence of FMA [SOURCE: Page_27]

on ecosystem biogeochemistry will increase with increasing tree age and management modifications. In [SOURCE: Page_27]

addition, the tested FMA have designs that include important treatments at mid-stage development. In [SOURCE: Page_27]

practice, the NuM FMA will receive a wood ash application to supply the ecosystem with nutrients [SOURCE: Page_27]

(Gomez-Rey et al., 2013), while WBP will be thinned and COM will be planted with Mediterranean oak [SOURCE: Page_27]

species. The functional consequences of these managements will be assessed in the monitoring site of [SOURCE: Page_27]

the XyloSylve platform but also in its trial site, which will enable to statistically test the observed effects. [SOURCE: Page_27]

FMA had stronger effects on biophysical processes (light transmittance, rainfall transmittance, [SOURCE: Page_27]

temperature and moisture) and biomass allocation in the ecosystem than on biogeochemical processes. [SOURCE: Page_27]

Within the scope of this study, the most intensive FMA –based on dense plantation dedicated to wood [SOURCE: Page_27]

biomass production (WBP)– showed important effects on stand microclimate with higher moisture and [SOURCE: Page_27]

lower temperature. This density-microclimate interaction in young plantations may be an important [SOURCE: Page_27]

indication of how forestry could be adapted to climate change, as the study region is possibly exposed [SOURCE: Page_27]

to a future reduction of plant growth due to water stress (Loustau et al., 2005). This is particularly [SOURCE: Page_27]

relevant in the driest parts of the Landes de Gascogne forest, which are known to be water-limited, [SOURCE: Page_27]

whereas the wettest parts (where the XyloSylve platform is located) are P-limited (Bon et al., 2023; [SOURCE: Page_27]

Trichet et al., 2009, 2008). [SOURCE: Page_27]

Finally, although the relationship between stand density and superficial microclimate was expected [SOURCE: Page_27]

(Kovács et al., 2017; Rambo and North, 2009), the recurrent effect on deep soil and water table [SOURCE: Page_27]

temperature was surprising and to our knowledge not yet reported. Also, taking into account the possible [SOURCE: Page_27]

role of the water table for tree water supply (Vincke and Thiry, 2008), we posit that future studies about [SOURCE: Page_27]

forest functioning should include the water table in the atmosphere-vegetation-soil continuum. [SOURCE: Page_27]
