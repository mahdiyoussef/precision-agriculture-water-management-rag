Headwaters can be characterized by perennial or intermittent flow regimes. They are classified as intermittent if they experience variable cycles of wetting and flow cessation, with flow sustained beyond a single storm event due to groundwater connectivity (Busch et al., 2020). Although the availability of data is increasing through a variety of approaches (field mapping, sensors, camera and drone surveys, remote sensing, etc.) (Christensen et al., 2022; Dralle, Lapides, et al., 2023, Dralle, Rossi, et al., 2023; Soares et al., 2025), understanding the hydrological processes behind streamflow intermittence in mountain headwaters remains challenging (Fovet et al., 2021; Tarasova et al., 2024; Zimmer et al., 2020). Besides climatic influences and human activities (Datry, Boulton, et al., 2023, Datry, Truchy, et al., 2023), intermittence is strongly influenced by landscape characteristics (Costigan et al., 2016; Winter 1999), such as geomorphic and geologic properties (Dralle, Lapides, et al., 2023; Dralle, Rossi, et al., 2023), which control subsurface‐surface interactions (Carlier et al., 2018; Godsey & Kirchner, 2014; Leibowitz et al., 2018; Warix et al., 2021). For a given topography, the elevation of the water table and its fluctuations depend on the groundwater recharge (R), and the aquifer's hydraulic properties (Haitjema & Mitchell‐Bruker, 2005), namely the transmissivity (T) defined as the hydraulic conductivity (K) integrated over the saturated aquifer thickness (h), and the drainable porosity, defined as the specific yield Sy for unconfined aquifers. In steady‐state conditions, aquifers modeled with low transmissivity typically result in higher water levels and, consequently, a dense network of springs and streams (Abhervé et al., 2023). At similar recharge, higher transmissivity leads to lower water levels and, consequently, a lower drainage density (Litwin et al., 2024). Under transient recharge, both transmissivity and specific yield shape the dynamic response of the water table fluctuations and, consequently, the expansion and contraction of the stream network (Abhervé et al., 2024; Lovill et al., 2018). Transmissivity primarily controls the rate at which the aquifer drains following recharge, while specific yield governs the magnitude of water table fluctuations and their combined influence dictates both the timing and spatial extent of stream network expansion and contraction. Furthermore, groundwater‐dependent ecosystems may be influenced by local, intermediate, and regional flow (Tóth, 1963) providing flow regimes with different response and transit times (Benettin et al., 2022; Gauvain et al., 2021; Hrachowitz et al., 2016; Kirchner et al., 2023). For example, groundwater residence times >50 years has been shown for headwaters in crystalline contexts through sampling and modeling results, indicating an important contribution of deep groundwater to streamflow (Ameli et al., 2018; Ayraud et al., 2008; Carroll et al., 2020; Goderniaux et al., 2013). In mountainous regions, aquifers are often conceptualized with a vertically compartmentalized structure, con- sisting of a shallow fractured zone that extends several meters above the underlying fresh bedrock (Welch & Allen, 2014). At depth, a decrease in hydraulic properties with depth is generally observed due the decreasing presence of fractures and the increasing impact of topographic stress that tends close the existing fractures (Achtziger‐Zupančič et al., 2017; Cardenas & Jiang, 2010; Grant & Dietrich, 2017; Hahm et al., 2019; Ranjram et al., 2015; Rempe & Dietrich, 2014; Ren et al., 2018; Riebe et al., 2017; Stober & Bucher, 2007; Wor- thington, 2024). Local studies performed in alpine contexts support this conceptualization, highlighting a transmissive near‐surface aquifer extending to maximum depths of 100–200 m (Bresciani et al., 2018; Markovich et al., 2019), along with exponential decay of hydraulic properties with depth (Ameli et al., 2016; Betterle & Bellin, 2024; Bishop et al., 2024; Rapp et al., 2020). Changes in snow and rainfall regimes, with increasing frequency of extreme events, along with more pronounced increase in temperatures significantly affect groundwater recharge dynamics and runoff (Clow, 2010; IPCC ‐ Hock et al., 2019; Ragettli et al., 2016; Siirila‐Woodburn et al., 2021; Stephan et al., 2021; Tague et al., 2008; Thornton, 2024). The gradual percolation of water from snowmelt, crucial for maintaining groundwater levels and supporting baseflow during dry periods, is disrupted by the shift from snowfall to rainfall. Rising temper- atures lead to earlier snowmelt in alpine regions, resulting in earlier peak flows in streams and altered seasonal distribution of water availability (Arnoux et al., 2021; Brunner et al., 2019; Halloran et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024; Hanus et al., 2021). In addition, increased vegetation cover in mountainous headwaters exacerbates water stress during dry periods through increase in evapotranspiration (Vicente‐Serrano et al., 2021). These changes in groundwater recharge have consequences on the timing, extent, and duration of spatio‐temporal dynamics of groundwater‐dependent streamflow. The hydrologic community has widely emphasized the need to develop hydrological models (Blöschl et al., 2019) that correctly represent and predict the expansion and contraction dynamics of the groundwater‐dependent springs and streams (Shanafield et al., 2021; Zimmer & McGlynn, 2017). While retrospective observations have demonstrated a progressive decline in the spatial extent of stream networks and flow permanence due to climate change (Ayers et al., 2024; Price et al., 2021; Tramblay et al., 2021; Ward et al., 2020; Zipper et al., 2021), modeling studies remain relatively limited to date (Jaeger et al., 2014; Pumo et al., 2016; Querner et al., 2016; Reynolds et al., 2015). Studies that have considered climate change scenarios have predicted a shift from a perennial to an intermittent flow regime across Europe, combined with an exacerbation of dry conditions, with significant impacts on freshwater ecosystems (Mimeau et al., 2024; Sauquet et al., 2021). Indeed, streamflow persistence is likely to decrease as droughts become more severe, with the intensity of change amplified under high greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions scenarios. Authors commonly employ statistical approaches or rainfall‐ runoff models to simulate streamflow intermittence, a method applied in mountain contexts as well (Döll et al., 2024; Durighetto et al., 2022; Sando & Blasch, 2015). However, these methods do not explicitly represent lateral and 3D groundwater flow patterns, which are crucial for understanding spatially distributed groundwater‐ surface water interaction in steep topographic gradients. In contrast, more complex fully‐integrated coupling surface‐subsurface processes are challenging to implement, deploy and calibrate at a study site, as they often require substantial amounts of data and computation time (Thornton et al., 2022; Zanetti et al., 2024). In this paper, we focus on a crystalline alpine headwater located in the French Pyrenees mountain range. This region is particularly affected by on‐going climate change (Amblar‐Francés et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2022; López‐de Sancha et al., 2022; López‐Moreno et al., 2008; Morán‐Tejeda et al., 2015; Strohmenger et al., 2024). We apply a process‐based 3D groundwater modeling framework to predict springs and streamflow intermittence at the catchment‐scale. In the absence of hydraulic head data, we build on recent advances for the calibration of our groundwater flow model considering observations of stream network maps (Stoll & Weiler, 2010) and streamflow measurements, while testing an ensemble of aquifer configurations. This calibration approach has been previously described and validated in Abhervé et al. (2023, 2024) and applied in other contexts (Floriancic et al., 2024; Guillaumot et al., 2024). Within this strategy, the position of springs serves as a proxy for the average hydraulic head, while its seasonal variation are reflected in the extent of the intermittent stream sections (Abhervé et al., 2024). This strategy allows estimation of effective hydraulic conductivity K, the specific yield Sy, and their exponential decay rate α with depth that are representative of the processes involved at the catchment scale. Using the best‐performing model, we simulate future recharge conditions based on IPCC GHG emission scenarios (RCP$_2$.6, 4.5, 8.5). We then assess changes in the spatio‐temporal dynamics of the spring and stream network, focusing on low‐flow periods when groundwater is the main contributor. The impacts are specifically evaluated for four sub‐catchments, each corresponding to the contributing area of a peatland identified by local nature protection managers. The shifts on groundwater system dynamics are examined through non‐linear recharge‐ storage‐discharge relationships and their associated aquifer response times. Finally, we discuss the main hydrogeological controls on future springs and streamflow intermittence highlighting the implications of our modeling approach for ecosystem sustainability as well as land and water resource management and protection. 2. Materials and Methods 2.1. Context of the Study Area 2.1.1. Geographic and Geological Description [SOURCE: Page_5]

The headwater catchment study site is located in the Pyrenees mountain range within the Massif of Saint‐Bar- thélemy in France (see Figures 1a–1c). The Lasset River, the mainstream, is approximately 10 km long. It is a tributary of the Ariège River, which ultimately flows into the Garonne River. The high‐altitude, alpine, moun- tainous context features a steep topographic gradient with elevations ranging from approximately 1,120 to 2,350 m and an average slope of 57% (Figure 1d). The geological context of the Saint‐Barthélemy Massif consists of Archean, Proterozoic, and Upper Precambrian rocks. The massif was affected by the Hercynian orogeny and shaped by Cambrian extension and Alpine compression. The bedrock area is characterized by basement formations, primarily consisting of crystalline rocks. The studied headwater catchment encompasses four major lithologies: migmatitic paragneiss (Precambrian), pelitic mica schists (Cambrian), quartzitic mica schists and ectinites (Silurian), and sandstones, schists, and limestones (Paleozoic). In addition to monzogranite outcrops, the site is scattered with quaternary talus deposits, alluvium and debris covers such as rock fall deposits. The lithological context favors the presence of shallow unconfined aquifers composed, from bottom to top, of a low‐permeability basement—except in fractured/faulted areas—and a fissured transmissive horizon located a few tenth of meters below the surface (Monod et al., 2018; Saplairoles et al., 2017). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Following the methodology of Jasiewicz and Stepinski (2013) available in WhiteBoxTools (Lindsay, 2016), the landforms were classified and mapped according to different geomorphons (calculated for each pixel within a radius of approximately 40 cells, corresponding to about 1 km) (Figure 1f). The geomorphons function is useful for identifying various topographical landscape features, ranging from ridges to valleys. Using CORINE Land Cover 2018 data (European Environment Agency, 2018), the catchment is covered by 37% of grasslands and natural pastures, 24% of deciduous forest, 17% of forest and shrub vegetation 15% of sparse vegetation, 6% of bare rocks and 1% of heathland and scrubland (Figure S$_2$ in Supporting Information S$_1$). [SOURCE: Page_5]

2.1.2. Socio‐Ecological Motivations for Research on Local Groundwater‐Dependent Ecosystems The Massif of Saint‐Barthélemy Nature Reserve (for more detailed information, the reader is referred to: https:// reserves‐naturelles.org/reserves/massif‐de‐saint‐barthelemy/) focuses on the impacts of anthropogenic and climate change on its ecosystems, particularly in maintaining the biodiversity and functionality of its alpine socio‐ ecosystems. The reserve serves as an “open‐air laboratory” (Shahgedanova et al., 2021), hosting scientific teams to study both biotic and abiotic aspects of these environments, with an emphasis on the functioning and resilience of wetland complexes, such as ponds, streams, and peatlands. Given its mountainous isolated location and unique geological features, the study site acts as a sentinel for monitoring climate change effects on—Pyrenean—high‐ altitude ecosystems. Peatlands within the region, which hold significant ecological, hydrological, and cultural importance, are a management priority. The involved ecosystems are essential for biodiversity, water—quality and quantity—regulation downstream, and could play a significant role in climate regulation through GHG storage (Evans et al., 2021; Leifeld & Menichetti, 2018). However, their unique functioning, such as the contribution of groundwater and the hydrogeological processes involved, remains poorly understood. Local managers—but more generally the stakeholders in high mountain regions—raises the need of ongoing research and modeling to guide management decisions aimed at preserving the wetland functions and mitigating climate change impacts on ecosystems. Since the establishment of the nature reserve in 2015, various consultancies and research laboratories have collaborated to enhance the understanding of four key wetlands using a transdisciplinary approach. More spe- cifically, these hydrosystems are peat complexes, representative of the diverse ecosystems within the study area. Peatlands are unique wetlands where net primary production exceeds the decomposition of organic matter, leading to peat accumulation. Their near‐surface water table is governed by a combination of internal and external factors, including short‐term weather patterns, long‐term climate trends, and site‐specific conditions such as peat properties, vegetation cover, aquifer hydraulic properties and hydrogeological settings. The unique characteristics of the four peatlands studied are briefly outlined below: P$_1$: The Lasset Peatland. A peat complex crossed by the Lasset stream features a wide variety of wetland habitats, including poor fens approximately 60 cm in depth, interspersed with wet meadows. This wetland is situated at the bottom of a forested valley. P$_2$: The Breton Peatland. A small minerotrophic peatland on a glacial umbilicus, composed of poor fens and a few ombrotrophic profiles assimilated to bog hummocks. P$_3$: The Grenouillère Peatland. A lacustrine peatland and limnogenic fen covering approximately 1.5 ha, with an ombrominerotrophic hydrological regime. This peatland developed about 4,500 years ago consists of several functional units, including transition mires (quaking mires and floating rafts), bog hummocks, and poor fens. P$_4$: The Soularac Peatland. A peatland ombrotrophic bog with a depth of about 1.40 m, is composed of raised bogs and bog hummocks. It is bordered by spring‐fed mires and poor fens. Located at an elevation of 2,040 m at the upstream part of the catchment, this site experiences the minimal anthropogenic pressure from pastoralism. ### 2.2.2. Streamflow Measurements

A gauging station was installed at the catchment outlet (WGS84: 1.79133, 42.83800) in July 2021, downstream of the Lasset peatland (P$_1$). Since installation, water level, electrical conductivity, and water temperature have been monitored at hourly intervals using a Hydros 21 probe from Meter Group. Stream discharge was measured during four field campaigns at different water level conditions to develop a rating curve, enabling the conversion of continuous water level data into a discharge time series. [SOURCE: Page_7]

2.3. Hydroclimatic Forcing Data From an Independent Land Surface Model Hydroclimatic data are provided by the land surface model SURFEX (EXternalized SURFace)—or SIM$_2$ model chain—version 8.1 developed by Météo‐France (Le Moigne et al., 2020; Noilhan & Mahfouf, 1996) (for more detailed information, the reader is referred to https://www.umr‐cnrm.fr/surfex/). SURFEX provides data at a spatial resolution of 8 × 8 km^2 for the French metropolitan area. Forced by meteorological variables, SURFEX computes the energy and water fluxes at the soil‐vegetation‐atmosphere interface (Masson et al., 2003). Pre- cipitation is partitioned into evapotranspiration, snowpack water equivalent, surface runoff, and soil drainage. Groundwater recharge, represented by deep soil infiltration from SURFEX, is used as the forcing input to our 3D catchment‐scale groundwater flow models (see Section 2.4.2). To obtain total catchment outflow, surface runoff from SURFEX—comprising both saturation‐excess and infiltration‐excess overland flow (Vergnes et al., 2020) —is added in post‐processing to the simulated groundwater discharge from seepage zones. This combined approach allows us to capture high‐flow events, such as peak flows, that are not represented by the groundwater model alone. [SOURCE: Page_7]

2.3.2. Projection Scenarios SURFEX was forced by the output of regional climate models (RCM) produced as part of the international EURO‐CORDEX project (Jacob et al., 2014; Kotlarski et al., 2014), which are driven by a subset of global climate models (GCM) from the CMIP$_5$ (Coupled Model Intercomparison Project 5) (Navarro‐Racines et al., 2020; Taylor et al., 2012). These GCMs simulate climate evolution according to different socio‐economic scenarios called RCPs (Representative Concentration Pathway) and corrected for residual systematic bias using the ADAMONT statistical method (Verfaillie et al., 2017), representing as closely as possible expected temperature and precipitation in France. These scenarios, developed in the fifth IPCC AR$_5$ report (IPCC, 2013), correspond to changes in GHG concentrations in the atmosphere (period 2010–2100), depending on the level of emissions in the coming years. RCPs are named according to the range of possible radiative forcing values that could be reached by 2100, from the most favorable to the most unfavorable: RCP$_2$.6 (2.6 W/m^2), RCP$_4$.5 (4.5 W/m^2), RCP$_6$.0 (6.0 W/m^2), RCP$_8$.5 (8.5 W/m^2). A list of 12 GCM/RCM pairs was selected using a variety of selection and evaluation criteria to retain the most coherent and realistic models for Europe (McSweeney et al., 2015). All data can be downloaded from the DRIAS (Météo‐France, 2021) web portal (for more detailed information, the reader is referred to: https://www. drias‐climat.fr/ and https://www.drias‐eau.fr/). For each climatic model pair, a scaling factor is obtained by dividing the recharge plus runoff for the rescaled historical reanalysis (Section 2.3.1) by the recharge plus runoff for the historical period from the projection simulations (1980–2010). This factor is then applied for both the historical period (1980–2010) and the future projections (2010–2100). [SOURCE: Page_8]

### 2.4. Groundwater Flow Modeling

2.4.1. Software and Process‐Based 3D Numerical Model HydroModPy (Gauvain et al., 2025), a newly developed Python toolbox for deploying catchment‐scale groundwater models, was utilized run the simulations and analyze the data. Groundwater flow equations were solved using MODFLOW‐NWT, an enhanced Newton formulation of MODFLOW‐2005, employing a three‐ dimensional finite difference approach (Harbaugh, 2005; Niswonger et al., 2011). Both included in Hydro- ModPy, the geospatial processing tool WhiteBoxTools (Lindsay, 2016) is combined with FloPy (Bakker et al., 2016; Leaf & Fienen, 2022) to set up the model and handle simulations at a weekly (for calibration) and monthly (for projection) time step. For the vertical discretization, 10 layers were applied with an exponential increase with depth by a factor of 1.25 from the surface to the bottom of the aquifer fixed at 1000 m elevation (Figure 2b). The fully convertible layer type of MODFLOW was applied, where a cell is classified as confined if the overlying layer contains groundwater; otherwise, it is considered unconfined. [SOURCE: Page_9]

2.4.2. Subsurface Conceptualization and Hydraulic Properties Parametrization As the study area is dominated by crystalline bedrock, the groundwater flow models assume uniform horizontal hydraulic conductivity K and porosity throughout the catchment. Note that the porosity set in MODFLOW in- cludes the specific yield (or drainable porosity) Sy and the specific storage Ss (activated for confined cells). Compared to gravity drainage, aquifer compression and water expansion produce relatively little water from storage, hence Sy ≫ Ss for unconfined aquifers. Thus, we only calibrate K in steady‐state (Section 2.5.1) and Sy in transient (Section 2.5.2), while Ss remains fixed across all tested cases, where hydraulic properties decrease more or less with depth. With this approach, both the superficial quaternary deposits and the fractured bedrock are treated as effective porous media (Figure 2 and Figure S$_7$ in Supporting Information S$_1$). At the catchment scale, these properties account for potential local effects of subsurface features, such as faults, fractures, or surface deposits, which are not explicitly modeled. This approach results in an aquifer characterized by a more or less thick shallow transmissive layer above the fresh bedrock, a conceptualization commonly applied in crystalline regions (Cornette et al., 2022; Dewandel et al., 2012; Lachassagne et al., 2021; Mougin et al., 2008; Roques et al., 2016) and specifically adapted for mountainous contexts (Welch & Allen, 2014). In this study, we tested 9 cases with varying vertical exponential decay rates α [m− 1] of hydraulic properties, while calibrating the maximum values for hydraulic conductivity K max and specific yield Symax at the topmost layer near the surface. The aquifer bottom is defined as flat at an altitude of 1,000 m, resulting in aquifer thicknesses ranging from 120 to 1,350 m. Based on typical boundary values found in classical hydrogeology textbooks for the lithology studied (Domenico & Schwartz, 1997; Freeze & Cherry, 1979; Singhal & Gupta, 2010), for all cases, K min and Symin values at the bottommost layer near the bottom are set to 10− 10 m/s and 0.01%, respectively. Similarly, the maximum Ssmax is set to 10− 5 m− 1, and the minimum Ssmin to 10− 10 m− 1, in accordance with typical values for crystalline rocks (Chowdhury et al., 2022). ### 2.5. Model Calibration on Surface Observations

The groundwater flow model is calibrated using two sources of data: the observed perennial hydrographic network map and the continuously measured streamflow at the catchment outlet. It is then validated against the fully expanded hydrographic network map, including the spatial extent of intermittent streams. The calibration methodology is detailed in the following sections. [SOURCE: Page_12]

2.5.1. Steady‐State Following the methodology proposed by Abhervé et al. (2023), we optimize the effective transmissivity T of the aquifer in steady‐state using the spatial distribution of the observed perennial hydrographic network. For fixed aquifer geometry and thickness, the method determines the optimal hydraulic conductivity relative to recharge, that is, the K/R ratio, by minimizing the distance between simulated and the observed perennial hydrographic network. This optimization is performed using a dichotomy approach. Two indicators are used to reach the optimal groundwater level, resulting in the best compromise between over‐ and undersaturation. From each pixel of the simulated hydrographic network (see Section 2.4.1), we calculated the downslope flowpath distance to the nearest observed perennial hydrographic network, Dso [m]. The mean value is expressed as Dso [m]. Conversely, the same operation is performed from the pixels of the observed perennial hydrographic network to the simulated one; Dos [m], averaged as Dos [m]. High Dso values indicate an overextension of the simulated hydrographic network compared to the observed one, whereas low Dos values reflect an underdeveloped network. The mini- mum absolute difference between Dso and Dos is used as the calibration criterion, representing the closest match between the observed and simulated hydrographic networks. When Dso and Dos equal, the optimization criterion is satisfied. We then compute the distance, named Doptim [m], as the mean mismatch distance between the observed and simulated hydrographic networks. A smaller Doptim [m] value indicates a better fit, thereby reflecting stronger calibration performance. A detailed and comprehensive description of the calibration meth- odology is available in previous papers of Abhervé et al. (2023, 2024). For each of the 9 cases tested with varying vertical exponential decay rates α, we specifically optimize the hy- draulic conductivity K max (or simply K for Case 1 homogeneous). For the optimization, the initial boundary values of K max are set within the range of 10− 10 to 10− 2 m/s. The optimal model among the 9 tested cases is the one with the best performance criterion, that is the lowest Doptim (Figure 3b). [SOURCE: Page_12]

Values range from about 10 to 200 days when considering historical conditions. tr decreases during recharge periods and increases during discharge periods. This dynamic reflects seasonal changes in the position of the water table, the extent of the hydrographic network and, consequently, the length of the flow paths involved. When the water table is high during recharge, short flow paths and steep hydraulic gradients make groundwater storage changes rapidly translate into discharge variations (small tr ). In contrast, during low‐flow periods, a lower water table and longer flow paths mean that large changes in storage produce only small changes in discharge, resulting in larger tr values. The variability in streamflow and water table depth (Figure S12 in Supporting Information S$_1$), which controls the aquifer response times throughout the year, is altered under future recharge conditions. The three RCP scenarios show distinct departures from historical behavior. Under RCP$_2$.6 and RCP$_4$.5, response timescales are shorter from January to April but remain similar to or slightly higher during the rest of the year. This reduction reflects higher winter recharge caused by a shift from snow‐to rainfall‐dominated regimes (Figure S10 in Supporting Information S$_1$). It also leads to an earlier period of fast aquifer response, shifting from May–June under historical conditions to April, consistent with earlier snowmelt and earlier onset of evapotranspiration (see Section 2.3.2). In contrast, RCP$_8$.5 produces significantly longer response timescales across the entire year, averaging 70 days compared to 40 days under historical conditions. ### 4.1. A Modeling Framework for Groundwater‐Surface Interactions Dynamics in Alpine Headwaters

As theory predicts, the positioning of springs and, consequently, the extent of the stream network are highly sensitive to transmissivity. Detailed mapping of perennial stream networks can thus provide valuable information to calibrate groundwater flow models, by optimizing both the hydraulic conductivity and its exponential decay rate under steady‐state conditions. In steep terrain, we also find that the spatial distribution of groundwater seepage is strongly influenced by the partitioning between shallow and deep flow paths, enabling calibration of the characteristic decay length (1/α in Equation 1). In our case, this characteristic length was estimated at 40 m, consistent with previous studies on shallow unconfined crystalline aquifers (Lachassagne et al., 2021; Maréchal et al., 2004; Welch & Allen, 2014). This vertical compartmentalization confers a shallow transmissive layer, ranging from a few meters to several hundred meters in thickness, overlying low‐permeability bedrock. The estimated decay length (1/α) in steady state, subsequently applied during the calibration of specific yield under transient conditions, proved well‐suited to reproducing both streamflow dynamics and the spatio‐temporal expansion and contraction of the stream network. The estimated values of K (K max = 2.9 × 10− 6 m/s, K mean = 1.7 × 10− 7 m/s) and Sy (Symax = 1.0%, Symean = 0.12%) align with those reported in local studies (Monod et al., 2018; Saplairoles et al., 2017) and field measurements in similar bedrock contexts (Dewandel et al., 2012; Maréchal et al., 2006), as well as classical textbook values for crystalline lithologies (Domenico & Schwartz, 1997; Freeze & Cherry, 1979; Singhal & Gupta, 2010). Because hydraulic head data from boreholes are typically unavailable in headwater catchments (Hrachowitz et al., 2013; Sivapalan et al., 2003), preventing traditional calibration, the proposed modeling strategy offers new opportunities (Schilling et al., 2019; Wagner et al., 2025). We provide means to estimate effective subsurface hydraulic properties solely based on surface information that are capable of representing the 3D groundwater flow processes expected in such context, including information on the effective thickness and first order heterogeneity of the aquifer (Condon et al., 2020). Indeed, it is accepted that combining observations from stream expansion/ contraction and streamflow measured at the outlet provide integrative information of the hillslope to catchment groundwater‐surface water processes to estimate effective hydraulic properties (Cornette et al., 2022; Marçais et al., 2022). This approach has particularly been developed and validated in recent works by Abhervé et al. (2023, 2024). The authors specifically found a good correlation between hydraulic conductivities derived from the model calibrated on the hydrographic network and those estimated from well tests and models calibrated with head data. We acknowledge that not considering head data for the validation of the model performance may introduce some uncertainties in the fine scale dynamics, but we demonstrate here the performance of our model to predict interannual spring intermittency and flux distributed across the catchment. Of course, well or borehole mea- surements are highly valuable; however, they often reflect small‐scale and localized conditions (Lachassagne et al., 2021; Maréchal et al., 2004; Nastev et al., 2004) that may not be representative of catchment‐scale dynamics (Carrera et al., 2005). [SOURCE: Page_19]

Moreover, in mountainous settings, drilling boreholes is costly, as sites are often difficult to access and the thick unsaturated zone requires heavy drilling equipment. In our context, model results suggest hydraulic heads reaching a maximum depth of 210 m beneath ridges, with a mean water table depth of about 44 m across the catchment over the simulation period (considering the calibration period, i.e. 1980–2010). Similar context has been either observed (de Palézieux & Loew, 2019; Gnann et al., 2025; Louis et al., 2025; Smerdon et al., 2009; Somers et al., 2019) or predicted (Carroll et al., 2024; De Graaf et al., 2015; Fan et al., 2013; Gleeson & Manning, 2008; Rapp et al., 2020; Somers & McKenzie, 2020). Overall, this calibration framework demonstrates that surface observations of stream networks and spring dynamics can serve as a powerful surrogate for regions with scarce borehole data, enabling robust estimation of catchment‐scale aquifer properties and improving our ability to predict groundwater–surface water interactions in mountain headwaters. [SOURCE: Page_19]

4.2. Perspectives for Groundwater Flow Modeling in Mountainous Headwater Catchments Although groundwater flow is modeled here with a 3D physically based approach, simplifications in concep- tualization, process representation, and parameterization were necessary. While the model accounts for first‐order heterogeneity (primarily the vertical decrease in hydraulic properties) it relies on effective parameters that hor- izontally integrate lateral heterogeneities. Subsurface features such as variations in lithology, Quaternary de- posits, or geological discontinuities (faults and fractures) are not explicitly represented. However, our results indicate good agreement with observations, suggesting that including such heterogeneity is not required given the available data. Recharge was estimated from a relatively coarse, independent coupled atmospheric–land surface model, which cannot resolve localized climatic or hydrological events. Our results indicate that the groundwater flow model satisfactorily represents seasonal streamflow variations and baseflow dynamics. In contrast, the modeling chain is less suited to capturing localized events and peak flows. In addition, the groundwater flow model does not account for certain feedback processes, such as streamflow reinfiltration, groundwater uptake through evapotranspiration, or permafrost thaw. Nevertheless, for the catchment studied here, streams are predominantly gaining, making reinfiltration of limited importance at the temporal and spatial scales considered. No rock glaciers or permafrost are present, and phreatophyte species that could strongly affect groundwater storage are not expected in this high‐ elevation setting. However, the importance of these processes should be reevaluated if the methodology is applied to other sites. [SOURCE: Page_20]

Recharge can be highly heterogeneous across the catchment, largely due to local spatial variations in cumulative effective precipitation, soil properties, and vadose zone storage capacity. The thickness of the shallower trans- missive zone is expected to varies significantly along the hillslope, that is generally thicker near the ridge and thinner near the valley bottom (Riebe et al., 2017), where incision into the densely fractured bedrock limits both infiltration and subsurface storage. In steep alpine regions in particular, numerous observations suggest that the permeability field is influenced by tectonic and topographic stresses, with fractures tending to close in valleys and open on crests (Chen et al., 2024; Miller & Dunne, 1996; Moon et al., 2017; St. Clair et al., 2015). The impacts of tectonic and gravitational stresses on permeability distribution and groundwater flow partitioning were recently investigated in Figueroa et al. (2025). The authors found that increasing lateral tectonic stress enhances shallow subsurface permeability, leading to more channelized flow and that this impact is even more pronounced on steep slopes. These results support the use of an exponential decrease in hydraulic properties with depth, which mimics the development of such channelized flow. [SOURCE: Page_20]

Characterizing the depth of the shallow transmissive zone is of high importance. Because drilling in remote mountain areas is challenging, geophysical measurements offer a valuable opportunity to estimate bedrock depth at the field scale (Callahan et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023; Flinchum et al., 2025; Gourdol et al., 2021; Lesparre et al., 2024; Olyphant et al., 2016; Pasquet et al., 2022), thereby helping to better constrain the interface between the highly fractured aquifer and the underlying low permeability bedrock. Our results are also consistent with recent modeling which shows that low‐flow dynamics are highly sensitive to the active depth of groundwater circulation and its associated storage capacity (Carroll et al., 2025; Roques et al., 2022). The model presented here provides a consistent representation of the subsurface critical zone, capturing both fast, shallow flows and slower, deeper pathways. Based on the best‐performing model calibrated against the perennial stream network, streamflow, and stream intermittency, we applied a particle‐tracking approach to compute flow‐ path lengths and residence times. Under steady‐state conditions with mean recharge derived from historical data (1980–2010), the calibrated model simulates a nested subsurface flow system dominated by local, short flow paths, while some pathlines extend to depths of up to 300 m (Figure S15a in Supporting Information S$_1$). We estimated an average residence time of ∼0.5 years considering all seepage zones across the catchment, with maximum values of up to 45 years, close to the outlet of the catchment where the Lasset Peatland (P$_1$) (Figures S15b and S15c in Supporting Information S$_1$). But questions remain on the transit times through the unsaturated zone which can be quite important in such context with thick water table depth. Groundwater dating could provide valuable data to be used for better constraining the groundwater flow partitioning in the model (Brooks et al., 2025; Thiros et al., 2023). In recent years, advanced coupled models (e.g., GSFLOW, FEFLOW, CWatM) and fully integrated surface‐ subsurface models (e.g., ParFlow, CATHY, HydroGeoSphere) have emerged in snow‐dominated and [SOURCE: Page_20]

4.3. Hydrogeological Processes and Future Evolution of Groundwater‐Dependent Streamflow Dynamics Predicted changes in groundwater flow and water table fluctuations under future climate scenarios are expected to significantly alter the spatiotemporal dynamics of wetlands and streamflow intermittence. Our results suggest that, under future RCP$_8$.5 scenarios, decreasing recharge rates will likely reduce groundwater–surface water connectivity during low‐flow periods compared to current situation. This is consistent with other large‐scale predictions that have been done on the Pyrenees region (Jaouen et al., 2025; Mimeau et al., 2024; Sauquet et al., 2021, 2025; Tramblay et al., 2021). Note that the monthly timestep used in our study may miss shorter‐term events, that is 1–3 weeks dry periods. The proportion of intermittent streams is expected to increase associated to the general decline in water table levels and the reorganization of the subsurface flow paths. This is particularly evident from the analysis of the characteristic response timescales. We found significant change from an average of 40 days under historical conditions, consistent with findings from similar crystalline lithological contexts (Abhervé et al., 2024; Guillaumot et al., 2022; Jiménez‐Martínez et al., 2013; Molénat et al., 1999; Roques et al., 2022), to 70 days at the horizon 2070–2100 (RCP$_8$.5 scenario). This reflects the impacts of the decreasing recharge on the lowering of the water tables, with two main mechanisms involved: (a) the decreasing effective transmissivity due to a lower saturation and lower permeability at depth, and (b) increasing flow path length (Figure 8b) caused by the reduction of the hydrographic network (van Meerveld et al., 2019). Particle tracking allowed us to estimate an increase in the average age of groundwater discharging to the streams due to the increasing flow path length. This is well‐documented through both modeling studies (Betterle & Bellin, 2024; Goderniaux et al., 2013) and field measurements (Warix et al., 2023), which highlight the sensitivity of travel time distribution to the actual water table position and stream network length (Prancevic & Kirch- ner, 2019). We also emphasize the critical role of geomorphological features in reshaping the evolution of groundwater surface water connectivity (Marani et al., 2001). We showed that ridge‐dominated sub‐catchments characterized by steep hillslopes and limited contributing areas (Breton Peatland (P$_2$)) are particularly sensitive to groundwater disconnection and, consequently, the reduction of baseflow. These findings highlight the need to explicitly account for landscape heterogeneity and hydrogeological processes to represent headwater systems and the ecosystems they support. This is particularly relevant when aiming at projecting their long‐term sustainability. While our current modeling framework can simulate spatially discontinuous seepage, capturing some aspects of stream intermittency, it does not resolve fine‐scale geomorphic factors like heterogeneity in hydraulic properties of the riverbed that may cause potential localized conditions of groundwater‐surface water connectivity (Botter et al., 2021).However, this is beyond the scope of this study and exceed the model's current 25 m resolution. Incorporating a detailed surface‐flow component or explicitly refining flux exchanges at the riverbed scale could address this limitation, though at the cost of increased model complexity and computational demand. [SOURCE: Page_21]

Implications of Water Availability for Ecosystem Conservation and Land Management The projected impacts of climate change on headwaters are likely to have significant consequences for down- stream water resources and the ecosystem services that depend on them (Cantonati et al., 2020; Datry, Boulton, et al., 2023, Datry, Truchy, et al., 2023; De Girolamo et al., 2017; Hanus et al., 2024; IPCC ‐ Parmesan et al., 2022; Kløve et al., 2011; Pérez‐Silos et al., 2025; Scanlon et al., 2023; Viviroli et al., 2020).
