23   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   Non-perennial river segments that recurrently cease to flow or typically dry occur in all river [SOURCE: Page_3]

25   networks and, globally, are more abundant than perennially flowing segments. Their biodiversity [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   and ecosystem processes are governed by the repeated cycles of flowing, non-flowing and dry [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   phases that, in turn, influence the ecosystem dynamics in connected perennial segments and [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   downstream waters. In this Review, we outline how the flow regime in non-perennial segments, [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   especially the dry phase, influences their biodiversity and ecosystem processes at different spatial [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   scales, from individual segments to entire river networks. Varying configurations of perennial and [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   non-perennial segments govern each river network's physical, chemical and ecological responses to [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   changes in flow regimes, especially in response to human activities. We discuss the likely future of [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   non-perennial segments from hydrological and ecological perspectives, then review the management [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   implications of the connections between perennial and non-perennial segments. We conclude by [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   suggesting five specific and actionable recommendations for research within the next 3–5 years to [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   clarify how non-perennial segments influence connected perennial waters and what this means for [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   effective management at the river network scale. Neglecting the important roles of non-perennial [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   streams compromises holistic river management. [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   Key points [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   Although rivers cover less than 2% of the Earth’s surface, they contain approximately 13% of all [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   described species and provide key ecosystem services such as provision of drinking water and food, [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   regulation of climate and opportunities for recreation^1,2. However, rivers are among the world’s [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   most threatened ecosystem types; one out of three species in these biodiversity hotspots is [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   threatened with extinction^3. River science and management have historically focused on perennial [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   segments, which flow year round, but rivers are increasingly being conceptualized as spatially [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   variable networks in which hydrological connections between perennial and non-perennial segments [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   (Box 1) support network-scale biodiversity and ecosystem functioning^4,5 (Figure 1a). [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   Non-perennial river segments (hereafter NPRs) recurrently cease to flow and most lose most or all [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   surface water (Figure 1c). They include ‘ephemeral’ or ‘intermittent’ river segments (Box 1), with [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   ‘ephemeral’ implying shorter and less-predictable flowing phases than ‘intermittent’, but no agreed [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   distinction exists. Despite efforts to classify river segments in which flows cease for varying periods [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   and with varying predictability^6–9, global consensus remains elusive because of the high temporal [SOURCE: Page_5]

73   and spatial variability within and among NPRs^10. Moreover, variability will further increase in the [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   future. In this Review, we refer to non-perennial rivers and segments, following Messager et al.4. [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   Every river network encompasses NPRs, particularly in the headwaters (Figure 1a), but sometimes [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   also substantial lengths of the lower segments. NPRs naturally constitute more than half of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   global river network length^4 (Figure 1b), a proportion that is expected to rise in some regions [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   because of climate change, land-use alteration and increased water abstraction^11,12. [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   When flowing (Figure 1c), NPRs supply water, aquatic biota, energy, nutrients and other materials [SOURCE: Page_5]

80   to connected waters, influencing their water quality, biodiversity and ecological integrity. These [SOURCE: Page_5]

81   pulsed hydrological connections are often irregular in time and space yet generate dynamic [SOURCE: Page_5]

82   transition zones between aquatic and terrestrial habitats that extend longitudinally down river [SOURCE: Page_5]

83   channels, laterally onto floodplains and vertically into the underlying groundwaters^13. [SOURCE: Page_5]

84   Despite the ubiquity of NPRs and the ecological importance of this hydrological connectivity, most [SOURCE: Page_5]

85   policies and management practices for river networks are tailored for rivers that are perennial. The [SOURCE: Page_5]

86   omission of NPRs from river management seriously risks undermining effective protection of [SOURCE: Page_5]

87   biodiversity and ecological integrity of entire river networks and their ecosystem services. Scientists [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

88    and managers need greater awareness of the importance of NPRs to biodiversity and ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_6]

89    processes in river networks. [SOURCE: Page_6]

90    In this Review, we explore key insights from research on NPRs in the context of network-scale [SOURCE: Page_6]

91    riverine connectivity. For brevity, we focus on aquatic responses to drying from segment to river [SOURCE: Page_6]

92    network scale but acknowledge that interactions with terrestrial ecosystems are also crucial in NPRs, [SOURCE: Page_6]

93    especially during dry phases. We conclude by exploring the drawbacks of excluding NPRs from [SOURCE: Page_6]

94    river network management. Our central message is that the current scientific conceptualization of [SOURCE: Page_6]

95    river networks as arrays of co-occurring perennial and NPRs must be matched with changes to river [SOURCE: Page_6]

96    management that recognize the essential importance of integrating NPRs into strategies and actions [SOURCE: Page_6]

97    that protect and, where possible, enhance network-scale biodiversity, ecosystem processes and [SOURCE: Page_6]

98    ecosystem services. [SOURCE: Page_6]

99    1. Characterizing non-perennial segments [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   NPRs are common across river networks. We now discuss their flow regimes, the types of [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   connections between perennial segments and NPRs in river networks, their distribution within [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   different networks and how such connections can alter fluxes of water, materials and organisms [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   across river networks. [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   1.1 Typology and connections [SOURCE: Page_6]

105   Flow regimes [SOURCE: Page_6]

106   The flow regime, defined by the temporal variability in the quantity and timing of discharge, [SOURCE: Page_6]

107   governs river geomorphology, water quality and ecology^14 and, thus, is crucial to the understanding [SOURCE: Page_6]

108   and management of river networks. In NPRs, the flow regime encompasses alternating flowing, [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

109   non-flowing and, in many cases, dry phases (Figure 1c, Box 1). The frequency, duration and timing [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   of each phase are powerful determinants of biodiversity, ecological integrity and ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   services, both at local scales within NPRs^15,16 and at the river-network scale^17,18. [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   Drying (Box 1) is a gradual process whose effects are separated by hydrological thresholds^19. Early [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   in the process while flow declines, surface water contracts and lateral aquatic habitats become [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   disconnected. As drying progresses, riffles and other flowing surface habitats disappear, leaving the [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   riverbed as disconnected pools (Figure 1c). Eventually, the riverbed dries, although subsurface flow [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   may continue in saturated sediments (the hyporheic zone) below the dry channel^19–21 (Figure 1c). [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   This loss of surface water is governed by the type of surface–groundwater interactions occurring at [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   the segment scale^22–24. When segments are losing surface water, the water table of the underlying [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   aquifer is generally deep and the hyporheic zone usually dries quickly^20,24, whereas in gaining [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   segments, upwelling groundwater can maintain a saturated hyporheic zone throughout the dry [SOURCE: Page_7]

121   phase^20,24. [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   During rewetting (Box 1), surface water returns to inundate the dry channels, sometimes quickly as [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   flash floods^25,26 or slowly as a rise in water table. Like all components of flow regimes, the [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   characteristics of drying and rewetting transitional phases are driven by both natural climatic and [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   geological factors and by human activities^27–29 and usually vary greatly within and between river [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   networks^30,31. The consequences of such variability remain poorly understood but are likely to be an [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   important determinant of a river network’s biodiversity and ecological integrity^18,32. [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   Perennial and non-perennial segment connections [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   Most NPRs are structurally connected to perennial segments by the spatial continuity of the fluvial [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   corridor (i.e., the channel, hyporheic and floodplain^33) (Figure 1a), reflecting their ubiquitous co- [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   occurrence in river networks globally (Figure 1b). This connectivity is well captured by the meta- [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   ecosystem perspective that considers river networks as mutually dependent arrays of perennial and [SOURCE: Page_8]

133   NPRs that are connected to adjacent aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems^34. The connected terrestrial [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   ecosystems range from riparian zones and uplands in the headwaters to floodplains downstream, and [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   the connected aquatic ecosystems encompass adjacent wetlands, lakes, subsurface groundwaters, [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   downstream reservoirs, estuaries and coastal waters (Figure 1a). [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   The spatial arrangements of perennial and NPRs and their connections are diverse, complex and [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   dynamic (Figure 2 a&b). Most river networks have naturally non-perennial headwaters that span [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   the interface between terrestrial and aquatic domains^35. Flow in headwater segments is driven by a [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   combination of surface runoff, groundwater inputs and/or meltwater and is often seasonal^17,36. NPRs [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   can also be in the downstream parts of river networks (Figure 2b). Typically, river networks that [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   flow out onto porous alluvial plains and recharge the groundwater below have downstream NPRs, [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   such as the Tagliamento River (Italy^25) and the Albarine River (France^37). Other causes of [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   downstream NPRs include excessive evaporation such as in the Diamantina River (Australia^38) or [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   human activities such as damming and water abstraction in the Colorado River (US) or Yellow [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   River (China^39). In other river networks, the upper and lower segments are perennial but the mid- [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   segments are non-perennial, such as in the Selwyn River (New Zealand^40). In arid regions, whole [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   river networks are often non-perennial, such as many rivers in northern and southwestern Africa^41 [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   and the Nordeste in Brazil^42. The varying physical settings and flow regimes of these different [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   network configurations drive physical, chemical and ecological responses to the connections [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   between NPRs and perennial segments. [SOURCE: Page_8]

152   Functional connections among river segments [SOURCE: Page_9]

153   The different functional roles played by the varying connections between perennial and NPRs in [SOURCE: Page_9]

154   space and time can be classified by the general mechanisms by which stream segments influence [SOURCE: Page_9]

155   fluxes to downstream waters^43 (Figure 2c, Supplementary 1). When fluxes of water, sediments, [SOURCE: Page_9]

156   material and organisms pass from perennial to NPRs and vice versa, they are often altered, which [SOURCE: Page_9]

157   may have contrasting effects on downstream river segments (Figure 2c). These variable effects [SOURCE: Page_9]

158   occur because fluxes of material or energy can cease, increase, decrease or be stored when they pass [SOURCE: Page_9]

159   through NPRs before entering downstream waters^43. Consequently, the functional connections [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   between perennial and NPRs usually modify the quantity and quality of material and energy moving [SOURCE: Page_9]

161   through the river networks. [SOURCE: Page_9]

162   Connections between perennial and NPRs can act as sources of material, notably when the organic [SOURCE: Page_9]

163   material accumulated during dry phases is leached during rewetting, causing high nutrient fluxes to [SOURCE: Page_9]

164   downstream waters^44 (Figure 2c). When NPRs dry, the exchange of water ceases, both vertically [SOURCE: Page_9]

165   between surface and groundwaters and longitudinally from upstream to downstream waters, [SOURCE: Page_9]

166   transforming NPRs into sinks of material^45 (Figure 2c). As dry phases progress, terrestrial leaf litter [SOURCE: Page_9]

167   from adjacent riparian zones is retained in NPRs and gradually accumulates at the bed surface^32 [SOURCE: Page_9]

168   (Figure 2c). Upon rewetting, this organic material is flushed downstream, sometimes massively^26, [SOURCE: Page_9]

169   with NPRs functioning to delay its release (Figure 2c). NPRs also offer refuges during the dry phase [SOURCE: Page_9]

170   for organisms including microbes, invertebrates and fish^46,47 (Figure 2c). Lastly, connections [SOURCE: Page_9]

171   between perennial and NPRs can transform spatial patterns in chemical parameters such as dissolved [SOURCE: Page_9]

172   oxygen when anoxic or hypoxic pulses of water from remnant pools or rewetting fronts are carried [SOURCE: Page_9]

173   downstream to connected aquatic ecosystems by rewetting flows^18,48 (Figure 2c). The types and [SOURCE: Page_9]

185   2. The ecology of non-perennial segments [SOURCE: Page_10]

186   Drying in NPRs acts as a master variable that controls local biotic communities, ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_10]

187   processes and ecosystem services. In addition, the spatial arrangement and type of connections [SOURCE: Page_10]

188   between perennial and NPRs at the river-network scale shape the ecological integrity of river [SOURCE: Page_10]

189   networks. In this section, we discuss how drying influences the ecology of NPRs at the segment [SOURCE: Page_10]

190   scale and how these effects propagate across the entire river network. [SOURCE: Page_10]

191   2.1. Non-perennial segment scale [SOURCE: Page_10]

192   From microorganisms to vertebrates and higher plants, all biotic groups respond to recurrent shifts [SOURCE: Page_10]

193   from flowing to non-flowing and dry conditions in NPRs (Figure 1c). Across biotic groups, aquatic [SOURCE: Page_10]

194   biodiversity declines steadily in response to non-perenniality as taxa lacking adaptations promoting [SOURCE: Page_10]

195   resistance or resilience to drying are lost15,16. The extent of these declines is governed by 9 [SOURCE: Page_10]

196   hydrological parameters such as the duration of non-flowing and dry phases, with longer dry phases [SOURCE: Page_11]

197   resulting in greater declines^37,46,52. For example, a ten-day increase in the dry phase in the Albarine [SOURCE: Page_11]

198   River, France, led to an additional loss of six taxa from the benthic invertebrate community and four [SOURCE: Page_11]

199   taxa from the hyporheic zone^37. An increase in the duration of the non-flowing phase from 0 to 78 [SOURCE: Page_11]

200   days reduced the survival of Coho salmon populations from 0.59 to 0.11 from tributaries of the [SOURCE: Page_11]

201   Russian River in California^46. Concurrent increases in terrestrial species richness occur as colonists [SOURCE: Page_11]

202   arrive from riparian and wider terrestrial habitats^53. For example, 22 and 12 invertebrate taxa [SOURCE: Page_11]

203   colonized the dry riverbeds of the Albarine River (France) and Oaky Creek (Australia), respectively, [SOURCE: Page_11]

204   within two months of the onset of the dry phase^53. Although flowing-phase aquatic species richness [SOURCE: Page_11]

205   at the site-scale can be considerably lower in NPRs than perennial segments^54, their contribution to [SOURCE: Page_11]

206   regional aquatic–terrestrial biodiversity can exceed those of perennial segments^51,55. [SOURCE: Page_11]

207   When flow resumes, aquatic organisms recolonize from various refuges, including upstream pools, [SOURCE: Page_11]

208   moist sediments and leaf litter^56-58. Some aquatic macroinvertebrates and diatoms have desiccation- [SOURCE: Page_11]

209   tolerant forms that can survive in moist riverbed sediments^59,60. Recovery rates and trajectories vary [SOURCE: Page_11]

210   depending on connectivity to refuges. Communities in NPRs connected to upstream perennial [SOURCE: Page_11]

211   waters are soon replenished by colonists^61 whereas community recovery in isolated NPRs may be [SOURCE: Page_11]

212   slower and more stochastic^57,62. Recovery can be modified by anthropogenic influences such as [SOURCE: Page_11]

213   fragmentation by instream barriers, which severs links between NPRs and sources of colonists in [SOURCE: Page_11]

214   perennial segments^63. Despite well-developed recovery mechanisms, differences typically persist [SOURCE: Page_11]

215   between communities in perennial and non-perennial segments^37,54. These differences can be [SOURCE: Page_11]

216   particularly pronounced and long-lasting when unprecedented dry phases occur during drought [SOURCE: Page_11]

217   events. In contrast, communities can recover rapidly, generally within a few weeks, after seasonal [SOURCE: Page_11]

218   dry phases^62,64. [SOURCE: Page_11]

219   Aquatic species carry out many fundamental ecosystem processes, and changes in aquatic [SOURCE: Page_12]

220   biodiversity related to non-perenniality therefore alter ecosystem functioning. Desiccation-tolerant [SOURCE: Page_12]

221   microorganisms at the base of food webs in natural NPRs mediate many biogeochemical functions. [SOURCE: Page_12]

222   When flow decreases, hypoxic conditions that develop in disconnected pools favor denitrification, [SOURCE: Page_12]

223   reducing nitrate concentrations^24. During dry phases, the microorganisms that make up the biofilms [SOURCE: Page_12]

224   coating the surfaces of sediment particles emit large quantities of CO265 and upon rewetting, massive [SOURCE: Page_12]

225   CO$_2$ pulses can occur from NPRs. Accounting for the global prevalence of NPRs, a single rewetting [SOURCE: Page_12]

226   event can contribute up to 10% of the daily carbon dioxide emissions from all perennial rivers and [SOURCE: Page_12]

227   streams, particularly in temperate climates^32. Recognizing the active contribution of NPRs to carbon [SOURCE: Page_12]

228   cycling – during both wet and dry phases – could improve the accuracy of local-to-global-scale [SOURCE: Page_12]

229   assessments^66. When water returns, ecosystem processes such as respiration^67, [SOURCE: Page_12]

230   nitrification/denitrification^68 and decomposition of leaf litter^69 quickly resume. [SOURCE: Page_12]

231   Due to drying-driven decreases in the functional diversity of their aquatic communities, NPRs can [SOURCE: Page_12]

232   collectively perform fewer ecosystem functions than perennial ones during flowing phases^70. These [SOURCE: Page_12]

233   declines may be mitigated by functional redundancy^71 (multiple species sharing traits), making the [SOURCE: Page_12]

234   drying-induced loss of individual species functionally inconsequential^72. But as losses accumulate, [SOURCE: Page_12]

235   the risk of losing a functionally unique species increases, potentially representing a tipping point that [SOURCE: Page_12]

236   drives the ecosystem to an alternative state. For example, by eliminating pivotal species, especially [SOURCE: Page_12]

237   predators, drying can alter the structure and functioning of food webs, potentially leading to partial [SOURCE: Page_12]

238   food-web collapse^73. Similarly, the loss of desiccation-sensitive microorganisms and invertebrate [SOURCE: Page_12]

242   2.2. River-network scale [SOURCE: Page_13]

243   The logical extension of how drying shapes the ecology of NPRs is to explore how the collective [SOURCE: Page_13]

244   effects of responses to segment-scale drying extend to river networks. Network-scale responses to [SOURCE: Page_13]

245   drying are unlikely to be simple additive effects of segment-scale responses, but are more likely a [SOURCE: Page_13]

246   result of synergistic effects of responses to segment-scale drying as they propagate through the [SOURCE: Page_13]

247   network. [SOURCE: Page_13]

248   Firstly, spatial and temporal patterns of drying vary among river networks^31,76,77 (Figure 2b). [SOURCE: Page_13]

249   Longitudinal trends related to elevation and channel form are superimposed on idiosyncratic drying [SOURCE: Page_13]

250   patterns, thwarting efforts to extrapolate segment-scale patterns or assume that ‘river continuum’ [SOURCE: Page_13]

251   predictions are realistic^17,18,40. [SOURCE: Page_13]

252   Secondly, surface and subsurface flows propagate biogeochemical and ecological influences [SOURCE: Page_13]

253   downstream during flowing phases but these transfers typically slow, cease or are amplified by [SOURCE: Page_13]

254   functional connections across hydrological phases that are likely to lead to ‘hotspots’ and ‘hot [SOURCE: Page_13]

255   moments’ of material processing along a river network^24. For example, terrestrial plant litter [SOURCE: Page_13]

256   accumulates in dry and non-flowing segments and this litter decomposes very slowly^32 (Figure 3). [SOURCE: Page_13]

257   When flow resumes in these segments, it can trigger rapid microbial decomposition of the labile [SOURCE: Page_13]

258   plant litter^32 and flush the decomposing litter downstream^26 to perennial segments as a delayed [SOURCE: Page_13]

259   subsidy of a resource that may be limiting to downstream consumers^78 (Figure 3). However, the [SOURCE: Page_13]

260   network-scale effects of non-perenniality largely depend on the spatial arrangement of NPRs within [SOURCE: Page_13]

261   the river network (Figure 3). Where NPRs are concentrated in the headwaters, in particular in [SOURCE: Page_13]

262   deciduous forested areas^35, the downstream effects of non-perenniality will be high seasonal inputs [SOURCE: Page_13]

263   of unprocessed litter (Figure 3) that can reduce downstream water quality^79 or cause technical [SOURCE: Page_13]

264   problems for dam intakes80. In contrast, where NPRs are in downstream sections of river networks, 12 [SOURCE: Page_13]

265   which are typically more autotrophic and less dependent on terrestrial litter inputs from adjacent [SOURCE: Page_14]

266   riparian zones, lower fluxes of unprocessed litter are expected in downstream river network [SOURCE: Page_14]

267   segments while headwaters could become hotspots of carbon cycling (Figure 3). [SOURCE: Page_14]

268   Synchrony (the degree of concurrent change across spatially distinct segments or populations) can [SOURCE: Page_14]

269   describe how local responses propagate at the river network scale. Synchrony can be enhanced by [SOURCE: Page_14]

270   connectivity (upstream dispersal, advective transport) between segments or when a driver such as [SOURCE: Page_14]

271   drying simultaneously affects multiple segments^81. By reducing longitudinal connectivity of flowing [SOURCE: Page_14]

272   water, the non-perenniality of river segments can reverberate throughout a river network and [SOURCE: Page_14]

273   desynchronize, for example, diel dissolved oxygen fluctuations 18 or the recovery of biological [SOURCE: Page_14]

274   communities during flowing phases^17. When flow resumes in NPRs, hydrological connectivity is [SOURCE: Page_14]

275   restored, promoting network-scale synchronization of such fluctuations and processes^18. [SOURCE: Page_14]

276   Widespread non-perenniality in river networks can also synchronize dynamics across populations, [SOURCE: Page_14]

277   reducing regional metapopulation stability and increasing the risk of regional extinctions^82,83. The [SOURCE: Page_14]

278   dynamics of aggregate stream systems are typically less variable than their individual contributing [SOURCE: Page_14]

279   segments^84,85 because combining variable, asynchronous contributions from many segments has a [SOURCE: Page_14]

280   stabilizing effect (the ‘portfolio concept’84). Therefore, when aggregated in river networks, the [SOURCE: Page_14]

281   variation in flow regimes between perennial and NPRs contributes to persistence of regional [SOURCE: Page_14]

282   biodiversity and thus stable ecosystem functioning and associated availability of ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_14]

283   services^83. [SOURCE: Page_14]

284   At the river-network scale, NPRs substantially increase the beta diversity (regional variability in [SOURCE: Page_14]

285   community composition between locations and/or times) of aquatic communities, because of the [SOURCE: Page_14]

286   simultaneous coexistence of different successional stages at the river network scale^17,39,86 (Figure 4). [SOURCE: Page_14]

287   Depending on the spatial arrangement of NPRs and perennial segments within the river network, 13 [SOURCE: Page_14]

288   communities in the former can comprise a subset of the taxa inhabiting the latter , notably when [SOURCE: Page_15]

289   NPRs are downstream of perennial segments acting as a source of coloniits^15 (Figure 4). [SOURCE: Page_15]

290   Alternatively, when non-perenniality is concentrated upstream, the composition of their biological [SOURCE: Page_15]

291   communities may be more variable in space or time compared to their downstream perennial [SOURCE: Page_15]

292   counterparts^86,87 (Figure 4). This variability is because recolonization from downstream perennial [SOURCE: Page_15]

293   segments is limited by the directional flow down river networks, promoting alternative sources of [SOURCE: Page_15]

294   colonists in the landscape such as perennial waterbodies and the underlying hyporheic zone. [SOURCE: Page_15]

295   This integration of NPRs into biodiversity assessment of river networks reveals the importance of [SOURCE: Page_15]

296   dispersal, a regional process which can dominate over the local process of species-sorting during [SOURCE: Page_15]

297   rewetting phases^17,88,89. The spatial extent of drying influences the access to and from refuges in the [SOURCE: Page_15]

298   network during the dry periods and thus controls the dispersal of organisms and overall [SOURCE: Page_15]

299   metacommunity dynamics^17,90,91. However, the relative contribution of species dispersal in shaping [SOURCE: Page_15]

300   riverine biodiversity patterns is highly context-dependent and extends to adjacent habitats such as [SOURCE: Page_15]

301   riparian zones^56, hyporheic zones and groundwaters^92 and downstream ecosystems^93. [SOURCE: Page_15]

302   2.3 The future of river networks [SOURCE: Page_15]

303   Changes to non-perennial flow regimes: widespread and increasing [SOURCE: Page_15]

304   The spatial extent of NPRs in global river networks has increased since 198011,94,95 (Figure 5a & b). [SOURCE: Page_15]

305   This increase is predicted to continue^12,42,96, driven in part by climatic trends such as rising [SOURCE: Page_15]

306   temperatures and associated increases in evaporation, changing precipitation patterns, and the [SOURCE: Page_15]

307   increasing occurrence of drought in many parts of the world^97–99. For example, previously perennial [SOURCE: Page_15]

308   rivers in Europe and China dried for the first time during the severe droughts that began in [SOURCE: Page_15]

309   2022100,101. In addition, intensifying use of water resources, including surface and groundwater [SOURCE: Page_15]

310   abstraction, storage and diversion, are driving shifts from perennial to artificially non-perennial [SOURCE: Page_15]

14 [SOURCE: Page_15]

311   flow^39,100. For example, in Kansas, USA, widespread irrigation from the High Plains aquifer since [SOURCE: Page_16]

312   the mid-1900s has lowered the regional water table, sometimes by more than 50 m, shifting rivers [SOURCE: Page_16]

313   and streams from naturally gaining and perennially flowing to artificially losing and non-perennial [SOURCE: Page_16]

314   flow^102,103. [SOURCE: Page_16]

315   The predicted increase in the spatial extent of NPRs represents a network-scale decline in aquatic [SOURCE: Page_16]

316   habitat availability and hydrological connectivity which will alter riverine biodiversity, with [SOURCE: Page_16]

317   consequences for ecosystem functioning and services. For example, a decrease in the availability [SOURCE: Page_16]

318   and quality of wet refuges that support aquatic organisms during dry phases will synchronize [SOURCE: Page_16]

319   biological responses to drying, reducing metacommunity resilience and local community recovery [SOURCE: Page_16]

320   after flow resumes^83. [SOURCE: Page_16]

321   Dry phases are getting longer, drier and more frequent [SOURCE: Page_16]

322   Flow regime characteristics such as the frequency, duration, severity and timing of flowing, non- [SOURCE: Page_16]

323   flowing and dry phases are also changing, as is the rate of change between transitional drying and [SOURCE: Page_16]

324   rewetting phases. Despite considerable regional variability in the evidence for such changes to [SOURCE: Page_16]

325   date^11,95 (Figure 5a & b), there is high confidence that changes will occur in the near future. These [SOURCE: Page_16]

326   predicted changes include increases in dry-phase duration and, based on studies of future drought [SOURCE: Page_16]

327   characteristics, increases in dry-phase frequency, severity^98 and rates of onset of both dry phases and [SOURCE: Page_16]

328   flow resumption^104. Inherent within the predicted increase in dry-phase duration is the earlier onset [SOURCE: Page_16]

329   and/or later cessation of dry phases in NPRs with seasonal flow regimes, as well as an increased co- [SOURCE: Page_16]

330   occurrence of dry phases and extreme climatic events, particularly heatwaves^105–107. [SOURCE: Page_16]

15 [SOURCE: Page_16]

331   In the context of emerging megadroughts^108,109, dry phases could continue uninterrupted for years in [SOURCE: Page_17]

332   NPRs that currently have seasonal flow regimes. Deglaciation and snow loss are expected to reduce [SOURCE: Page_17]

333   summer flows, resulting in shifts to non-perennial flow^110,111. Conversely, warmer winters with [SOURCE: Page_17]

334   greater snowmelt and glacial melting could cause NPRs to become perennial at northern latitudes^42. [SOURCE: Page_17]

335   Biological responses to future changes in non-perennial flow regimes [SOURCE: Page_17]

336   How will biological communities in NPRs respond to future changes in flow regime characteristics? [SOURCE: Page_17]

337   An increase in dry-phase duration and severity (caused by a lack of precipitation and/or high [SOURCE: Page_17]

338   temperatures and manifesting as reduced in-channel water availability) could reduce the survival of [SOURCE: Page_17]

339   desiccation-tolerant life stages of aquatic organisms within the riverbed sediments^59. An increase in [SOURCE: Page_17]

340   dry-phase duration and frequency in NPRs could eliminate desiccation-sensitive species without [SOURCE: Page_17]

341   enabling colonization by tolerant equivalents^71 (Figure 5c). Faster wet-to-dry transitions could [SOURCE: Page_17]

342   shorten the time between environmental cues that trigger insect metamorphosis and its completion, [SOURCE: Page_17]

343   reducing the emergence of adults^112. Rapid-onset rewetting phases that wash insects, crustaceans, [SOURCE: Page_17]

344   amphibians or fish straight from refuges to downstream segments could reduce local community [SOURCE: Page_17]

345   recovery rates^113–115. Both earlier dry-phase onset and later dry-phase termination could reduce [SOURCE: Page_17]

346   successful completion of aquatic stages of life-cycles by riverine animals. For example, earlier onset [SOURCE: Page_17]

347   of drying could reduce the proportion of aquatic juvenile insects that emerge as terrestrial adults in [SOURCE: Page_17]

348   time to avoid a dry phase^112. Later dry-phase termination could prevent egg-laying behaviors by [SOURCE: Page_17]

349   species that oviposit on water^116. Distances and connectivity to perennial refuges in the landscape [SOURCE: Page_17]

350   may determine post-drying community composition^83,117. In cases of shifts from perennial to non- [SOURCE: Page_17]

351   perennial flow regimes, biological responses might be particularly dramatic because species may [SOURCE: Page_17]

352   lack adaptations to drying. However, if NPRs are abundant in a river network, they may provide [SOURCE: Page_17]

353   colonists adapted to the newly non-perennial conditions^83,100. [SOURCE: Page_17]

16 [SOURCE: Page_17]

354   In terms of ecosystem processes, biofilms generally recover within a few days upon flow [SOURCE: Page_18]

355   resumption, from dormant forms and through drift^118. Some ecosystem processes, such as primary [SOURCE: Page_18]

356   production or ecosystem respiration, are therefore highly resilient to drying. As such, natural NPRs [SOURCE: Page_18]

357   experiencing longer or more frequent dry periods might not be severely affected in the near future, [SOURCE: Page_18]

358   although shifts towards greater heterotrophy could occur^119,120. Other processes could be more [SOURCE: Page_18]

359   dramatically affected by increasing drying, such as the decomposition of terrestrial leaf litter, [SOURCE: Page_18]

360   because they are more dependent on macroorganisms^100. In situations where once-perennial [SOURCE: Page_18]

361   segments become non-perennial, biodiversity is predicted to respond strongly, with multiple [SOURCE: Page_18]

362   cascading effects on ecosystem processes, although these effects will depend on the functional [SOURCE: Page_18]

363   redundancy of a community and the types of organisms involved^100,119,121. Finally, where increasing [SOURCE: Page_18]

364   non-perenniality is coupled with other human-induced stressors (channelisation, contamination, [SOURCE: Page_18]

365   water abstraction), how ecosystem processes respond to drying could be highly complex. [SOURCE: Page_18]

366   In most cases, a reduction in aquatic biodiversity is the initial ecological effect of predicted future [SOURCE: Page_18]

367   increases in drying. These taxonomic changes have functional consequences, altering ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_18]

368   processes and associated ecosystem services. Although biological communities have, to date, proven [SOURCE: Page_18]

369   resilient to drying, recovering within weeks to a few years even from rare dry phases in NPRs^122,123, [SOURCE: Page_18]

370   predicted future changes in riverine flow regimes have increasing potential to tip ecosystems to new, [SOURCE: Page_18]

371   functionally compromised stable states. For example, decreases in flowing-phase duration and [SOURCE: Page_18]

372   frequency could interact with concurrent stressors such as artificial enrichment by inorganic [SOURCE: Page_18]

373   nutrients, to shift aquatic vegetation communities from habitat-forming plants to filamentous algae. [SOURCE: Page_18]

374   This change would alter basal food resources and habitat availability for invertebrates and fish, [SOURCE: Page_18]

375   triggering trophic cascades that extend through food webs. In addition, plants act as ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_18]

376   engineers that alter sediment dynamics, and their loss could thus alter river shape^124,125. In contrast, [SOURCE: Page_18]

17 [SOURCE: Page_18]

377   the ecological consequences of region-specific shifts towards perenniality could increase network- [SOURCE: Page_19]

378   scale hydrological connectivity, promoting biotic dispersal and thus homogenizing [SOURCE: Page_19]

379   communities^89,126,127. [SOURCE: Page_19]

380   3. Non-perenniality in river network management [SOURCE: Page_19]

381   The effects of alternating flowing, non-flowing and dry phases on water quality, ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_19]

382   processes, biodiversity and ecosystem services at the river network scale mean that management [SOURCE: Page_19]

383   expectations of naturally NPRs must differ from those of perennial segments^92,128,129. In particular, [SOURCE: Page_19]

384   effective management, conservation and restoration of river networks must explicitly recognize [SOURCE: Page_19]

385   perennial and NPRs and their connections. Below, we discuss management implications generated [SOURCE: Page_19]

386   by the connections between perennial and NPRs and how management should account for NPRs in [SOURCE: Page_19]

387   river networks. [SOURCE: Page_19]

388   3.1 Management implications of non-perenniality [SOURCE: Page_19]

389   Understanding the connections between perennial and NPRs, and with other connected waters [SOURCE: Page_19]

390   (lakes, reservoirs, wetlands, aquifers, estuaries and in coastal areas) is a crucial step towards [SOURCE: Page_19]

391   integrated management of river networks. The processes enabled by these connections have major, [SOURCE: Page_19]

392   network-scale implications for biodiversity conservation, water quality management, risk mitigation [SOURCE: Page_19]

393   suh as floods and the availability of ecosystem services. [SOURCE: Page_19]

394   Dry-phase refuges located in NPRs are crucial to maintain freshwater biodiversity at the river [SOURCE: Page_19]

395   network scale^46,130. For example, isolated pools maintained in intermittent streams in coastal [SOURCE: Page_19]

396   Oregon^131 and in the Russian River in California^46 during dry phases provide refuges that promote [SOURCE: Page_19]

397   the survival of juvenile Coho salmon (Oncorhynchus kisutch) (Figure 6a). In the Russian River, the [SOURCE: Page_19]

398   mean cumulative survival of salmon in these pools reached 50%46. The deterioration or loss of these 18 [SOURCE: Page_19]

399   habitats, together with the lack of access to them due to fragmentation by anthropogenic barriers^132, [SOURCE: Page_20]

400   reduces fish populations and thus impacts subsistence fishers^46. Management efforts for biodiversity [SOURCE: Page_20]

401   conservation may require adaptation to prioritize the identification and protection of these [SOURCE: Page_20]

402   refuges^133,134. [SOURCE: Page_20]

403   NPRs can attenuate floods and act as flood protection zones in the catchment (Figure 6b). For [SOURCE: Page_20]

404   example, the dry channel of the ephemeral river Rambla de Nogalte, SE Spain, efficiently absorbed [SOURCE: Page_20]

405   flash flood waters and sediments – except where walls and embankments had been built^135. [SOURCE: Page_20]

406   Infiltration potential (the extent to which water can enter the sediments) in NPRs should be [SOURCE: Page_20]

407   integrated in flood risk management at the river network scale by identifying priority zones where [SOURCE: Page_20]

408   this function is maximized. The high infiltration capacity of dry riverbeds in NPRs can limit [SOURCE: Page_20]

409   evaporative losses and facilitate groundwater recharge (Figure 6c). For example, flooding after a [SOURCE: Page_20]

410   rain event in the ephemeral Sand River, Kenya, recharged the groundwater level in only 1.5 [SOURCE: Page_20]

411   hours^136. Similarly, 49% of the monsoon flood volume from ephemeral Río Puerco basin, New [SOURCE: Page_20]

412   Mexico, USA, recharged the aquifer and the rest entered a downstream reservoir^45. The resultant [SOURCE: Page_20]

413   availability of water resources can be a major benefit for people living and depending on these water [SOURCE: Page_20]

414   resources in arid and semi-arid regions. [SOURCE: Page_20]

415   Nutrients released from NPRs during flowing phases can subsidize downstream connected waters [SOURCE: Page_20]

416   and support biodiversity and ecosystem functioning downstream, enhancing services provided by [SOURCE: Page_20]

417   freshwater and marine fisheries (Figure 6d). For example, the timing of a nutrient pulse from the [SOURCE: Page_20]

418   ephemeral Santa Clara River to at least 20 km from the California coast during the 1998 floods was [SOURCE: Page_20]

419   key to supporting marine productivity at a time when nutrient inputs from oceanic upwelling were [SOURCE: Page_20]

420   less available^137. [SOURCE: Page_20]

19 [SOURCE: Page_20]

421   Non-perenniality has major consequences for provisioning, regulating and cultural ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_21]

422   services made available by river networks. River drying prevents surface water abstraction for uses [SOURCE: Page_21]

423   including crop irrigation and public water supply^138. However, in many regions, a substantial [SOURCE: Page_21]

424   fraction of public water supply comes from NPRs^139 or underlying groundwaters^140. Surface water [SOURCE: Page_21]

425   loss is also likely to reduce fish populations that support subsistence fishers^46. Regulating services [SOURCE: Page_21]

426   such as water purification, flood mitigation and climate regulation are all compromised by drying^141. [SOURCE: Page_21]

427   For example, drying eliminates desiccation-sensitive microorganisms from biofilms and slows [SOURCE: Page_21]

428   assimilation of inorganic nutrients (including anthropogenic pollutants) after flow resumes^142. [SOURCE: Page_21]

429   Drying also affects cultural services of river networks by limiting water-associated activities such as [SOURCE: Page_21]

430   boating while creating opportunities for new activities such as rambling^138,143,144. These impacts on [SOURCE: Page_21]

431   cultural services depend on people’s perceptions of drying^145,146 which has a major effect on how [SOURCE: Page_21]

432   river networks with extensive NPRs are likely to be managed. [SOURCE: Page_21]

433   3.2 Improving river network management [SOURCE: Page_21]

434   Human activities alter flow regimes in NPRs with major implications for functional connections and [SOURCE: Page_21]

435   adjacent perennial segments. However, compared to perennial segments, these NPRs are seldom as [SOURCE: Page_21]

436   well-protected by legislation from the impacts of human activities that might alter their flow^100,147 [SOURCE: Page_21]

437   (Figure 7) because management practices have yet to be adapted to match conceptual developments [SOURCE: Page_21]

438   in river science that recognize the ecological importance of non-perenniality. Such adaptation in [SOURCE: Page_21]

439   management practices would necessarily extend to other connected waters, such as floodplain [SOURCE: Page_21]

440   wetlands, lakes and alluvial groundwaters, as well as the estuaries and near-shore coastal waters [SOURCE: Page_21]

441   associated with river networks that drain to the sea. [SOURCE: Page_21]

442   River network management can be improved by applying insights from scientific research [SOURCE: Page_21]

443   demonstrating the implications of the functional connections between perennial and NPRs in 20 [SOURCE: Page_21]

444   spatially variable river networks. For example, if the legislative protection of perennial segments in [SOURCE: Page_22]

445   many countries were automatically extended to NPRs and their catchments, it could likely reduce [SOURCE: Page_22]

446   the impacts of human activities that threaten biodiversity and ecosystem function of river networks [SOURCE: Page_22]

447   and their connected ecosystems (Figure 7). Some nations already have limited legislative protection [SOURCE: Page_22]

448   for NPRs. In Australia, independent scientific assessment of the potential environmental impacts of [SOURCE: Page_22]

449   large coal mining and coal seam gas developments on water-dependent biota and ecosystems in [SOURCE: Page_22]

450   perennial segments and NPRs in the disturbance footprint is expressly required under legislation (the [SOURCE: Page_22]

451   2013 ‘water trigger’ amendment of the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act [SOURCE: Page_22]

452   1999148). This example, from a single country, demonstrates how legislation can protect valued [SOURCE: Page_22]

453   natural assets in both perennial and non-perennial waterbodies. [SOURCE: Page_22]

454   Major obstacles need to be overcome to extend legislative protection for NPRs^147,149. In Europea, [SOURCE: Page_22]

455   NPRs are largely omitted from conservation, restoration and biomonitoring^153 by the Water [SOURCE: Page_22]

456   Framework Directive (WFD)150. For example, the WFD only recognizes NPRs in one 'river type' in [SOURCE: Page_22]

457   some countries in the Mediterranean region. Elsewhere, where NPRs are less prevalent but [SOURCE: Page_22]

458   nonetheless diverse and extensive such as France^151, the UK55 and the Czech Republic^152, river [SOURCE: Page_22]

459   typologies used to implement the WFD do not distinguish between perennial and NPRs. In some [SOURCE: Page_22]

460   cases, such as France, there are attempts to remove NPRs from national? legislation (Figure 7h). [SOURCE: Page_22]

461   Similarly, in the US, the Clean Water Act does not specify NPRs as Waters of the United States, [SOURCE: Page_22]

462   exposing them to impacts such as dredging and waste dumping. [SOURCE: Page_22]

463   Another obstacle is the public perception of NPRs in river networks as less valuable than flowing [SOURCE: Page_22]

464   waters^145,146,154. Consequently, NPRs are often overlooked in restoration and conservation plans^146 [SOURCE: Page_22]

465   and there is little appreciation of their ecosystem services^141,144. Even in Australia, where the need [SOURCE: Page_22]

466   for separate water quality guidelines for NPRs is accepted^155, there is limited appreciation of the [SOURCE: Page_22]

21 [SOURCE: Page_22]

467   importance of connectivity between perennial and NPRs in influencing water quality. Scientists [SOURCE: Page_23]

468   need to communicate the importance of ecosystem services provided by NPRs to the general public, [SOURCE: Page_23]

469   river managers, politicians, policy makers and other stakeholders. Such evidence-informed actions [SOURCE: Page_23]

470   may include producing fact sheets and policy briefs, using social media and conducting [SOURCE: Page_23]

471   collaborative research projects with citizen scientists, river managers and stakeholders. For example, [SOURCE: Page_23]

472   the open-source smartphone application DryRivers enables both citizen and professional scientists to [SOURCE: Page_23]

473   map NPRs throughout Europe^156 and has substantially increased public appreciation of the extent of [SOURCE: Page_23]

474   NPRs. [SOURCE: Page_23]

475   Logistically, there are serious limitations in fundamental hydrological data and hydrographic [SOURCE: Page_23]

476   mapping for NPRs in river networks. Stream gauges that quantify flow and describe flow regime [SOURCE: Page_23]

477   components of a stream segment are typically placed along larger perennial streams and fail to [SOURCE: Page_23]

478   capture flow regimes of NPRs^157. Most available maps are based on static, low-resolution surveys [SOURCE: Page_23]

479   and cartography that omit many headwater NPRs^35,158. Despite increasing efforts at various scales to [SOURCE: Page_23]

480   statistically 4,159–161 and mechanistically model^162,163 distributions and flow regimes of NPRs, such [SOURCE: Page_23]

481   efforts are hampered by the scant stream gauge and groundwater-level data, which amplifies [SOURCE: Page_23]

482   uncertainty over large spatial extents 4,161. Further development and refinement of multi-platform [SOURCE: Page_23]

483   remote-sensing technology could be combined with modeling approaches that target stream gauging [SOURCE: Page_23]

484   or field observations to reduce bias and fill gaps, enabling the production of hydrographic maps that [SOURCE: Page_23]

485   better reflect the dynamic connections between all segments in river networks. [SOURCE: Page_23]

486   Another obstacle to more effective management of connected perennial and NPRs in river networks [SOURCE: Page_23]

487   is the limited availability of tools and approaches that perform well in both segment types. As in [SOURCE: Page_23]

488   river sciences, most river management tools have been developed primarily or exclusively for [SOURCE: Page_23]

489   perennial segments^129,164,165 and typically perform poorly in NPRs. For example, biomonitoring [SOURCE: Page_23]

22 [SOURCE: Page_23]

490   indices used to indicate river health are rarely able to disentangle the effects of drying from those of [SOURCE: Page_24]

491   stressors associated with human activities^129,164,166,167. Functional traits^167, metasystem [SOURCE: Page_24]

492   approaches^168, molecular tools^169 and terrestrial and semi-aquatic species^170,171 have the potential to [SOURCE: Page_24]

493   enhance assessment of NPR health. Developing common tools and approaches that are equally [SOURCE: Page_24]

494   effective in both perennial and NPRs would encourage river managers to include both types of [SOURCE: Page_24]

495   segments in river network biomonitoring. For some NPRs, specialized tools may not be [SOURCE: Page_24]

496   required^129,172 (but see 92,166,170); effective biomonitoring could be achieved by timing monitoring [SOURCE: Page_24]

497   periods to coincide with flowing phases, and by setting appropriate expectations of the values of [SOURCE: Page_24]

498   biomonitoring indices used to indicate ecological integrity, to match those in natural NPRs rather [SOURCE: Page_24]

499   than perennial ones. [SOURCE: Page_24]

501   4. Summary and future perspectives [SOURCE: Page_24]

502   Repeated cycles of flowing, non-flowing and, in particular, dry phases govern the biodiversity and [SOURCE: Page_24]

503   ecosystem processes of NPRs, in turn influencing ecosystem dynamics in connected perennial [SOURCE: Page_24]

504   segments and downstream waters. NPR can function as sources, sinks and refuges for water, energy, [SOURCE: Page_24]

505   nutrients and organisms, and can delay and transform such ecosystem components, thus governing [SOURCE: Page_24]

506   their fluxes across these connections. We contend that scientific recognition of the importance of [SOURCE: Page_24]

507   these hydrological connections between perennial and NPRs in spatially variable river networks [SOURCE: Page_24]

508   must be matched with a shift in river management. To facilitate such a shift, policy developments [SOURCE: Page_24]

509   are needed to extend the legislation that protects perennial rivers to include NPRs. [SOURCE: Page_24]

510   We identify five specific and actionable research within the next 3-5 years to further characterize [SOURCE: Page_24]

511   how NPRs influence connected perennial segments and what this means for effective management [SOURCE: Page_24]

512   at the river network scale: [SOURCE: Page_24]

23 [SOURCE: Page_24]

513      1. Identify how different functional connections affect fluxes of materials, organisms and water [SOURCE: Page_25]

514          from NPRs to perennial segments in different parts of river networks (e.g. upstream [SOURCE: Page_25]

515          headwaters vs downstream segments). One approach is to use experimental manipulations to [SOURCE: Page_25]

516          demonstrate causal mechanisms. Such experiments should explore multiple fluxes [SOURCE: Page_25]

517          concurrently due to their interacting effects. [SOURCE: Page_25]

518      2. Characterize how physical and hydrological features influence different types of fluxes. For [SOURCE: Page_25]

519          example, how different are fluxes and their effects in small ephemeral NPRs compared to [SOURCE: Page_25]

520          larger intermittent ones. [SOURCE: Page_25]

521      3. Evaluate how the collective effects of different functional connections affect ecosystem [SOURCE: Page_25]

522          services provided by whole river networks. For example, lagged connections may nullify or [SOURCE: Page_25]

975   Box 1: Glossary [SOURCE: Page_45]

976   Dry phase. In a non-perennial river segment, a period of time with no spatially continuous flowing 977   or non-flowing surface water, although isolated surface-water pools and subsurface water may be 978   present. [SOURCE: Page_45]

979   Drying phase. In a non-perennial river segment, the transitional period between a flowing or non- 980   flowing phase and a dry phase, during which most or all surface water is lost [SOURCE: Page_45]

981   Ephemeral. A non-perennial flow regime in which water only flows in response to rainfall events, 982   and flowing phases are thus unpredictable and typically short (hours to weeks). [SOURCE: Page_45]

983   Flow cessation. The point in time at which surface water ceases to flow from upstream to 984   downstream in a non-perennial segment. [SOURCE: Page_45]

985   Flowing phase. In a non-perennial river segment, a period of time in which water flows from 986   upstream to downstream. [SOURCE: Page_45]

987   Intermittent. A non-perennial flow regime, often seasonal, that is typically characterized by long 988   flowing phases (usually multiple months) and shorter dry phases. [SOURCE: Page_45]

989   Non-flowing phase. In a non-perennial river segment, a period of time when spatially continuous 990   non-flowing (still or lentic) surface water is present. [SOURCE: Page_45]

991   Non-perennial. Non-perennial river segments are those in which surface water recurrently stops 992   flowing. These segments lose all or most of their surface water. [SOURCE: Page_45]

993   Perennial. Perennial river segments are those in which surface water never stops flowing. [SOURCE: Page_45]

994   Rewetting phase. In a non-perennial river segment, the transitional period between a dry phase and [SOURCE: Page_45]

995   a flowing or non-flowing phase. [SOURCE: Page_45]
