### 1.    Introduction

In West Africa, and particularly in the semi-arid Sahel region, ponds and lakes play a [SOURCE: Page_3]

crucial role for the local population and their livestock (Diop, 2007). Temporary water [SOURCE: Page_3]

bodies are often the primary water supply for human and animal consumption, along with [SOURCE: Page_3]

bores and wells that are periodically made available by the local or national authorities [SOURCE: Page_3]

(Diop et al., 2004). Open water surfaces also enable vital ecological functions and provide [SOURCE: Page_3]

the necessary natural habitats for a wide range of fauna. However, these are also sites with [SOURCE: Page_3]

dense human and livestock populations, which favors the development and transmission of [SOURCE: Page_3]

infectious and parasitic diseases. The biological diversity and resources of these fragile [SOURCE: Page_3]

aquatic ecosystems are subject to various natural (recurrent drought) or anthropogenic [SOURCE: Page_3]

(overexploitation, dams, pollution, drainage) threats that also need to be monitored. [SOURCE: Page_3]

However, it is considered particularly challenging to inventory these water bodies as they [SOURCE: Page_3]

are generally small, numerous, temporary, and spread over large and often poorly [SOURCE: Page_3]

accessible areas. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Data acquired by Earth Observation systems covering a wide range of spectral, spatial, and [SOURCE: Page_3]

temporal characteristics can be used for locating these ponds over large areas. For instance, [SOURCE: Page_3]

the Near Infra-Red (NIR) band is theoretically well-suited for detecting open water surfaces [SOURCE: Page_3]

from optical images, due to the strong water absorption in the NIR range (Verdin, 1996). [SOURCE: Page_3]

However, because of some complicating water characteristics such as turbidity and/or the [SOURCE: Page_3]

presence of aquatic vegetation (seaweed, duckweed, and others), the NIR band alone is not [SOURCE: Page_3]

sufficient to properly distinguish open water surfaces (Puech, 1994). Indices were therefore [SOURCE: Page_3]

developed combining the NIR band with other bands, such as the Normalized Difference [SOURCE: Page_3]

Water Index as defined by Gao et al. (1996) (NDWI$_1$) and the Normalized Difference [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

International journal of applied earth observation and geoinformation [SOURCE: Page_4]

Water Index as defined by Mc Feeters et al (1996) (NDWI 2). The former is a combination [SOURCE: Page_4]

of NIR and Green (G) bands, while the latter combines NIR and Middle Infrared (MIR). [SOURCE: Page_4]

The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) initially defined for vegetation [SOURCE: Page_4]

studies (Townshend, 1986; Tucker, 1979) was also proven useful for detecting water and [SOURCE: Page_4]

silt-laden open water from lateritic soils (Caloz et al., 1996). Lastly, the Modified [SOURCE: Page_4]

Normalized Difference Water Index (MNDWI) was derived from the NDWI 2 by the use of [SOURCE: Page_4]

MIR instead of NIR (Xu, 2006). The negative of the MNDWI has also been used in other [SOURCE: Page_4]

studies, and is called the Normalized Humidity Index in (Clandillon et al., 1995) or [SOURCE: Page_4]

Normalized Difference Pond Index (NDPI) in (Lacaux et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_4]

A method using NDVI and NDWI$_2$ indices was developed by the Global Environment [SOURCE: Page_4]

Monitoring Unit of the European Joint Research Centre to map temporary ponds of about [SOURCE: Page_4]

100 ha in size in Sahelian areas with SPOT$_4$-Vegetation (Haas et al., 2006). Puech (1996) [SOURCE: Page_4]

showed that SPOT$_4$-XS images with 20 m pixel resolution could be used for estimating [SOURCE: Page_4]

pond areas (> 10 ha) in Niger with 95% accuracy, and their water volume with 70% [SOURCE: Page_4]

accuracy. Using high spatial resolution data from SPOT$_5$-HRG (10 m pixel resolution), [SOURCE: Page_4]

Lacaux et al (2007) proposed the NDPI to detect small ponds of 0.5 ha. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Several studies have also reported the potential for producing time series images by coarse- [SOURCE: Page_4]

scale satellite sensors like AVHRR (Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer), MODIS [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer), and SPOT-Vegetation for vegetation [SOURCE: Page_4]

monitoring (Beck et al., 2006; Boles et al., 2004; Maignan, 2008; McCloy and Lucht, [SOURCE: Page_4]

2004). Nevertheless, there are relatively few such studies on water body monitoring, as [SOURCE: Page_4]

most of them monitor large water areas and are concerned with either flood monitoring [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Barton and Bathols, 1989; Sandholt et al., 2003) or water storage in large lakes (Dingzhi P. [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

International journal of applied earth observation and geoinformation [SOURCE: Page_5]

et al., 2005). The spatio-temporal dynamics of large water bodies can be monitored every [SOURCE: Page_5]

10 days using SPOT-Vegetation images time series (Haas et al., 2006), whereas the follow- [SOURCE: Page_5]

up of small ponds derived from high spatial resolution images was only possible with 5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

images/year (Lacaux et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_5]

When facing the problem of detecting and monitoring temporary ponds over large areas, [SOURCE: Page_5]

the task of choosing among different remote sensing options in terms of spatial and [SOURCE: Page_5]

temporal resolutions, spectral indices, or methodological approaches can be quite [SOURCE: Page_5]

challenging. A trade-off between spatial and temporal resolutions is often inevitable, but [SOURCE: Page_5]

can be based only on a priori reasoning due to the lack of appropriate comparative studies. [SOURCE: Page_5]

The objective of this study is to address this issue by reviewing available methods for the [SOURCE: Page_5]

selection of optical sensors to detect, map, and monitor water bodies in arid areas. Methods [SOURCE: Page_5]

built on radiometric, spatial, and multi-temporal characteristics are applied using different [SOURCE: Page_5]

optical remote sensing datasets from the same study area, which is representative of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

West African Sahel region, and then comparatively assessed. [SOURCE: Page_5]

### 3.    Method

3.1 Spectral analysis [SOURCE: Page_7]

We used the ETM+ image (radiance values) to assess the capacity of different spectral [SOURCE: Page_7]

indices to correctly map the ponds within our study area. Indeed, with six spectral bands, [SOURCE: Page_7]

the ETM+ sensor enabled us to compare the main water indices described in the scientific [SOURCE: Page_7]

literature (Table 2). [SOURCE: Page_7]

(Table 2 here) [SOURCE: Page_7]

The 98 GPS-located ponds were used as ground-truth reference (“pond pixels”). One [SOURCE: Page_7]

hundred additional pixels were randomly selected outside of the ponds (“non-pond pixels”) [SOURCE: Page_7]

using Geographic Information System (GIS) functionalities (GIS software: ESRI [SOURCE: Page_7]

ArcGISTM). [SOURCE: Page_7]

The predictive accuracy of the indices was assessed using the ROC (Receiver Operating [SOURCE: Page_7]

Characteristic) curve method (Park et al., 2004). The ROC curve represents variations in [SOURCE: Page_7]

the sensitivity (% of pond-pixels properly classified) and the specificity (% of non-pond [SOURCE: Page_7]

pixels properly classified) of an index with varying threshold-value. The AUC (Area Under [SOURCE: Page_7]

Curve) of the ROC curve and its 95% confidence interval was calculated for the four [SOURCE: Page_7]

indices. The greater the AUC, the more discriminating the index, is and the closer the [SOURCE: Page_7]

predictions are to the observed data. [SOURCE: Page_7]

We completed this analysis by studying the spectral signatures of the primary land cover [SOURCE: Page_7]

types represented in the study area. The values of the four water indices were extracted [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

International journal of applied earth observation and geoinformation [SOURCE: Page_8]

from pixel samples of water, water colonized by aquatic vegetation, active vegetation, and [SOURCE: Page_8]

bare soil and lateritic soil. [SOURCE: Page_8]

3.2 Spatial resolution analysis [SOURCE: Page_8]

The impact of spatial resolution on pond detection was studied using the Quickbird image. [SOURCE: Page_8]

The initial radiance image was gradually degraded from high (pixel size of 2.44 m x 2.44 [SOURCE: Page_8]

m) to low spatial resolution (1 km x 1 km) by factors of 2. We obtained 159 images in order [SOURCE: Page_8]

to create a continuous spatial resolution gradient. NDVI was derived from the NIR and R [SOURCE: Page_8]

bands (in radiance unit) from each image. The ponds were detected in each image by [SOURCE: Page_8]

radiometric thresholding. A threshold value of 0.041 was chosen according to the spectral [SOURCE: Page_8]

analysis results. For each spatial resolution, we then calculated the number of detected [SOURCE: Page_8]

ponds and their mean area. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Finally, the Landsat image and a MODIS image (10 August 2005) were processed to detect [SOURCE: Page_8]

the water bodies with the same method (NDVI / threshold 0.041) in order to compare the [SOURCE: Page_8]

results obtained from the Quickbird image when resampled at 30 m and 232 m, with results [SOURCE: Page_8]

from real EOS data. [SOURCE: Page_8]

3.3 Times series analysis [SOURCE: Page_8]

The ability of optical EOS to monitor temporary ponds was assessed using a time series of [SOURCE: Page_8]

MODIS NDVI images. Due to their simplicity, we used the MODIS NDVI layer. NDVI [SOURCE: Page_8]

mean values were extracted within a 3x^3 pixel window at the Mous, Furdu and Barkedji [SOURCE: Page_8]

ponds (Figure 1) to avoid geometric transformation uncertainty. Because of the MODIS [SOURCE: Page_8]

spatial resolution and the small size of the ponds, this value reflects the state of the water [SOURCE: Page_8]

area and of its surrounding environment (i.e., vegetation and soils). [SOURCE: Page_8]

### 5.    Discussion

Unlike a review article, the results presented above pertain to one particular study area for [SOURCE: Page_12]

which significant baseline, or ground-truthing, work had also been carried out. This ensures [SOURCE: Page_12]

that different methods or indices can be compared directly for identical situations, and their [SOURCE: Page_12]

relative performances can thus be quantitatively assessed. In this way, the present work [SOURCE: Page_12]

brings useful new information that may help substantiate the choice of an appropriate [SOURCE: Page_12]

remote sensing option for monitoring water bodies in arid areas. [SOURCE: Page_12]

The study on spectral indices showed that the indices using the MIR band as the MNDWI [SOURCE: Page_12]

(Xu, 2006) and the NDWI$_1$ (Gao, 1996; Hardisky, 1983) are the most efficient indices for [SOURCE: Page_12]

detecting water bodies in arid areas. According to our results, the MNDWI is particularly [SOURCE: Page_12]

suited to the detection of free water (Xu, 2006). Neither of those two indices enables the [SOURCE: Page_12]

distinction of aquatic vegetation from the vegetation surrounding the ponds. However, this [SOURCE: Page_12]

may not limit the use of remote sensing for the detection of water bodies in Sahel areas, as [SOURCE: Page_12]

in such areas ponds are typically surrounded by land with sparse vegetation cover, which is [SOURCE: Page_12]

a symptom of continuous over-use, trampling, and overgrazing. The two other indices [SOURCE: Page_12]

tested in our study, the NDVI (Townshend, 1986; Tucker, 1979) and NDWI 2 (McFeeters, [SOURCE: Page_12]

1996), showed a low capacity for detecting free water bodies at the spatial resolution of [SOURCE: Page_12]

Landsat ETM+. Nevertheless, higher scores might be expected if these indices were to be [SOURCE: Page_12]

derived from higher spatial resolution data. [SOURCE: Page_12]

The results of the spatial analysis highlighted the strong impact of the spatial resolution on [SOURCE: Page_12]

the characteristics of the detected ponds. Moreover, the results lead to a recommendation in [SOURCE: Page_12]

terms of sensor system choice for a given minimal pond area. According to our results (see [SOURCE: Page_12]

1 [SOURCE: Page_12]

International journal of applied earth observation and geoinformation [SOURCE: Page_13]

Figure 4), Very High Spatial Resolution imagery such as Quickbird, Ikonos and SPOT$_5$ [SOURCE: Page_13]

sensors may be useful to detect small ponds with an area of as low as 70 m 2 (one pond in [SOURCE: Page_13]

our study area). High-resolution satellite data such as SPOT 4-HRVIR and Landsat ETM+ [SOURCE: Page_13]

allows the identification of ponds with sizes ranging from about 1100 m 2 to 2500 m^2 (27 % [SOURCE: Page_13]

ponds). Medium spatial resolution sensors like MODIS allow the detection of ponds greater [SOURCE: Page_13]

than 1,700,000 m² (3). Finally, the SPOT Vegetation sensor, with a pixel size of 1 km x 1 [SOURCE: Page_13]

km, is found inappropriate for identifying the ponds in the study area. [SOURCE: Page_13]

Since optical EOS allows the detection of water bodies in arid areas at different spatial [SOURCE: Page_13]

resolutions as assessed in the previous section, the monitoring of the ponds’ temporal [SOURCE: Page_13]

dynamics may be performed using the same type of imagery. Nevertheless, because of a [SOURCE: Page_13]

common compromise between EOS spatial and temporal resolutions, high spatial resolution [SOURCE: Page_13]

sensors may only provide a punctual information, such as about 5 images/year for one [SOURCE: Page_13]

given site covered by SPOT$_5$-HRG data (Lacaux et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_13]

Thus, the utility of medium spatial resolution image time series for monitoring the [SOURCE: Page_13]

hydrologic dynamics of water bodies in arid areas with a high temporal frequency appears [SOURCE: Page_13]

very promising. Our results show a strong correlation between MODIS NDVI values and [SOURCE: Page_13]

water heights collected in the field. Further, the MODIS NDVI time series data seem to be [SOURCE: Page_13]

efficient at identifying the filling phases of the ponds. Indeed, the statistical relationship [SOURCE: Page_13]

between MODIS NDVI and the water height time series was strengthened by removing all [SOURCE: Page_13]

water height data corresponding to the emptying phase of the ponds. [SOURCE: Page_13]

These results highlight the added value of using remotely sensed data over meteorological [SOURCE: Page_13]

data for monitoring ponds in arid areas. The shortened time lag (-2 to -6 days) between the [SOURCE: Page_13]

NDVI value and the water height is in agreement with hydrological studies showing that [SOURCE: Page_13]

the filling of ponds in arid areas is mainly due to water streaming, and not to direct rain [SOURCE: Page_14]

contribution (Desconnets et al., 1993) In the Sahelian context of the study, the vegetation [SOURCE: Page_14]

indices such as the NDVI are good proxies for detecting variations in humidity and water [SOURCE: Page_14]

heights, as they are linked to the chlorophylian activity of the vegetation surrounding the [SOURCE: Page_14]

ponds. [SOURCE: Page_14]
