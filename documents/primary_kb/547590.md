1                                                ABSTRACT 2            Predicting soil hydraulic properties and understanding their temporal variability during 3   the irrigated cropping season are required to mitigate agro-environmental risks. This paper 4   reports field measurements of soil hydraulic properties under two drip irrigation treatments, 5   full (FT) and limited (LT). The objective was to identify the temporal variability of the 6   hydraulic properties of field soil under high-frequency water application during a maize 7   cropping season. Soil hydraulics were characterized using the Beerkan infiltration method. 8   Seven sets of infiltration measurements were taken for each irrigation treatment during the 9   cropping season between June and September 2007. The first set was measured two weeks 10   before the first irrigation event. The results demonstrated that both soil porosity and hydraulic 11   properties changed over time. These temporal changes occurred in two distinct stages. The 12   first stage lasted from the first irrigation event until the root system was well established. 13   During this stage, soil porosity was significantly affected by the first irrigation event, 14   resulting in a decrease in both the saturated hydraulic conductivity Ks and the mean pore 15   effective radius ξm and in an increase in capillary length αh. These hydraulic parameters 16   reached their extreme values at the end of this stage. This behavior was explained by the 17   “hydraulic” compaction of the surface soil following irrigation. During the second stage, there 18   was a gradual increase in both Ks and ξm and a gradual decrease in αh when the effect of 19   irrigation was overtaken by other phenomena. The latter was put down to the effects of 20   wetting and drying cycles, soil biological activity and the effects of the root system, which 21   could be asymmetric as a result of irrigation with only one drip line installed for every two 22   plant rows. 23            The processes that affected soil hydraulic properties in the two irrigation treatments 24   were similar. No significant change in ξm and αh was observed between FT and LT. However, 25   as a result of daily wetting and drying cycles, which were strongest in LT, the soil in this 26   treatment was found to be more conductive than that of FT. This showed that most of the 27   changes in pore-size distribution occurred in the larger fraction of pores. 28            The impact of these temporal changes on the dimensions of the wetting bulb was 29   studied using a simplified modeling approach. Our results showed that there were marked 30   differences in the computed width and depth of wetting bulb when model input parameters 31   measured before and after irrigation were used. A temporal increase in capillary length led to 32   a more horizontally elongated wetting bulb. This could improve both watering and 33   fertilization of the root zone and reduce losses due to deep percolation. As a practical result of 34   this study, in order to mitigate agro-environmental risks we recommend applying fertilizers 35   after the restructuration of tilled soil. Further studies using improved models accounting for 36   temporal changes in soil hydraulic properties are needed. 37 [SOURCE: Page_3]

1                                          INTRODUCTION 2 3            Drip irrigation has become quite common thanks to its great potential to use less water 4   and to localize chemical applications, thereby enhancing the efficiency of irrigation and 5   fertilization and reducing the risk of pollution. However, these objectives can only be 6   achieved if the irrigation system is correctly designed (e.g. emitter discharge rate, emitter 7   spacing, tape lateral spacing, diameter and length of the lateral system) and well managed 8   (e.g. irrigation scheduling and fertilization strategy) for any given set of soil, crop and 9   climatic conditions. 10            In contrast to surface or sprinkler systems, the frequency of the water application 11   under drip irrigation is high. This means the infiltration period is a very important stage of the 12   irrigation cycle (Rawlins, 1973). A good knowledge of the soil hydraulic properties involved 13   in the multidirectional infiltration process during the course of this cycle is required to 14   optimize water applications. The ability to estimate the dimensions of the wetting bulb i.e., 15   water extending laterally and vertically away from an emitter is an important criterion for the 16   design of drip systems to ensure efficient irrigation and to avoid the movement of water 17   beyond the root zone (Bresler, 1978; Zur et al., 1994; Zur, 1996 and Revol et al., 1997). 18   Because analytical models provide a rapid way of determining the position of the wetting 19   front (Revol et al., 1997; Cook et al., 2003; Thorburn et al., 2003), researchers have tried to 20   develop a simple model to describe the soil wetting pattern with micro-irrigation systems. 21   Schwartzman and Zur (1986) developed a simplified semi-empirical model of wetted soil 22   geometry with surface trickle irrigation, which depends on specific parameters i.e., soil type 23   (saturated hydraulic conductivity), emitter discharge per unit length of laterals, and total 24   amount of water in the soil. Al-Qinna and Abu-Awwad (2001) estimated an exponential 25   function with a water application rate to describe the horizontal width and the vertical depth 26   of the advance of the wetting front. In their field study, Revol et al. (1997) found that the 27   infiltration solutions of Philip (1984) provided good estimations of the radial (r) and vertical 28   (z) distance of the wetted zone from the water sources. Warrick (2003) reviewed many 29   analytical solutions describing water infiltration from point and line sources. 30            In a particular soil-water-plant system and climatic conditions, the transport properties 31   of the soil surface layer can change during the growing season. This temporal variation is 32   likely due to modifications in surface soil conditions resulting from tillage practices (Mohanty 33   et al., 1996; Cameira et al., 2003), and to the effects of the rooting system (Shirmohammadi 34   and Skaggs, 1984; Rasse et al., 2000 and Iqbal et al., 2005). Wetting and drying cycles and 35   the irrigation system can also alter the soil structure. Hydrodynamic behavior is consequently 36   affected primarily by the current state of the soil structure, as well as its texture (Mapa et al., 37   1986; Messing and Jarvis, 1993; Angulo-Jaramillo et al., 1997; Cameira et al., 2003; Mailhol 38   et al., 2005). However, the above-mentioned studies dealt with traditional irrigation systems 39   that supply large amounts of water to the soil system at low frequencies. Only Mapa et al. 40   (1986) addressed the effects on soil hydraulics of the wetting and drying cycles caused by 41   drip irrigation following tillage. These authors found that soil hydraulic properties changed 42   significantly after only one wetting/drying cycle in a silty clay loam and in a clay loam. 43   However, in their study, each wetting/drying cycle included wetting (18 h of irrigation) 44   followed by drying (7–10 days). In our opinion, this irrigation schedule thus more resembled 45   that of traditional irrigation systems. To our knowledge, no study has tried to identify 46   temporal variations in soil hydraulic properties during a cropping season under high- 47   frequency water application. 48            The objectives of the present study were (i) to characterize temporal variability of soil 49   hydraulic properties due to changes in soil structure under high-frequency drip irrigation, 50   using the Beerkan infiltration method, and (ii) to illustrate the effects of temporal variability [SOURCE: Page_4]

The 6   experimental field is located at the Cemagref Experimental Station in Montpellier, France 7   (43°40’ N, 3°50’ E) where there is a fully equipped meteorological station. In the 2006-2007 8   cultivation years, the field was plowed to a depth of 35 cm on November 15 with a moldboard 9   plow. Water was delivered through a line drip irrigation system with 13   an emitter spacing of 30 cm and a flow rate of 3.67 L h-1 m-1. 1                                          CONCLUSIONS 2 3           In this study, the behavior of a loamy soil under drip irrigation was analyzed using the 4   Beerkan infiltration method to identify the temporal variability of its hydraulic properties 5   caused by high-frequency irrigation during a maize cropping season. Two different irrigation 6   treatments, a full (FT) and a limited (LT), were investigated. Our results demonstrated that 7   both soil porosity and hydraulic properties varied over time. Soil behavior can be divided into 8   two separate stages. The first stage lasted from the first irrigation event (56 days after sowing 9   of the crop) until the root system was well established (75 days after sowing). During this 10   stage, soil porosity was significantly affected by the first irrigation event, resulting in a 11   decrease in both saturated hydraulic conductivity Ks and in the mean pore effective radius ξm, 12   and in an increase in capillary length αh. These hydraulic parameters reached their extreme 13   values at the end of this stage. This behavior was explained by the “hydraulic” compaction of 14   the surface soil following irrigation. 15           Later in the season, during the second stage, a gradual increase in both Ks and ξm and a 16   gradual decrease in αh were observed in both FT and LT, when the effect of irrigation was 17   overtaken by other phenomena. The latter was put down to the effects of wetting and drying 18   cycles, soil biological activity, and the effect of the root system, which could be asymmetric 19   as a result of irrigation with only one drip line installed for every two plant rows. 20           The processes that affect soil hydraulic properties in the two irrigation treatments were 21   found to be similar. No significant change in mean effective pore radius and in capillary 22   length was observed between FT and LT. However, as a result of daily wetting and drying 23   cycles, which were very marked in LT, the soil was found to be more conductive than in the 24   FT. This shows that different irrigation management systems mainly affect the larger fraction 25   of pores i.e., structural pores. 26           Our work raises questions about the usefulness of taking this phenomenon into 27   account to improve the efficiency of both water and fertilizer under drip irrigation. To answer 28   this question, we studied the impact of temporal changes in soil hydraulic properties on the 29   dimension of the wetting bulb using a simplified modeling approach. Our results 30   demonstrated that there were major differences in the computed width and depth of the wetted 31   bulb when model input parameters measured before and after irrigation were used. An 32   increase in capillary length over time led to the more horizontally elongated bulb. This change 33   can improve the efficiency of both irrigation and fertilization of the root zone and reduce 34   losses of both water and solute due to deep percolation. As a practical result of this study, we 35   recommend fertigation after the restructuration of tilled soil in order to mitigate agro- 36   environmental risks and to improve the efficiency of the use of solutes. The temporal changes 37   in soil hydraulic properties identified in our study should be taken into account in future 38   studies when simulating soil water transfer under drip irrigation in order to improve irrigation 39   scheduling practices. 40 [SOURCE: Page_12]
