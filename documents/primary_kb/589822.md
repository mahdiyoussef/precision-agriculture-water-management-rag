1   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

2      Compaction of tilled layers under the single effect of rainfall or irrigation was called slumping. [SOURCE: Page_3]

3   Slumping affects strongly root development and plant biomass production and was observed in [SOURCE: Page_3]

4   different soil types but sandy soils appear particularly prone to this physical degradation. Our [SOURCE: Page_3]

5   objectives in this study were (i) to monitor in the field the changes in soil structure and water status [SOURCE: Page_3]

6   simultaneously, (ii) to study the effects of rainfall and management practices on slumping and (iii) [SOURCE: Page_3]

7   to propose a conceptual model for sandy soil slumping. [SOURCE: Page_3]

8      An experimental site was selected in Northeast Thailand and we tested the effect of tillage [SOURCE: Page_3]

9   depth and initial water content on slumping dynamic. Plots (9 m×15 m) were tilled at (i) two depths [SOURCE: Page_3]

10   (20 and 40 cm, called S and D respectively) in dry conditions, (ii) at 20 cm depth in dry or wet [SOURCE: Page_3]

11   conditions (called Y and W respectively). These plots were submitted to natural rainfall for 20 or [SOURCE: Page_3]

12   61 days to get different total rainfall amounts (114 and 212 mm respectively). In addition, smaller [SOURCE: Page_3]

13   plots (0.24 m^2) were used for experimental flooding irrigation (equivalent to measured rainfalls, i.e. [SOURCE: Page_3]

14   100 and 200 mm). Soil bulk density, soil surface elevation, soil water content and matric potential [SOURCE: Page_3]

15   were recorded. [SOURCE: Page_3]

16      A decrease in soil elevation was observed in all treatments. In the absence of erosion it was [SOURCE: Page_3]

17   interpreted as a loss of porosity which resulted from slumping. Bulk density increased in all layers [SOURCE: Page_3]

18   of the tilled profile (from 1.38 to 1.57 g cm-3). In the surface layer (0-5 cm) this increase was [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   systematically higher compared to deeper layer. No significant difference in final bulk density was [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   found between the S and W treatments, and between the Y and W treatments. Bulk density [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   increased more rapidly in the Y and W treatments than in the S and D treatments, even though the [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   cumulative rainfall was lower. After flooding experiments, bulk density was higher than after [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   natural rainfall despite similar water amount brought to the soil. [SOURCE: Page_3]

24      The existence of a layer with 50 % porosity straight after tillage was explained by the capillary [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

1   forces developed by water bridges between the elementary grains. Models of wet granular material [SOURCE: Page_4]

2   indicate that a drastic loss of cohesion can occur when the liquid phase becomes continuous. [SOURCE: Page_4]

3   Indeed slumping was observed during the downward movement of the water front inside the [SOURCE: Page_4]

4   profile which is consistent with this hypothesis. As the existence of a continuous liquid phase along [SOURCE: Page_4]

5   the profile is the most determinant factor of slumping, all rainfall characteristics determining the [SOURCE: Page_4]

6   existence of such a continuous liquid phase will affect slumping, i.e. rainfall intensity, duration and [SOURCE: Page_4]

7   frequency. For given rainfall characteristics, slumping dynamic and intensity depend on the bulk [SOURCE: Page_4]

1   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_5]

2      Soil tillage aims at creating favorable physical conditions for crop growth by modifying the soil [SOURCE: Page_5]

3   structure and associated properties of the tilled layer (Guérif et al., 2001). However, the desired [SOURCE: Page_5]

4   loose structure tends to be structurally unstable and is susceptible to structural collapse thus leading [SOURCE: Page_5]

5   to a loss of the benefits for root development provided by soil (Hartmann et al., 2008a; Hamza and [SOURCE: Page_5]

6   Anderson, 2005). Soil recompaction (i.e. bulk density increase to initial level) is a general [SOURCE: Page_5]

7   phenomenon which has several causes related to external and internal forces. External force is [SOURCE: Page_5]

8   defined as loading from outside the soil profile, such as tractor loading, rainfall kinetic energy; [SOURCE: Page_5]

9   internal force is defined as force inside the soil profile, such as overburden pressure, capillary force. [SOURCE: Page_5]

10   Soil recompaction is commonly attributed to mechanized agriculture alone because the weight of [SOURCE: Page_5]

11   the agricultural and forestry machinery has been increased three- to fourfold as well as the [SOURCE: Page_5]

12   frequency of wheeling during recent decades (e.g. Soane et al., 1981a, b; Soane et al., 1982; Soane, [SOURCE: Page_5]

13   1990; Horn et al., 1995; Soane and Van Ouwerkerk, 1995; Soane and Ball, 1998; Hamza and [SOURCE: Page_5]

14   Anderson, 2005). The phenomenon of bulk density increase recorded after wetting and without [SOURCE: Page_5]

15   application of any external load was termed ‘slumping’ (Kemper and Rosenau, 1984; Mullins et [SOURCE: Page_5]

16   al.1990). While compaction, i.e. the bulk density increase resulting from mechanical loading was [SOURCE: Page_5]

17   widely studied, much less attention has been paid to slumping. [SOURCE: Page_5]

18      For coarse-textured soils, Kemper and Rosenau (1984) suggested that slumping is related to [SOURCE: Page_5]

19   soils saturation or matric potential ψ close to 0 hPa. Slumping is a ubiquitous phenomenon and was [SOURCE: Page_5]

20   recorded under a wide range of conditions. It was recorded after a single rainfall event (20 mm in [SOURCE: Page_5]

21   one hour) (Mead and Chan, 1988), two natural rainfall events (80 mm in 160 min) (Hartmann et al., [SOURCE: Page_5]

22   1999), eight weeks of measuring (Osunbitan et al., 2005), and a cropping season (Hamblin and [SOURCE: Page_5]

23   Tennant, 1979). Moreover, slumping can affect the whole tilled layer (such as down to 40 cm depth [SOURCE: Page_5]

24   in Moffat and Boswell, 1997), and can have similar adverse effects on crop development as well as [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

1   compaction resulting from mechanical loading (Kozlowski, 1999). Despite its ubiquity and [SOURCE: Page_6]

2   importance, no study has provided yet a detailed description of the dynamics of slumping and its [SOURCE: Page_6]

3   main field characteristics. In the context of the extension of cultivated lands to marginal areas, [SOURCE: Page_6]

4   which often include a high proportion of sandy soils (Eswaran et al., 2007), it is a challenge to look [SOURCE: Page_6]

5   for soil management that could minimize the occurrence of slumping. As a first step in this [SOURCE: Page_6]

6   direction, the objectives of our study were: (i) to monitor the changes in soil structure and water [SOURCE: Page_6]

7   status simultaneously at the field scale; (ii) to study the effects of rainfall and management [SOURCE: Page_6]

8   practices on slumping and iii) to propose a conceptual model of sandy soil slumping. [SOURCE: Page_6]

13   layer ρb was about 0.3 g cm-3 higher than at the surface (Fig. 7). At the end of the experiment, for [SOURCE: Page_13]

14   both Df and Sf, ρb shifted to higher values on all the profile, ρb being higher close to the surface [SOURCE: Page_13]

17   4. Discussion and conclusion [SOURCE: Page_13]

18   4.1. Evidence of slumping [SOURCE: Page_13]

19       The soil surface reorganisation which was recorded during Exp. 1 and 2 and which consisted [SOURCE: Page_13]

20   in a decrease in the soil roughness (Fig. 3) was commonly observed in coarse-textured soils and [SOURCE: Page_13]

21   discussed as closely related to their low structural stability when submitted to rainfall (Mwendera [SOURCE: Page_13]

22   and Feyen, 1994; Rudolph et al., 1997; Twomlow and Bruneau, 2000). Our results show that soil [SOURCE: Page_13]

23   reorganisation affected not only surface but also the whole tilled horizon. In the three experiments [SOURCE: Page_13]

12 [SOURCE: Page_13]

1   and whatever the treatment, a soil sinkage was recorded, i.e. a decrease in soil surface elevation [SOURCE: Page_14]

2   (Fig. 4 and Fig. 6b). As no erosion was observed during the experiment (i.e. no mass movement out [SOURCE: Page_14]

3   of the field), such an elevation decrease in soil surface necessarily resulted from a loss of pore [SOURCE: Page_14]

4   volume, i.e. from an increase in soil compactness. Soil sinkage did not happen regularly over time [SOURCE: Page_14]

5   but was related to the occurrence of rainfall events, and consequently can be considered as an [SOURCE: Page_14]

6   indicator of soil slumping as defined by Mullins et al. (1990). Soil sinkage cannot be observed by [SOURCE: Page_14]

7   the naked eye even if it is probably a common phenomenon in coarse textured soils, thus explaining [SOURCE: Page_14]

8   the rarity of its description (Wilton, 1964; Young et al., 1991; Moffat et al., 1997; Or and Ghezzehei, [SOURCE: Page_14]

9   2002). Even if soil sinkage is a relevant indicator of slumping occurrence, it does not provide any [SOURCE: Page_14]

10   information about how compactness evolves with depth in the soil. [SOURCE: Page_14]

11 [SOURCE: Page_14]

12   4.2. Soil structure after tillage [SOURCE: Page_14]

13       After tillage, the ρb profile presents two striking aspects. First, the ρb increased regularly with [SOURCE: Page_14]

14   depth (from 1.26 g cm-3 at surface to 1.44 g cm-3 at 40 cm depth), thus indicating a closer packing [SOURCE: Page_14]

15   of the soil particles when going deeper in the profile (Fig.5 and 7). Second, the ρb values [SOURCE: Page_14]

16   (correspond to porosity ranging from 53 to 45%) were very low in sandy soils since it results [SOURCE: Page_14]

17   mainly from the packing of coarse grains. [SOURCE: Page_14]

18       This loose packing was created by tillage: the energy developed by the disks was enough to [SOURCE: Page_14]

19   separate most elementary particles which were lifted up, increasing their potential energy. This [SOURCE: Page_14]

20   energy was dissipated when the particles moved downward under the effect of gravity. During the [SOURCE: Page_14]

21   piling process, each time a contact occurred between neighbouring particles, the downward [SOURCE: Page_14]

22   movement was hampered due to (i) mechanical friction, and (ii) capillary bridges developing [SOURCE: Page_14]

23   between the grains. At Wc <0.12 g cm-3, as measured before tillage (data not presented), water can [SOURCE: Page_14]

24   be located only at the contact points between two particles, forming bridges (Willet et al., 2000; [SOURCE: Page_14]

13 [SOURCE: Page_14]

1   Adams et al., 2002; Herminghaus, 2005). These bridges act as glue bonding the particles together [SOURCE: Page_15]

2   in relation with the water interfacial tension and negative Laplace pressure (Kemper and Rosenau, [SOURCE: Page_15]

3   1984). The development of water bridges hampered the flow of solid particles and blocked the sand [SOURCE: Page_15]

4   grains into a loose packing. But during the piling process, the overburden pressure was gradually [SOURCE: Page_15]

5   increasing with depth, forcing the grains to rearrange in a continuously closer packing, resulting in [SOURCE: Page_15]

6   a regular ρb increase with depth. This could be a general behaviour in tilled sandy soils (Ampoorter [SOURCE: Page_15]

7   et al., 2007). The stability of the tilled layer results from capillary bridges and is consequently low, [SOURCE: Page_15]

8   but in the absence of mechanical loading, it is stable until occurrence of rain and subsequent [SOURCE: Page_15]

9   slumping. [SOURCE: Page_15]

10 [SOURCE: Page_15]

11   4.3. Water characteristics inducing slumping [SOURCE: Page_15]

12      No relation was found between rainfall amount and slumping characteristics (occurrence, [SOURCE: Page_15]

13   intensity, dynamic): (i) slumping was triggered by a small but variable amount of water (34 mm in [SOURCE: Page_15]

14   Exp. 2 but only 25 mm in Exp. 3), (ii) despite similar large amounts of water brought to the soil [SOURCE: Page_15]

15   (200 mm), the intensity of slumping was much higher in Exp. 3 than in Exp.1 (soil sinkage of 4.6 [SOURCE: Page_15]

16   and 1.2 cm respectively), (iii) when at the end of Exp.1 and 2, AR were very different (200 mm and [SOURCE: Page_15]

17   100 mm respectively), intensity of slumping was similar, i.e. same final ρb (>1.4 g cm-3 below [SOURCE: Page_15]

18   10 cm), and (iv) slumping was faster in Exp. 3 compared to Exp 1 (nearly finished after 75 mm [SOURCE: Page_15]

19   water input in Exp. 3 when still observed after 132 mm water input in Exp.1) (Fig. 5). [SOURCE: Page_15]

20      The lack of a global relation between slumping and rainfall characteristics, suggests that not all [SOURCE: Page_15]

21   rainfall events have the same potential to induce slumping. A soil material is stable as long as it [SOURCE: Page_15]

22   meets the Mohr-Coulomb criterions: [SOURCE: Page_15]

23                                        τ = μσ+c                                      (1) [SOURCE: Page_15]

14 [SOURCE: Page_15]

1   where τ is the shear stress, σ is the normal force, μ is the internal friction coefficient and c is [SOURCE: Page_16]

2   cohesion. The cohesion is equivalent to the shear stress at zero normal force and it depends on (i) [SOURCE: Page_16]

3   both the amount and type of clay and organic matter (i.e. permanent bonds), and (ii) the water [SOURCE: Page_16]

4   content (Le Bissonnais et al., 1995). Sandy soils have low clay and organic carbon content, [SOURCE: Page_16]

5   consequently water becomes a major factor of cohesion between the elementary particles and [SOURCE: Page_16]

6   globally of structural stability (Panayiotopoulos and Mullins, 1985, Panayiotopoulos, 1989). [SOURCE: Page_16]

7   Kemper et Rosenau (1984) suggested that slumping was related to soil saturation and resulting lack [SOURCE: Page_16]

8   of cohesion between the elementary particles. Recent development on the physics of wet granular [SOURCE: Page_16]

9   material, like sandy soils, suggest that slumping would occur before reaching water saturation. [SOURCE: Page_16]

10       Indeed, in a sandy material, the relation between soil cohesion and water content is not linear, [SOURCE: Page_16]

11   two successive stages need to be considered (Pierrat and Caram, 1997; Iveson et al., 2002; Mitarai [SOURCE: Page_16]

12   and Nori, 2006): at the first stage, water is located only at the contact between grains and is filling [SOURCE: Page_16]

13   the packing pores between neighbouring grains creating separated clusters of grains (Kohonen et [SOURCE: Page_16]

14   al., 2004; Scheel et al., 2008). The second stage starts after all the packing pores were filled with [SOURCE: Page_16]

15   water when the liquid phase becomes continuous (Herminghaus, 2005). The second stage is [SOURCE: Page_16]

16   characterised by a drastic decrease in development of air/water interfaces and thus a drastic [SOURCE: Page_16]

17   drecrease in macroscopic cohesion (Soulié et al., 2006; Grof et al., 2008). Such a model suggests [SOURCE: Page_16]

18   that slumping in sandy soils would be triggered before reaching saturation, when rainfall amount is [SOURCE: Page_16]

19   high enough to obtain a continuous liquid phase draining down in the profile. Compared to natural [SOURCE: Page_16]

20   tilled sandy soil, the experimental and mathematical models studied by physicists are simplified [SOURCE: Page_16]

21   (the particles size distribution is limited and the grains are in close packing). Even so, it is still a [SOURCE: Page_16]

22   challenge to determine Wc or ψ that corresponds to a continuous liquid phase in these models and it [SOURCE: Page_16]

23   is not yet possible for real soils. Anyway, such liquid phase continuity in sandy material can be [SOURCE: Page_16]

24   observed during fast downward movement of the wetting front that correspond to a locally high Wc [SOURCE: Page_16]

15 [SOURCE: Page_16]

1   and ψ (Kawamoto et al., 2004) [SOURCE: Page_17]

2 [SOURCE: Page_17]

3   4.4. Slumping dynamics and water infiltration [SOURCE: Page_17]

4      In Exp.3 water supply was continuous, creating an infiltration and thus a downward movement [SOURCE: Page_17]

5   of the wetting front. Soil and water characteristics were recorded during and immediately after [SOURCE: Page_17]

6   (<1h) water supply. During the first water input, soil surface sinkage (indicating a rearrangement of [SOURCE: Page_17]

7   elementary grains) was observed; sinkage intensity increased until the third addition of water, then [SOURCE: Page_17]

8   it decreased again and even if it was small, sinkage was observed until the last water input (Fig. 6b). [SOURCE: Page_17]

9   This result confirms that slumping occurred during the downward movement of the wetting front, [SOURCE: Page_17]

10   i.e. before saturation, contrary to the suggestion of Kemper and Rosenau (1984). Similar fast [SOURCE: Page_17]

11   rearrangement was already recorded in an independent field experiment during which the largest ρb [SOURCE: Page_17]

12   increase occurred within the first 10 min of rainfall (Bedaiwy and Rolston, 1993). [SOURCE: Page_17]

13      Exp. 1 and 2 were conducted under natural rainfall events and were characterised by slower [SOURCE: Page_17]

14   slumping dynamic. Soil and water characteristics (ρb, ψ, Wc) were monitored manually at the [SOURCE: Page_17]

15   interval of one to seven days; consequently our device could not record any fast water front [SOURCE: Page_17]

16   movement. Anyway, structural changes (soil surface sinkage and bulk density increased) were [SOURCE: Page_17]

17   observed only after the limited number of major rainfall events, events that occurred similarly [SOURCE: Page_17]

18   during Exp. 1 and 2 (Fig. 1). This observation is consistent with the hypothesis that all rainfall [SOURCE: Page_17]

19   events do not have the same capability to induce slumping; in the context of our experiment, only [SOURCE: Page_17]

20   events > 15 mm induced fast wetting front infiltration and significant structural changes (surface [SOURCE: Page_17]

21   level decrease, bulk density increase). [SOURCE: Page_17]

22      In Exp. 3, a steady state was reached earlier for infiltration rate than for soil elevation decrease [SOURCE: Page_17]

23   (Fig. 6a and Fig. 6b). This suggests that infiltrability is not a good indicator of slumping (direct [SOURCE: Page_17]

24   observations of structural changes have to be preferred). On the other hand, these data can also [SOURCE: Page_17]

16 [SOURCE: Page_17]

1   provide some indications on the characteristics of sand grains rearrangement at the onset of the first [SOURCE: Page_18]

2   major rainfall. The steady state observed for infiltration when slumping was still on process [SOURCE: Page_18]

3   indicates that the continuity of the porosity (a major factor of infiltration rate) decreased faster than [SOURCE: Page_18]

4   total pore volume (Nimmo and Akstin, 1988; Meek et al., 1992). In a pile of grains that results from [SOURCE: Page_18]

5   a loose packing of grains linked together only by capillary water bridges, the first grains to be [SOURCE: Page_18]

6   unbalanced are probably the biggest ones. They are indeed the most strongly pulled by gravity and [SOURCE: Page_18]

7   less strongly hold by capillary bridges. Once unbalanced, those grains can ‘fall’ in the large pores [SOURCE: Page_18]

8   (with 50% porosity, the volume occupied by porosity is the same as occupied by particles). This [SOURCE: Page_18]

9   reorganisation would explain the fast infiltration decrease related to (i) a decrease in the volume of [SOURCE: Page_18]

10   large pores (i.e. that allows fast water movement), and (ii) a decrease in the continuity of the [SOURCE: Page_18]

11   remaining smaller packing pores. [SOURCE: Page_18]

13   4.5. Soil factors affecting slumping [SOURCE: Page_18]

14       Previous experiment demonstrated that under similar rainfall events, slumping could differ in [SOURCE: Page_18]

15   relation with soil management (Meek et al., 1988; Bedaiwy and Rolston 1993, Hartmann et al, [SOURCE: Page_18]

16   2008a,b). Our experiment confirmed that the relative change of ρb was inversely correlated with the [SOURCE: Page_18]

17   initial ρb as already suggested by Bedaiwy and Rolston (1993). For exemple, compared to the [SOURCE: Page_18]

18   shallow layers, the deep layers had the highest ρb after tillage, and they had also the smallest ρb [SOURCE: Page_18]

19   increase during the experiment. Less slumping was also observed when the soil was initially wet [SOURCE: Page_18]

20   compared to when it was initially dry (Fig. 6). As the wet sandy soils are more sensitive to [SOURCE: Page_18]

21   mechanical stresses than dry soils (Panayiatopoulos et al., 1989; Meek et al., 1992), during the [SOURCE: Page_18]

22   tillage operations the W plots were probably more affected by mechanical constraints induced by [SOURCE: Page_18]

23   the disk than the Y plots. This suggests that the factor hampering the slumping process in W [SOURCE: Page_18]

24   compared to Y treatment was its higher initial ρb and not its higher Wc. [SOURCE: Page_18]

17 [SOURCE: Page_18]

1       While inside the profile, the relative ρb change was inversely correlated to the initial ρb, the top [SOURCE: Page_19]

2   layer behaved differently: ρb increase was constant and was finally huge compared to the [SOURCE: Page_19]

3   underlying layers. In first estimate, a different behaviour of the top layer seemed to be related to a [SOURCE: Page_19]

4   lateral sand movement from the ridge and accumulation on the surface of the furrow (Exp.1 and 2, [SOURCE: Page_19]

5   Fig. 3). But similar increase was recorded in Exp. 3 which had a flat surface and where no lateral [SOURCE: Page_19]

6   movement occurred. On a flat surface, the soil structure can also be degraded by vertical [SOURCE: Page_19]

7   segregation of clay and sand fractions due to the kinetic energy of the raindrops and result in [SOURCE: Page_19]

8   different surface crusts (Casenave and Valentin, 1992). Such crusts are only some millimeters thick [SOURCE: Page_19]

9   (Bielders and Bavey, 1995; Roth, 1997; Fohrer et al., 1999; Ndiaye et al., 2005) and cannot explain [SOURCE: Page_19]

10   changes at several centimeters depth and deeper in the tilled layer. Indeed, the surface layer is [SOURCE: Page_19]

11   characterised by (i) a very small normal force σ (Eq (1)) due to quasi absence of overburden [SOURCE: Page_19]

12   pressure, and (ii) a frequent occurrence of high Wc, even during minor rainfall events. These two [SOURCE: Page_19]

13   specific characteristics can explain that the particle reorganisation at the surface is different from [SOURCE: Page_19]

14   that observed below the surface layer. [SOURCE: Page_19]

15       Since surface heterogeneity can induce preferential water infiltration (Twomlow and Bruneau, [SOURCE: Page_19]

16   2000), the presence of ridges and furrows has to be mentionned: slumping was perhaps also [SOURCE: Page_19]

17   influenced by that specific surface topography, but we have not collected any data that allows a [SOURCE: Page_19]

18   estimation on the effect of this factor . [SOURCE: Page_19]

20   4.6. A conceptual model of soil slumping [SOURCE: Page_19]

21       When Kemper and Rosenau (1984) and Mullins et al. (1990) suggested the existence of a [SOURCE: Page_19]

22   specific degradation process named slumping, they did not provide any information about its [SOURCE: Page_19]

23   kinetic, and the underlying processes and determinant factors. Here we suggest a scenario of [SOURCE: Page_19]

24   slumping in sandy soils and a list of determinant factors based on our experiment. Slumping kinetic [SOURCE: Page_19]

18 [SOURCE: Page_19]

1   can be separated in three main steps (Fig. 8): (i) creation of an initial loose profile with a regular ρb [SOURCE: Page_20]

2   increase with depth after tillage, the cohesion and stability of these layers resulting from the strong [SOURCE: Page_20]

3   capillary forces between elementary grains (Fig. 8-a), (ii) during major rainfall events or under [SOURCE: Page_20]

4   irrigation, a fast increase in ρb in all tilled layers, the loss of cohesion resulting from the [SOURCE: Page_20]

5   development of a continuous (but transient) liquid phase along the profile (Fig. 8-b), and (iii) after [SOURCE: Page_20]

6   each major rainfall, when the soil is gradually getting denser, the slumping intensity is decreasing [SOURCE: Page_20]

7   because of steric constraints to reorganisation, except for the surface layer where bulk density [SOURCE: Page_20]

8   seems to be continuously increasing (Fig. 8-c). As the existence of a continuous liquid phase along [SOURCE: Page_20]

9   the profile is the most determinant factor of slumping, all rainfall characteristic determining the [SOURCE: Page_20]

10   existence of such continuous liquid phase will affect slumping, i.e. rainfall intensity, duration and [SOURCE: Page_20]

11   frequency. For given rainfall characteristics, slumping dynamic and intensity depends on the bulk [SOURCE: Page_20]

12   density at the onset of the rainfall or irrigation event. The high variability recorded in earlier field [SOURCE: Page_20]

13   works (for example: Mead and Chan, 1988; Meek et al., 1988, Hartmann et al., 1999 ; Osunbitan et [SOURCE: Page_20]

14   al., 2005) result from the many possible interactions between these determining factors. [SOURCE: Page_20]

15       Further experiments should include a better control of experimental conditions associated to [SOURCE: Page_20]

16   more accurate recording at the time scale of the rainy events to obtain new data enabling to [SOURCE: Page_20]

17   establish a quantitative model and consequently improve our knowledge and prevision of the [SOURCE: Page_20]

18   elementary mechanisms of slumping. [SOURCE: Page_20]
