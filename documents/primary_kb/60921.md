1   Abstract. A French data set was used in evaluating how well two widely used [SOURCE: Page_3]

2   analytical functions describe measured soil water characteristic (SWC) data. Both the [SOURCE: Page_3]

3   van Genuchten (sigmoidal) and Campbell (power-law) equations gave good [SOURCE: Page_3]

4   descriptions of the data (mean R$_2$ of 98.1% and 97.1% respectively). Methods of [SOURCE: Page_3]

5   predicting SWC data were also evaluated. When a power-law equation was [SOURCE: Page_3]

6   parameterised using just two measured SWC points and bulk density (the ‘two-point’ [SOURCE: Page_3]

7   method), a very good SWC prediction was obtained for the French data (mean R$_2$ of [SOURCE: Page_3]

8   94.8%). An empirical equation for prediction of the SWC was also assessed using the [SOURCE: Page_3]

9   French data set. This method was developed using multiple regression analysis from [SOURCE: Page_3]

10   Australian soil data and requires soil texture and bulk density as input. The [SOURCE: Page_3]

11   predictions (mean R$_2$ of 85.2%) lacked accuracy and precision in comparison to the [SOURCE: Page_3]

12   two-point method but uses more readily available input data. The accuracy of [SOURCE: Page_3]

13   prediction from both methods was similar to that observed previously for Australian [SOURCE: Page_3]

14   data sets. The empirical approach developed from Australian soil data has reasonable [SOURCE: Page_3]

15   applicability to French soils. The approach of assuming a power-law model and [SOURCE: Page_3]

16   empirically predicting slope and air entry potential is shown to have merit. A strategy [SOURCE: Page_3]

17   for achieving adequate coverage of soil hydraulic property data for France is [SOURCE: Page_3]

18   suggested incorporating hydraulic prediction methods such as those evaluated here. [SOURCE: Page_3]

## 1                                   INTRODUCTION

2   Soil hydraulic property data is a requirement for models simulating water and [SOURCE: Page_4]

3   chemical transport in soil. With the increased application of such models, as well as [SOURCE: Page_4]

4   for climate impact modelling, land resource assessment, and regional risk assessment [SOURCE: Page_4]

5   there is a growing demand for soil hydraulic data in France and elsewhere. It would [SOURCE: Page_4]

6   be ideal to have detailed soil and land information at scales relevant to management [SOURCE: Page_4]

7   (e.g. similar to the existing coverage in The Netherlands) but obtaining such coverage [SOURCE: Page_4]

8   in France is a huge task. The soil water characteristic (SWC) can only be measured at [SOURCE: Page_4]

9   a limited number of sites during a routine survey because of time and cost. Strategies [SOURCE: Page_4]

10   for acquiring appropriate hydraulic data have to balance the costs of detailed direct [SOURCE: Page_4]

11   measurement against the reduced accuracy and precision of using minimal direct [SOURCE: Page_4]

12   measurement with simple extrapolation, or alternatively adopting indirect methods for [SOURCE: Page_4]

13   estimation. [SOURCE: Page_4]

14      In France Bruand et al. (2003) have recently developed pedotransfer functions [SOURCE: Page_4]

15   (PTFs) that use texture and bulk density to predict gravimetric water content at seven [SOURCE: Page_4]

16   matric potentials ranging from -0.10 to -150 m of water. Apart from Bruand (1990), [SOURCE: Page_4]

17   Bruand et al. (1994) and Bruand et al. (2004) there are few other examples of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

18   development of PTFs for French soils. In Australia systems developed for predicting [SOURCE: Page_4]

19   the SWC for Australian soils from morphological data, or from combinations of [SOURCE: Page_4]

20   physical and morphological data, include those of Williams et al. (1992), Cresswell [SOURCE: Page_4]

21   and Paydar (1996), Paydar and Cresswell (1996), Smettem and Gregory (1996) and [SOURCE: Page_4]

22   Minasny et al. (1999). These approaches have often aimed to predict the variables in [SOURCE: Page_4]

23   models of the soil water characteristic. [SOURCE: Page_4]

24      Most mechanistic soil water simulation models adopt one or more closed-form [SOURCE: Page_4]

25   models for describing soil hydraulic properties and using them in solutions of soil [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

1   water flow equations. In Australia the hydraulic models of Campbell (1974) have [SOURCE: Page_5]

2   been widely used both in soil water simulation models and in methods for predicting [SOURCE: Page_5]

3   soil hydraulic properties. The Campbell equations have been favoured due to their [SOURCE: Page_5]

4   simplicity and adequacy of description of measured hydraulic data (e.g. Cresswell and [SOURCE: Page_5]

5   Paydar 1996). The model of van Genuchten (1980) is also in widespread usage. For [SOURCE: Page_5]

6   the use of closed-form equations describing soil hydraulic properties to be effective in [SOURCE: Page_5]

7   either simulation modelling or with continuous PTFs, such equations must give a [SOURCE: Page_5]

8   good description of measured soil hydraulic data. [SOURCE: Page_5]

9      The aim of this work was to use the French soil hydraulic database of Bruand et al. [SOURCE: Page_5]

10   (2003) and: [SOURCE: Page_5]

11   (a) test the utility of closed-form hydraulic models for describing the soil water [SOURCE: Page_5]

12   characteristics of French soils, [SOURCE: Page_5]

13   (b) assess the effectiveness of methods developed in Australia for the prediction of [SOURCE: Page_5]

14   water characteristics of French soils, and [SOURCE: Page_5]

15   (c) suggest a role for such prediction methods within a strategy for the hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_5]

16   characterisation of French soils. [SOURCE: Page_5]

14   France and Australia have similar challenges if they are to achieve detailed soil and [SOURCE: Page_18]

15   land information at scales relevant to management decision making. Earlier work [SOURCE: Page_18]

19   (Bouma 1989) and a series of reference sites where soil hydraulic properties are [SOURCE: Page_18]

20   measured comprehensively. The functional horizon method recognises the soil [SOURCE: Page_18]

21   horizon rather than the profile as the individual or building block for prediction [SOURCE: Page_18]

## 8                                     CONCLUSIONS

9   The three levels of SWC characterisation considered here – fitting to measured () [SOURCE: Page_20]

10   data, ‘two-point’ prediction, and empirical prediction from soil texture and bulk [SOURCE: Page_20]

11   density – all have apparent value when applied to French soils. Methods without an [SOURCE: Page_20]

12   empirical basis should be widely applicable and the analysis here supports this in [SOURCE: Page_20]

13   showing comparable results from fitting Australian and French soil data with the van [SOURCE: Page_20]

14   Genuchten and Campbell SWC equations and from prediction with the two-point [SOURCE: Page_20]

15   method. Empirical prediction methods that use texture and bulk density as inputs [SOURCE: Page_20]

16   would usually be expected to have more limited transferability. The performance of [SOURCE: Page_20]

17   the Williams et al. (1992) SWC prediction method on French soils was surprising [SOURCE: Page_20]

18   given that it was as good as on Australian soils similar to those on which it was [SOURCE: Page_20]

19   developed. Such indirect empirical methods do however lack the accuracy and [SOURCE: Page_20]

20   precision of methods that incorporate measured () points and will probably require [SOURCE: Page_20]

21   local calibration to achieve sufficient accuracy and precision for use in local [SOURCE: Page_20]

22   hydrological analysis. [SOURCE: Page_20]

23      Bulk density is a required input for many of the indirect empirical SWC prediction [SOURCE: Page_20]

24   methods. If core samples or clods are collected in the field for this purpose then it [SOURCE: Page_20]

19 [SOURCE: Page_20]

1   would seem cost effective to measure water retention at least at one matric potential [SOURCE: Page_21]

2   (e.g. -1.0 m) so that the significantly more accurate two point method can be used. [SOURCE: Page_21]

3   The second () point required can be easily determined on laboratory pressure plate [SOURCE: Page_21]

4   apparatus using disturbed soil material at little cost. [SOURCE: Page_21]

5      An efficient strategy for the hydraulic characterisation of soils will combine [SOURCE: Page_21]

6   comprehensive direct measurement at a small number of carefully chosen reference [SOURCE: Page_21]

7   sites, with an intermediate level of direct hydraulic characterisation that includes the [SOURCE: Page_21]

8   inputs for the two-point method (plus soil texture) at many more sites. If indirect [SOURCE: Page_21]

9   empirical pedotransfer functions are used then they can be calibrated locally against [SOURCE: Page_21]

10   the reference site data. [SOURCE: Page_21]

11      Evaluations of the performance and transferability of methods to predict soil [SOURCE: Page_21]

12   hydraulic properties contribute to the design of workable strategies for soil hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_21]

13   characterisation. Description of the functional attributes of our soils and landscapes [SOURCE: Page_21]
