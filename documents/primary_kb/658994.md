9   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

10   Groundwater management policies will need to be revised in many Mediterranean countries, in light [SOURCE: Page_2]

11   of the impact of climate change and the increasing demand for water. In this paper, we analyze [SOURCE: Page_2]

12   stakeholder perceptions of three groundwater policy scenarios which respectively assume a [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   strengthening of state intervention, the introduction of market regulation mechanisms, and the [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   transfer of regulation responsibility to farmers. The method consists of organizing scenario workshops [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   with experts, institutional representatives, and farmers. It is applied in two case studies in France and [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   Portugal. From a methodological viewpoint, the research demonstrates the farmers’ ability to [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   contribute to an exploratory assessment of possible future water management scenarios. From a [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   policy viewpoint it clarifies expectations concerning State intervention and self-regulation by farmers. [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   It also provides some insights regarding the acceptability of tradable groundwater permits in two [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   European contexts. [SOURCE: Page_2]

## 4   INTRODUCTION

5   Over the last several decades, groundwater abstraction has increased considerably in northern [SOURCE: Page_4]

6   Mediterranean countries (EASAC 2010) and even more in southern Mediterranean countries. [SOURCE: Page_4]

7   Government agencies have not paid much attention to this ‘silent revolution’ (Llamas and Garrido [SOURCE: Page_4]

8   2007), and groundwater development has thus taken place in an institutional setting that placed no [SOURCE: Page_4]

9   or few limits on groundwater use (Schlager 2007). As a result, it is only after several decades, and [SOURCE: Page_4]

10   investments made by thousands of farmers, that the consequences of these overdrafts, e.g., sea [SOURCE: Page_4]

11   water intrusion, have started to appear, conflicts are escalating, and that establishing new rules for [SOURCE: Page_4]

12   governing access to groundwater is seen as an absolute necessity. [SOURCE: Page_4]

13   In France and Portugal, the two countries considered in this paper, controlling groundwater [SOURCE: Page_4]

14   abstraction is becoming an significant water policy issue. To comply with the requirements of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

15   European Union Water Framework Directive, water authorities now have to develop a program of [SOURCE: Page_4]

16   actions leading to the “good quantitative status” of all groundwater bodies. One of the first actions [SOURCE: Page_4]

17   implemented has consisted of estimating the maximum inter-annual average volume that can be [SOURCE: Page_4]

18   pumped from a number of critical groundwater basins characterized by a risk of over-exploitation. In [SOURCE: Page_4]

19   parallel, specific efforts are made to complete the registration of all existing boreholes, including [SOURCE: Page_4]

20   illegal ones. The next step for groundwater policy development will consist of developing and [SOURCE: Page_4]

21   implementing new rules for sharing a limited quantity of water among economic sectors, and [SOURCE: Page_4]

22   between users within each sector. Long-term climate change prospects will need to be considered [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

Forthcoming in Irrigation and Drainage – the ICID Journal – Springer (2012) [SOURCE: Page_5]

1   while crafting such new rules for governing groundwater allocation, as they will probably have to be [SOURCE: Page_5]

2   enforced in a context of increased water scarcity (García-Ruiz et al. 2011). [SOURCE: Page_5]

3   For agricultural water users and their institutional representatives, the development and [SOURCE: Page_5]

4   implementation of rules regulating access to and use of groundwater will be perceived as a paradigm [SOURCE: Page_5]

5   shift, since most of them still consider groundwater to be an open-access good. To enhance the [SOURCE: Page_5]

6   adequacy of regulations, as regards equity but also acceptability, and to reduce future [SOURCE: Page_5]

7   implementation and enforcement problems, the stakeholders and users should be involved in the [SOURCE: Page_5]

8   development of such regulations (Ostrom 1990). Their participation is essential because the policy [SOURCE: Page_5]

9   options considered represent drastic changes that will call into question established institutions, [SOURCE: Page_5]

10   societal values, and dominant ideologies and beliefs. Moreover, the practical (lay) knowledge of the [SOURCE: Page_5]

11   stakeholders is essential for crafting rules that can work effectively in the context of local, technical, [SOURCE: Page_5]

12   environmental, institutional, socio-economic, and. more generally cultural constraints (Rinaudo and [SOURCE: Page_5]

13   Garin 2005). There are very few ‘off-the-shelf’ groundwater regulation models that could be [SOURCE: Page_5]

14   imported from other countries without significant adaptation to the local context. This adaptation [SOURCE: Page_5]

15   may require a lot of creativity which is likely to be enhanced by the confrontation of multiple visions [SOURCE: Page_5]

16   of existing constraints and possible futures. [SOURCE: Page_5]

17   This paper aims to contribute to the scientific literature which explores how to craft rules for [SOURCE: Page_5]

18   regulating groundwater usage. It is based on two concrete case studies conducted in France and [SOURCE: Page_5]

19   Portugal, where a similar methodology was used. This consisted of organizing workshops with a [SOURCE: Page_5]

20   group of institutional stakeholders, i.e., local administration representatives, economic actors, NGO’s [SOURCE: Page_5]

21   staff, and scientific experts, and several groups of farmers, during which three contrasted [SOURCE: Page_5]

22   groundwater regulation scenarios were scrutinized and debated. The paper describes both the [SOURCE: Page_5]

23   visions that emerged from the workshops and the social learning process that took place during the [SOURCE: Page_5]

24   workshops. [SOURCE: Page_5]

## 8   METHODOLOGY AND CASE STUDIES

9   Unfolding the future in three steps [SOURCE: Page_6]

10   The common methodology implemented in the French and Portuguese case studies was inspired by [SOURCE: Page_6]

11   the emerging literature on scenario planning and participatory foresight for water resources [SOURCE: Page_6]

12   management (see for instance van der Helm 2003, and Hatzilacou, Kallis et al. 2007). Foresight is [SOURCE: Page_6]

13   defined here as a multi-disciplinary approach whose goal is to help decision-makers explore the [SOURCE: Page_6]

14   uncertainty associated with future developments, and to assess the consequences of the various [SOURCE: Page_6]

15   strategic choices available. The approach here consisted of exploring a limited number of scenarios, [SOURCE: Page_6]

16   each of which describes a possible future institutional set-up for groundwater regulation. The aim [SOURCE: Page_6]

17   here is "to illuminate the choices of the present in the light of possible futures" (Godet and Roubelat [SOURCE: Page_6]

18   1996), rather than to make predictions. [SOURCE: Page_6]

19   A group of institutional stakeholders and several groups of farmers were invited to participate in [SOURCE: Page_6]

20   three series of workshops. In order to enhance the envisioning capacity of the participants, we [SOURCE: Page_6]

21   adopted a sequential approach to future exploration. The first sequence was devoted to the analysis [SOURCE: Page_6]

22   of past trends and the exploration of the future evolution of agricultural production in the case study [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

Forthcoming in Irrigation and Drainage – the ICID Journal – Springer (2012) [SOURCE: Page_7]

1   area, at the 2030 time horizon^1. Discussions focussed on agricultural system dynamics, water being [SOURCE: Page_7]

2   considered only as a factor of production. This sequence aimed at developing temporal awareness. It [SOURCE: Page_7]

3   also helped to reveal the common goals and shared assumptions of the participants, contributed to [SOURCE: Page_7]

4   forging a team spirit, and generated confidence (among the participants, and with the researchers). [SOURCE: Page_7]

5   Water-management-related issues were not specifically debated and nothing in the scenarios [SOURCE: Page_7]

6   discussed in this first phase challenged the established water-management paradigm. [SOURCE: Page_7]

7   The second sequence introduced a major uncertainty with the prospect of climate change. The [SOURCE: Page_7]

8   impacts of climate change on the hydrological cycle were described in detail, using the results of [SOURCE: Page_7]

9   research conducted in the case-study areas. Climate change forecasts introduce an anomaly that [SOURCE: Page_7]

10   challenges the prevailing mind-set and forces participants to imagine a different future in terms of [SOURCE: Page_7]

11   farming practices and water management. In this second sequence, we asked the participants to [SOURCE: Page_7]

12   focus on changes that might occur at the farm level (cropping and irrigation practices) while issues [SOURCE: Page_7]

13   related to water resource management in the river basins were postponed to the third sequence. [SOURCE: Page_7]

14   The third and last sequence was devoted to envisioning possible future groundwater regulation [SOURCE: Page_7]

15   mechanisms, under three alternative models based on (i) strengthened administrative regulation, (ii) [SOURCE: Page_7]

16   market-based regulation, and (iii) self-regulation by the farming community. In this sequence, [SOURCE: Page_7]

17   participants were asked to build on the results of the first two sequences. The objective was to [SOURCE: Page_7]

18   mobilize their analytical and creative capacities and to overcome the limitations imposed by the [SOURCE: Page_7]

19   currently established institutional framework, and also by received values, beliefs, and expectations, [SOURCE: Page_7]

20   when thinking about the future. The present paper focuses on this last sequence; the results of the [SOURCE: Page_7]

21   two others are described elsewhere (Maton et al. 2010, Rinaudo et al. forthcoming). The following [SOURCE: Page_7]

22   paragraphs describe how the groups were established and the workshops organized. [SOURCE: Page_7]

1   Constitution of the group, and workshop organization [SOURCE: Page_8]

2   Although the scenario-workshop method usually involves the mixing of policy makers, business [SOURCE: Page_8]

3   representatives, experts, and citizens (Street 1997, Andersen and Jaeger 1999, Lienert et al. 2006, [SOURCE: Page_8]

4   Hatzilacou et al. 2007), we decided to set up separate groups for various public – institutional [SOURCE: Page_8]

5   representatives and experts on the one hand, and farmers on the other hand. We also opted for [SOURCE: Page_8]

6   smaller groups (five to fifteen persons) in contrast to the scenario groups described in the literature, [SOURCE: Page_8]

7   where each workshop comprises between 25 and 40 persons (Andersen and Jaeger 1999, Hatzilacou [SOURCE: Page_8]

8   et al. 2007, Patel et al. 2007). This methodological choice was adopted to overcome the mistrust [SOURCE: Page_8]

9   which characterizes relationships between farmers and public-sector experts. [SOURCE: Page_8]

10   To facilitate the debates, three contrasted, predefined future water management scenarios were [SOURCE: Page_8]

11   used as material to initiate the debates. Scenario narratives were written by researchers in advance [SOURCE: Page_8]

12   of the workshops, because we assumed that we would not have sufficient time to make trend- [SOURCE: Page_8]

13   breaking scenarios emerge during the 4-5 hour scenario workshops. Each scenario consisted of a [SOURCE: Page_8]

14   narrative describing groundwater management rules in 2030-2040 (see next section for a detailed [SOURCE: Page_8]

15   presentation of the scenarios). The narratives used in Portugal and France were consistent, although [SOURCE: Page_8]

16   adapted to the specific regional contexts. The three scenarios were clearly identified as starting [SOURCE: Page_8]

17   points for discussion, which participants should deconstruct, criticize, and adapt according to their [SOURCE: Page_8]

18   views. The workshops were tape-recorded to allow detailed analysis of the views expressed by [SOURCE: Page_8]

19   participants. [SOURCE: Page_8]

20   In France, discussions of the scenarios took place during workshops held over a half-day period in the [SOURCE: Page_8]

21   evening (6 to 11 PM), including a meal for the farmers. The workshops were organized as follows. [SOURCE: Page_8]

22   After a brief presentation of the workshop’s objectives, the groundwater-management scenarios [SOURCE: Page_8]

23   were successively presented, using posters as support. After each presentation, the participants were [SOURCE: Page_8]

24   invited to comment individually during a round-table session before discussing any conflicting points [SOURCE: Page_8]

7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

1   of view. After all the scenarios had been discussed, the participants were invited to prepare [SOURCE: Page_9]

2   individual rankings of the three scenarios according to their probability and desirability. Comparison [SOURCE: Page_9]

3   of these pictures of the probable and desirable futures gave rise to a final group discussion. [SOURCE: Page_9]

4   A similar approach was implemented in the Portuguese case study. Prior to debating the scenarios, [SOURCE: Page_9]

5   the research team presented the current administrative framework of water management. Then a [SOURCE: Page_9]

6   reference scenario was presented followed by three separate presentations of the alternative [SOURCE: Page_9]

7   groundwater-regulation scenarios, and each was discussed first in small groups and then in a round- [SOURCE: Page_9]

8   table format. The issues of probability and desirability for each of these three scenarios were also [SOURCE: Page_9]

9   discussed. Whereas the farmers were invited to a workshop, the experts and institutional [SOURCE: Page_9]

10   stakeholders were met individually in face-to-face interviews. In both case studies, a final meeting [SOURCE: Page_9]

11   was then organized with farmers and institutional stakeholders together. [SOURCE: Page_9]

12   A key feature of our approach is that, since the workshops were organized in a research context, the [SOURCE: Page_9]

13   aim was to encourage the expression of various pictures of the future, and to facilitate the mutual [SOURCE: Page_9]

14   discovery of differing points of view concerning the future development of agriculture, and the [SOURCE: Page_9]

15   associated irrigation-water demand and water-management frameworks. The posture adopted by [SOURCE: Page_9]

16   the team was thus exploratory and not normative. [SOURCE: Page_9]
