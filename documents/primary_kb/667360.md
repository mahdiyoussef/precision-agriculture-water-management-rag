24   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

25          The main goal of this research was to evaluate the potential of the dual approach of [SOURCE: Page_3]

26   FAO-56 for estimating actual crop evapotranspiration (AET) and its components (crop [SOURCE: Page_3]

27   transpiration and soil evaporation) of an olive (Olea europaea L.) orchard in the semi arid [SOURCE: Page_3]

28   region of Tensift-basin (central of Morocco). Two years (2003 and 2004) of continuous [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   measurements of AET with the eddy covariance technique were used to test the performance [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   of the model. The results showed that, by using the local values of basal crop coefficients, the [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   approach simulates reasonably well AET over two growing seasons. The Root Mean Square [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   Error (RMSE) between measured and simulated AET values during 2003 and 2004 were [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   respectively about 0.54 and 0.71 mm per day. The obtained value of basal crop coefficient [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   ( K cb ) for the olive orchard was similar in both seasons with an average of 0.54. This value [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   was lower than that suggested by the FAO-56 (0.62). Similarly, the single approach of FAO- [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   56 has been tested in the previous work (Er-Raki et al., 2008) over the same study site and it [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   has been shown that this approach also simulates correctly AET when using the local crop [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   coefficient and under no stress conditions. [SOURCE: Page_3]

39          Since the dual approach predicts separately soil evaporation and plant transpiration, an [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   attempt was made to compare the simulated components of AET with measurements obtained [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   through a combination of eddy covariance and scaled-up sap flow measurements. The results [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   showed that the model gives an acceptable estimate of plant transpiration and soil [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   evaporation. The associated RMSE of plant transpiration and soil evaporation were 0.59 and [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   0.73 mm per day, respectively. [SOURCE: Page_3]

45          Additionally, the irrigation efficiency was investigated by comparing the irrigation [SOURCE: Page_3]

46   scheduling design used by the farmer to those recommended by the FAO model. It was found [SOURCE: Page_3]

47   that although the amount of irrigation applied by the farmer (800 mm) during the growing [SOURCE: Page_3]

48   season of olives was twice that recommended one by the FAO model (411 mm), the [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

49   vegetation suffered from water stress during the summer. Such behaviour can be explained by [SOURCE: Page_4]

50   inadequate distribution of irrigation. Consequently, the FAO model can be considered as a [SOURCE: Page_4]

51   potentially useful tool for planning irrigation schedules on an operational basis. [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   Keywords: Crop coefficient, Evapotranspiration, Eddy covariance, FAO-56 model, Olea [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   europaea, Sap flow. [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_5]

55          Regions classified as semi-arid or arid constitute roughly one third of the total global [SOURCE: Page_5]

56   land cover. Within these regions, water scarcity is one of the main factors limiting agricultural [SOURCE: Page_5]

57   development. The impact of such water scarcity is amplified by inefficient irrigation practices, [SOURCE: Page_5]

58   especially since the irrigation consumes more than 85% of the available water in these regions [SOURCE: Page_5]

59   (Chehbouni et al., 2008). Therefore, the first step toward sound management of the scarce [SOURCE: Page_5]

60   water resources in these regions requires an accurate estimation of the water needs and [SOURCE: Page_5]

61   consumption of irrigated agriculture. The crop water need is defined as the amount of water [SOURCE: Page_5]

62   needed to meet the amount of water lost to the atmosphere through evapotranspiration. [SOURCE: Page_5]

63          During the last two decades, several models have been developed to simulate crop [SOURCE: Page_5]

64   evapotranspiration (ET) and in some cases, its components (soil evaporation and plant [SOURCE: Page_5]

65   transpiration). These models ranged from complex, physically based ones such as the Simple [SOURCE: Page_5]

66   Soil Plant Atmosphere SiSPAT (Braud et al., 1995), ISBA (Noilhan and Mahfouf, 1996), to [SOURCE: Page_5]

67   more simple and conceptual ones (Sinclair and Seligman, 1996; Olioso et al., 1999) such as [SOURCE: Page_5]

68   SVAT simple (Boulet et al., 2000). Other models such as STICS (Brisson, 1998) or CERRES [SOURCE: Page_5]

69   (Ritchie, 1986) simulate ET through the combination of a water balance with a crop growth [SOURCE: Page_5]

70   component. All of these models need several input parameters which cannot easily be [SOURCE: Page_5]

71   obtained at the appropriate space-time scale, and therefore difficult for operational [SOURCE: Page_5]

72   applications. [SOURCE: Page_5]

73          In addition to the above models/methods, the FAO-56 approach has been extensively [SOURCE: Page_5]

74   used to derive ET and schedule irrigation on an operational basis. This approach is often [SOURCE: Page_5]

75   preferred due to its simplicity and its robustness for operational applications. It requires fewer [SOURCE: Page_5]

76   input data, and provides acceptable ET estimates when compared to heavily parameterized [SOURCE: Page_5]

77   physically-based models (Evett et al., 1995; Kite and Droogers, 2000; Eitzinger et al., 2002), [SOURCE: Page_5]

78   to ground measurement (e.g. Paço et al., 2006; Er-Raki et al., 2007, 2008, 2009; Liu and Luo, [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

79   2010) or to satellite measurements (e.g. Allen, 2000; Kite and Droogers, 2000; Duchemin et [SOURCE: Page_6]

80   al., 2006; Er-Raki et al., 2006, 2010). FAO-56 is based on the concepts of reference [SOURCE: Page_6]

81   evapotranspiration ET 0 and crop coefficients K c , which have been introduced to separate the [SOURCE: Page_6]

82   climatic demand from the plant response (Allen et al., 1998). There are two approaches to [SOURCE: Page_6]

83   estimate crop evapotranspiration: the single and the dual crop coefficients. The FAO-56 dual [SOURCE: Page_6]

84   crop coefficient approach (Allen et al., 1998) describes the relationship between maximal [SOURCE: Page_6]

85   evapotranspiration ET c and reference evapotranspiration ET 0 by separating the single K c [SOURCE: Page_6]

86   into the basal crop K cb and soil water evaporation K e coefficients, while in the FAO-56 [SOURCE: Page_6]

87   single crop coefficient approach, the effect of both crop transpiration and soil evaporation are [SOURCE: Page_6]

88   integrated into a single crop coefficient. Many studies have focused on the application of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

89   single approach for determining olive water requirement within Mediterranean regions (e.g. [SOURCE: Page_6]

90   Palomo et al., 2002; Abid Karray et al., 2008; Martınez-Cob and Faci, 2010). In semi-arid [SOURCE: Page_6]

91   Mediterranean region of southern Morocco, Er-Raki et al. (2008) applied also the single [SOURCE: Page_6]

92   approach over the same study site of this work, and they found that the approach [SOURCE: Page_6]

93   overestimates AET by about 18% when using the crop coefficient suggested by Allen et al. [SOURCE: Page_6]

94   (1998). Knowing that the flood irrigation is the most widely used method by the majority of [SOURCE: Page_6]

95   the farmers in Morocco (as the case of our study site), which accompanied with a large [SOURCE: Page_6]

96   amount of the water lost through direct soil evaporation (Yunusa et al., 1997), it is worthwhile [SOURCE: Page_6]

97   to estimate this substantial amount. This can be achieved by partitioning ET into soil and plant [SOURCE: Page_6]

98   components, which is the main objective of this present study. For the FAO-56 dual crop [SOURCE: Page_6]

99   coefficient approach, it has been tested for many crops (bean, corn, and sugar beet) at [SOURCE: Page_6]

100   Kimberly, Idaho (Allen et al., 1996) and tomato and cotton at Fresno, California by Itenfisu [SOURCE: Page_6]

101   (1998). As reported by Allen (2000), the comparison between estimated and measured ET by [SOURCE: Page_6]

102   precision weighing lysimeters in all situations gives an error less than 10% for daily ET [SOURCE: Page_6]

103   estimates. Allen (1999) applied the FAO-56 procedure to a 200,000 ha irrigation project in [SOURCE: Page_6]

5 [SOURCE: Page_6]

104   California to compare the estimated ET with that determined by water balance, and the results [SOURCE: Page_7]

105   showed that despite the simplicity of the FAO model, estimates of ET were reasonable with a [SOURCE: Page_7]

106   good accuracy (overestimation of 6%). Allen (2000) also applied his methodology to an [SOURCE: Page_7]

107   extensive multiple-cropped surface, and compared the estimated ET with one obtained by [SOURCE: Page_7]

108   remote sensing. Results have shown that the FAO-56 approach overestimated ET by more [SOURCE: Page_7]

109   than 20%. Recently, several studies used the FAO-56 dual crop coefficient for estimating [SOURCE: Page_7]

110   water consumptions of different crops (Allen et al., 2005 a, b; Hunsaker et al., 2003, 2005; [SOURCE: Page_7]

111   Paço et al., 2006; Er-Raki et al., 2007). Some of these studies adopted the FAO-56 dual [SOURCE: Page_7]

112   approach to use satellite based vegetation index (Hunsaker et al., 2003, 2005; Er-Raki et al., [SOURCE: Page_7]

113   2007; González-Dugo and Mateo, 2008; Er-Raki et al., 2010). The results show that relating [SOURCE: Page_7]

114   the basal crop coefficient K cb to remotely sensed vegetation index greatly improves the [SOURCE: Page_7]

115   performance of FAO-56 method. However, Er-Raki et al. (2006) showed that the performance [SOURCE: Page_7]

116   of the FAO-56 method has some limitations when there is high soil evaporation or when [SOURCE: Page_7]

117   stress occurs. To overcome this problem and then enhance the FAO-56 performances, ET [SOURCE: Page_7]

118   derived from thermal infrared (TIR) observations was assimilated into FAO-56 single source [SOURCE: Page_7]

119   model (Er-Raki et al., 2008) in order to estimate accurately the water consumption of olive [SOURCE: Page_7]

120   orchards in the semi-arid region of the Tensift basin (central of Morocco). [SOURCE: Page_7]

121          Several studies have been specifically dedicated to estimate ET over olive trees. [SOURCE: Page_7]

122   Villalobos et al. (2000) determined olive ET by the estimation of its two components, through [SOURCE: Page_7]

123   a combination of a transpiration model based on the equation of Penmann-Monteith [SOURCE: Page_7]

124   (Monteith, 1965) and a soil evaporation model similar to that of Ritchie (1972). Palomo et al. [SOURCE: Page_7]

125   (2002) used a water balance approach, to determine water consumption in olive orchards. [SOURCE: Page_7]

126   Among the several disadvantages of this approach, summarized by Fernández and Moreno [SOURCE: Page_7]

127   (1999), is the variability of the hydraulic properties of the soil profile, such as the hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_7]

128   conductivity-soil water content relationship. Recently, Testi et al. (2004) used the eddy- [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

129   covariance technique to obtain direct estimates of ET of young irrigated olive orchards in [SOURCE: Page_8]

130   southern Spain. In the context of SudMed project (Chehbouni et al., 2008), Williams et al. [SOURCE: Page_8]

131   (2004) used the sap flow method combined with the isotopic method to estimate plant [SOURCE: Page_8]

132   transpiration and soil evaporation over olive orchards in southern Morocco. [SOURCE: Page_8]

133          Estimating total ET over olive orchards has been investigated by many studies in semi [SOURCE: Page_8]

134   arid regions (e.g. Fernández et al., 2001; Testi et al., 2004; Ezzahar et al., 2007; Er-Raki et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

135   2008), but there is little information on the partitioning of the relevant components of ET in [SOURCE: Page_8]

136   such areas; this is one of the most important ecohydrological challenges in understanding [SOURCE: Page_8]

137   water exchange and vegetation dynamics in arid and semi arid ecosystems (Reynolds et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

138   2000; Huxman et al., 2005). Partitioning ET is possible by using a combination of micro- [SOURCE: Page_8]

139   meteorological measurements (e.g. Bowen ratio, eddy covariance system), and eco- [SOURCE: Page_8]

140   physiological techniques (e.g. sap flow, stable isotopes) (Williams et al., 2004; Yepez et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

141   2005; Scott et al., 2006). However, these methods are expensive and difficult to deploy and [SOURCE: Page_8]

142   maintain in both time and space. Recently, Moran et al. (2009) developed an operational [SOURCE: Page_8]

143   approach for partitioning ET with a minimal cost and suitable for operation over long time [SOURCE: Page_8]

144   periods. This approach is based on the difference between the mid-afternoon and pre-dawn [SOURCE: Page_8]

145   soil surface temperature, which is considered as the indicator of the soil evaporation. In the [SOURCE: Page_8]

146   same context, the present study aims to use an operational model of FAO-56 dual crop [SOURCE: Page_8]

147   coefficient approach for partitioning ET. We first compared actual ET derived from this [SOURCE: Page_8]

148   approach to that measured using an eddy covariance device, then the issue of the ability of [SOURCE: Page_8]

149   this approach to provide accurate estimates of components of AET through a comparison of [SOURCE: Page_8]

150   field data obtained from a combination of eddy covariance based AET measurements and [SOURCE: Page_8]

151   spatialized or scaled-up sap flow measurements of transpiration. In this context the objectives [SOURCE: Page_8]

152   of this study were: [SOURCE: Page_8]

7 [SOURCE: Page_8]

153          1. to analyze the ability of the FAO-56 dual crop coefficient model to reproduce the [SOURCE: Page_9]

154              temporal evolution of evapotranspiration and its components: plant transpiration [SOURCE: Page_9]

155              and soil evaporation. Actual plant transpiration was compared with scaled-up sap [SOURCE: Page_9]

156              flow measurements. [SOURCE: Page_9]

157          2. to estimate the most adequate water quantity needed for the olive and to determine [SOURCE: Page_9]

158              the best timing of irrigation by using the FAO-dual approach. [SOURCE: Page_9]

159          This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents a description of study site and [SOURCE: Page_9]

160   data collected during the 2003 and 2004 growing season. In Section 3, we provide a brief [SOURCE: Page_9]

161   theoretical overview of the FAO-56 dual crop coefficient approach. Section 4 presents an [SOURCE: Page_9]

162   application of this approach over an olive site. Also in this section, we analyze the ability of [SOURCE: Page_9]

163   this approach to reproduce the temporal evolution of both components of evapotranspiration [SOURCE: Page_9]

164   (plant transpiration and soil evaporation), and to derive the soil water stress coefficient K s of [SOURCE: Page_9]

165   olives orchards during growing season in order to determine the best timing of irrigation. In [SOURCE: Page_9]

166   the final section, summary and conclusion are provided. [SOURCE: Page_9]

233   2.2.2. Sap flow measurements [SOURCE: Page_12]

234   Heat-pulse sap flow sensors (Heat Ratio Method, HRM, Burgess et al., 2001) were used to [SOURCE: Page_12]

235   measure xylem sap flux on eight olive trees. The HRM method has been described in detail in [SOURCE: Page_12]

236   Burgess et al. (1998) and Burgess et al. (2001). Briefly, the HRM is a modification of the [SOURCE: Page_12]

237   Heat Pulse Method (HPM) and it employs temperature probes inserted into the active xylem [SOURCE: Page_12]

238   at equal distances down- and upstream from a heat source. This method improves on the HPM [SOURCE: Page_12]

239   by its precision at very slow flow rates and even reverses sap flow can be measured. [SOURCE: Page_12]

240   Reliability of this technique for determining transpiration has been demonstrated by several [SOURCE: Page_12]

241   studies (Burgess et al., 2001; Fernández et al., 2001 and Williams et al., 2004). The heat pulse [SOURCE: Page_12]

242   sensors were installed on four large single-stemmed and on four large multi-stemmed trees [SOURCE: Page_12]

243   adjacent to the eddy covariance tower during the summer of 2003 and 2004. Sap flow [SOURCE: Page_12]

244   measurements were conducted from the 14th of June (DOY 165) through the 30th of July [SOURCE: Page_12]

245   (DOY 211) during 2003 and from the 9th of May (DOY 130) through the 28th of September [SOURCE: Page_12]

246   (DOY 272) during 2004. Missing data in some days (from DOY 183 to DOY 194 and from [SOURCE: Page_12]

247   DOY 222 to DOY 224) is due to problems with the power supply. The same measurements of [SOURCE: Page_12]

248   sap flow, with the same sensors and over the same field have been made by Williams et al. [SOURCE: Page_12]

249   (2004) except in other climatic conditions (during the winter). A detailed description of HRM [SOURCE: Page_12]

250   technique and the principle of measurements can be found in Williams et al. (2004). [SOURCE: Page_12]

525   irrigations are missing or unnecessary. For example on DOY 201 (July, 19), the model [SOURCE: Page_24]

526   recommends the irrigation while the farmer didn‘t apply it in this day. Effectively, in this [SOURCE: Page_24]

527   period, the irrigation is needed because the climatic demand was very higher in the summer. [SOURCE: Page_24]

528   During the rainfall period (e.g. DOY 169), the irrigation is not necessary, but the farmer apply [SOURCE: Page_24]

529   one. The results revealed that the method of irrigation applied by the farmer was not [SOURCE: Page_24]

530   appropriate for the olive orchards in the Tensift plain. It would be advisable to improve the [SOURCE: Page_24]

531   irrigation management and to recommend the farmer to use the simple FAO model, which can [SOURCE: Page_24]

532   be considered as a potentially useful tool for planning irrigation schedules on an operational [SOURCE: Page_24]

533   basis. [SOURCE: Page_24]

534   5. Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_24]

535            The main objective of this study was to investigate the potential of the FAO-56 dual [SOURCE: Page_24]

536   crop coefficient approach to provide accurate estimates of actual evapotranspiration (AET) [SOURCE: Page_24]

537   and its components of the olive orchard in semi-arid region. Model simulations of evaporation [SOURCE: Page_24]

538   and transpiration were compared to data obtained from a combination of eddy covariance [SOURCE: Page_24]

539   based AET measurements and scaled-up sap flow measurements of transpiration. [SOURCE: Page_24]

540            The results showed that, by using the local values of basal crop coefficients derived [SOURCE: Page_24]

541   from sap flow measurements, the approach simulates reasonably well AET over two growing [SOURCE: Page_24]

542   seasons. The Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) between measured and simulated AET values [SOURCE: Page_24]

543   during 2003 and 2004 were respectively about 0.54 and 0.71 mm per day. The value of basal [SOURCE: Page_24]

23 [SOURCE: Page_24]

544   crop coefficient for the olive orchard used in this study was about 0.54. This value was lower [SOURCE: Page_25]

545   than that suggested by the FAO-56 (0.62). [SOURCE: Page_25]

546          Since the FAO-56 dual crop coefficient approach predicts separately soil evaporation [SOURCE: Page_25]

547   and plant transpiration, an attempt of comparison of the simulated components of [SOURCE: Page_25]

548   evapotranspiration (soil evaporation and plant transpiration) with the measurements showed [SOURCE: Page_25]

549   that the model gives an acceptable estimate of plant transpiration and soil evaporation. The [SOURCE: Page_25]

550   RMSE between measured and simulated transpiration and soil evaporation (resp) were 0.59 [SOURCE: Page_25]

551   and 0.73 mm per day. In conclusion, it can be stated that the FAO-56 dual crop coefficient [SOURCE: Page_25]

552   gives an encouraging result for partitioning of evaporation and transpiration despite the [SOURCE: Page_25]

553   simplicity of the formulation used to derive such partition. Further effort will be necessary if [SOURCE: Page_25]

554   more accurate simulation of ET partitioning is needed by this approach. This can be achieved [SOURCE: Page_25]

555   by subdividing the plant transpiration into olive transpiration and understory transpiration and [SOURCE: Page_25]

556   so two water balance are necessary for calculation stress coefficient K s . [SOURCE: Page_25]

557          Additionally, the results of this study revealed that the irrigation scheme used by the [SOURCE: Page_25]

558   farmer was not appropriate for the olive orchards in the Tensift plain. It was found that [SOURCE: Page_25]

559   although the amount of irrigation applied by the farmer (800 mm) during the growing season [SOURCE: Page_25]

560   of olive orchards was greater than the simulated one by the FAO model (411 mm), the [SOURCE: Page_25]

561   vegetation suffered from water stress especially during the summer. Such behaviour can be [SOURCE: Page_25]

562   explained by inadequate distribution of irrigation. It would be advisable to improve the [SOURCE: Page_25]

563   irrigation management and to recommend the farmer to use the simple and operational FAO [SOURCE: Page_25]

564   model for planning irrigation schedules. [SOURCE: Page_25]
