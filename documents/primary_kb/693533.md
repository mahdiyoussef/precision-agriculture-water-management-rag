12   Abstract. The objective of this study is to develop an approach for monitoring the [SOURCE: Page_1]

13   land use over the semi-arid Tensift-Marrakech plain, a 3000 km² intensively cropped [SOURCE: Page_1]

14   area in Morocco. In this objective, the linear unmixing method is adapted to process a 6- [SOURCE: Page_1]

15   year archive of MODIS NDVI 16-day composite data at 250 m spatial resolution. The [SOURCE: Page_1]

16   result of the processing is a description of land use in term of fractions of three [SOURCE: Page_1]

17   predominant classes: orchard, non-cultivated areas and annual crop. The typical [SOURCE: Page_1]

18   signatures of land classes – endmembers – are retrieved on a yearly basis using an [SOURCE: Page_1]

19   automated algorithm that detects the most pure pixels in the study area. The algorithm [SOURCE: Page_1]

20   first extracts typical NDVI profiles as potential endmembers, then selects the profiles [SOURCE: Page_1]

21   that have the best ability to reproduce the variability of MODIS NDVI time series [SOURCE: Page_1]

1 [SOURCE: Page_1]

1   within all the study area. The endmembers appear stable over the 6 years of study and [SOURCE: Page_2]

2   coherent with the vegetation seasonality of the three targeted land classes. Validation [SOURCE: Page_2]

3   data allows to quantify the error on land class fractions to about 0.1 at 1 km resolution. [SOURCE: Page_2]

4   Land use fraction maps are consistent in space and time: the orchard class is stable, and [SOURCE: Page_2]

5   differences in water availability (irrigation and rainfall) partly explain a part of the inter- [SOURCE: Page_2]

6   annual variations observed for the annual crop class. The advantages and drawbacks of [SOURCE: Page_2]

7   the approach are discussed in the conclusion. [SOURCE: Page_2]

11   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

12 [SOURCE: Page_2]

13   Changes in Land Use and Land Cover (LULC) is a major issue in Environmental [SOURCE: Page_2]

14   Science, interconnected with many question concerning climate change, carbon cycle [SOURCE: Page_2]

15   and biodiversity (Aspinall and Justice 2003; Lepers et al. 2005). The monitoring of [SOURCE: Page_2]

16   LULC is also vital for managers and policy makers to make informed decisions [SOURCE: Page_2]

17   regarding the sustainability of agriculture and provision of safe drinking water, [SOURCE: Page_2]

18   especially in semi-arid areas. Remote sensing is very well-suited to achieve this [SOURCE: Page_2]

19   monitoring since it allows observations regularly distributed in space and time (Rogan [SOURCE: Page_2]

20   and Chen 2004, Prenzel 2004). [SOURCE: Page_2]

21 [SOURCE: Page_2]

22   Multi-temporal images are widely investigated for mapping and monitoring land-cover [SOURCE: Page_2]

23   and land-use changes. At the present time, time series of images can be obtained at a [SOURCE: Page_2]

24   high spatial resolution by programming a series of SPOT or FORMOSAT-2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

1   acquisitions. These images with both high spatial resolution (~10 m) and high temporal [SOURCE: Page_3]

2   repetitivity (a few days) offer strong opportunities to monitor land surfaces over small [SOURCE: Page_3]

3   areas: 25x^25 km² for FORMOSAT-2, 60x^60 km² for SPOT. However, constraints [SOURCE: Page_3]

4   related to acquisition, cost and processing often prevent the use of high spatial [SOURCE: Page_3]

5   resolution data. Multi-temporal data acquired by low or moderate spatial resolution [SOURCE: Page_3]

6   sensors such as NOAA-AVHRR, SPOT- VEGETATION or TERRA-MODIS are thus [SOURCE: Page_3]

7   preferred for regional and continental studies (e.g. Lambin and Ehrlich 1997, Hansen et [SOURCE: Page_3]

8   al. 2000; Lunetta et al. 2006, Matsuoka et al. 2007; Stibig et al. 2007). Indeed, they [SOURCE: Page_3]

9   offer a costless global coverage of the Earth on a daily basis. However, the spatial [SOURCE: Page_3]

10   resolution of large field of views sensors – from 250m for MODIS to 1 km for [SOURCE: Page_3]

11   VEGETATION and AVHRR – is generally much higher than the size of homogeneous [SOURCE: Page_3]

12   areas (units) at the Earth surfaces. These sensors generally provide images with pixels [SOURCE: Page_3]

13   that include a mixture of different units (mixed pixels). Consequently, the use of low [SOURCE: Page_3]

14   spatial resolution data for a directly monitoring of LULC is not straightforward. [SOURCE: Page_3]

15   Furthermore, conventional classification approaches based on signature clustering (like [SOURCE: Page_3]

16   maximum likelihood, Richards 1999) are not suitable since they aim to identify an [SOURCE: Page_3]

17   unique class for each pixel. [SOURCE: Page_3]

18 [SOURCE: Page_3]

19   For these reasons, the linear unmixing model has been developed (Adams et al. 1986, [SOURCE: Page_3]

20   Smith et al. 1990, Elmore et al. 2000) based on the following assumption: the signature [SOURCE: Page_3]

21   of a mixed pixel results from a linear combination of the distinctive signatures [SOURCE: Page_3]

22   (endmembers) that are representative of the various land surfaces included in the study [SOURCE: Page_3]

23   area. These typical signatures must describe as well as possible a pure component [SOURCE: Page_3]

24   having meaningful features for an observer (Strahler et al. 1986). Knowing these [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

1   signatures is a prerequisite for applying the linear unmixing model (Cross et al. 1991, [SOURCE: Page_4]

2   Quarmby et al. 1992, Foody and Cox 1994, Milton and Emery 1995). Unmixing [SOURCE: Page_4]

3   approaches can be divided into two categories depending on how the endmembers are [SOURCE: Page_4]

4   estimated: [SOURCE: Page_4]

5   •   Supervised approaches use the spectral signatures of endmembers as a priori [SOURCE: Page_4]

6   information. These typical spectra can be collected at field or laboratory to define [SOURCE: Page_4]

7   predefined library endmembers (Adams et al. 1995, Roberts et al. 1998, Smith et al. [SOURCE: Page_4]

8   1990). They can also be derived from high spatial images using a training data set [SOURCE: Page_4]

9   (small region where the land use is known). The use of predefined libraries may be not [SOURCE: Page_4]

10   appropriate since differences in the acquisition conditions (e.g. sun-target-sensor [SOURCE: Page_4]

11   geometry, atmospheric effects) may occur between endmembers and the data to be [SOURCE: Page_4]

12   unmixed (Song and Woodcock 2003). [SOURCE: Page_4]

13   •   In unsupervised approaches (see Plaza et al. 2004 for a review), the identification of [SOURCE: Page_4]

14   endmembers is automated. The common point in unsupervised algorithms is that they [SOURCE: Page_4]

15   search endmembers directly from images (Atkinson et al. 1997, Elmore et al. 2000, [SOURCE: Page_4]

16   Ridd 1995, Wessman et al. 1997). In this case, the endmembers are retrieved at the [SOURCE: Page_4]

17   same scale and conditions than the data to be unmixed. [SOURCE: Page_4]

18 [SOURCE: Page_4]

19   The temporal variability of the observations is generally not considered in the above- [SOURCE: Page_4]

20   mentioned studies, though it is also an important source of information. In particular, [SOURCE: Page_4]

21   the time courses of vegetation indices such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation [SOURCE: Page_4]

22   Index NDVI allow to monitor the phenology of vegetation (Gutman and Ignatov 1995, [SOURCE: Page_4]

23   Justice et al. 1998, Duchemin et al. 1999). This may be very useful for discriminating [SOURCE: Page_4]

4 [SOURCE: Page_4]

1    land classes. Differences in phenology depicted by vegetation indices can be used to [SOURCE: Page_5]

2    map land surfaces using low spatial resolution data (e.g. Kerdiles and Grondona 1995, [SOURCE: Page_5]

3    Cardot and Faivre 2003, Ballantine et al. 2005, Knight et al. 2006). These studies [SOURCE: Page_5]

4    showed that: 1) land use maps are more accurate when vegetation indices are used [SOURCE: Page_5]

5    instead of reflectances; 2) the use of NDVI with a linear approximation for its [SOURCE: Page_5]

6   combination results in minor inaccuracies; 3) linear unmixing provides satisfactory [SOURCE: Page_5]

7   results when the number of endmembers is limited. These considerations, which are of [SOURCE: Page_5]

8   prime importance in unmixing procedure, are accounted for in this study. [SOURCE: Page_5]

9 [SOURCE: Page_5]

10   In this context, the primary objective of this study is to evaluate the potential of MODIS [SOURCE: Page_5]

11   data for monitoring the land use on the semi-arid Tensift/Marrakech plain. A secondary [SOURCE: Page_5]

12   objective is to analyse the space-time variability of land classes in relation with water [SOURCE: Page_5]

13   availability. The methodology is based on the unmixing of MODIS multi-temporal [SOURCE: Page_5]

14   NDVI images. Land use maps are evaluated using ground data and high spatial [SOURCE: Page_5]

15   resolution images, and their space-time variability is analysed together with information [SOURCE: Page_5]

16   on irrigation water. [SOURCE: Page_5]

19   2. Research Design [SOURCE: Page_5]

20 [SOURCE: Page_5]

21   The methodology is an unsupervised unmixing approach based on a statistical analysis [SOURCE: Page_5]

22   for identifying endmembers directly from MODIS multi-temporal images at 250 spatial [SOURCE: Page_5]

23   resolution (MOD13Q$_1$ product, i.e. 16-day NDVI composite images by CV-MVC [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

1   algorithm, Huete et al. 2002). The algorithm first extracts typical NDVI profiles, then [SOURCE: Page_6]

2   selects the endmembers amongst these profiles based on their ability to reproduce the [SOURCE: Page_6]

3   space time variability of MODIS NDVI time series. The approach requires the two [SOURCE: Page_6]

4   following assumptions: (1) pure pixels can be identified at the 250m resolution and [SOURCE: Page_6]

5   (2) endmembers are stationary over the Tensift-Marrakech plain. [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

7   The approach is set up to retrieve the fractions (surface covered by homogeneous units [SOURCE: Page_6]

8   within each pixel) of three classes: orchard, non-cultivated areas and annual crop. These [SOURCE: Page_6]

9   classes are predominant in the study area, they display distinct phenological features [SOURCE: Page_6]

10   and they encompass the range of crop water needs: non-cultivated areas (no needs), [SOURCE: Page_6]

11   annual crops (water needs ~ 400 mm/y) and orchards (water needs ~ 1000 mm/y). [SOURCE: Page_6]

12 [SOURCE: Page_6]

13   The algorithm is applied to a six-year archive of MODIS NDVI to obtain maps of land [SOURCE: Page_6]

14   use fractions on a yearly basis, from agricultural season 2000-2001 to 2005-2006. The [SOURCE: Page_6]

15   algorithm is applied on two different areas, the whole study area and a subpart of the [SOURCE: Page_6]

16   study area where the landscape is rather regular and where more data are available for [SOURCE: Page_6]

17   evaluation. The processing results in 12 land use maps (6 years x 2 training areas) in [SOURCE: Page_6]

18   term of the fractions of the three predominant classes (orchard, non-cultivated areas and [SOURCE: Page_6]

19   annual crop). [SOURCE: Page_6]

20 [SOURCE: Page_6]

21   MODIS estimates are quantitatively evaluated against ground truth collected on a 9 km² [SOURCE: Page_6]

22   area and a reference land use map derived from a time series of high spatial resolution [SOURCE: Page_6]

23   images (SPOT and Landsat). These data were collected during the 2002-2003 [SOURCE: Page_6]

24   agricultural season. The evaluation is based on classical statistical variables (correlation [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

1   R², efficiency EFF, RMSE and bias) computed between land use fractions estimated [SOURCE: Page_7]

2   with MODIS and derived from the validation data sets at 1 km resolution. In order to [SOURCE: Page_7]

3   test the robustness of the algorithm, the performance of the algorithm is also discussed [SOURCE: Page_7]

4   from the results obtained with the whole MODIS data set (2000-2006 period). Here we [SOURCE: Page_7]

5   analyse the inter-annual variability of both endmembers and land use maps using [SOURCE: Page_7]

6   rainfall and irrigation data as an indicator of water availability and vegetation growth. [SOURCE: Page_7]

10   5. Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_25]

11 [SOURCE: Page_25]

12   In this study, we investigate the potential of time series of MODIS data (MOD13Q$_1$ [SOURCE: Page_25]

13   product, i.e. 16-day NDVI composite images by CVMVC algorithm, Huete et al. 2002) [SOURCE: Page_25]

14   to monitor the land-use of the Tensift plain, a semi-arid region located in the [SOURCE: Page_25]

15   surrounding of the Marrakech city. MODIS data offers a costless coverage of the Earth [SOURCE: Page_25]

16   with a high temporal resolution, but its spatial resolution (250m) is large compared to [SOURCE: Page_25]

17   the average field size in the study site. Thus, we develop an approach based on the [SOURCE: Page_25]

18   linear unmixing of multi-temporal MODIS data. In this approach, the identification of [SOURCE: Page_25]

19   endmembers - key point in linear unmixing - is performed on an annual basis following [SOURCE: Page_25]

20   a two-step procedure: 1) pixels are grouped according to the vegetation seasonality; [SOURCE: Page_25]

21   (2) the set of groups that displays the best ability to explain all NDVI time courses are [SOURCE: Page_25]

22   automatically extracted using a statistical analysis. Some advantages can be mentioned [SOURCE: Page_25]

23   here. Firstly, there is no need of extra information such as a training set where the land [SOURCE: Page_25]

24   use is known. Secondly, there are no substantial differences in the acquisition conditions [SOURCE: Page_25]

25 [SOURCE: Page_25]

1    between endmembers and the data that are unmixed. Thirdly, the regional conditions on [SOURCE: Page_26]

2    which the vegetation growth (e.g. dry or humid year) are integrated to the endmembers. [SOURCE: Page_26]

3 [SOURCE: Page_26]

4    This procedure provides a continuous description of the land use in term of fractions of [SOURCE: Page_26]

5    three classes (orchard, annual crop, non-cultivated areas) and on an annual basis [SOURCE: Page_26]

6    (September to August, i.e. the agricultural season). These three classes are the most [SOURCE: Page_26]

7    important for agricultural water management because they are predominant and they [SOURCE: Page_26]

8    corresponds to very different water needs. The use of these three broad categories also [SOURCE: Page_26]

9    facilitate the analysis of the inter-annual variability of MODIS estimates of land use [SOURCE: Page_26]

10   fractions as well as its evaluation against additional data sets (ground truth and high [SOURCE: Page_26]

11   spatial resolution images). [SOURCE: Page_26]

12 [SOURCE: Page_26]

13   The analysis of typical NDVI profiles firstly demonstrates that our working assumption, [SOURCE: Page_26]

14   i.e. quite pure pixels exist at the spatial resolution of 250m, is valid. Secondly, the [SOURCE: Page_26]

15   algorithm is able to identify the most pure areas associated to each of the three classes [SOURCE: Page_26]

16   of interest. The NDVI profiles retained as endmembers match with phenological [SOURCE: Page_26]

17   features of non-cultivated areas (flat profiles with low values on the bare soil class), [SOURCE: Page_26]

18   dense perennial vegetation (flat profiles with rather high values on the orchard class) [SOURCE: Page_26]

19   and cereals (largest NDVI seasonality on the annual crop class). Thirdly, the algorithm [SOURCE: Page_26]

20   is robust since the endmembers generally slightly differ between years. The inter-annual [SOURCE: Page_26]

21   stability of endmembers is particularly true for orchards and bare soils, while the [SOURCE: Page_26]

22   endmembers associated to the annual crop class display a larger inter-annual variability, [SOURCE: Page_26]

23   in relation with changes in water availability (dam irrigation water, seasonal amount and [SOURCE: Page_26]

24   distribution of rainfall). [SOURCE: Page_26]
