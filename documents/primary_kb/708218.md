16    Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

17    The Upper Rhine alluvial aquifer is an important transboundary water resource. However, as [SOURCE: Page_1]

18    in many alluvial systems, the aquifer inflows and outflows are not precisely known, due to the [SOURCE: Page_1]

19    difficulty in estimating the river infiltration flux and the boundary subsurface flow. To [SOURCE: Page_1]

20    provide a thorough representation of the aquifer system, a coupled surface-subsurface model [SOURCE: Page_1]

21    was applied on the whole aquifer basin, and several parameter sets were tested to investigate [SOURCE: Page_1]

22    the uncertainty due to poorly known parameters (e.g. aquifer transmissivity computed by an [SOURCE: Page_1]

23    inverse model, river bed characteristics). Twelve simulations were run and analysed using [SOURCE: Page_1]

24    standard statistical criteria, as well as a more advanced statistical method, the Karhunen [SOURCE: Page_1]

1 [SOURCE: Page_1]

25   Loève Transform (KLT). This analysis showed that although the model performs reasonably [SOURCE: Page_2]

26   well, some piezometric levels underestimations persist in the south of the basin. An accurate [SOURCE: Page_2]

27   representation of the aquifer behaviour requires taking into account river infiltration and the [SOURCE: Page_2]

28   functioning of irrigation canals in the Hardt area. It also appeared that increasing the [SOURCE: Page_2]

29   maximum river infiltration flow leads to altered results. [SOURCE: Page_2]

30   River infiltration to the aquifer was estimated to represent about 80 % of the aquifer inflows [SOURCE: Page_2]

31   with a mean annual value around 115 +/- 16.5 m^3/s, therefore with an uncertainty of 14 %. [SOURCE: Page_2]

32   This quantity is larger than estimated in previous studies, but also in agreement with some [SOURCE: Page_2]

33   results obtained during low water periods. This important conclusion highlights the [SOURCE: Page_2]

34   vulnerability of the Upper Rhine Graben aquifer to pollution from the rivers and to climate [SOURCE: Page_2]

35   change since it is highly probable that the rivers’ regime from the neighbouring mountain [SOURCE: Page_2]

36   ranges will be affected by a reduced snow cover. [SOURCE: Page_2]

40   1 Introduction [SOURCE: Page_2]

41   Alluvial hydrosystems have been recognised as important water resources but also very [SOURCE: Page_2]

42   vulnerable systems to pollution or change in water availability (Allen et al., 2004). [SOURCE: Page_2]

43   Knowledge of the different components of the water balance is therefore of significant [SOURCE: Page_2]

44   importance to optimize water management and/or to estimate the impacts of anthropogenic [SOURCE: Page_2]

45   activities or climate changes. These water balance components are difficult to measure, as the [SOURCE: Page_2]

46   observations of water fluxes provide only local information (Sanford 2002, Kalbus et al., [SOURCE: Page_2]

47   2006). Thus there is a need to assess these fluxes by modelling, as they are linked to the [SOURCE: Page_2]

48   boundary conditions, the sink/source terms and the hydrodynamic parameters, which are [SOURCE: Page_2]

49   mostly unknown and estimated through model calibration. Model calibration leads to non- [SOURCE: Page_2]

2 [SOURCE: Page_2]

50   unique solutions and therefore, the evaluation of some components of the water balance is [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   uncertain (Konikow and Bredehoeft 1992, Beven 2006). [SOURCE: Page_3]

52   The components of the water balance for unconfined groundwater are recharge due to [SOURCE: Page_3]

53   precipitations, exchanges with surface water, in/out-flows through the boundaries (either [SOURCE: Page_3]

54   prescribed heads or prescribed fluxes), storage in the aquifer, and water uptake by pumping [SOURCE: Page_3]

55   wells. Recharge and surface-groundwater interactions constitute key processes in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

56   behaviour and the evolution of these systems (Sanford, 2002, Woessner, 2000, Sophocleous, [SOURCE: Page_3]

57   2002). These fluxes can be estimated by fully coupled surface-subsurface models including [SOURCE: Page_3]

58   the unsaturated zone such as ParFlow (Kollet and Maxwell, 2006), Hydrogeosphere (Therrien [SOURCE: Page_3]

59   et al. 2007), and CATHY (Camporese et al., 2008) among others. However, applications of [SOURCE: Page_3]

60   these fully coupled models are generally limited to small areas. For larger systems, coupled [SOURCE: Page_3]

61   surface-subsurface models are usually simplified (Hu et al. 2007, Rushton 2007) and the [SOURCE: Page_3]

62   recharge is estimated separately using simplified hydrological models based on 1D Richards [SOURCE: Page_3]

63   equation (e.g. MIKE SHE, Reefsgard 1997) or more conceptual schemes (e.g. MARTHE, [SOURCE: Page_3]

64   Noyer and Elsass 2006). [SOURCE: Page_3]

65   This work aims at estimating the different components of the water balance and its associated [SOURCE: Page_3]

66   uncertainties for the Upper Rhine aquifer. The studied hydrosystem is one of the most [SOURCE: Page_3]

67   important water resources in Western Europe, and several regional scale models were [SOURCE: Page_3]

68   previously developed, as for example in the LIFE project in 1996 (LfU 1996, LfU 2005), and [SOURCE: Page_3]

69   in the INTERREG project Monit (LUBW 2006) focusing on nitrate contamination of [SOURCE: Page_3]

70   groundwater. These two studies led to different findings about aquifer recharge: river [SOURCE: Page_3]

71   infiltration was found to be the main recharge component in LIFE, whereas in Monit it was [SOURCE: Page_3]

72   effective rainfall. However, these discrepancies may be linked to the fact that the models were [SOURCE: Page_3]

73   limited to the alluvial plain, and used simple methods to assess the subsurface flows from the [SOURCE: Page_3]

74   Vosges and the Black Forest surrounding mountains. [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

75   In the present work a modelling of the whole Rhine basin between Basel and Lauterbourg is [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   performed (figure 1). This enables to better constrain the lateral water inputs to the aquifer, [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   and thus, to have a homogenous representation of the whole Upper Rhine graben basin. [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   Moreover it allows assessing the impacts of climate change on the hydrosystem, which could [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   lead to altered conditions over the mountainous catchments. [SOURCE: Page_4]

80   The Upper Rhine alluvial aquifer groundwater dynamics is modelled using different [SOURCE: Page_4]

81   assumptions and calibration methods based on trial and errors or inverse approach. The use of [SOURCE: Page_4]

82   several likely parameter values for a given model leads to different likely water balances and [SOURCE: Page_4]

83   provides an estimate of the associated uncertainty. Since several possible calibrated models [SOURCE: Page_4]

84   are obtained, we associated classic statistical criteria (average error, Nash criteria) with a [SOURCE: Page_4]

85   more advanced statistical method, the Karhunen Loève transform (KLT) to evaluate the [SOURCE: Page_4]

86   model performance (Wilks, 1995, Longuevergne et al., 2007). The KLT allows the [SOURCE: Page_4]

87   decomposition of piezometric time series into several independent temporal vectors, [SOURCE: Page_4]

88   corresponding to the main influential processes. This gives a different insight into the [SOURCE: Page_4]

89   performances of the simulations, focused on the representation of the main influential [SOURCE: Page_4]

90   processes over the piezometric head temporal evolution, and helps in discriminating and [SOURCE: Page_4]

91   quantifying the model ability to represent these processes. Using the results of this analysis, [SOURCE: Page_4]

92   the aquifer water budget and recharge components are assessed, as well as the uncertainty in [SOURCE: Page_4]

93   their computation. [SOURCE: Page_4]

94   This article is organised as follows: first, the characteristics of the basin are presented. Then, [SOURCE: Page_4]

95   the models used in this work are described (section 3), and the calibration of the surface water [SOURCE: Page_4]

96   budget is presented (section 4). In section 5, the different parameter sets are presented, and the [SOURCE: Page_4]

97   sensitivity of model results is analysed using the classic statistical criteria and the Karhunen [SOURCE: Page_4]

98   Loève transform. Eventually, section 6 presents the estimation of the aquifer water budget and [SOURCE: Page_4]

99   stock variations and the uncertainty on the recharge/discharge fluxes. [SOURCE: Page_4]

108   2.1 Geographical and geological setting [SOURCE: Page_5]

109   The Upper Rhine graben hydrosystem, situated at the French-German border (Figure 1), is [SOURCE: Page_5]

110   composed of three units: i) a tectonic graben containing Secondary to Tertiary sediments, [SOURCE: Page_5]

111   several kilometres thick (Figure 2) and forming the substratum of the Rhine alluvial plain [SOURCE: Page_5]

112   (Illies, 1972),   ii) the graben shoulders with much older materials such as crystalline, [SOURCE: Page_5]

113   metamorphic and ancient sedimentary rocks constituting the Black Forest mountains in the [SOURCE: Page_5]

114   East and the Vosges mountains in the West, and iii) the Sundgau hills in the South, formed by [SOURCE: Page_5]

115   marly Oligocene substratum of the Quaternary sediments which was relatively less subsided [SOURCE: Page_5]

116   than in the rest of the graben during the continental rifting. [SOURCE: Page_5]

117   The graben contains Tertiary marls and clays, covered by Quaternary alluvium deposited by [SOURCE: Page_5]

118   the Rhine River and forming the studied alluvial aquifer (cf. figure 2) (Duprat et al., 1979, [SOURCE: Page_5]

119   Bauer et al. 2005). The hydrographic network is very dense in the plain, due to the presence [SOURCE: Page_5]

120   of many groundwater-fed streams, allowing significant exchange of water between rivers and [SOURCE: Page_5]

121   the aquifer (Schmitt, 2001). [SOURCE: Page_5]

122   Figure 2 : Geological cross section of the Rhine Graben, showing the sediment succession in the plain and [SOURCE: Page_5]

123                                relative thickness (modified from Illies (1972)) [SOURCE: Page_5]

124 [SOURCE: Page_5]

5 [SOURCE: Page_5]

125   The main Rhine’s tributary in this part of its basin, the Ill River (figure 1), has its source in the [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   Sundgau, while some small rivers having their sources in the same area fully infiltrate towards [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   the aquifer. The other most important rivers have their source in the Vosges and Black Forest [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   mountains West and East of the plain. [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   The material of the Rhine alluvial aquifer is mainly coarse quaternary gravels and sands with [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   good hydrogeological properties. The hydraulic conductivities are in the order of 10-4 – 10- 3 131       m/s (Duprat et al., 1979; LfU, 1996; LUBW, 2006b). On the other hand the rocks forming [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   the mountains are rather impervious material containing only local aquifer formations of [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   small extension. The alluvial aquifer thickness reaches more than 200 m in its centre, East of [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   Colmar (LUBW, 2006). Mineral composition and thus structure and texture of the sediments [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   deposited by the Rhine River are marked by their Alpine origin, whereas towards the edges of [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   the graben, Vosgian and Black Forest rivers have deposited alluvial material having different [SOURCE: Page_6]

137   hydrogeological properties. These elements contribute to the heterogeneity of the aquifer [SOURCE: Page_6]

138   material, which in turn impacts the geometry of the river-aquifer interactions. [SOURCE: Page_6]

139   The aquifer substratum consists of the Oligocene marls considered impervious. The [SOURCE: Page_6]

140   groundwater flows from South to North and is closer to the surface in its northern part. Water [SOURCE: Page_6]

141   table depths range from 0 to 20 m (Hardt area), and groundwater fed wetlands are present in [SOURCE: Page_6]

142   the middle and the northern parts of the plain. [SOURCE: Page_6]

143   2.2 Climatic conditions [SOURCE: Page_6]

144   Precipitations show large contrasts over the basin, with annual values ranging from 550 [SOURCE: Page_6]

145   mm/year in the plain, to more than 2000 mm/year in the Vosges and Black Forest mountains [SOURCE: Page_6]

146   (Figure 1). Snowfalls account for around 3 % of total precipitations in the plain, and up to [SOURCE: Page_6]

147   37 % at the mountains tops. Thus snow accumulation and melting are important processes for [SOURCE: Page_6]

148   the dynamics of river flows in these catchments. Moreover potential evapotranspiration, [SOURCE: Page_6]

149   computed with Penman formula on the 1983-2006 time period, is weaker on the mountains [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

150   tops (around 600 mm/year) than in the plain (around 800 mm/year). As a consequence the [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   rivers flowing from the mountainous catchments carry an important proportion of the water [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   involved in the hydrosystem budget and play a crucial role in the recharge of the Rhine [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   alluvial aquifer. [SOURCE: Page_7]

154   Daily precipitations and potential evapotranspiration (PET) for the modelling are provided by [SOURCE: Page_7]

155   the SAFRAN analysis performed by Météo France, based on the definition of climatologically [SOURCE: Page_7]

156   homogeneous zones and on altitudinal variation of the meteorological variables (Quintana- [SOURCE: Page_7]

157   Seguì et al 2008, Vidal et al., 2010). They are used here at a daily time step from 1985 to [SOURCE: Page_7]

158   2003, which is about the period of time used in the previous Monit project (LUBW 2006). For [SOURCE: Page_7]

159   the present study the SAFRAN analysis that is usually provided on a regular grid of 8x^8 km^2 [SOURCE: Page_7]

160   was projected over a specific grid with cells varying from 1 to 8 km size in order to better [SOURCE: Page_7]

161   account for the topographic gradient of precipitation (Figure 1). [SOURCE: Page_7]

## 194          3.1. MODCOU

195   MODCOU is a spatially distributed coupled hydrogeological modelling tool that was already [SOURCE: Page_8]

196   used in several large basins in France (Golaz-Cavazzi et al., 2001, Ledoux et al., 2007, [SOURCE: Page_8]

197   Korkmaz et al., 2009). [SOURCE: Page_8]

198   It is built up on several interconnected modules. The first one is dedicated to the estimation of [SOURCE: Page_8]

199   the surface water budget computed with a simple reservoir scheme, the production functions [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

200   (Figure 4). Inputs to this conceptual model are the daily precipitation and potential [SOURCE: Page_9]

201   evapotranspiration (PET) data on a spatial grid. Outputs are actual evapotranspiration (AET), [SOURCE: Page_9]

202   infiltration and runoff on each grid cell and at a daily time step. More details on the [SOURCE: Page_9]

203   production functions are given in the next section where the calibration of the surface water [SOURCE: Page_9]

204   budget is presented. [SOURCE: Page_9]

205   The direct infiltration of water from effective rainfall through the unsaturated zone is [SOURCE: Page_9]

206   simulated with a Nash cascade (Philippe et al., 2010). [SOURCE: Page_9]

207   The aquifer representation is based on a square grid discretization, with a finite differences [SOURCE: Page_9]

208   resolution of the diffusivity equation. The rivers flows are simulated according to a [SOURCE: Page_9]

209   Muskingum scheme (David et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_9]

210   There are two kinds of interactions between the surface and the groundwater: 1) the aquifer [SOURCE: Page_9]

211   can be drained where piezometric level reaches the soil level, and 2) drainage or infiltration of [SOURCE: Page_9]

212   water towards the aquifer can occur on river cells. Owing to the major role of the river-aquifer [SOURCE: Page_9]

213   interactions on the Upper Rhine Graben aquifer, the description of these processes in [SOURCE: Page_9]

214   MODCOU is further detailed here. [SOURCE: Page_9]

215   On river cells, the river-aquifer exchange flow is proportional to the head difference between [SOURCE: Page_9]

216   the river and the aquifer, to permeability and width of the riverbed, and inversely proportional [SOURCE: Page_9]

217   to its thickness. As these characteristics of the river bed are mostly unknown, they are [SOURCE: Page_9]

218   integrated in two parameters called the transfer coefficient (Tp), for the case when the aquifer [SOURCE: Page_9]

219   and the river are connected, and the maximum river infiltration flow (Qlim) for the [SOURCE: Page_9]

220   disconnected case (Rushton 2007). The exchange flow is therefore computed as follows: [SOURCE: Page_9]

221     Qexch  max TP H gw  H riv ;Qlim ;Qdis  ,                         (1) [SOURCE: Page_9]

222   With Qexch the exchange flow between the river and the aquifer which is negative when water [SOURCE: Page_9]

223   flows from the river to the aquifer, Hgw the piezometric head in the aquifer, Hriv the water head [SOURCE: Page_9]

224   in the river, and Qdis the available flow in the river cell. Qlim is a maximum infiltration flow [SOURCE: Page_9]

9 [SOURCE: Page_9]

225   from the river to the aquifer, corresponding to the case when the aquifer head is below the [SOURCE: Page_10]

226   river bed (Rushton, 2007). A steady river head Hriv is usually considered in MODCOU, [SOURCE: Page_10]

227   however, due to the impact of river-aquifer interactions in the Rhine hydrosystem, time [SOURCE: Page_10]

228   varying heads based on observations were included for the Rhine River, as well as for the [SOURCE: Page_10]

229   Hardt Canal. This waterway is used for irrigation purposes, and preliminary results have [SOURCE: Page_10]

230   shown it was important to explicitly simulate its losses to the aquifer. [SOURCE: Page_10]

231   MODCOU uses a daily time step to allow a better estimation of the river-aquifer exchange [SOURCE: Page_10]

232   dynamics. [SOURCE: Page_10]

233   MODCOU can also be coupled with the soil-vegetation-atmosphere transfer scheme ISBA [SOURCE: Page_10]

234   (Noilhan and Planton 1989) to form the SIM model (Habets et al., 2008). SIM is used here to [SOURCE: Page_10]

235   have another estimation of the effective rainfall infiltration over the aquifer. [SOURCE: Page_10]

## 236          3.2 HPP-INV

237   The HPP-INV model (Chardigny 1999, Majdalani and Ackerer, 2010) is a modelling tool [SOURCE: Page_10]

238   using an inversion algorithm in order to assess aquifer hydrodynamic parameters. It is based [SOURCE: Page_10]

239   on a classical hydrogeological model, and includes an adaptative multiscale triangulation for [SOURCE: Page_10]

240   the inversion algorithm (Majdalani and Ackerer, 2010). [SOURCE: Page_10]

241   Piezometric levels simulation is based on a finite elements scheme and the river flows are [SOURCE: Page_10]

242   simulated with a Muskingum scheme (Majdalani and Ackerer, 2010). Rivers-aquifer [SOURCE: Page_10]

243   interactions are computed from the difference between river and aquifer heads, with transfer [SOURCE: Page_10]

244   coefficients values calibrated during the inversion process (LUBW, 2006). In the present [SOURCE: Page_10]

245   application, this model is run at a monthly time step. [SOURCE: Page_10]

246   There is no computation of a surface water budget, and groundwater recharge by rainfall is [SOURCE: Page_10]

247   provided by MODCOU. Moreover, the model is limited to the aquifer in the plain, and the [SOURCE: Page_10]

248   lateral inflows from bordering catchments are calibrated. [SOURCE: Page_10]

10 [SOURCE: Page_10]

249   The inversion method used to derive the spatial distribution of permeability is based on [SOURCE: Page_11]

250   minimization of the error between simulated and observed piezometric heads and river flow [SOURCE: Page_11]

251   data. Several distributions can be obtained according to different inversion conditions. [SOURCE: Page_11]

252 [SOURCE: Page_11]

253   4. Model set up [SOURCE: Page_11]

254          4.1 Discretization [SOURCE: Page_11]

255   The area modelled with MODCOU corresponds to the Rhine basin between Basel in the [SOURCE: Page_11]

256   South and Lauterbourg in the North. The basin total area is approximately 13900 km², and the [SOURCE: Page_11]

257   area of the alluvial aquifer is 4655 km² (Figure 3). Two different boundary conditions are [SOURCE: Page_11]

258   imposed: the Rhine river discharge at Basel, and fixed piezometric heads at the northern and [SOURCE: Page_11]

259   southern boundaries. [SOURCE: Page_11]

260   The basin was discretized with square cells ranging in size from 200 m for the river cells to [SOURCE: Page_11]

261   1600 m. There are 99715 surface cells, and 34180 aquifer cells (Figure 3). The alluvial [SOURCE: Page_11]

262   aquifer is represented with a single layer. [SOURCE: Page_11]

263   Subsurface flow at the western and eastern borders of the aquifer is known to be a significant [SOURCE: Page_11]

264   part of the recharge (LfU, 1996, LUBW, 2006). It is represented by specific boundary [SOURCE: Page_11]

265   conditions in the model: the surface runoff reaching the aquifer surface on the bordering cells [SOURCE: Page_11]

266   infiltrates to the water table and thus contributes to the aquifer recharge. This process is called [SOURCE: Page_11]

267   hereafter lateral subsurface flow. [SOURCE: Page_11]

281          4.2 Calibration of the surface water budget model [SOURCE: Page_12]

282   The parameter calibration process can be problematic, because of non-uniqueness (see for [SOURCE: Page_12]

283   example Beven, 1993; Gupta et al, 1998; Refsgaard, 1997). To avoid this kind of issues [SOURCE: Page_12]

284   during the calibration process, the number of parameters to be adjusted is usually reduced. A [SOURCE: Page_12]

285   manner to do this is to assess values for parameters based on their physical significance in the [SOURCE: Page_12]

286   hydrosystem. However the spatial distribution of these physical parameters is often not well [SOURCE: Page_12]

287   known, and the point measurement values may not be representative of grid cell scale [SOURCE: Page_12]

653   To summarize, the aquifer stock variations are rather sensitive to the maximum river [SOURCE: Page_28]

659   really affected. On the other hand when the maximum river infiltration rate is modified, it [SOURCE: Page_28]

660   only affects the exchange from the rivers to the aquifer, leading to increased aquifer stock [SOURCE: Page_28]

662   Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_28]

663   The objective of the study was to increase understanding of the Upper Rhine Graben aquifer [SOURCE: Page_28]

664   functioning. Modelling of the Upper Rhine graben aquifer was performed with the MODCOU [SOURCE: Page_28]

665   coupled hydrogeological model computing the surface water budget, groundwater flow and [SOURCE: Page_28]

666   interactions between the aquifer and the surface. Distributions of aquifer transmissivities were [SOURCE: Page_28]

667   provided by the inversion algorithm of the HPP-INV hydrogeological model. In the present [SOURCE: Page_28]

668   study, the sensitivity of the MODCOU model results to some model parameters governing the [SOURCE: Page_28]

669   surface / groundwater interactions was assessed, specifically in terms of aquifer behaviour. [SOURCE: Page_28]

670   Although the developed model is not suited to represent fine scale processes and fully coupled [SOURCE: Page_28]

671   interactions between surface water and groundwater, it represents the main processes that [SOURCE: Page_28]

672   impact the aquifer water budget, and thus, allows an analysis of their spatial distribution. [SOURCE: Page_28]

673 [SOURCE: Page_28]

674   Twelve model runs were performed using several parameter sets for the transmissivity, the [SOURCE: Page_28]

675   surface/groundwater transfer coefficient, the maximum river infiltration flow, Hardt Canal [SOURCE: Page_28]

28 [SOURCE: Page_28]

676   water levels and flows and Rhine river water levels, and two estimations of the surface water [SOURCE: Page_29]

677   budget in the plain. [SOURCE: Page_29]

678   These twelve runs were assessed using classic statistical criteria: the daily Nash efficiency for [SOURCE: Page_29]

679   the river flows, and biases for the piezometric levels. These analyses show that for each run, [SOURCE: Page_29]

680   the model had some problems reproducing the daily discharges on the German side, probably [SOURCE: Page_29]

681   due to some weaknesses of the atmospheric analysis using fewer meteorological stations in [SOURCE: Page_29]

682   Germany than in France. The biases on the simulated piezometric heads are rather weak [SOURCE: Page_29]

683   except on the southern part of the basin, where the discrepancy between the twelve [SOURCE: Page_29]

684   simulations is large. These classical analyses were able to highlight that one set of parameters [SOURCE: Page_29]

685   was not coherent with the functioning of the aquifer system, and thus that it is required to take [SOURCE: Page_29]

686   into account the possibility for rivers to infiltrate. [SOURCE: Page_29]

687   In order to go further in the assessment of the simulations results, a mathematical analysis [SOURCE: Page_29]

688   method based on a Karhunen Loève transform (KLT) of piezometric time series was used to [SOURCE: Page_29]

689   analyse the simulated piezometric levels temporal variations, as well as the observed ones. [SOURCE: Page_29]

690   This method allows separating the different processes influencing the water table evolution, in [SOURCE: Page_29]

691   the form of eigenvectors representing river infiltration and the effective rainfall influences. [SOURCE: Page_29]

692   The spatial distribution of the projections stemming from the decomposition of piezometric [SOURCE: Page_29]

693   chronicles as linear combinations of the eigenvectors allows having an idea of the area of [SOURCE: Page_29]

694   dominating influence of the three main processes. Comparison of the projections obtained [SOURCE: Page_29]

695   with simulated and observed piezometric time series also highlights the main areas where [SOURCE: Page_29]

696   difficulties arise to reproduce the water table behaviour. KLT showed that all model runs [SOURCE: Page_29]

697   failed to represent the annual component of the second eigenvector, related to the impact of [SOURCE: Page_29]

698   effective rainfall, mainly due to large errors in the Hardt area in the south of the domain, and [SOURCE: Page_29]

699   in the Eastern part of the plain. This error is however reduced when taking into account the [SOURCE: Page_29]

700   irrigation practises in the Hardt area, with irrigation water flowing through channels with high [SOURCE: Page_29]

29 [SOURCE: Page_29]

701   bed permeability and thus large river-aquifer exchanges. The KLT method underlines the [SOURCE: Page_30]

702   need to better take into account these processes in the model. This method also proved an [SOURCE: Page_30]

703   interesting and original technique to assess the model performances in reproducing the spatial [SOURCE: Page_30]

704   and temporal variability of water table levels, as well as the recharge/discharge processes [SOURCE: Page_30]

705   inducing these variations. It might be considered using this method in order to test spatial [SOURCE: Page_30]

706   patterns of some parameters taken homogeneous for now, such as the transfer coefficient Tp [SOURCE: Page_30]

707   and the maximum river infiltration rate Qlim. [SOURCE: Page_30]

708   Comparison of the aquifer water budgets obtained with different parameter sets shows that [SOURCE: Page_30]

709   there is a balance between the magnitude of river to aquifer infiltration and drainage of the [SOURCE: Page_30]

710   aquifer so that the aquifer stock is rather stable for each run. [SOURCE: Page_30]

711   The intensity of the simulated river-aquifer exchange was compared to available observations, [SOURCE: Page_30]

712   mainly on the Ill River. Estimated values are in the same order as the observed ones. However [SOURCE: Page_30]

713   the model presents smoother spatial variations of the simulated fluxes than observed, which [SOURCE: Page_30]

714   could be due to the use of spatially homogeneous river bed parameters. [SOURCE: Page_30]

715   The KLT method has shown that nine over twelve simulations were able to have a similarly [SOURCE: Page_30]

716   satisfying representation of the aquifer behaviour. This allows reducing the uncertainty of [SOURCE: Page_30]

717   aquifer recharge by river infiltration from 35 % to 14 % with an average flux of 115 m^3/s. [SOURCE: Page_30]

718   This represents about 80% of the aquifer inflow. [SOURCE: Page_30]

719   This proportion compares well to the LIFE project estimate for a low water situation, but is [SOURCE: Page_30]

720   more important than the one from the Monit project for a transient state simulation. [SOURCE: Page_30]

721   This study brings increased comprehension of the functioning of an alluvial aquifer, which is [SOURCE: Page_30]

722   strongly influenced by groundwater /surface water interactions. It shows that modelling, [SOURCE: Page_30]

723   together with inversion and advanced statistical methods are powerful tools to quantify the [SOURCE: Page_30]

724   water budget of an alluvial aquifer. [SOURCE: Page_30]
