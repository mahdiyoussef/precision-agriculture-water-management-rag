Abstract [SOURCE: Page_1]

The case study conducted in this paper looks at residential water pricing from three different points of view. It first describes existing urban water-pricing practices in Southern France, emphasizing that pricing is not yet being used as a tool for providing economic incentives to save water. It then looks at the observed impact of pricing on water consumption, through an econometric analysis of a cross-sectional data set. The analysis suggests that demand, with an estimated price elasticity of -0.2, is not yet very responsive to price variation. A regional water model (300 municipalities) is then developed and used to simulate the potential impact of various water-pricing scenarios on aggregate water demand, aggregate water sales revenue, and consumer surpluses. The results illustrate the trade-offs that have to be made between the search for environmental effectiveness, cost recovery, and equity when implementing complex water-pricing structures such as block rates or seasonal water pricing. [SOURCE: Page_1]

Keywords: demand elasticity, France, water pricing, residential water demand, simulation. [SOURCE: Page_1]

### 6. Discussion and conclusion

The results of this study illustrate that the choice of a water-pricing structure is a complex exercise, [SOURCE: Page_14]

involving trade-offs between cost recovery, equity, and environmental efficiency. The simulation [SOURCE: Page_14]

tool presented in this paper can be helpful in understanding this trade-off and in exploring the [SOURCE: Page_14]

various impacts of alternative scenarios. The main advantage of this model lies in the scale at which [SOURCE: Page_14]

it can be implemented (river basin) and its ease of implementation by water managers and planners, [SOURCE: Page_14]

without involving the use of complex econometric techniques. [SOURCE: Page_14]

From a policy perspective, the results of our simulations confirm previous findings concerning the [SOURCE: Page_14]

use of IBT as a redistributive tool. Cross subsidies between blocks will most likely cause cost [SOURCE: Page_14]

recovery problems. To avoid such problems, more sophisticated IBTs have been considered by [SOURCE: Page_14]

scholars and practitioners, including for instance option tariffs specifically designed to reduce the [SOURCE: Page_14]

bills of large families (Barberán and Arbués 2009) or the use of per capita IBTs as already [SOURCE: Page_14]

practiced by many water utilities in Southern California. It is however often recommended that [SOURCE: Page_14]

social issues should be addressed “outside the water bill”, as already suggested by both academic [SOURCE: Page_14]

authors (Barraque 2011) and practitioners (AWWA 2004). [SOURCE: Page_14]

From a methodological perspective, our simple modelling approach is characterised by a number of [SOURCE: Page_14]

assumptions which can be debated. The first problematic assumption is that price elasticity values [SOURCE: Page_14]

remain valid outside the price range within which they were estimated. Results from empirical [SOURCE: Page_14]

studies suggest that this may not be the case. In their meta-analysis of price elasticity, (Espey et al. [SOURCE: Page_14]

1997) have shown that elasticity values reported in case studies are positively correlated with price [SOURCE: Page_14]

levels and that they can be influenced by the pricing structure used, climate, and other factors. [SOURCE: Page_15]

However, given the difficulty of predicting future changes in price elasticity, the authors [SOURCE: Page_15]

recommend the adoption of a conservative assumption, e.g.,. keeping elasticity constant, as in our [SOURCE: Page_15]

own case study. [SOURCE: Page_15]

The second debatable assumption is that households perfectly understand their rate structure and [SOURCE: Page_15]

respond to marginal price changes. This has been challenged by (Nieswiadomy 1992) and by later [SOURCE: Page_15]

studies showing that the mere existence of IBT can reduce demand, irrespective of the change in [SOURCE: Page_15]

price level (Kenney et al. 2008). Other studies have also shown that price response was positively [SOURCE: Page_15]

correlated with households’ level of information concerning tariffs. As an illustration, one [SOURCE: Page_15]

American study found that a utility that provides detailed tariff information on its water bill can [SOURCE: Page_15]

attain the same level of conservation with a 30 to 40% lower rate increase (Gaudin 2006). The [SOURCE: Page_15]

model presented in this study can clearly not account for this effect of information and awareness- [SOURCE: Page_15]

raising programs, thus probably underestimating the water-conservation effect of water-pricing [SOURCE: Page_15]

policies. [SOURCE: Page_15]

The third caveat regarding our modelling approach is that it does not account for the possibility that [SOURCE: Page_15]

households may develop alternative water sources, such as gray water recycling, rainwater [SOURCE: Page_15]

harvesting, or private borehole drilling in response to tariff increases. This trend is already being [SOURCE: Page_15]

observed in many industrialised countries such as France (Montginoul and Rinaudo 2011), [SOURCE: Page_15]

Australia (Hurlimann 2011), and Belgium (AQUAWAL 2010) among other countries. Indeed, an [SOURCE: Page_15]

increased marginal price is likely to provide significant incentives for large consumers to invest in [SOURCE: Page_15]

such alternative, decentralized water-supply systems, as shown by (Montginoul and Rinaudo 2011) [SOURCE: Page_15]

in the case of private boreholes in France. However, if some consumers quit or use less, then others [SOURCE: Page_15]

will have to pay more, since all consumers are bound together by the heavy fixed cost of [SOURCE: Page_15]

infrastructure (Barraque). This shows the need for more comprehensive modelling approaches that [SOURCE: Page_15]

will account for the possibility of resource substitution– a new area of research which the authors [SOURCE: Page_15]
