28   Abstract [SOURCE: Page_3]

29   In the Water Framework Directive (European Union) context, a multimetric fish based index is required [SOURCE: Page_3]

30   to assess the ecological status of French estuarine water bodies. A first indicator called ELFI was [SOURCE: Page_3]

31   developed, however similarly to most indicators, the method to combine the core metrics was rather [SOURCE: Page_3]

32   subjective and this indicator does not provide uncertainty assessment. Recently, a Bayesian method [SOURCE: Page_3]

33   to build indicators was developed and appeared relevant to select metrics sensitive to global [SOURCE: Page_3]

34   anthropogenic pressure, to combine them objectively in an index and to provide a measure of [SOURCE: Page_3]

35   uncertainty around the diagnostic. Moreover, the Bayesian framework is especially well adapted to [SOURCE: Page_3]

36   integrate knowledge and information not included in surveys data. In this context, the present study [SOURCE: Page_3]

37   used this Bayesian method to build a multimetric fish based index of ecological quality accounting for [SOURCE: Page_3]

38   experts knowledge. The first step consisted in elaborating a questionnaire to collect assessments from [SOURCE: Page_3]

39   different experts then in building relevant priors to summarize those assessments for each water body. [SOURCE: Page_3]

40   Then, these priors were combined with surveys data in the index to complement the diagnosis of [SOURCE: Page_3]

41   quality. Finally, a comparison between diagnoses using only fish data and using both information [SOURCE: Page_3]

42   sources underlined experts knowledge contribution. Regarding the results, 68% of the diagnosis [SOURCE: Page_3]

43   matched demonstrating that including experts knowledge thanks to the Bayesian framework confirmed [SOURCE: Page_3]

44   or slightly modified the diagnosis provided by survey data but influenced uncertainty around the [SOURCE: Page_3]

45   diagnostic and appeared especially relevant in terms of risk management. [SOURCE: Page_3]

51   1. Introduction [SOURCE: Page_4]

52   Coastal and estuarine ecosystems are particularly vulnerable to evolution of human activities [SOURCE: Page_4]

53   (Henocque and Denis, 2001; Hoegh-Guldberg and Bruno, 2010) and their degradation is widely [SOURCE: Page_4]

54   observed, e.g. Elliott and Hemingway (2002). In that context, regulation tools such as the European [SOURCE: Page_4]

55   Water Framework Directive (WFD) aims at stopping this degradation process and at restoring aquatic [SOURCE: Page_4]

56   ecosystems to a good ecological status (WFD – Directive 2000/60/EC; European Council, 2000). To [SOURCE: Page_4]

57   fulfil this objective, multimetric indices are widely used to assess the ecological quality of aquatic [SOURCE: Page_4]

58   ecosystems (Hughes and Oberdorff, 1999). A metric is defined as a measurable variable having an [SOURCE: Page_4]

59   ecological meaning, which can be associated to any structural or functional aspect of biological [SOURCE: Page_4]

60   assemblages (Coates et al., 2007). Combining several metrics in a multimetric index assures that the [SOURCE: Page_4]

61   resulting indices are holistic and sensitive (Deegan et al., 1997; Karr and Chu, 1999). A large variety of [SOURCE: Page_4]

62   multimetric indices aims at detecting the ecological impact of stressors, e.g Hering et al. (2006). [SOURCE: Page_4]

63   As fish integrate a large variety of anthropogenic pressures (Elliott et al., 1988; Karr, 1981), fish [SOURCE: Page_4]

64   assemblages are generally considered as appropriate to develop indicators of ecosystem quality. [SOURCE: Page_4]

65   Consequently, numerous fish-based multimetric indices have been developed in the context of the [SOURCE: Page_4]

66   WFD (Pont et al., 2006), especially in transitional waters, e.g. Borja et al. (2004), Breine et al. (2010), [SOURCE: Page_4]

67   Breine et al. (2007) and Delpech et al. (2010). However, most of those indices suffered from two main [SOURCE: Page_4]

68   weaknesses. First, qualitative estimates of the respective weight of metrics, correlations among them [SOURCE: Page_4]

69   and redundancy in information made combinations of metrics in those indices sensitive to the [SOURCE: Page_4]

70   calculation method. Second, most of those indices did not quantify uncertainty around their [SOURCE: Page_4]

71   assessment (Perez-Dominguez et al., 2012) though it is especially important for managers (Breine et [SOURCE: Page_4]

72   al., 2007; McAllister and Kirkwood, 1998). Indeed, the probability for a water body to reach a score [SOURCE: Page_4]

73   below good status is necessary in terms of risk management (WFD – Directive 2000/60/EC; European [SOURCE: Page_4]

74   Council, 2000). Bootstrap methods were applied with success by Pont et al. (2006) to estimate these [SOURCE: Page_4]

75   probabilities but they required a large amount of data, making them inappropriate in data poor [SOURCE: Page_4]

76   situations, especially in estuarine systems where large standardized sets of surveys data are lacking [SOURCE: Page_4]

77   (Nicolas et al., 2011). [SOURCE: Page_4]

78   Delpech et al. (2010) proposed an indicator called ELFI to assess the ecological status of French [SOURCE: Page_4]

79   estuaries. This indicator was based on pressure-impact models, as proposed by Courrat et al. (2009), [SOURCE: Page_4]

Author-produced version of the article published in Ecological Indicators, 2013, 32, 147-156. The original publication is available at http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1470160X1300143X http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolind.2013.03.030 [SOURCE: Page_5]

80    in order to select relevant metrics that are sensitive to anthropogenic pressures. However, this [SOURCE: Page_5]

81    indicator suffered from the two weaknesses previously mentioned. Recently, an original method to [SOURCE: Page_5]

82    build multimetric stressor specific indices was proposed and applied on the French lagoons [SOURCE: Page_5]

83    (Drouineau et al., 2012). This approach based on the Bayesian theory took up both two challenges. [SOURCE: Page_5]

84    First, based on the Bayesian theory, it allows an objective combination of the metrics by incorporating [SOURCE: Page_5]

85    all the information in a probabilistic framework. Secondly, this method provides a measure of [SOURCE: Page_5]

86    uncertainty in its assessment. Consequently, it is hoped that applying this framework to the indicator [SOURCE: Page_5]

87    developed by Delpech et al. (2010) would significantly improve the indicator. [SOURCE: Page_5]

88    Drouineau et al. (2012) proposed to further incorporate experts knowledge within the French lagoons [SOURCE: Page_5]

89    fish-based index in order to combine both experts knowledge and fish data in the ecological [SOURCE: Page_5]

90    assessment. Integrating experts knowledge in such multimetric index could lead to valuable [SOURCE: Page_5]

91    improvement (Martin et al., 2005; Murray et al., 2009). Experts knowledge may provide a qualitative [SOURCE: Page_5]

92    but global image (Knapp et al., 2011) of the ecological status of an ecosystem not only based on a [SOURCE: Page_5]

93    particular ecological feature (ecological communities or habitats). Furthermore, it does not require [SOURCE: Page_5]

94    expensive scientific surveys. Indeed, most indices are based on surveys with a limited time and space [SOURCE: Page_5]

95    scale, sampling a limited fraction of fish assemblages. Consequently, they are based on restricted [SOURCE: Page_5]

96    image of the ecosystem. [SOURCE: Page_5]

97    In this context, the present approach developed a fish-based multimetric index for French estuaries, [SOURCE: Page_5]

98    applying the Bayesian method proposed by Drouineau et al. (2012) and incorporating experts [SOURCE: Page_5]

99    knowledge. This index proposed to fulfil weaknesses of the index developed by Delpech et al. (2010) [SOURCE: Page_5]

100   while using its pressure-impact approach and the method developed by Courrat et al. (2009) to select [SOURCE: Page_5]

101   core metrics. The combination of fish data and experts information into the Bayesian framework is [SOURCE: Page_5]

102   described, the impact of the integration of the experts knowledge in the ecological assessment and its [SOURCE: Page_5]

103   interest for monitoring estuarine ecological status are analysed. [SOURCE: Page_5]

115       2.1. Data sets [SOURCE: Page_6]

116           2.1.1. Fish data and pressure-impact models [SOURCE: Page_6]

117   A panel of 36 water bodies located along the French coasts of the English Channel and the Bay of [SOURCE: Page_6]

118   Biscay were sampled between 2005 and 2009. A subset of 22 water bodies was sampled in 2010 to [SOURCE: Page_6]

119   complete the WFD schedule (Fig. 2). Indeed, each water body had to be sampled at least 3 years [SOURCE: Page_6]

120   during the 6-year WFD program. A detailed description of the sampling protocol was provided by [SOURCE: Page_6]

121   Delpech et al. (2010) and Lepage and Girardin (2006). Each monitored water body was sampled in [SOURCE: Page_6]

122   spring and autumn with a beam trawl. Hauls were distributed along the salinity gradient and 3 salinity [SOURCE: Page_6]

123   classes were defined (Delpech et al., 2010): oligohaline class ([0–5] g.L-1), mesohaline class (]5–18] [SOURCE: Page_6]

124   g.L-1) and polyhaline class (>18 g.L-1). In each season, at least 6 hauls were carried out in each [SOURCE: Page_6]

125   salinity zone of each water body. A minimum of 12 hauls was carried out in the water bodies having [SOURCE: Page_6]

126   only one salinity zone (e.g. Downstream Seine). In each trawl haul (sample), each fish was identified [SOURCE: Page_6]

127   to the species level and each species was assigned to functional ecological guilds related to its diet [SOURCE: Page_6]

128   and its use of the estuarine ecosystems along its life cycle (Elliott and Dewailly, 1995). [SOURCE: Page_6]

129   The lack of pristine estuaries to define reference conditions involved the use of statistical modelling [SOURCE: Page_6]

130   (Delpech et al., 2010; Pont et al., 2006). A solution was to develop pressure-impact models (Borja et [SOURCE: Page_6]

131   al., 2006). However, a proxy of anthropogenic pressure is generally required to link fish data to human [SOURCE: Page_6]

132   disturbances. In this study, an index developed by Courrat et al. (2009) was used. This index was [SOURCE: Page_6]

133   based on a principal component analysis carried out on heavy metals concentrations measured by the [SOURCE: Page_6]

134   French national network of quality of the French marine environment since 1979 in suspension feeder [SOURCE: Page_6]

135   molluscs from various French estuaries. This index was normalized on the range [0;1]. [SOURCE: Page_6]

136   Based on those data, Courrat et al. (2009) proposed to build pressure-impact models (generalized [SOURCE: Page_7]

137   linear models) to select metrics that are sensitive to anthropogenic pressure. Delpech et al. (2010) [SOURCE: Page_7]

138   selected 4 metrics among the 12 metrics tested (density of (i) benthic fish, (ii) diadromous species, (iii) [SOURCE: Page_7]

139   marine juveniles migrants and (iv) total density of fish respectively denoted DB, DDIA, DMJ and TD) [SOURCE: Page_7]

140   because they significantly responded to a variation of anthropogenic pressures, and consistently with [SOURCE: Page_7]

141   expert judgments (Delpech et al., 2010). The pressure-impact models were then used to predict the [SOURCE: Page_7]

142   expected value of each metric at 3 distinct levels of anthropogenic pressures, providing thresholds for [SOURCE: Page_7]

143   each metric. A scoring method was then applied to combine the 4 metrics in the indicator. [SOURCE: Page_7]

144   Pressure-impact models as proposed by Courrat et al.( 2009) and Delpech et al. (2010) appeared [SOURCE: Page_7]

145   appropriate to select metrics negatively correlated to pressure, and potentially relevant to be included [SOURCE: Page_7]

146   in the index. Consequently, the metrics selected by Delpech et al. (2010) were used, except the total [SOURCE: Page_7]

147   density metric (TD) given its redundancy with the combination of the 3 others (DB, DDIA and DMJ). [SOURCE: Page_7]

148   Pressure-impact models were fitted and included in the framework proposed by Drouineau et al. [SOURCE: Page_7]

149   (2012). Models options depended on data distribution of the different metrics. The use of linear [SOURCE: Page_7]

150   models, consistent for DB metric, was inappropriate for the other metrics composed of 0 inflated. [SOURCE: Page_7]

151   Thus, a delta type model that consisted in a combination of two models was used: one for [SOURCE: Page_7]

152   presence/absence modelling, another one for positive values modelling. Those models were similar to [SOURCE: Page_7]

153   models developed by Delpech et al. (2010), except for the use of generalized linear mixed models [SOURCE: Page_7]

172           2.1.2. Experts knowledge [SOURCE: Page_8]

173   A panel of 23 experts was interviewed to collect knowledge on the different water bodies. Experts [SOURCE: Page_8]

174   having information on fish data collected in 2010 were not included in the panel to avoid hindsight bias [SOURCE: Page_8]

175   (redundant information between fish data and experts opinions) (Morgan and Henrion, 1990). Experts [SOURCE: Page_8]

176   were selected for their abilities to assess a global pressure level for one part of the sampled water [SOURCE: Page_8]

177   bodies. Finally, the 23 experts provided 100 assessments for the 22 studied water bodies (between 3 [SOURCE: Page_8]

178   and 8 assessments per water body except Risle with only 2 assessments). [SOURCE: Page_8]

179   A user-friendly graphical interface was developed to collect experts assessment per water body. A [SOURCE: Page_8]

180   cursors system was used to assess both mean level of pressure and to provide a reliability measure of [SOURCE: Page_8]

181   the assessment (Fig. 3). This reliability box is a self-evaluation of the level of confidence the expert [SOURCE: Page_8]

182   have on his assessment. [SOURCE: Page_8]

183   In order to guide his reflection, three sub-pressures (pollution, morphology and hydrology) had to be [SOURCE: Page_8]

184   considered by the expert before assessing the global pressure (Fig.3). A manual provided [SOURCE: Page_8]

185   explanations about the definition of each sub-pressure, the scale corresponding to the WFD reference [SOURCE: Page_8]

186   conditions and the use of probability distributions. Moreover, a table at the end of the questionnaire [SOURCE: Page_8]

187   presented a summary of all water bodies assessments realized by the expert, so that he could [SOURCE: Page_8]

188   compare and adjust his assessments. [SOURCE: Page_8]

189   Experts assessments were aggregated in a unique informative prior per water body. To represent the [SOURCE: Page_8]

190   experts diversity and get a stable prior, a minimum number of opinions is required (Kuhnert et al., [SOURCE: Page_8]

191   2005), but a limited number of 3 to 5 experts is suggested to provide a compromise between [SOURCE: Page_8]

278   4. Discussion [SOURCE: Page_12]

279   Many fish indicators were recently developed in the context of the Water Framework Directive. [SOURCE: Page_12]

280   However, many of those indicators suffered from two main drawbacks: (i) assessments of [SOURCE: Page_12]

281   uncertainties around quality diagnostics are missing (Perez-Dominguez et al., 2012) and (ii) the choice [SOURCE: Page_12]

282   of core metrics combination is rather subjective. Regarding French estuaries, an indicator called ELFI [SOURCE: Page_12]

283   was developed by Delpech et al. (Delpech et al., 2010), based on pressure-impact models proposed [SOURCE: Page_12]

284   by Courrat et al. (Courrat et al., 2009). Pressure-impact approach proved to be a relevant method to [SOURCE: Page_12]

285   choose appropriate metrics, however ELFI still suffered from the two weaknesses. Consequently, this [SOURCE: Page_12]

286   paper developed an indicator based on ELFI pressure-impact models but applying a Bayesian [SOURCE: Page_12]

287   framework developed by Drouineau et al. (2012). In addition, a method was proposed to collect and [SOURCE: Page_12]

288   summarise expert judgments in order to combine them in the Bayesian framework with fish data. This [SOURCE: Page_12]

289   approach found several interests detailed further. [SOURCE: Page_12]

290       4.1. The pressure-impact Bayesian models [SOURCE: Page_12]

291   The index was based on generalised linear mixed models, and not on previously used generalised [SOURCE: Page_12]

292   linear models (Delpech et al., 2010). Indeed, fish data were not perfect replicates but pseudo- [SOURCE: Page_12]

293   replicates: several trawl hauls were collected in each estuary, generating dependency. Given that the [SOURCE: Page_12]

294   estuary effect could not be taken into account, the independence assumption of data was corrupted. A [SOURCE: Page_12]

295   rigorous solution was to use random effect in mixed models, with, as a consequence, an increase of [SOURCE: Page_12]

296   the standard error and uncertainty around the parameters. A random effect of year was also tested but [SOURCE: Page_12]

297   was not significant. Other random effects such the interaction between salinity class and estuary were [SOURCE: Page_12]

298   also considered but the present GLMMs were finally considered as suitable. The index was finally [SOURCE: Page_12]

Author-produced version of the article published in Ecological Indicators, 2013, 32, 147-156. The original publication is available at http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1470160X1300143X http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolind.2013.03.030 [SOURCE: Page_13]

299   assessed on a Bayesian framework (Drouineau et al., 2012) and presented the advantages provided [SOURCE: Page_13]

300   by this framework: (i) an objective combination of the core metrics and (ii) a quantification of the [SOURCE: Page_13]

301   uncertainty around the diagnostic. [SOURCE: Page_13]

302   GLMMs were fitted with a pressure index based on water contamination in heavy metals (Courrat et [SOURCE: Page_13]

303   al., 2009). As mentioned by Courrat et al. (2009), this index is highly correlated to many anthropogenic [SOURCE: Page_13]

304   activities (industry, urbanisation, agriculture), consequently resulting pressure-impact models should [SOURCE: Page_13]

305   be more considered as the global effect anthropogenic pressures than a direct effect of heavy metal [SOURCE: Page_13]

306   contaminations. However, comparing results with models fitted on others global pressure indices could [SOURCE: Page_13]

307   be relevant to assess the robustness of this hypothesis. It would also be interesting to apply the [SOURCE: Page_13]

308   method to each different type of pressure separately (for example pollution, hydrology and [SOURCE: Page_13]

309   morphology) to identify the more likely type of pressure impacting the ecological status. However, the [SOURCE: Page_13]

310   water bodies are generally affected by various pressures simultaneously, consequently pressures are [SOURCE: Page_13]

311   highly correlated and it would probably not be possible to conclude on the most likely pressure [SOURCE: Page_13]

312   impacting a given water body. Moreover, to fulfil the WFD requirements, a method to combine the [SOURCE: Page_13]

313   results on the different pressures in a unique indicator would be required. Bayesian Network may be [SOURCE: Page_13]

314   an interesting method in the future to fulfil those challenges. [SOURCE: Page_13]

315   Regarding the indicator calibration and validation, it was chosen to calibrate the indicator on data [SOURCE: Page_13]

316   collected from 2005 to 2009 and then to apply it on data collected in 2010. Usually, to cross-validate a [SOURCE: Page_13]

317   model, a random subset of the entire dataset is used to calibrate and the other part of the data-set is [SOURCE: Page_13]

318   used to validate the model. However, as proposed by Wenger and Olden (2012), it was chosen to [SOURCE: Page_13]

319   apply a non-random cross-validation strategy which is appropriate to assess time transferability of the [SOURCE: Page_13]

320   model. This strategy is consistent with the Water Framework Directive context: to assess the [SOURCE: Page_13]

321   ecological status in a given year, all data from previous years will be used to calibrate the indicator [SOURCE: Page_13]

322   since time series are often limited. [SOURCE: Page_13]

323       4.2. Taking experts knowledge into account [SOURCE: Page_13]

324   The assessment realized in the Bayesian framework was based on both experts opinions and [SOURCE: Page_13]

325   scientific surveys. Only another WFD-like index integrated directly experts knowledge in its [SOURCE: Page_13]

326   architecture (Cabral et al., 2012). Indeed, experts knowledge was rarely considered in ecological [SOURCE: Page_13]

327   indicators (Carpenter, 2002). [SOURCE: Page_13]

Author-produced version of the article published in Ecological Indicators, 2013, 32, 147-156. The original publication is available at http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1470160X1300143X http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolind.2013.03.030 [SOURCE: Page_14]

328           4.2.1. A method to collect and standardize knowledge [SOURCE: Page_14]

329   Before assessing the global pressure, it was asked the experts to assess three sub-pressures for [SOURCE: Page_14]

330   every water body. But assessing sub-pressures before the global one may introduce bias since it may [SOURCE: Page_14]

331   influence experts judgments. However, it is required to enhance the reproducibility of those experts [SOURCE: Page_14]

332   assessments. To limit the potential bias, no weighting rates were proposed to establish global [SOURCE: Page_14]

333   pressure, so each expert provided them on his own. [SOURCE: Page_14]

334   In addition, experts assessments may contain biases linked to the lack of neutrality (Choy et al., 2009; [SOURCE: Page_14]

335   Dennis, 1996). Moreover, experts are usually overconfident (Hora et al., 1992; Kadane and Wolfson, [SOURCE: Page_14]

336   1998; Winkler, 1967a, b). Consequently, too precise priors may drive parameters assessment rather [SOURCE: Page_14]

337   than only provide a direction (Dennis, 1996). To get around both issues, knowledge of several experts [SOURCE: Page_14]

338   was used to build priors to counterbalance both the bias linked to each expert background (Martin et [SOURCE: Page_14]

339   al., 2005) and overconfidence. Indeed, the inter-variability between experts is generally higher than [SOURCE: Page_14]

340   the variability of any expert opinion (Kuhnert et al., 2005; Uusitalo et al., 2005). Consequently, prior [SOURCE: Page_14]

341   precision was mainly influenced by the agreement level between experts rather than the confidence [SOURCE: Page_14]

342   level of each assessment. [SOURCE: Page_14]

343   Alternative methods exist to collect experts opinions. For instance, a simple choice in a range of [SOURCE: Page_14]

344   different values is efficient if the number of experts is sufficient (Kuhnert et al., 2005; Martin et al., [SOURCE: Page_14]

345   2005). In other cases, and especially in the present approach, the limited number of experts involved [SOURCE: Page_14]

346   the use of probability distributions. Assessing successive percentiles (Garthwaite and Dickey, 1996; [SOURCE: Page_14]

347   Kadane and Wolfson, 1998) is a precise method but time consuming. A faster but less precise method [SOURCE: Page_14]

348   (O'Hagan, 1998) aims at assessing a mean and a credibility interval of 50%. Both these methods can [SOURCE: Page_14]

349   suffer of underestimation of distribution tails (Hora et al., 1992; Winkler, 1967a, b). Accordingly, [SOURCE: Page_14]

350   assessing a graphical distribution directly was here preferred for its simplicity and user-friendliness, in [SOURCE: Page_14]

351   order to use the instinct qualities of experts. A pressure scale with a colour gradient was developed [SOURCE: Page_14]

352   instead of any common numerical scale to prevent the halo effect (Nisbett and Wilson, 1977), i.e. bias [SOURCE: Page_14]

353   linked to the distorted perception of numerical scales of a repeated question. [SOURCE: Page_14]

354   Different solutions may be investigated to improve the precision of priors. The DELPHI method [SOURCE: Page_14]

355   (Jacobs, 1995; Linstone et al., 1975) allows a prior to be elicited from several experts. A consensus is [SOURCE: Page_14]

356   constructed indirectly after several assessment rounds. Between each round, each expert consults the [SOURCE: Page_14]

Author-produced version of the article published in Ecological Indicators, 2013, 32, 147-156. The original publication is available at http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1470160X1300143X http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolind.2013.03.030 [SOURCE: Page_15]

357   assessments and associated rationales provided by the others before updating his opinion. This kind [SOURCE: Page_15]

358   of method generally provides a more precise prior though a direct consensus between experts could [SOURCE: Page_15]

359   also be supported (O'Hagan, 1998). However, such method is time and costs consuming for the [SOURCE: Page_15]

360   experts. In addition, they may suffer from a potential strong influence of few dominating experts, [SOURCE: Page_15]

361   leading to a precision overestimation (Kuhnert et al., 2005). Another method (Coolen and Newby, [SOURCE: Page_15]

362   1994) consists in defining a range of possible values, i.e. possible pressure levels here, selected by [SOURCE: Page_15]

363   experts. A uniform prior is defined on the range. Such a prior is very informative because some [SOURCE: Page_15]

364   pressure levels are unconsidered in the final index, leading to an unsuitable minor influence of fish [SOURCE: Page_15]

365   data, and often to some problems in the convergence of Monte-Carlo Markov Chains. [SOURCE: Page_15]

366           4.2.2. Insights provided by experts knowledge [SOURCE: Page_15]

367   The questionnaire used in the present approach proved to be efficient, with an answer rate greater [SOURCE: Page_15]

368   than 75%. As a positive side-effect, it also proved to be an interesting communication tool: experts [SOURCE: Page_15]

369   were interested by sharing their opinions and felt involved in the index development. On the whole, [SOURCE: Page_15]

370   experts knowledge was rather consistent with fish data, demonstrating that the indicator provides an [SOURCE: Page_15]

371   objective consensus between both data sources. Nevertheless, some discrepancies allowed to point [SOURCE: Page_15]

372   out that experts knowledge may provide information not included in the data. Especially, the Vilaine [SOURCE: Page_15]

373   estuary appeared rather specific, with a large disagreement between fish data and experts knowledge. [SOURCE: Page_15]

374   In this estuary, a dam was built in 1970 and greatly impacted the water body (i.e. meso-haline and [SOURCE: Page_15]

375   oligo-haline have entirely disappeared and this water body is considered as heavily modified in the [SOURCE: Page_15]

376   WFD (Borja and Elliott, 2007)) explaining the negative assessment provided by the experts. However, [SOURCE: Page_15]

377   the impact was not detected from surveys in the remaining poly-haline zone and the assessment [SOURCE: Page_15]

378   based on fish data only was positive. Indeed, the impacts of removing parts of estuarine system were [SOURCE: Page_15]

379   difficult to quantify from fish data given samplings were performed in remaining surfaces (Courrat et [SOURCE: Page_15]

380   al., 2009). Though the small prior precision did not allow the assessment to be influenced significantly, [SOURCE: Page_15]

381   this example legitimises the interest of experts knowledge in the indicator. [SOURCE: Page_15]

382   On one hand, when experts disagreed with the conclusion provided by fish data (e.g Vilaine but also [SOURCE: Page_15]

383   Charente and Baie du Mont Saint-Michel), the impact of the prior on the indicator depended on two [SOURCE: Page_15]

384   factors. The more precise and in contradiction with fish data was the experts consensus, the more the [SOURCE: Page_15]

385   final main quality class was modified. So, the weighting between fish data and experts knowledge in [SOURCE: Page_15]

Author-produced version of the article published in Ecological Indicators, 2013, 32, 147-156. The original publication is available at http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1470160X1300143X http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolind.2013.03.030 [SOURCE: Page_16]

386   the final index is directly linked to their relative precisions. On the other hand, when experts knowledge [SOURCE: Page_16]

387   and fish data matched, experts knowledge increased the final index precision (e.g. Risle). This last [SOURCE: Page_16]

388   point illustrates another yet main advantage when using experts knowledge as the final assessment [SOURCE: Page_16]

389   gains precision in a legitimate way. Applying experts knowledge appeared particularly essential in [SOURCE: Page_16]

390   those situations where the most reliable assessment as possible should be obtained. [SOURCE: Page_16]

391       4.3. Ecological status assessment of estuarine water bodies from fish data and experts knowledge [SOURCE: Page_16]

392   Using the present approach, the probability of the fish based index to be in any range of stressor [SOURCE: Page_16]

393   values can be easily calculated. This allocation was here based on an arbitrary decomposition into 5 [SOURCE: Page_16]

394   equal classes of quality. A calibrating phase of the thresholds with the other European indices would [SOURCE: Page_16]

395   be necessary to provide a relevant index with the WFD. Indeed, this approach focused on the interest [SOURCE: Page_16]

396   of incorporating experts knowledge in fish index to improve quality assessment. It appeared especially [SOURCE: Page_16]

397   appropriate to visualize sensitivity in allocation on a range of classes with well distributed data. In a [SOURCE: Page_16]

398   WFD context, these classes do not necessary fit with five classes of environmental status. A [SOURCE: Page_16]

399   calibrating phase of the thresholds with the other European indices will be necessary to provide a [SOURCE: Page_16]

400   relevant index with the WFD. Nevertheless, this approach allowed to analyse patterns in quality [SOURCE: Page_16]

401   assessment and associated variability among both estimation methods and water bodies. [SOURCE: Page_16]

402   The current index appeared particularly appropriate in the context of risk management, a notion [SOURCE: Page_16]

403   developed by McAllister and Kirkwood (1998), and precautionary approach. In the WFD context, the [SOURCE: Page_16]

404   risk may be defined by the probability of not being in a good ecological status which is provided by the [SOURCE: Page_16]

405   Bayesian method (Drouineau et al., 2012). This might lead to different management measures for two [SOURCE: Page_16]

406   water bodies such as Baie des Veys and Blavet. Both were qualified as having a good ecological [SOURCE: Page_16]

407   status, but with different levels of risks (respectively 18% and 45%). In that context, though it had a [SOURCE: Page_16]

408   moderate effect on the final mean class, including experts knowledge was a significant improvement [SOURCE: Page_16]

409   because it often had a significant effect on the assessment precision. [SOURCE: Page_16]

410   According to the indicator, large estuaries (Seine, Gironde and Loire) were shown to be the most [SOURCE: Page_16]

411   deteriorated estuaries of their district. As precision was high for these estuaries, this statement could [SOURCE: Page_16]

412   be considered as reliable. As a consequence, specific restoration effort should be dedicated to large [SOURCE: Page_16]

413   estuaries. Assessments from fish data tended to be less precise for upstream water bodies than for [SOURCE: Page_16]

414   corresponding downstream water bodies. This indicated more restricted knowledge, perhaps linked to [SOURCE: Page_16]

Author-produced version of the article published in Ecological Indicators, 2013, 32, 147-156. The original publication is available at http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1470160X1300143X http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolind.2013.03.030 [SOURCE: Page_17]

415   the choice of metrics or the fishing gear (beam trawl) used during the surveys, less appropriate for [SOURCE: Page_17]

416   upstream water body. Similar remarks could be made when comparing small estuaries, which quality [SOURCE: Page_17]

417   estimates were uncertain, to large ones. Two converging facts explained that point. First, the size of [SOURCE: Page_17]

418   small estuaries limited sometimes the number of trawl hauls and consequently the assessment [SOURCE: Page_17]

419   precision. Secondly, given small estuaries were not as studied as the large ones in the past, prior [SOURCE: Page_17]

420   precision was globally smaller and did not match with fish data as much as for the large water bodies. [SOURCE: Page_17]

421   In that context, new data acquisition should be obtained from upstream and small water bodies in [SOURCE: Page_17]

422   priority, either from other surveys or by consulting specific experts. [SOURCE: Page_17]

423   The index proposed here appears especially relevant in data poor situation. For example in France, [SOURCE: Page_17]

424   only 22 water bodies have presently been monitored (for 14 more, a previous estimate was realized [SOURCE: Page_17]

425   between 2005 and 2009) though 54 estuarine transitional water bodies are listed in the WFD context. [SOURCE: Page_17]

426   For the remaining estuaries, given the cost of scientific surveys, the index may first provide an [SOURCE: Page_17]

427   assessment only based on experts knowledge. However, in this situation, these very preliminary [SOURCE: Page_17]

428   assessments would be based on experts opinion including all the implied subjectivity. Moreover, the [SOURCE: Page_17]

429   lack of quantitative assessment will prevent from measuring effects of restoration if water bodies are [SOURCE: Page_17]

430   not considered in good ecological status. [SOURCE: Page_17]

431   In conclusion, the Bayesian approach is a generic method fulfilling WFD index objectives. It can be [SOURCE: Page_17]

432   used for any index based on a pressure-impact approach. Its main advantages are (i) its flexibility in [SOURCE: Page_17]

433   the modelling phase, (ii) its estimates of uncertainty and (iii) its possibility to integrate easily and [SOURCE: Page_17]

434   rigorously experts knowledge. The presented method to collect and combine experts opinions in a [SOURCE: Page_17]

435   prior should not be ignored given its cost-efficiency and its adequacy with the common availability of [SOURCE: Page_17]

436   experts. [SOURCE: Page_17]
