Abstract. L-PEACH is an L-system-based functional-structural model for simulating [SOURCE: Page_2]

architectural growth and carbohydrate partitioning among individual organs in peach [SOURCE: Page_2]

(Prunus persica (L.) Batsch) trees. The original model provided a prototype for how tree [SOURCE: Page_2]

architecture and carbon economy could be integrated but did not simulate peach tree [SOURCE: Page_2]

architecture realistically. Moreover, evaluation of the functional characteristics of the [SOURCE: Page_2]

individual organs and the whole-tree remained a largely open issue. In this work we [SOURCE: Page_2]

incorporated Markovian models into L-PEACH in order to improve the architecture of [SOURCE: Page_2]

simulated trees. The model was also calibrated to grams of carbohydrate, and tools for [SOURCE: Page_2]

systematically displaying quantitative outputs and evaluating the behaviour of the model [SOURCE: Page_2]

were developed. The use of the Markovian model concept to model tree architecture in L- [SOURCE: Page_2]

PEACH reproduced tree behavior and responses to management practices visually similar [SOURCE: Page_2]

to trees in commercial orchards. The new architectural model along with a number of [SOURCE: Page_2]

improvements in the carbohydrate partitioning algorithms derived from the model [SOURCE: Page_2]

evaluation significantly improved results related to carbon allocation, such as organ [SOURCE: Page_2]

growth, carbohydrate assimilation, reserve dynamics, and maintenance respiration. The [SOURCE: Page_2]

model results are now consistent within the modelled tree structure and are in general [SOURCE: Page_2]

agreement with observations of peach trees growing under field conditions. [SOURCE: Page_2]

Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

PEACH (Grossman and DeJong 1994), a mechanistic computer model, was developed to [SOURCE: Page_3]

understand the functional carbon economy of peach trees, understand how fruit trees [SOURCE: Page_3]

function in the field environment, and predict tree growth and crop yield responses of [SOURCE: Page_3]

commercial peach trees. Although PEACH was able to simulate reproductive and [SOURCE: Page_3]

vegetative growth of peach trees, responses to variable environmental conditions, and [SOURCE: Page_3]

crop yield responses to commercial practices, it ignored interactions between tree [SOURCE: Page_3]

architecture and carbon allocation. Each organ type was treated collectively as a single [SOURCE: Page_3]

compartment and consequently all organs of a given type grew at the same rate. Because [SOURCE: Page_3]

of these limitations PEACH did not simulate changes in architecture over time and intra- [SOURCE: Page_3]

canopy variability among organs of the same type. These limitations were overcome in L- [SOURCE: Page_3]

PEACH (Allen et al. 2005; 2007), a more detailed simulation model of carbon economy, [SOURCE: Page_3]

in which the growth and function of organs were modeled individually within an [SOURCE: Page_3]

architectural   model   of   canopy     development.   L-systems   (Lindenmayer     1968; [SOURCE: Page_3]

Prusinkiewicz and Lindemayer 1990) were used to simulate the architectural [SOURCE: Page_3]

development of the tree and keep track of all its functional elements as it grows. The [SOURCE: Page_3]

partitioning of carbohydrates between individual tree components was modelled using an [SOURCE: Page_3]

analogy between the flow of resources in a plant and the flow of current in an electric [SOURCE: Page_3]

circuit (Prusinkiewicz et al. 2007b). [SOURCE: Page_3]

L-PEACH had thus been designed as a functional-structural plant model that [SOURCE: Page_3]

simulates growth and carbon source-sink relationships within the architectural framework [SOURCE: Page_3]

of a plant (Allen et al. 2005). However, substantial improvements of the model were [SOURCE: Page_3]

needed to increase the realism of the simulated trees, as the topology and geometry of the [SOURCE: Page_3]

3 [SOURCE: Page_3]

modelled tree (Allen et al. 2005) and the quantitative outputs generated by L-PEACH [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Allen et al. 2007) did not correspond closely with observations of peach trees growing [SOURCE: Page_4]

under field conditions. [SOURCE: Page_4]

To develop a more realistic model of tree architecture, we incorporated into L- [SOURCE: Page_4]

PEACH a Markovian model of shoot topology and bud fate. A similar approach was [SOURCE: Page_4]

previously used to simulate development of fruiting apple trees by incorporating a [SOURCE: Page_4]

Markovian model into L-systems-based architectural tree models (Renton et al. 2006; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Costes et al. this issue). The objectives of the present study were to improve the [SOURCE: Page_4]

architectural development of simulated trees in L-PEACH by using Markovian models, [SOURCE: Page_4]

evaluate the physiological characteristics of simulated trees within the new architectural [SOURCE: Page_4]

model, and document the most significant improvements in the model algorithms [SOURCE: Page_4]

obtained from this evaluation. To demonstrate the potential of the new version, we [SOURCE: Page_4]

simulated tree architectural development, individual organ growth and functionality, [SOURCE: Page_4]

carbohydrate assimilation, reserve storage and mobilization, and maintenance respiration [SOURCE: Page_4]

of peach trees during three consecutive years. [SOURCE: Page_4]

Tree architecture [SOURCE: Page_5]

The model is implemented using the L-system-based plant simulator LPFG included in [SOURCE: Page_5]

L-studio (http://www.algorithmicbotany.org/virtual_laboratory; Karwowski and Lane [SOURCE: Page_5]

2006) combined with Markovian models that are fully implemented in V-Plants software [SOURCE: Page_5]

(http://www-sop.inria.fr/virtualplants; Guédon et al., 2001). The conceptual framework [SOURCE: Page_5]

of L-systems is used to simulate carbohydrate allocation and integrate all the architectural [SOURCE: Page_5]

elements of the plant, while the Markovian models define the succession of shoots along [SOURCE: Page_5]

an axis and the branching pattern of the shoots. We selected the strategy developed in [SOURCE: Page_5]

In the L-system formalism, a plant is treated as a collection of semi-autonomous [SOURCE: Page_6]

modules (Prusinkiewicz 2004b). Specifically, L-PEACH modules represent stem [SOURCE: Page_6]

segments (internodes), buds, leaves, flowers, or fruits. The root system is treated [SOURCE: Page_6]

collectively as a single module. The modelled tree is then described as a branching [SOURCE: Page_6]

network of phytomers. Each phytomer consists of an internode with a specified initial [SOURCE: Page_6]

length, and a node that has a leaf and different types of buds attached to it. The bud [SOURCE: Page_6]

modules play a significant role in the tree architectural model: vegetative buds produce [SOURCE: Page_6]

new phytomers, which accommodate shoot growth, whereas floral buds produce flowers, [SOURCE: Page_6]

which accommodate reproductive growth. Buds can be terminal or axillary. Terminal [SOURCE: Page_6]

buds, which are only located at the end of a shoot, are always vegetative. Regarding [SOURCE: Page_6]

axillary buds, each phytomer has a central axillary bud, which can be blind (failing to [SOURCE: Page_6]

produce phytomers or flowers), floral or vegetative, with zero to two lateral floral axillary [SOURCE: Page_6]

buds. The number and characteristics of axillary buds, within a specific phytomer and [SOURCE: Page_6]

along the parent shoot, are modelled according to bivariate statistical models estimated [SOURCE: Page_6]

for three shoot types characterizing unpruned peach trees (brindles, mixed shoots and [SOURCE: Page_6]

vigorous shoots; Fournier et al. 1998) and adjusted in L-PEACH based on observations [SOURCE: Page_6]

of shoots from pruned trees. In the bivariate models, the first variable controls the fate of [SOURCE: Page_6]

the central bud, while the second variable controls the fate of the lateral buds associated [SOURCE: Page_6]

with the central bud. Branching organisation is modelled by hidden semi-Markov chains [SOURCE: Page_6]

(HSMCs) that are indexed by the node rank from the base to the top of the shoot, as a [SOURCE: Page_6]

succession of zones that significantly differ in their axillary bud fates (Table 1). Four sets [SOURCE: Page_6]

of parameters are estimated for each shoot type: initial probabilities that determine the [SOURCE: Page_6]

first zone at the base of the shoots, transition probabilities between zones, occupancy [SOURCE: Page_6]

6 [SOURCE: Page_6]

distributions representing the length of each zone, and two observation distributions [SOURCE: Page_7]

representing the fate of the central bud and the fate of the lateral buds within each zone, [SOURCE: Page_7]

respectively (see Guédon et al., 2001 and Renton et al, 2006 for details). These [SOURCE: Page_7]

distributions are the same in a given zone for all the shoot types, whereas transition [SOURCE: Page_7]

probabilities depend on shoot type, the median zones being progressively skipped when [SOURCE: Page_7]

the shoot length decreases (Table 1 and Fournier et al., 1998). [SOURCE: Page_7]

L-PEACH is initiated with a root and a stem segment that has a leaf, a vegetative [SOURCE: Page_7]

terminal bud, a vegetative axillary bud, and an axillary latent bud. Simulation begins with [SOURCE: Page_7]

the terminal bud break, and shoot growth is simulated through creation of new [SOURCE: Page_7]

phytomers. At this point the branching pattern of the tree is modelled with hidden semi- [SOURCE: Page_7]

Markov chains in a two-step process: selection of the shoot type and generation of a [SOURCE: Page_7]

succession of zones within each shoot, as determined by the bivariate model outlined [SOURCE: Page_7]

above. The shoot types are categorized by their length (number of stem segments in the [SOURCE: Page_7]

shoot) as small (5), medium-small (7-17), medium (16-35), long (36-56), and very-long [SOURCE: Page_7]

(59-87). The succession of zones within shoots is presented in Table 1. Small shoots are [SOURCE: Page_7]

assumed to have five blind nodes. The remaining shoots have different lengths, but they [SOURCE: Page_7]

all start with a blind zone and end with a floral and blind zone. In spite of this similarity, [SOURCE: Page_7]

the shoots differ in the number of zones and the number of vegetative and flower buds [SOURCE: Page_7]

(Table 1). These vegetative buds may become active in the same season (sylleptic [SOURCE: Page_7]

shoots), in the next growing season (proleptic shoots) or remain dormant. Regarding [SOURCE: Page_7]

terminal bud fate, potential length of the new shoots is based on the concept that [SOURCE: Page_7]

succeeding shoots have less vigour than their parent shoot (Durand et al. 2005). This is [SOURCE: Page_7]

modelled by a transition matrix representing a first order Markov chain, as proposed in [SOURCE: Page_7]

7 [SOURCE: Page_7]

MappleT (Costes et al., this issue). In addition, potential shoot length is reduced for [SOURCE: Page_8]

shoots produced late in the season (Costes et al. 2007). Once the type of shoot is [SOURCE: Page_8]

determined by either the Markov chain for terminal buds or the HSMCs for axillary buds, [SOURCE: Page_8]

if there is no carbohydrate limitation the shoot will grow to its full size. If there is a [SOURCE: Page_8]

carbohydrate limitation, the realized length will be reduced (Costes et al. 2007). The [SOURCE: Page_8]

flower buds remain dormant in the season in which they have been generated and set fruit [SOURCE: Page_8]

Functional characteristics of source and sinks [SOURCE: Page_8]

The original model (Allen et al. 2005) contained more than thirty functions describing [SOURCE: Page_8]

relationships between specific variables in the model, and was not calibrated to specific [SOURCE: Page_8]

carbohydrate, and many of the original functions were simplified by using common [SOURCE: Page_8]

sigmoidal functions to model functions with similar shapes, with scaling parameters for [SOURCE: Page_8]

calibrating these functions to specific components. These parameters can be easily [SOURCE: Page_8]

modified by the user in order to simulate different organ behavior or parameterize [SOURCE: Page_8]

8 [SOURCE: Page_8]

specific simulations. The parameters used to determine the functional characteristics of [SOURCE: Page_9]

sources and sinks are based in concepts of carbohydrate partitioning from literature. [SOURCE: Page_9]

However, when quantitative detail on the functionality of a given source-sink component [SOURCE: Page_9]

was unavailable, the functionality of the component was determined from simulated [SOURCE: Page_9]

Carbohydrate assimilation, transport and partitioning algorithm [SOURCE: Page_13]

For the purpose of carbohydrate assimilation, transport and partitioning within the [SOURCE: Page_13]

modelled tree, the tree branching network described in the tree architecture section is [SOURCE: Page_13]

abstracted into a dynamically reconfigured, non-linear and non-stationary electric circuit. [SOURCE: Page_13]

Its sub-circuits represent individual stem segments or plant organs (buds, flowers, fruits, [SOURCE: Page_13]

and leaves), connected into a network with transport resistances (Allen et al. 2005; [SOURCE: Page_13]

Prusinkiewicz et al. 2007b). Roots are treated as one large single module. Each sub- [SOURCE: Page_13]

circuit has components (sources of electromotive force in series with resistances) that [SOURCE: Page_13]

represent primary growth (elongation growth), secondary growth (girth growth), storage, [SOURCE: Page_13]

and maintenance respiration (Figure 2). Parameters of these components capture plant [SOURCE: Page_13]

module’s physiological potential to utilize carbohydrates for growth, respiration or [SOURCE: Page_13]

storage. Physiological potentials for growth and growth respiration are primarily based on [SOURCE: Page_13]

defined relative growth rate functions for each organ, while maintenance respiration is [SOURCE: Page_13]

estimated from temperature and empirically derived relationships (Grossman and DeJong [SOURCE: Page_13]

1994b). The physiological potential of storage sinks to take up carbohydrates is estimated [SOURCE: Page_13]

by assigning a storage capacity as a percent of dry weight to each module capable of [SOURCE: Page_13]

storage. In each simulation step, the electric circuit is used to calculate the amount of [SOURCE: Page_13]

carbohydrate exchanged between all the elements of the electric circuit. The circuit is [SOURCE: Page_13]

13 [SOURCE: Page_13]

then updated to reflect the resulting changes, and the next simulation step proceeds. A [SOURCE: Page_14]

numerical method implemented using L-systems was developed to iteratively solve the [SOURCE: Page_14]

equations for carbohydrate flow and allocation (Prusinkiewicz et al. 2007b). [SOURCE: Page_14]

Simulation of commercial practices [SOURCE: Page_14]

In the current version of L-PEACH we included modules in that make it possible to [SOURCE: Page_14]

simulate management operations typically conducted in the field including: pruning, [SOURCE: Page_14]

budding, fruit thinning, and harvesting. [SOURCE: Page_14]

Pruning is performed by directly manipulating the tree displayed on the screen with [SOURCE: Page_14]

the LPFG plant modeling program (Prusinkiewicz et al. 2007a). Tree responses to [SOURCE: Page_14]

pruning are modelled using the concepts of apical dominance (Wilson 2000) and [SOURCE: Page_14]

reiteration (Hallé et al., 1978). When a pruning cut is made, the fates of the buds between [SOURCE: Page_14]

the cut and the next branching point are reassigned. Following reiteration concepts, we [SOURCE: Page_14]

assumed that, if axillary vegetative buds are present, the distal buds are assigned to the [SOURCE: Page_14]

same shoot category as the shoot that has been removed. Following the apical control [SOURCE: Page_14]

concept (Wilson 2000), we also assumed that only a few axillary buds become active, [SOURCE: Page_14]

while the rest remain latent. This follows the idea that the distal buds are no longer under [SOURCE: Page_14]

14 [SOURCE: Page_14]

apical dominance, but as they emerge they have a dominance effect on the more proximal [SOURCE: Page_15]

buds. The number of activated buds is determined by the stem segment circumference [SOURCE: Page_15]

below the pruning cut. If only axillary latent buds are present then the distal latent buds [SOURCE: Page_15]

become active and develop very-long shoots (Pernice et al. 2006). In any case, if the [SOURCE: Page_15]

pruning cut is made during the growing season, the distal buds become active [SOURCE: Page_15]

immediately. If pruning is done during the dormant period, the distal buds become active [SOURCE: Page_15]

at vegetative bud break. [SOURCE: Page_15]

To increase the realism of the model while simulating commercial fruit tree growth, [SOURCE: Page_15]

vegetative propagation of the tree by budding can be simulated by pruning a simulated [SOURCE: Page_15]

young seedling tree back to a few centimetres above the ground, and growing a new tree [SOURCE: Page_15]

from the new shoot that grew in response to the hard pruning cut. As budding is usually [SOURCE: Page_15]

carried out in the spring (about May, Table 2) and potential length of shoots is based on [SOURCE: Page_15]

how late in the growing season the shoot starts to grow, a budded tree has a final size [SOURCE: Page_15]

limitation dependent on the “budding” date. [SOURCE: Page_15]

or more peaches are separated by less than a specified number of stem segments, the [SOURCE: Page_15]

more distal fruits of are removed. Thus the fruit than remain after thinning can be [SOURCE: Page_15]

important component of simulating different peach cultivars. In the future versions of the [SOURCE: Page_16]

Evaluation of the new architectural model and its subsequent effects on source-sink [SOURCE: Page_16]

behaviour of simulated peach trees [SOURCE: Page_16]

Evaluation of the model was carried out by simulating peach tree development during [SOURCE: Page_16]

three consecutive years, with the parameters presented in Table 2. Weather data was [SOURCE: Page_16]

obtained from CIMIS (California Irrigation Management Information System, Davis, CA [SOURCE: Page_16]

station). The simulated trees were either left unpruned or were trained to a perpendicular [SOURCE: Page_16]

V system, following procedures described by DeJong et al. (1994) and simulated as [SOURCE: Page_16]

interactive pruning cuts in winter. [SOURCE: Page_16]

Tree architecture [SOURCE: Page_16]

In the first growing season, prior to budding, a simulated tree had a single, very-long [SOURCE: Page_16]

shoot that was cut before reaching its final length (Figure 3, Table 1). After budding, a [SOURCE: Page_16]

very-long shoot emerged and grew in the direction of the main axis, while axillary [SOURCE: Page_16]

vegetative shoots grew sylleptically (Genard et al. 1994). The resulting simulated trees [SOURCE: Page_16]

were similar to those found in a fruit tree nursery just prior to sale. Prior to the beginning [SOURCE: Page_16]

of the second year, the trunk of pruned trees was cut to half a meter (Figure 3A). This cut [SOURCE: Page_16]

prompted a reiteration response, resulting in the production of new very-long shoots [SOURCE: Page_16]

16 [SOURCE: Page_16]

below the cut after bud break. This new shoot growth compensated for the perturbed [SOURCE: Page_17]

equilibrium between the shoot and the root after the pruning cut (Genard et al. 1998; [SOURCE: Page_17]

Pernice et al. 2006). The sylleptic shoot growth behaviour within the new very-long [SOURCE: Page_17]

shoots was similar to that of the first growing season (Figure 3A). This response is the [SOURCE: Page_17]

key to eventually developing the strong, open structure of commercial peach trees. In [SOURCE: Page_17]

contrast, in unpruned trees new shoots grew mainly from the terminal buds, and thus [SOURCE: Page_17]

were less vigorous than their parent shoots. The first crop of fruit was produced on shoots [SOURCE: Page_17]

formed in the previous (first) year (Figure 3B). [SOURCE: Page_17]

At the beginning of the third year, two branches oriented in the same vertical plane [SOURCE: Page_17]

were selected for developing the main scaffolds of the V-shaped training system (Figure [SOURCE: Page_17]

3A). The selected branches received heading cuts, and thus very-long shoots grew again [SOURCE: Page_17]

from their terminal ends after vegetative bud break. Sylleptic shoots that were formed [SOURCE: Page_17]

during the previous year on the previously established main scaffolds remained unpruned. [SOURCE: Page_17]

These shoots produced flowers, fruits and proleptic shoot growth after bud break (Figure [SOURCE: Page_17]

3A). During the third growing season, unpruned trees produced a large crop causing a [SOURCE: Page_17]

significant reduction in tree vigour compared with pruned trees (Figure 3B). [SOURCE: Page_17]

Source-sink behaviour of simulated tree [SOURCE: Page_17]

The complexity of L-PEACH makes it difficult to analyze the relationships between [SOURCE: Page_17]

individual components of the model. For instance, the direction and quantity of carbon [SOURCE: Page_17]

flux through a stem segment at any given time step depends on the sink demands and [SOURCE: Page_17]

source supplies above, below and within this segment, and it is difficult to predict these [SOURCE: Page_17]

values a priori. For the same reason, quantitative verification of all the individual [SOURCE: Page_17]

17 [SOURCE: Page_17]

components of the model is difficult, and it would be virtually impossible to design a [SOURCE: Page_18]

field experiment to independently evaluate the physiological characteristics of all the [SOURCE: Page_18]

components of a tree. However, in spite of these limitations, L-PEACH is a potential tool [SOURCE: Page_18]

to integrate and evaluate source-sink relationships in peach trees and elucidate seasonal [SOURCE: Page_18]

organ and whole-tree behaviour at higher levels of organization. For demonstration [SOURCE: Page_18]

purposes some quantitative outputs are presented here to illustrate simulated results [SOURCE: Page_18]

related to carbon allocation, such as carbohydrate assimilation, maintenance respiration, [SOURCE: Page_18]

reserve dynamics, and organ growth. [SOURCE: Page_18]

As illustrated in Figure 4A,C, L-PEACH effectively models variations in net [SOURCE: Page_18]

photosynthesis as a consequence of variable weather conditions. The model is sensitive to [SOURCE: Page_18]

cloudy days, although differences in the leaf export rate are reduced, compared to the [SOURCE: Page_18]

differences in photosynthesis (Figure 4C). This phenomenon may be explained by an [SOURCE: Page_18]

increase in the amount of carbohydrates mobilized from the leaf storage compartment in [SOURCE: Page_18]

cloudy days. This is consistent with the observation by Wardlaw (1990) that carbohydrate [SOURCE: Page_18]

reserves built up within the leaf may be mobilized and exported when current [SOURCE: Page_18]

photosynthesis is decreased. The amount of carbohydrate assimilated by individual leaves [SOURCE: Page_18]

was also a function of the physiological state of the leaves. Our basic approach for [SOURCE: Page_18]

dynamically modelling leaf growth as a function of available resources and potential leaf [SOURCE: Page_18]

size (2) was also successful in reproducing peach leaf expansion over time (Figure 4B, [SOURCE: Page_18]

4D): leaf maturity was achieved about 25 days after vegetative break, consistent with [SOURCE: Page_18]

observations by Steinberg et al. 1990. However, we recognize that our modelling [SOURCE: Page_18]

approach could be improved using more detailed numerical methods (Seleznyova 2007). [SOURCE: Page_18]

Simulated seasonal patterns of tree daily carbohydrate assimilation increased with [SOURCE: Page_19]

increases in total leaf biomass (Figure 5). Between 33% and 50% of the tree carbohydrate [SOURCE: Page_19]

assimilation was used for tree maintenance respiration (Figure 5), indicating the [SOURCE: Page_19]

importance of respiratory cost in the carbohydrate balance of the tree (Grossman and [SOURCE: Page_19]

DeJong 1994b; Vivin et al. 2002). Simulated tree daily maintenance respiration increased [SOURCE: Page_19]

with increases in temperature and whole-tree biomass (Figure 5, Figure 6A). When tree [SOURCE: Page_19]

daily maintenance respiration reached its maximum rates, it accounted for a relatively [SOURCE: Page_19]

constant amount of carbohydrate usage (Figure 5) (Grossman and DeJong 1994b). [SOURCE: Page_19]

Similar behaviour to that reported for total tree maintenance respiration was observed for [SOURCE: Page_19]

both the above- and below-ground structural organs (Figure 6B). Assuming that fruits [SOURCE: Page_19]

were harvested before reaching the constant amount of carbohydrate usage (Figure 6C); [SOURCE: Page_19]

the shape and magnitude of simulated fruit respiration curves were consistent with the [SOURCE: Page_19]

results of DeJong and Walton (1989) for late maturing peach trees. [SOURCE: Page_19]

Part of the carbohydrates assimilated by the tree during the growing season was [SOURCE: Page_19]

stored in the root and stem segments. Root carbohydrate reserves were subsequently used [SOURCE: Page_19]

for maintenance respiration during the winter season and to support early tree growth [SOURCE: Page_19]

after bud break (Figure 7) (Loescher et al. 1990; Jordan and Habib 1996). The seasonal [SOURCE: Page_19]

dynamics of reserves in the stem segments was not as clear as the dynamics observed in [SOURCE: Page_19]

the root. This was because the root supplied carbohydrates to the stem segments before [SOURCE: Page_19]

bud break and because part of the carbohydrates stored in the stem segments were [SOURCE: Page_19]

removed after winter pruning (results not shown). More refinement is needed to quantify [SOURCE: Page_19]

carbohydrate storage and mobilization functioning in the model, but modelling stem and [SOURCE: Page_19]

19 [SOURCE: Page_19]

root storage as explicit sinks and sources for specified periods of time, as suggested by [SOURCE: Page_20]

Cannell and Dewar (1994), appears to yield generally correct model behaviour. [SOURCE: Page_20]

Simulated tree carbon assimilation provided sufficient carbohydrate to support organ [SOURCE: Page_20]

growth (Figure 8). During the three years of simulation, most biomass was accumulated [SOURCE: Page_20]

in the stem segments, and root weight was about one-third of total stem segment biomass [SOURCE: Page_20]

(Grossman and DeJong 1994a). The fruits also accumulated a significant amount of [SOURCE: Page_20]

assimilates while the leaves had lower amounts of standing biomass. The simulated [SOURCE: Page_20]

patterns of organ growth also reflected the interaction with the tree architecture and the [SOURCE: Page_20]

different components of the models (Figure 8). The reduction in total leaf and stem [SOURCE: Page_20]

segment weights after the growing season was a consequence of leaf abscission and [SOURCE: Page_20]

winter pruning, respectively. The seasonal patterns of simulated root weight reflected that [SOURCE: Page_20]

the tree carbohydrate balance was source-limited at the beginning of the growing season. [SOURCE: Page_20]

Total fruit carbohydrate mass per tree decreased with a reduction in the number of fruits [SOURCE: Page_20]

per tree after fruit thinning (day 870 in Figure 8). The substantial reduction in fruit [SOURCE: Page_20]

competition after fruit thinning allowed optimization on average fruit carbohydrate mass [SOURCE: Page_20]

at harvest (~33 g fruit-1) (Figure 8). [SOURCE: Page_20]

Conclusion [SOURCE: Page_20]

The use of the hidden semi-Markov chain concepts for modelling branching [SOURCE: Page_20]

structures in L-PEACH was successful at reproducing trees that were similar to peach [SOURCE: Page_20]

trees observed in orchards. The new architectural model along with a number of [SOURCE: Page_20]

improvements in the carbohydrate partitioning algorithms significantly improved results [SOURCE: Page_20]

related to carbon allocation, such as organ growth, carbohydrate assimilation, reserve [SOURCE: Page_20]

20 [SOURCE: Page_20]

dynamics, and maintenance respiration. The model results were in general agreement [SOURCE: Page_21]

with observations of peach trees growing under field conditions. [SOURCE: Page_21]

The current L-PEACH model substantially improved previous three-dimensional [SOURCE: Page_21]

depictions of peach trees presented by Allen et al. (2005; 2007). The realism of the new [SOURCE: Page_21]

architecture and the pruning responses can help understand the general practices that [SOURCE: Page_21]

should be followed for training systems designed to obtain specific tree shapes. Users can [SOURCE: Page_21]

observe how the natural growth habit of the tree is modified by pruning, how the tree [SOURCE: Page_21]

responds to different types of pruning cuts, including improper cuts, without dramatic [SOURCE: Page_21]

commercial consequences. These features allow L-PEACH to be used as a tool for [SOURCE: Page_21]

teaching, including interactive lessons on common training practices. [SOURCE: Page_21]

Although L-PEACH is still in the early stages of development, it is a useful tool [SOURCE: Page_21]

for simultaneously modelling tree architectural growth and carbohydrate source-sink [SOURCE: Page_21]

relationships in peach trees. More quantitative validation of the model at the whole plant [SOURCE: Page_21]

and individual organ levels is needed to increase the accuracy of simulated trees. Since [SOURCE: Page_21]

much of the available data from previous experiments and literature are not suitable for [SOURCE: Page_21]

quantitatively validating L-PEACH, there is a need to collect additional quantitative data [SOURCE: Page_21]

for this purpose. L-PEACH can be used for identifying the relevant outputs of the model [SOURCE: Page_21]

to be measured, developing new quantitative hypotheses and guiding experimental [SOURCE: Page_21]

research. This will result in a greater understanding on the environmental physiology of [SOURCE: Page_21]

peach trees, which is the overarching objective of the peach modelling project. [SOURCE: Page_21]
