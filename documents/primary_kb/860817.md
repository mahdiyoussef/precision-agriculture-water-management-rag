Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

Among the numerous pedotransfer functions (PTFs) published, class-PTfs have received [SOURCE: Page_2]

little attention because their accuracy is often considered as limited. However, recent studies [SOURCE: Page_2]

show that performance of class-PTFs can be similar to the more popular continuous-PTFs. In [SOURCE: Page_2]

this study, we compare the performance of PTFs that were derived from a set of 456 horizons [SOURCE: Page_2]

collected in France grouped by combinations of texture, bulk density and type of horizon [SOURCE: Page_2]

(topsoil and subsoil). The performance of these class-PTFs was validated against water [SOURCE: Page_2]

retained at -33 and -1500 kPa. Our results show that the best performance was obtained with [SOURCE: Page_2]

1 [SOURCE: Page_2]

class-PTFs that used both texture and bulk density (texture-structural class-PTFs). They [SOURCE: Page_3]

showed also that incorporation of horizon type into the PTF did not improve prediction [SOURCE: Page_3]

performance. Comparison of performance at -33 and -1500 kPa showed very little difference, [SOURCE: Page_3]

thus indicating no bias according to the value of water potential. Finally, the class-PTFs [SOURCE: Page_3]

developed are well suited for predicting water retention properties at continental and national [SOURCE: Page_3]

scales because only very basic soils data is available at these scales. A map of the available [SOURCE: Page_3]

water capacity (AWC) was established for France using the 1:1 000 000 Soil Geographical [SOURCE: Page_3]

Database of France and an averaged AWC of 104 mm was computed for France. [SOURCE: Page_3]

## INTRODUCTION

Pedotransfer functions (PTFs) use basic soil properties that are relatively easily available [SOURCE: Page_3]

to less frequent and more difficult to measure soil properties such as hydraulic ones (Bouma [SOURCE: Page_3]

and van Lanen, 1987). Many are continuous pedotransfer functions (continuous-PTFs) [SOURCE: Page_3]

developed over the last three decades and are empirical regression functions relating hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_3]

parameters to basic soil properties including texture, organic matter content and bulk density [SOURCE: Page_3]

(e.g. Bastet et al., 1999; Wösten et al., 2001; Pachepsky et al., 2006). Thus continuous-PTFs [SOURCE: Page_3]

enabling the prediction of water content at particular water potentials (Rawls et al., 1982 & [SOURCE: Page_3]

2004) or the estimation of the parameters of models of the water retention curve (Vereecken [SOURCE: Page_3]

et al., 1989; Bruand et al., 1994; Leenhardt, 1995; Minasny et al., 1999; Wösten et al., 2001; [SOURCE: Page_3]

Cresswell et al., 2006; Tranter et al., 2007). [SOURCE: Page_3]

2 [SOURCE: Page_3]

In addition to the development of continuous-PTFs, class pedotransfer functions (class- [SOURCE: Page_4]

PTFs) were also developed (Wösten et al., 1995; Pachepsky et al., 2003; Rawls et al., 2003). [SOURCE: Page_4]

Most class-PTFs provide class average water contents at particular water potentials or one [SOURCE: Page_4]

average water retention curve for every textural class (e.g. Nemes et al, 2001; Nemes, 2002; [SOURCE: Page_4]

Bruand et al., 2003 & 2004). They received little attention because their accuracy was [SOURCE: Page_4]

considered limited (Wösten et al., 1995). Due to the large range in particle size distribution, [SOURCE: Page_4]

clay mineralogy, organic matter content and structural development within each texture class, [SOURCE: Page_4]

water retention properties for individual soils vary considerably (Wösten et al., 1999). Class- [SOURCE: Page_4]

PTFs are easy to use given that they require little soil information and are well suited for [SOURCE: Page_4]

predicting water retention properties at continental and national scales because only very [SOURCE: Page_4]

basic soils data is available at these scales (Wösten et al. 1995; Lilly et al., 1999; Wösten et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al. 1999; Nemes et al., 2003). [SOURCE: Page_4]

Several studies provide information on the performance of continuous-PTFs (Minasny et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al., 1999; Wösten et al., 2001; Cornelis et al., 2001; Donatelli et al., 2004) and class-PTFs [SOURCE: Page_4]

(Pachepsky and Rawls, 1999; Wösten et al., 2001; Ungaro et al., 2005). However, there are [SOURCE: Page_4]

very few studies comparing the performance of continuous- and class-PTFs when applied to [SOURCE: Page_4]

the same dataset (Wösten et al., 1995). Al Majou et al. (2007) compared the performance of [SOURCE: Page_4]

class- and continuous-PTFs and showed that they perform equally well despite better [SOURCE: Page_4]

incorporation of individual soil properties within the continuous-PTFs. These results [SOURCE: Page_4]

reinforced the significance of class-PTFs as developed by Bruand et al. (2003) that were [SOURCE: Page_4]

based on texture alone or on both texture and clod bulk density, the latter giving the best [SOURCE: Page_4]

performance. However, use of these class-PTFs has remained limited because clod bulk [SOURCE: Page_4]

density is not available in most soil databases This study develops the study by Al Majou et [SOURCE: Page_4]

al. (2007) for predicting volumetric water content at several water potentials by combining [SOURCE: Page_4]

texture, bulk density and type of horizon. The validity of these class-PTFs was assessed at -33 [SOURCE: Page_4]

3 [SOURCE: Page_4]

and -1500 kPa water potential and the class-PTFs developed in this study were used to derive [SOURCE: Page_5]

maps of available water capacity for France. [SOURCE: Page_5]

## MATERIAL AND METHODS

Data collection [SOURCE: Page_5]

Class-PTFs were developed using a set of 456 horizons comprising 138 topsoil horizons [SOURCE: Page_5]

(from 0 to 30 cm depth) and 318 subsoil horizons (> 30 cm depth) collected from Cambisols, [SOURCE: Page_5]

Luvisols, Planosols, Albeluvisols, Podzols and Fluvisols (ISSS Working Group WRB, 1998) [SOURCE: Page_5]

located in the Paris basin, Brittany, the western coastal marshlands and Pyrenean piedmont [SOURCE: Page_5]

plain (Figure 1a). A set of 197 horizons from Cambisols, Luvisols and Fluvisols (ISSS [SOURCE: Page_5]

Working Group WRB, 1998), from several areas of France and developed on a large range of [SOURCE: Page_5]

parent materials was collated in order to test the derived class-PTFs (Figure 1b). [SOURCE: Page_5]

Basic and water retention properties [SOURCE: Page_5]

Particle size distribution was measured using the pipette method after pre-treatment with [SOURCE: Page_5]

hydrogen peroxide and sodium hexametaphosphate (Robert & Tessier 1974). The soil textural [SOURCE: Page_5]

triangle of the Commission of the European Communities was used to derive classes [SOURCE: Page_5]

(Commission of the European Communities, 1985) (Figure 2). The cation exchange capacity [SOURCE: Page_5]

(cmolc kg-1 of oven-dried soil) was measured using the cobalt-hexamine trichloride method [SOURCE: Page_5]

(Ciesielski & Sterckeman 1997) and organic carbon by oxidation using excess potassium [SOURCE: Page_5]

dichromate in sulphuric acid at 135°C (Baize 2000). Bulk density (Db) was measured by using [SOURCE: Page_5]

cylinders 1236 cm^3 in volume taken when the soil was near to field capacity. The gravimetric [SOURCE: Page_5]

water content was determined by using pressure plate apparatus for the 456 horizons data set [SOURCE: Page_5]

at –1, –3.3, –10, –33, –100, –330 and –1500 kPa water potential, and for the 197 horizon data [SOURCE: Page_5]

4 [SOURCE: Page_5]

set at –33 and –1500 kPa water potential, by using undisturbed samples (10–15 cm^3) collected [SOURCE: Page_6]

when the soil was near to field capacity for both sets (Bruand and Tessier, 2000). Then, the [SOURCE: Page_6]

volumetric water content (θ) for each horizon was computed using the bulk density of horizon [SOURCE: Page_6]

(Table 1). [SOURCE: Page_6]

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Deriving the class-PTFs [SOURCE: Page_7]

The class-PTFs developed in this study comprised average water content at seven water [SOURCE: Page_7]

potentials. They were first established according to the soil texture classes (texture class- [SOURCE: Page_7]

PTFs) used by the Commission of European Communities (1985) for all horizons (Table 2). [SOURCE: Page_7]

Then, as topsoils and subsoils often have different pore size distribution particularly with [SOURCE: Page_7]

respect to macroporosity, texture class-PTFs were also developed after stratification by type [SOURCE: Page_7]

of horizon (topsoil and subsoil horizons) (Table 3). Then, due to differences in bulk density [SOURCE: Page_7]

(Db), class-PTFs were established according to both texture and Db (texture-structural class- [SOURCE: Page_7]

PTFs) for the whole set of horizons without any other stratification (Table 4) and also after [SOURCE: Page_7]

stratification by the type of horizon (topsoil and subsoil horizons) (Table 5). [SOURCE: Page_7]

Validity of the textural and texturo-structural class-PTFs [SOURCE: Page_7]

The texture class-PTFs underestimated water retained (MEP = –0.015 cm^3.cm-3) when [SOURCE: Page_7]

applied to the test dataset (Table 6). The precision of the estimation was small with SDP = [SOURCE: Page_7]

0.041 cm^3.cm-3. There was a 0.011 cm^3.cm-3 decrease in the prediction bias and a 0.009 [SOURCE: Page_7]

cm^3.cm-3 increase in the precision with texture-structural class-PTFs. With the texture class- [SOURCE: Page_7]

PTFs, the greatest bias and the least precision were recorded for the Fine texture class (MEP = [SOURCE: Page_7]

-0.025 cm^3.cm-3 and SDP = 0.042 cm^3.cm-3), and the improvement in estimation performance [SOURCE: Page_7]

was particularly significant for that texture with the texture-structural class-PTFs (MEP = - [SOURCE: Page_7]

0.005 cm^3.cm-3 and SDP = 0.032 cm^3.cm-3). Therefore, the high RMSE recorded with the [SOURCE: Page_7]

texture class-PTFs (RMSE = 0.044 cm^3.cm-3) was related to a relatively poor prediction [SOURCE: Page_7]

precision (SDP = 0.041 cm^3.cm-3), the bias being small (MEP = -0.015 cm^3.cm-3). However, [SOURCE: Page_7]

6 [SOURCE: Page_7]

this RMSE was smaller than the RMSE recorded by Bruand et al. (2003) for volumetric water [SOURCE: Page_8]

content with texture class-PTFs that enabled prediction of the gravimetric water content at -33 [SOURCE: Page_8]

and -1500 kPa water potential. The smaller RMSE recorded with the texture-structural class- [SOURCE: Page_8]

PTFs (ΔRMSE = 0.011 cm^3.cm-3) was related to the significant decrease in the estimation bias [SOURCE: Page_8]

and increase in precision. The RMSE recorded with the texture-structural class-PTFs was [SOURCE: Page_8]

again smaller than the RMSE recorded by Bruand et al. (2003) for the volumetric water [SOURCE: Page_8]

content with texture-structural class-PTFs developed in their study. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Validity of the texture and texture-structural class-PTFs after stratification by horizon type [SOURCE: Page_8]

Establishing textural class-PTFs after stratification according to the type of horizon (i.e. [SOURCE: Page_8]

by separating topsoil and subsoil horizons) did not improve the performance of the texture [SOURCE: Page_8]

class-PTFs (ΔMEP = 0.001 cm^3.cm-3 and ΔSDP = 0.002 cm^3.cm-3) (Table 6). There was also [SOURCE: Page_8]

no improvement in the performance with the texture-structural class-PTFs after stratification [SOURCE: Page_8]

by horizon type (ΔMEP = 0.001 cm^3.cm-3 and ΔSDP = 0.003 cm^3.cm-3) (Table 6). This lack [SOURCE: Page_8]

of improvement explains the similar RMSE that were recorded with the texture and texture- [SOURCE: Page_8]

structural class-PTFs with or without stratification by horizon type (Table 6). [SOURCE: Page_8]

Validity of the texture and texture-structural class-PTFs according to water potential [SOURCE: Page_8]

Analysis of the results according to water potential showed that each type of class-PTF [SOURCE: Page_8]

studied led to roughly similar performance at -33 and -1500 kPa (Figure 3). The bias was [SOURCE: Page_8]

however slightly improved at -33 kPa for each type of PTF discussed (Figure 3). On the other [SOURCE: Page_8]

hand, the precision was a little greater and the RMSE smaller at -1500 kPa except for the [SOURCE: Page_8]

texture-structural PTFs (Table 7). This weak difference in performance at -33 and -1500 kPa [SOURCE: Page_8]

means similar performance in a large range of water potential for the discussed class-PTFs. [SOURCE: Page_8]

Application of class-PTFs to France [SOURCE: Page_9]

Class-PTFs as developed in this study were used to compute available water capacity [SOURCE: Page_9]

(AWC) for Soil Typological Units (STU) in the 1:1 000 000 Soil Geographical Database of [SOURCE: Page_9]

France (King et al., 1995). Available water was taken as the water held between wilting point [SOURCE: Page_9]

(-1500 kPa water potential) and field capacity (-10 kPa water potential). A water potential of - [SOURCE: Page_9]

10 kPa was shown as the water potential at field capacity for the studied soil (Al Majou et al., [SOURCE: Page_9]

2008). The depth, texture and bulk density of the topsoils and subsoils were based on [SOURCE: Page_9]

available descriptions of STU attributes (King et al., 1995). The amount of available water for [SOURCE: Page_9]

each topsoil and subsoil was derived from the appropriate class-PTFs multiplied by the [SOURCE: Page_9]

thickness of each horizon. Then, the total available water in mm for each STU was computed [SOURCE: Page_9]

by summation of the corresponding topsoil and subsoil. Next, the available water in mm for [SOURCE: Page_9]

each Soil Mapping Unit (SMU) was computed according to the proportion of the different [SOURCE: Page_9]

STU present in each SMU (King et al., 1995; Wösten et al., 1999). A map of the AWC was [SOURCE: Page_9]

established by using the texture-structural class-PTFs that showed the best performance [SOURCE: Page_9]

(Table 4, Figure 4). The average AWC of 104 mm was computed for France by taking into [SOURCE: Page_9]

account the surface area of each SMU. [SOURCE: Page_9]

## CONCLUSION

Our results show that the best performing PTF was based on both texture and bulk density [SOURCE: Page_9]

(texture-structural class-PTFs). It was also shown that incorporation of horizon type did not [SOURCE: Page_9]

improve prediction performance. Comparison of the performance at -33 and -1500 kPa [SOURCE: Page_9]

showed very little difference, thus indicating no bias according to value of water potential. [SOURCE: Page_9]

Finally, the class-PTFs developed require little soil information and are well suited for [SOURCE: Page_9]

predicting water retention properties at continental and national scales because only very [SOURCE: Page_9]

basic soils data is available at these scales. This was illustrated with the use of the class-PTFs [SOURCE: Page_9]

8 [SOURCE: Page_9]

developed to generate a map of the available water capacity (AWC) for the whole of France [SOURCE: Page_10]

using the 1:1 000 000 Soil Geographical Database of France; as a result the average AWC [SOURCE: Page_10]

was computed for France. [SOURCE: Page_10]
