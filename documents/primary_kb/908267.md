Abstract [SOURCE: Page_2]

With rapid flood extent mapping capabilities, Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) im- [SOURCE: Page_2]

ages of river inundation prove to be very relevant to operational flood management. [SOURCE: Page_2]

In this context, a recently developed method provides distributed water levels from [SOURCE: Page_2]

SAR images. Furthermore, in view of improving numerical flood prediction, a vari- [SOURCE: Page_2]

ational data assimilation method (4D-var) using such distributed water level has [SOURCE: Page_2]

been developed in Part I of this study. This method combines an optimal sense [SOURCE: Page_2]

remote sensing data (distributed water levels extracted from spatial images) and a [SOURCE: Page_2]

2D shallow water model. In the present article (Part II of the study), we also derive [SOURCE: Page_2]

water levels with a ± 40 cm average vertical uncertainty from a RADARSAT-1 im- [SOURCE: Page_2]

Preprint submitted to Journal of hydrology                            29 April 2010 [SOURCE: Page_2]

age of a Mosel River flood event (1997, France). Assimilated in a 2D shallow water [SOURCE: Page_3]

hydraulic model using the 4D-var developed method, these SAR derived spatially [SOURCE: Page_3]

distributed water levels prove to be capable of enhancing model calibration. Indeed, [SOURCE: Page_3]

the assimilation process can identify optimal Manning friction coefficients, at least [SOURCE: Page_3]

in the river channel. Moreover, used as a guide for sensitivity analysis, remote sens- [SOURCE: Page_3]

ing water levels allow also identifying some areas in the floodplain and the channel [SOURCE: Page_3]

where Manning friction coefficients are homogeneous. This allows basing the spatial [SOURCE: Page_3]

segmentation of roughness coefficient on floodplain hydraulic functioning. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Digital Elevation Model, variational data assimilation, hydraulic coherence. [SOURCE: Page_3]

1    Introduction [SOURCE: Page_3]

Over the last decades, important efforts have been set up to better understand [SOURCE: Page_3]

and manage floods, which is one of the most important natural hazard in the [SOURCE: Page_3]

world. To identify flood prone areas and to manage flood events, satellite [SOURCE: Page_3]

images could be very useful. Especially because of their all weather image [SOURCE: Page_3]

acquisition capability, Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) sensors are relevant [SOURCE: Page_3]

tools for the spatial characterization of floods. In this context, radar images [SOURCE: Page_3]

of floods are nowadays mostly used for instantaneous flood extent extraction. [SOURCE: Page_3]

Nevertheless, as mentioned by [25], there is no doubt that earth observation [SOURCE: Page_3]

images contain information that goes beyond simple flood extents. In this [SOURCE: Page_3]

context, [23] shown that high spatial resolution (25 m) SAR spaceborne images [SOURCE: Page_3]

allow the estimation of distributed water levels in floodplains with reasonable [SOURCE: Page_3]

Furthermore, hydraulic modelling is of paramount importance in most flood [SOURCE: Page_4]

forecasting and management systems. Due to huge stakes in flood manage- [SOURCE: Page_4]

ficult to determine a priori. In an operational context, a “hand” calibration [SOURCE: Page_4]

sufficient to make the calibration reliable [1] as no reference data is available [SOURCE: Page_4]

in the regions in-between those point measurements and a “hand” calibration [SOURCE: Page_4]

model and in return a better reliability. Spatially distributed data of a flood [SOURCE: Page_4]

data is satellite imagery. Recent studies, [24, 14] have shown that SAR de- [SOURCE: Page_4]

one dimensional hydraulic models with a GLUE approach [3]. Furthermore, [SOURCE: Page_4]

3   Water level estimation using SAR image [SOURCE: Page_10]

Based on the method developed by [21] providing water level estimates with [SOURCE: Page_10]

a ± 18 cm mean uncertainty using flood aerial photographs, the water level [SOURCE: Page_10]

estimation method used in this study is detailed in [15]. The current section [SOURCE: Page_10]

presents the main steps and the general philosophy of this method. Using the [SOURCE: Page_10]

RADARSAT image, this approach provides spatially distributed water level [SOURCE: Page_10]

estimates within a ± 40 cm mean uncertainty. It can seem strange to reach [SOURCE: Page_10]

such accuracy with large pixels (25m). Nevertheless, more than to pixel size, [SOURCE: Page_10]

the accuracy in this process relates to DTM accuracy and the fact that the [SOURCE: Page_10]

vertical estimates concern only places with very low slopes and no perturbing [SOURCE: Page_10]

items (trees, houses ..). [SOURCE: Page_10]

3.1 Flood extent extraction [SOURCE: Page_10]

Flood extent mapping using SAR images is now a quite common issue [25] [SOURCE: Page_10]

because water appears with very low backscatter compared to other objects. [SOURCE: Page_10]

9 [SOURCE: Page_10]

In this study, to discriminate water from no-water on SAR images, radiomet- [SOURCE: Page_11]

ric thresholding has been used because it is a robust and reliable way [9]. [SOURCE: Page_11]

[15] shown that the use of a unique threshold value does not allow a satisfy- [SOURCE: Page_11]

ing flood extent extraction since flooded areas may sometimes have the same [SOURCE: Page_11]

backscattering values as non-flooded one in a SAR image. To deal with this [SOURCE: Page_11]

radiometric uncertainty, the discrimination of flooded and non-flooded pixels [SOURCE: Page_11]

is done using two threshold values. The first threshold value, Tmin , aims at de- [SOURCE: Page_11]

tecting only pixels that correspond to water bodies. Tmin has been determined [SOURCE: Page_11]

as the minimum radiometric value of non-flooded pixels inside grassland ar- [SOURCE: Page_11]

eas (outside the floodplain and outside the permanent water surfaces) [15]. [SOURCE: Page_11]

The second threshold value, Tmax , aims at detecting all flooded areas, at the [SOURCE: Page_11]

risk of detecting in addition non-flooded areas that have a similar radiometric [SOURCE: Page_11]

value to the flooded one. Tmax has been determined as the maximum radio- [SOURCE: Page_11]

metric value of water bodies outside the flooded area, using the SAR image [SOURCE: Page_11]

pixels located inside the Mirgenbach lake (see Fig. 1). The thresholding of the [SOURCE: Page_11]

SAR image using Tmin and Tmax provide a flood extent map with fuzzy limits, [SOURCE: Page_11]

coded as follows (see [15]), depending on the intensity I of the SAR image [SOURCE: Page_11]

pixels: 0 = non-flooded (I > Tmax ), 1 = flooded (I < Tmin ), 2 = fuzzy limit [SOURCE: Page_11]

(≈ potentially flooded) (Tmin ≤ I ≤ Tmax ). [SOURCE: Page_11]

The innovative point of the SAR image processing is the analysis of the rele- [SOURCE: Page_11]

vance of the remote sensing-derived flood extent limits for hydraulic purpose, [SOURCE: Page_11]

and especially for water level estimation. To estimate water levels, the flood [SOURCE: Page_11]

extent limits are merged with the underlying DEM. Using such a merging, [SOURCE: Page_11]

any erroneous flood extent limit will lead to errors in water level estimation. [SOURCE: Page_11]

Consequently, flood extent limits prone to error have to be identified before [SOURCE: Page_11]

the merging. Errors in the flood extent limits are mainly due to emerging [SOURCE: Page_11]

10 [SOURCE: Page_11]

objects such as building and high vegetation [15] that may mask water. To [SOURCE: Page_12]

treat this potential errors, it has been chosen to remove all SAR derived flood [SOURCE: Page_12]

extent limits located in habitat or vegetation areas. The remaining limits will [SOURCE: Page_12]

be called relevant limits hereafter. Furthermore, since the flood extent limits [SOURCE: Page_12]

are SAR image derived, they are prone to uncertainties arising mainly from [SOURCE: Page_12]

the image spatial resolution and its georeferencing precision. To take account [SOURCE: Page_12]

of these spatial uncertainties, the relevant limits have been enlarged (on both [SOURCE: Page_12]

sides) using a distance equal to the sum of the image spatial resolution (25 m [SOURCE: Page_12]

for the RADARSAT image) and the precision of the georeferencing (10 m for [SOURCE: Page_12]

the RADARSAT image), as proposed in [15]. Considering that errors have [SOURCE: Page_12]

been removed and that radiometric and spatial uncertainties have been taken [SOURCE: Page_12]

into account previously, these “enlarged” relevant limits are then assumed to [SOURCE: Page_12]

include the real flood extent limits (Hyp. 1 ). These are shaped as small patches [SOURCE: Page_12]

which are sparsely distributed along the floodplain (Fig. 4). [SOURCE: Page_12]

3.2 Preliminary water level estimation [SOURCE: Page_12]

The second part of the process estimates one range of possible water levels [SOURCE: Page_12]

IW LE = [W Lmin ; W Lmax ] for each relevant patch. To do so, the maximum [SOURCE: Page_12]

and the minimum elevation values are first extracted inside each relevant patch [SOURCE: Page_12]

using the DEM Z values. Next, the DEM altimetric uncertainty (uncertDEM ) [SOURCE: Page_12]

is taken into account by being respectively added/subtracted to the maxi- [SOURCE: Page_12]

mum/minimum values extracted previously for each relevant patch: [SOURCE: Page_12]

IW LE = [min (Zpatch ) − uncertDEM ; max (Zpatch ) + uncertDEM ]. [SOURCE: Page_12]

Since the DEM altimetric uncertainty is taken into account, Hyp. 1 allows to [SOURCE: Page_12]

assume that each range of water level estimation - IW LE - includes the real [SOURCE: Page_12]

11 [SOURCE: Page_12]

water level (Hyp. 2 ). [SOURCE: Page_13]

3.3 Final water level estimation [SOURCE: Page_13]

The last part of the process uses hydraulic rules to constrain the water level [SOURCE: Page_13]

estimates and reduce their uncertainty, as proposed firstly by [20, 21]. In a [SOURCE: Page_13]

floodplain, hydraulic laws manage the flow, so that water levels must follow [SOURCE: Page_13]

an hydraulic logic : hydraulic energy decreases from upstream to downstream. [SOURCE: Page_13]

With low flow velocity, like in the Mosel floodplain, this hydraulic rule can [SOURCE: Page_13]

be simplified into a decrease of water level in the flow direction (Hyp. 3 ). To [SOURCE: Page_13]

apply Hyp. 3 on the IW LE intervals, flow directions between patches (loca- [SOURCE: Page_13]

tions of the water levels) have to be determined. As proposed in [15], some [SOURCE: Page_13]

flow directions between patches have been determined using the shape of the [SOURCE: Page_13]

SAR derived flood extent and the lines perpendicular to the elevation contour [SOURCE: Page_13]

lines, oriented from the highest to the lowest elevation, called steeper lines [SOURCE: Page_13]

hereafter. As a matter of fact, the knowledge of a up-/downstream relation- [SOURCE: Page_13]

ship between two patches is conditioned by the connectivity of those on the [SOURCE: Page_13]

SAR derived flood extent. If no hole in the flood extent is present between [SOURCE: Page_13]

these two patches, an up-/downstream relationship can be determine. Fur- [SOURCE: Page_13]

thermore, since the image has been acquired at the beginning of the flood [SOURCE: Page_13]

recession, the main flow directions are assumed to be convergent toward the [SOURCE: Page_13]

river channel, following the steeper lines. The steeper lines around relevant [SOURCE: Page_13]

patches have been determined using the contour lines derived from the DEM. [SOURCE: Page_13]

As a matter of fact, the sense of an up-/downstream relationship is given by [SOURCE: Page_13]

the orientation of the steeper line. Using this criterion, some up/-downstream [SOURCE: Page_13]

12 [SOURCE: Page_13]

relationship between patches have been determined. At the floodplain scale, [SOURCE: Page_14]

these relationships constitute an hydraulic hierarchy of the relevant patches. [SOURCE: Page_14]

Consequently, according to Hyp. 3, the water level must decrease from the [SOURCE: Page_14]

patch A to the patch B if A is upstream of B. Due to Hyp. 2 this induce the [SOURCE: Page_14]

following constraints: [SOURCE: Page_14]

Nmax (B) ≤ Nmax (A) (constraint on the maxima), [SOURCE: Page_14]

Nmin (A) ≥ Nmin (B) (constraint on the minima). [SOURCE: Page_14]

To apply these constraints, the algorithm that has been developed, is flow [SOURCE: Page_14]

oriented and impose a decrease on the maxima from upstream to down- [SOURCE: Page_14]

stream, and vice et versa, an increase on the minima from downstream to [SOURCE: Page_14]

upstream. This finally provides intervals of constrained water level estimation [SOURCE: Page_14]

IW LE = [W Lmin ; W Lmax ], with a half mean range of about ± 40 cm. [SOURCE: Page_14]

As a consequence, the method allow the definition of a set of distributed [SOURCE: Page_14]

water levels across the floodplain at the satellite overpass time. These remote [SOURCE: Page_14]

sensing-derived water levels are then used as new observation for the hydraulic [SOURCE: Page_14]

modelling. [SOURCE: Page_14]

As a matter of fact, remote sensing derived data are assumed to be an obser- [SOURCE: Page_14]

vation of water depth along the floodplain. The single image is extracted from [SOURCE: Page_14]

the satellite image at 6:00 AM,Feb 28th,1997; The locations where the water [SOURCE: Page_14]

7   Conclusions [SOURCE: Page_26]

Part I of this study [16], presents a method to assimilate spatially distributed [SOURCE: Page_26]

water levels into a shallow water model. The present article (Part II) deals with [SOURCE: Page_26]

an application of the assimilation method into a real test case, in order to in- [SOURCE: Page_26]

vestigate the value of remote sensing-derived water levels for improving model [SOURCE: Page_26]

reliability, variational methods are used in assimilation. By using a variational [SOURCE: Page_26]

data assimilation approach (4D-var), we have investigated potential contribu- [SOURCE: Page_26]

tions of SAR derived spatially distributed water levels for the identification of [SOURCE: Page_26]

time-independent parameters (Manning coefficients) in a shallow-water flood [SOURCE: Page_26]

model. [SOURCE: Page_26]

The spatially distributed water levels have been derived from a SAR image [SOURCE: Page_26]

by employing the method developed in [15]. They have been obtained with a [SOURCE: Page_26]

± 40 cm mean uncertainty, using a RADARSAT-1 image of the 1997 flood [SOURCE: Page_26]

event of the Mosel river. This has been possible by using both an analysis [SOURCE: Page_26]

of the relevance of SAR derived flood extent limits for hydraulic purposes, [SOURCE: Page_26]

and a merge between the relevant limits and a highly resolution DEM, under [SOURCE: Page_26]

hydraulic coherence constraints inspired from [20, 21]. Such a water level es- [SOURCE: Page_26]

timation provides spatially distributed information at the time of a satellite [SOURCE: Page_26]

overpass while classical in situ measurements are punctually available. [SOURCE: Page_26]

25 [SOURCE: Page_26]

Furthermore, numerical experiments conducted in this study indicate that a [SOURCE: Page_27]

rather dense information in space is of great benefit for the identification of [SOURCE: Page_27]

unknown parameters (Manning friction coefficient). Indeed, the assimilation [SOURCE: Page_27]

of the SAR derived water levels, in addition to an incomplete discharge hy- [SOURCE: Page_27]

drograph, proves to be capable of identifying Manning friction coefficients, [SOURCE: Page_27]

while the ground data alone does not allow such an identification. Further- [SOURCE: Page_27]

more, a sensitivity analysis conducted by using the SAR derived water levels, [SOURCE: Page_27]

shows that a spatial distribution of the friction coefficient based on land-cover [SOURCE: Page_27]

may not necessarily lead to the better model results. Indeed, these water lev- [SOURCE: Page_27]

els, used as a guide in the sensitivity analysis, can define areas of Manning [SOURCE: Page_27]

friction homogeneity, without apparent link with land-cover. Such sensitivity [SOURCE: Page_27]

analysis may finally base the Manning parameter spatial distribution more on [SOURCE: Page_27]

the model hydraulic functioning, than on the land-cover. [SOURCE: Page_27]

The most important issue in flood forecast is to predict the flood peak. We [SOURCE: Page_27]

know that remote sensing images cannot be useful in predicting directly flood [SOURCE: Page_27]

peaks except in regions with very slow flood events. Our process leads to a [SOURCE: Page_27]

better model, more physically constrained, which helps to reduce equifinality, [SOURCE: Page_27]

and can be used for future predictions. [SOURCE: Page_27]
