1 Introduction 1   This special issue of the journal Environmental Science and Policy presents the outcomes of the 2   WETwin project (Enhancing the role of wetlands in integrated water resources management for 3 4   twinned river basins in EU, Africa and South-America in support of EU Water Initiatives), an 5   international research project funded by the FP7 program of the European Commission from 2008 to 6   2011. The overall objective of WETwin was to enhance the role of wetlands in basin-scale integrated 7 8   water resources management, with the aim of improving the community service functions while 9   conserving good ecological status. The project consortium consisted of research, educational and 10 governmental institutions from Africa, South-America and Europe, and the project drew on case 11 12   studies from the three continents, supporting the global exchange of expertise on wetland 13   management. 14 15 This paper introduces the overall approach developed under WETwin to evaluate wetland 16 17   management structures and solutions in data-poor contexts. It is primarily a theoretical discussion, 18   summarizing a conceptual framework which has evolved from seven very diverse case studies. A 19 20   structured, modular approach was devised which combined multi-criteria analysis (MCA), trade-off 21   analysis (TOA) and vulnerability analysis (VA), drawing on best available information, including 22   quantitative modelling, qualitative “expert opinion”, and local stakeholders' knowledge and values. 23 24 25 Details of implementation of the approach in the case studies are presented in other papers in this 26   volume. Depending on the concerns and skills of the stakeholders involved, individual case studies 27   focused on specific components of the framework, and this is reflected in the diversity of the papers 28 29   in this volume. Arias-Hidalgo et al. 2012 describe application of MCA to prioritise management 30   measures for the Abras de Mantequilla wetland in Ecuador. Liersch et al. (2012) and Pataki et al. 31   (2012) focus on vulnerability assessment in the Inner Niger Delta (IND) and Gemenc wetlands 32 33   respectively, while Cools et al. 2012a extend the concepts of VA to analyse adaptive capacity in the 34   IND, specifically in the context of human health. Namaalwa et al. (2012) illustrate the process of 35 characterizing the ecosystem services provided by the Namatala wetland in Uganda, and the current 36 37   trends in land use and management that jeopardise those services. Other papers report on technical 38   studies which underpin management approaches - for example, ecological niche models (Funk et al. 39 40 2012) and floodplain restoration options (Baart et al. 2012) for the Lobau wetlands in Austria; and a 41   study of the impact of water quality on aquatic biota in Abras de Mantequilla (Alvarez-Mieles et al. 42   2012). Two papers contrast experiences from the developed and developing worlds relating to 43 44   integrating wetlands into broader catchment management (Rebelo et al. 2012), and the impact of 45   institutional capacity on wetland management in different contexts in Africa and Europe 46   (Ostrovskaya et al. 2012). Finally the outcomes and conclusions of the project, drawing from all the 47 48   case studies, are synthesised in a closing paper (Cools et al. 2012b). 49 50   2 Project context and objectives 51 52 53   Wetlands are amongst the world’s most threatened ecosystems (MA 2005). The reasons behind this 54   are complex, related not only to land and water use within the wetland, but also to management of 55   upstream catchments, external pressures such as climate change and population growth, and 56 57   institutional factors affecting management such as unclear or overlapping spheres of authorities and 58   lack of effective power to enforce laws and regulations (Finlayson et al., 2005; IPCC 2007). As such, the aim is to manage for a range of functions, but to do this in ways that 33   protect and enhance ecological status. Adaptive management recognises management as an on- 34 going cyclical process, not an end point; the critical components of such an approach for wetlands 35 36   have been described by Dickens et al. Integrated water 38 39   resource management acknowledges that wetlands function within a hydrological context, where 40   the management of the catchment impacts on the health of the wetland; and the wetland 41   contributes to the overall functioning of the catchment (CIS, 2003; UNESCO, 2009). Participatory 42 43   planning and management recognise that local communities and stakeholders are ultimately both 44   the actors and the beneficiaries of management, and must be involved at all stages (UN, 1994). 45 46   The Conceptual Framework for wetland management developed in the WETwin project nests 47 48   adaptive management of the wetland within the adaptive management cycle of the river basin, with 49   on-going feedback between the two (Figure 2). In this framework, resilience is 16 considered to be a characteristic of the state of the whole system (including the institutional, bio- 17 18   physical, infrastructural and behavioural aspects); while robustness relates to specific management 19   options or solutions. 20 21   Vulnerability is usually described in terms of three components: exposure, sensitivity and adaptive 22 23   capacity.
